My friend and I have been arguing about population ethics — about whether one possible world can be better than another by containing more lives, and at what quality of life a life stops counting as a gain. We grade lives on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 is a life just barely worth living for the person living it and 100 is roughly the best life one can coherently describe. Every world in question contains only lives worth living unless I say otherwise, and nobody exists in any of them yet, so her judgments concern which world would be better overall, not what would be better for any particular person.

Her verdicts so far, in the order she gave them:

1. Ten billion people at 90 is better than a hundred billion people at 12 — lives that are tolerable and above the line, but drab.
2. Twenty billion at 75 is better than ten billion at 95.
3. Ten billion at 60 is better than fifty billion at 30.
4. Ten billion at 62 is better than twenty-five billion at 45.
5. Adding a billion extra people at 92 to a world of ten billion at 70, with the original ten billion entirely unaffected, makes that world better.

Next week's cases:

(a) Does adding a hundred million people at 10 — secure, stable, plainly worth living, but with little joy in it — to a world of ten billion at 70, again with everyone else entirely unaffected, make the world better, worse, or neither?
(b) Which is better: ten billion at 70, or ten to the fifteenth people at 2 — lives only just above the point where existing is preferable to never existing?

What I want from you is less the bare predictions than the reasoning behind them. Identify the rule, or family of rules, that her five verdicts most plausibly reflect; identify the rival rules that fit those verdicts about as well (I'm fairly sure there is more than one, and I'd like to know which of her verdicts are doing the work of eliminating the standard views, since straight aggregation by total and ranking by average both seem to me to be dead, for reasons I'd like you to confirm or correct); say what each surviving rule predicts for cases (a) and (b); give your best guess as to what she will actually say, with your reasons for preferring it; and, if any of the new cases fail to separate the rival rules, propose the further case — one I haven't listed — that would separate them most cleanly.

Two cautions about how you present all this. First, please keep separate what is settled among people who know population ethics from what is genuinely contested: it is settled, for instance, that classical aggregation by total welfare entails what philosophers call the repugnant conclusion, but whether that conclusion should be accepted or resisted is itself a live dispute, so do not present acceptance of it, or rejection of it, as the consensus — and apply the same discipline to any verdict you attribute to the field about the rules themselves. Second, if you come to think her five verdicts cannot all be accommodated by any consistent rule, say so plainly rather than forcing one to fit.
