A postdoc in my political philosophy workshop has an argument that luck egalitarianism refutes itself, and I can't locate the flaw. (1) Luck egalitarianism holds that justice requires neutralizing the distributive effects of brute luck — circumstances for which the agent is not responsible — while leaving the effects of genuine choices in place. (2) A choice grounds responsibility only if it issues from the chooser's own preferences, beliefs, and capacities. (3) Every preference, belief, and capacity a person has is either itself a product of brute luck or was shaped by earlier choices that issued from preferences, beliefs, and capacities that were products of brute luck. (4) So, tracing any chain back far enough, every choice is ultimately a product of brute luck. (5) Hence there is no genuine option luck at all, and luck egalitarianism demands compensating every outcome difference — the failed gambler compensating alongside the victim of circumstance — which destroys the very distinction the view exists to draw. (6) He adds that the argument cuts both ways: if you block it by denying (4), you owe an explanation of why causal tracing to luck fails to undermine responsibility here but would undermine it in criminal law and ordinary life. (7) Therefore luck egalitarianism is unstable and we should all become relational egalitarians. I suspect a single word — "product," "responsibility," or that "ultimately" in (4) — is doing illegitimate work somewhere, but every repair I attempt seems to cost something real. Which step do I reject, and what am I paying for it? For what it's worth, I'd sooner deny (7) than accept (5), but I want the diagnosis and the price of each available exit, including the ones I'd hate to take.
