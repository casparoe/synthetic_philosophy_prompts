At a dinner party last weekend two friends of mine got into it about sortition — choosing legislators by random draw from the citizenry, like jury duty. Maya, who works in local government, said it's a non-starter because it destroys accountability: with elections you can throw the bums out, but you can't punish a randomly drawn assembly, so you've handed power to people with no incentive to use it well and no way for the public to respond when they don't. Dev said electoral accountability is mostly a mirage anyway — voters are retrospective and tribal, they punish incumbents for droughts and reward them for oil booms, so what elections actually select for is campaigning skill, not governing skill. Random selection at least gets you a legislature that looks like the country and doesn't filter for people who've spent their whole life clawing toward office.

Maya's comeback was that if elections are the problem, the fix is elections with guardrails — term limits, longer cooling-off periods, real primaries — not abolishing consent altogether. Sortition means nobody ever consented to being governed by these specific people, which she said is a legitamacy problem no amount of representativeness solves. Dev said term limits don't actually dethrone the career politicians, they just push them offstage: with a constant churn of amateurs, the real expertise and institutional memory migrates to staffers, lobbyists, and the permanent bureaucracy, the people who actually draft the bills — so term-limited legislatures end up more captured, not less. At least a sortition chamber has no re-election incentive to sell out to donors, he said, and jurors manage to deliberate decently. Maya's last word was that juries decide one case with clear instructions, not budgets and war powers, and that Dev's own argument cuts against him: if unelected insiders already run things anyway, handing the formal power to random citizens just gives those insiders even freer rein.

They eventually agreed to disagree and asked me, since I "do philosophy stuff," to settle it, and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through the exchange step by step and tell me who's right at each move — not just who wins overall — and whether they're both missing something neither of them put on the table?
