My brother-in-law and my roommate got into it at Thanksgiving, and I couldn't follow who was actually winning. Some background: I've been building a little VR project on the side, and the docs keep going on about keeping "motion-to-photon latency" under 20ms or people feel sick, which somehow spiraled into me reading Dennett's Consciousness Explained. That's what started the whole thing.

Anyway, the argument. My roommate (neuro-ish, reads a lot) said the old idea of a Cartesian theater — some central place in the brain where everything "comes together" for an audience, i.e., you — is refuted. His exhibit was the color phi phenomenon: flash a red dot on one side of a screen and a blue dot on the other in quick succession, and people see one dot moving across and changing color midway. He said the brain obviously can't have shown you the dot changing color as it happened, since whether it changes at all depends on what happens later; so the brain must be editing and backdating a narrative, which means there's no single live feed, just multiple drafts, and "the moment it all comes together" never comes.

My brother-in-law wasn't convinced. He said all phi shows is that editing happens somewhere before experience; an editor can still hand a final cut to an audience. If the brain delivers an already-backdated story to consciousness, you've moved the script around in time but kept the theater, and nothing in the experiment rules that out.

Roommate answered that this just pushes the same question back a step: if you say the edited content is finally presented somewhere, you owe an account of what makes THAT moment the moment of presentation — Dennett's point that you can keep promising the show will start one screen further in, but at no screen does anything happen except ordinary neural stuff. Content is just fixed at different times for different features (when it affects memory or report), and that's it.

Brother-in-law's last word: fine for color, but what about unity? We experience the dot's motion, its color, and its position as one scene, not as separately fixed features trickling in. If fixation is smeared across time like the roommate says, something has to glue the features into a single experience — and whatever does the gluing looks an awful lot like the very "comes together" place they just buried. So either there's a late stage that is basically a theater again, or the multiple-drafts view has to deny the unified experience we all know from the inside, which seems like a lot to give up.

They eventually moved on to pie, and I still don't know who got the better of it.
