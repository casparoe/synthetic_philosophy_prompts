Please argue for the following view of Newcomb's problem: the whole one-boxing/two-boxing fight is an artifact of classifying the problem wrong. People normally present it as you versus a state of nature ("box B already contains either $1M or nothing, that's just a fact about the world when you choose"), and once you buy that description, two-boxing is obviously dominant and the only way to rescue one-boxing is to smuggle in some evidential hocus-pocus. But argue instead that Newcomb's problem is really a Stackelberg game: the predictor is a leader who moved first and committed to a conditional policy — fill box B if and only if it predicts you'll one-box — and the "already fixed" contents of the box are just the residue of that policy having played out. From that angle, the question isn't "how should I treat this opaque box," it's "what is my best response to a leader who has locked in a commitment device," and the answer is as unexciting as not defecting against someone who credibly committed to rewarding cooperation. The paradox, on this view, is generated entirely by re-describing the predictor's strategic move as a move by nature, which is exactly the kind of re-description that would make any commitment look irrational in hindsight ("the promise has already been made, so I might as well break it"). Please develop this argument, say what it gets right about why two-boxers feel so confident, and then consider the strongest objection you can think of: namely, that the predictor is NOT a strategic player with its own payoffs but just a mechanism, in which case modeling it as a Stackelberg leader attributes agency where there is only machinery — and assess whether the commitment-based argument survives that objection or not. Bonus points if you can connect it to Schelling's old point about how rational agents pay a real cost for the ability to make binding threats and promises.
