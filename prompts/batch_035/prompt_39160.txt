I'm taking a seminar on collective responsibility, and we've just finished a couple of weeks on the Fischer controversy, which I gather was a huge fight among German historians in the 60s. My understanding, sketchy as it is: Fritz Fischer published a book arguing that Germany's leadership in 1914 didn't stumble into the First World War but deliberately risked it to grab territory, that the famous September Programme of war aims shows how expansive their ambitions were, and -- this was the really explosive bit -- that there was a straightish line from those Wilhelmine ambitions to Nazi expansionism later on. The book detonated a national argument in West Germany, partly because it looked like it reopened the war-guilt question that Versailles had supposedly settled, and partly because the continuity claim seemed to point a finger at something bigger than a Kaiser and his generals.

For my final paper I want to write about what all this means for whether you can blame a *nation* for a war, as opposed to a government or a set of decision-makers. But I'm honestly not sure what the real takeaway is, and the seminar readings mostly argue about the historiography (was Fischer right about the documents, how much did his critics have their own agenda) rather than about the conceptual fallout.

Please describe the most important implications of the Fischer controversy for the question of holding a nation responsible for a war -- things like: does it matter for blame whether the population was never consulted but plenty of people cheered the war on, or profited from it afterwards; what happens to the idea of national responsibility if you accept Fischer's continuity thesis (a whole society supposedly carrying a thread of aggressive intent across a regime change) versus if you reject it and blame falls on a few dozen men in 1914; and whether the fierce resistence to Fischer in Germany itself tells us something about how nations metabolise blame, e.g. that the refusal to accept the charge was itself part of the phenomenon we'd be trying to assign responsibility for. I'd especially like the implications that would still matter even if Fischer's archival claims turned out to be half-wrong, since the conceptual questions about collective responsiblity seem to survive whatever the historical details do.
