Background: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist (40 years, mostly superconductivity and soft matter, so I've lived my whole life with effective theories and order parameters). I got dragged into philosophy around retirement by a former student and now I can't stop reading it, which my wife finds hilarious.

Lately I've been trying to follow the interpretability literature on neural networks, and there's apparently a live debate about whether the things researchers find in these models — "features," "circuits," "representations of concepts" — are really there, or whether they're useful fictions, artifacts of the tools, something the researchers read into the network. People talk about this like it's a new question. To me it smells extremely familiar, but I want to check that instinct against someone who knows both sides.

So here's what I'm asking for. Restate the debate over whether neural networks have representations (the realism question in interpretability — are the features discovered things or invented things, is "the network represents dogs" like a measurement or like a convenient bookkeeping device) in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. I mean things like: effective degrees of freedom, phonons and quasiparticles, order parameters, renormalization and which variables survive coarse-graining, phenomenological models vs. microscopic ones, the difference between a latent variable in a statistical model and an entity you'd say is physically real. I'm fluent in all of that, treat me accordingly. I'm NOT fluent in the philosophy of mind literature, so if you need a term from there, define it when it shows up rather than assuming I've read Fodor or whoever.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly onto, e.g., "is a phonon real," which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary. I have a tentative guess I want pressure-tested: that "the network represents X" is just what an effective degree of freedom looks like when the coarse-graining was selected by training on a task, and the realism question dissolves the same way it dissolved for phonons — they're as real as anything gets at that level of description. But I can see one thing my vocabulary has no slot for: a phonon can't be *wrong*. It doesn't represent anything, it just propagates. Whereas people say a neural network can misrepresent, it can have a concept that it applies to the wrong things, it can be decieved by inputs. Is that a genuine residue — something my physics vocabulary cannot say and maybe needs to — or is "misrepresentation" just a fancy word for "the classifier made an error," which after all is just a fact about the input-output map, no more mysterious than a mean-field approximation failing outside its regime? Or to put it the other way around: is there anything the interpretability people are fighting about that has no counterpart in my world, and if so, is that because their debate is less substantive than it looks, or because my vocabulary is missing something?

Don't be shy about telling me if my phonon analogy is smuggling in an assumption — e.g. that the relevant level of description is fixed by the system rather than partly by what we're trying to do with it. I'd rather find out I'm wrong now than after I've embarrassed myself at the reading group.
