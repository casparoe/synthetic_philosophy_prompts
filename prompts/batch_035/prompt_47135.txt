I write the wall labels and tour captions for a small regional museum — local history and natural history — and the final wording of every claim is mine to settle, though a curator signs off. Each week brings about a dozen decisions of the same shape: a label says something, and I have to choose between stating it flat, hedging it, attributing it to someone, or leaving it out. Stating flat: "This bowl was made around 1200 BCE." Hedging: "was probably made." Attributing: "our curator believes." Leaving out: no claim at all.

The problem is that we have no rule for which of these a given claim deserves, and I have been trying to build one on my own. Here is as far as I have got, and where I am stuck.

Flat statements seem to require more than being true. A claim about a famous oak tree might be true, but if our only ground for it is that older guidebooks repeated it for a century, the flat statement still seems wrong even in the lucky case where it turns out true. On the other hand, some claims have been stated flat for decades on solid documentary grounds, and then the grounds collapse — a reattribution, a redating — and I cannot decide whether those flat statements were already wrong at the time or only became wrong. So the distinction I keep circling is between what is merely true, what we have good grounds for, and what we actually know. The middle one and the last one come apart in exactly our hard cases, and I do not know which of them should govern a flat statement on a museum wall. I suspect philosophers have a name for this question — when it is permissible to assert something outright — and I would like the procedure grounded in whatever has actually been worked out there rather than in my half-theory. Please do not attach a reading list or name-drop articles; I want the reasoning itself, and I will chase references later if I ever need them.

Write me a procedure I can run in a few minutes per label. Specify: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the output at each exit point — flat, hedged, attributed, or dropped; the point at which the questions stop and a human decides, because what remains is a matter of judgment about our audience or our mission rather than about the claim; and where the procedure should be honest that a step is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item. Then run it on three cases we face or have recently faced:

1. A fossil label where the dating method is one we trust, applied by people we trust, with a published margin of error.
2. A portrait long labeled as the work of a minor master, which one respected specialist has recently declared a workshop copy while the rest of the field has not engaged; the old label appears in every guidebook, and changing it will draw complaints.
3. A local legend that a famous historical figure planted the oak at our entrance — repeated in our own older materials and beloved by visitors, with no primary evidence either way. The director wants it kept because it draws people in. This is the case where I expect the procedure to give an answer the director will not like, and I want it to give that answer anyway, with the reasoning stated openly.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the cases where two careful people running it in good faith will reach different outputs, and what I should do when that happens. Keep it usable by someone with no philosophical training — me — under deadline, with the curator reading over my shoulder.
