my partner and i keep fighting about whether you're ever allowed to believe things ahead of the evidence (she's basically team clifford, i have jamesian sympathies but not much more than sympathies), and before i go read anything else i want the spec first. write me the desiderata for an adequate ethics of belief: what a theory in this area actually has to deliver, with the case behind each requirement. it has to give a verdict on the shipowner, including the version where the ship arrives safely anyway — does the wrongness depend on the outcome? it has to handle james's forced, momentous, live options like the religious one, where suspending belief isn't neutral because you lose the good either way. and it has to deal with two cases i keep stubbing my toe on:

1. self-fulfilling beliefs — the patient who believes she'll recover, where the believing itself causally raises her chances, so the belief ends up generating its own evidence. is believing ahead of the evidence a wrong there, or maybe even a duty, or does the theory need a whole third category?
2. beliefs about people — someone who without evidence believes their partner is faithful vs someone who without evidence believes their coworker is dishonest. same epistemic crime? or does the content of a belief change its epistemic status and not just its moral one?

it also has to respect that belief isn't under direct voluntary control — i can't just decide to believe there's a million dollars in my account — without letting "i couldn't help believing it" become a universal excuse, and i'd like to know how you'd draw that line. flag any requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, that's the part i actually care about most, and end with a couple sentences on which single requirement clifford fails and which one james fails, if either does.
