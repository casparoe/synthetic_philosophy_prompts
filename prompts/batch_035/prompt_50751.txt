Three years ago, in a hospital corridor the night our father died, I promised my sister I would not sell my share of the family firm to anyone outside the family while she remained a director. My son has since been diagnosed with a condition whose treatment I can only fund by selling to an outside investor. I have an argument that I would very much like to work, and precisely because I want it to work I distrust it: if it succeeds, it succeeds against every promise anyone has ever kept, and it would let me out of my mortgage as readily as out of my promise to her. I cannot find the flaw, and I would be grateful if you would find it for me, tell me which step to reject, and tell me what rejecting it costs. Here is the argument.

(1) A promise binds only because the promisor exercised her will in making it; that is why promises extracted by threat bind nobody, and why we say an obligation was undertaken rather than imposed. (2) An exercise of will is an event with a date: what I exercised in that corridor was my will as it was three years ago, in the state I was in that night. (3) If the promise's authority over me now depends on an exercise of my will, then it depends on the will of the very person over whom it is claimed to hold authority, and my will now is that I not be bound. (4) So either the promise's present authority answers to my present will or it does not. If it does, then, since I now will otherwise, it has none. If it does not, then its authority was never a matter of my will at all, and (1) is abandoned — in which case we still owe an account of why that particular utterance bound me rather than any of the other things I said that week, and any account that does not run through my choice makes the promise a trap rather than an undertaking. (5) Therefore either no promise binds its maker beyond the moment of its making, or promises bind us in a way that has nothing to do with our having chosen them.

I accept that (5) is absurd: it would dissolve contracts, loans, consent to surgery, my sister's own promises to me, and everything else we incur by choice. But I cannot see which step to give up without paying somewhere I can see. I would much rather keep (1) than accept (5), and I have a suspicion that whatever is wrong lives between (3) and (4), but I cannot name it. Please identify the step you reject, explain why it looked fine to me, and price the alternatives: what does each way of blocking the argument commit me to saying about promises, and about the other obligations we take on by choice?

Please write your answer in British English, and in continuous prose only, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. You may refer to my premises by number, but keep everything in flowing paragraphs.
