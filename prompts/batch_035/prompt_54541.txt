I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions to unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them or if I'm just vibing. Frictionless planes and point masses: obviously fine, that's just physics. Aquaplanet simulations, where climate scientists delete all the continents and run the atmosphere on a water world: also fine, you learn how circulation works. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in genetics, which assumes infinite population, random mating, no mutation, no selection — every assumption literally false, and yet fine, it's a useful null model. The no-trade theorem in finance, which rests on common priors basically nobody has: fine, and somehow it's *because* the assumptions are false that it teaches you something about why trade happens. Moving up the ladder: Friedman's line that an expert pool player acts *as if* he solves the differential equations of the shot — I buy that for predicting where the ball goes, and I'll even go along with the version for firms acting as if they maximize profits, when it's used to predict prices. But when a central bank takes a DSGE model with a single representative household and uses it to evaluate the welfare effects of a tax reform, something in me rebels — the false assumption seems to be doing the work of the conclusion. And a model where unemployment comes out "voluntary" by construction feels straight up dishonest, the answer is baked in before you start. My friend Marco, who does macro, says I'm being inconsistent: if a water-world Earth is a legitimate move, a one-household economy is too, and I can't quite articulate why he's wrong.

For what it's worth, I have a tentative taxonomy from another friend, who says unrealistic assumptions come in three flavors: ones that say "this effect is real but small, ignore it" (like friction on a slow ramp), ones that restrict the domain ("true only for small oscillations," "only in the near-field"), and ones that are flat out false but used as a stepping stone to get somewhere tractable (perfectly rational agents, ideal gases). She claims that once you see the three types you can stop arguing about whether models are "realistic" and just ask which type an assumption is and whether the conclusion is sensitive to it. Maybe. But it's not obvious to me that the taxonomy actually predicts my verdicts — the representative household and the aquaplanet both look like stepping-stone assumptions, and I give them opposite grades. And it doesn't touch Friedman's bigger claim, the one people call the F-twist, that the more significant a theory is the more unrealistic its assumptions are going to be — which I find simultaneously obviously deranged and weirdly right, and I'd like to know if any principle can make sense of that ambivalence instead of just ignoring it.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to have a verdict on new cases. Say which of my verdicts motivates which clause, and be honest about which verdict(s) it can't save — I'd rather lose a verdict than keep them all with some baroque epicycle. Then run it on some cases I haven't mentioned, like assuming agents never make mistakes in an evolutionary model of cooperation, or calibrating a safety valve with the ideal gas law. And most importantly, settle the one I keep going back and forth on: Rawls's original position — parties behind a veil of ignorance, perfectly rational, perfectly impartial, used to derive principles of justice. Is that a frictionless plane for normative theory, a legitimate stepping stone, or is it the unemployment-is-voluntary problem all over again, with the conclusion smuggled in via the idealization? My intuitions genuinely point both ways depending on the day and I'd like to know what your principle says and how confident it should make me.
