A friend of mine who's way into philosophy of religion tried to explain the ontological argument to me at a party last week, and I wrote down his summary after. Here it is more or less verbatim-ish: "The ontological argument is the claim that you can prove God exists purely from the definition of God, no empirical premises at all. Anselm started it in the 11th century: God is 'a being than which nothing greater can be conceived,' and existing in reality is greater than existing only in the mind, so God has to exist in reality. Descartes cleaned this up by defining God as a supremely perfect being and noting that existence is one of the perfections, so God exists by definition. Kant refuted the whole family of arguments by showing that existence isn't a real predicate — you can't build existence into a definition the way 'unmarried' is built into 'bachelor.' Case closed for two centuries, until Gödel of all people revived it with modal logic: he proved that if God's existence is even possible, then God necessarily exists, and since no one can prove God's existence is impossible, God exists. Everyone agrees the derivation is formally valid, so the only question is whether Gödel's axioms are true, and they basically just assume the conclusion in mathematical dress."

Okay so, some of this smells off to me but I don't have the background to say where. A couple things I'd want checked. He presents Anselm and Descartes as doing the same basic move — is that right, or is that a conflation of two arguments that a defender would want to keep apart (the Anselm version sounds more like a reductio about existing "in the understanding" vs. "in reality," whatever exactly that means, while the Descartes version is about predicates/perfections)? Did Kant's existence-isn't-a-predicate point actually kill all the versions, or does it just target some of them — I have a vague sense the modal versions don't treat existence as an ordinary predicate, but I might be making that up. On Gödel: did his proof really go "if possible then necessary, and nobody can rule out possible"? That last bit ("since no one can prove God's existence is impossible") feels like a sleight of hand to me — going from *we can't disprove it* to *it's possible* — but maybe that's not what Gödel actually claimed, maybe his axioms do something different. Also my friend says Gödel apparently never published the thing in his lifetime and only showed it to a few logicians, which seems odd if it's as airtight as all that. Please go through the summary claim by claim: what would a philosopher of religion accept as basically fine, where is he conflating two things or overstating, and does his overall takeaway (it's all just trying to define something into existence, Gödel just dressed that up in symbols) actually follow from whichever parts are true? For what it's worth I'm half-inclined to take the Gödel part seriously purely because it's Gödel, so feel free to disabuse me of that too.
