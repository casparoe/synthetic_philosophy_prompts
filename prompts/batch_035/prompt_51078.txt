I'm a retired electrician who watches a lot of nature documentaries, and something about the way they talk has started to bother me. The narrators are constantly explaining things by what they're for: the woodpecker's skull is built thick so the brain doesn't get rattled, the moth has eye spots so predators attack the wrong end, we blush so others can see we're embarrassed. Nobody designed any of this, and no woodpecker ever sat down and decided to grow a thicker skull. Yet these explanations feel genuinely informative — you can test them, and they can turn out to be wrong (I gather the giraffe's neck story is more complicated than just reaching high leaves). A friend who took a couple of philosophy courses told me there's a name for this phenomenon: free-floating rationales, reasons floating around with no reasoner attached to them. And it's not only biology. My grandmother's jam recipe has you invert the hot jars for ten minutes, and nobody in the family remembers why; when I finally looked it up, it turns out the seal forms better that way. So there was a reason for that step all along, apparently, even after the person who knew it died, and even though my mother kept doing it without any reason in mind at all.

Here's what I'd like someone to untangle for me. What does this whole practice of giving, checking, and disputing what-is-it-for explanations presuppose? It seems like the practice only makes sense if reasons can exist without anyone to have them — that the moth's spots have a reason even though no one, moth or man, ever reasoned about them. But maybe that's too strong: perhaps the documentaries are just speaking in shorthand, and talk about what a thick skull is for really means something with no purposes in it at all, just a causal story about which variants happened to survive. If it's only shorthand, though, why does it feel like there's a genuine fact of the matter to be right or wrong about, one that isn't merely the question of what historically happened? And where would the line fall? Does a river have a reason to meander, or a planet a reason to orbit, or does the practice quietly stop somewhere short of that? I'd also like to know whether the practice is even consistent with itself, since the same programs cheerfully insist that nature has no purposes whatsoever and then go on explaining everything as though it did. And if we instead insisted, strictly, that reasons require reasoners, what exactly would we have to give up or say differently — could the biologist still say everything the old way said, just after some elaborate translation, or would something real be lost?
