I'm a high school junior taking a comparative government elective, and we've just started a unit on political corruption. Our teacher drew a distinction between two ways of defining corruption that I keep coming back to. One centers on public office and rules: corruption is the misuse of a public position for private advantage, so what makes an act corrupt is that it breaks or evades the norms of impartiality attached to the role — concealment, favoritism, self-dealing — regardless of whether anyone is actually made worse off. The other centers on outcomes and fairness: corruption is an exchange that cheats the public or entrenches inequality, so an act can be corrupt because of what it does to people, even if no written rule was ever touched. Nearly every example in our textbook is one where the two definitions agree — a hidden bribe, a bag of cash — and our class debate about whether Western anti-corruption standards are universal keeps stalling because nobody has a case where the definitions actually point in different directions.

Construct a thought experiment for me in which the two definitions give opposite verdicts, and specifically in the harder direction: the exchange must come out clean under the office-and-rules conception — fully disclosed in advance, no personal enrichment, no rule broken or bent — yet still come out corrupt under the outcome-and-inequality conception. In other words, I want a case where the official does everything by the book, in the open, and the corruption verdict has to be generated entirely by the pattern of who gets what, not by any misconduct. Constraints: keep it realistic and local — a city council, a school district, a municipal services office, no cartoon villains and no dramatic crimes — and confine the scenario itself to one paragraph. It also must not cheat by stipulation: the case should not simply announce that harm occurred; the unequal or entrenching character of the exchange should be visible in the structure of the scenario itself.

After the scenario, walk through what each conception says about it and why, and identify which single feature of the case is doing the work in producing the disagreement. Then tell me what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into an ordinary example of legal-but-just-politics? Finally, explain briefly how a defender of the office-and-rules conception would attack the case rather than accept its verdict, since I want to understand whether they would deny the facts, reframe them as ordinary governance, or bite the bullet and say a pattern of individually clean acts can never compose into corruption. Please assume no background beyond an AP Government level, and feel free to point out if you think one of my constraints makes the case harder than it needs to be.
