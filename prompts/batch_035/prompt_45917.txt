Having followed rationalist blogs since the early LessWrong days, I've long noticed a parallel that I'd like an expert to unpack. Assume the role of a professional moral philosopher with a strong grasp of both the academic literature on consequentialism and the rationalist blogosphere. Compare the problem of consequentialist cluelessness, as Hilary Greaves develops it, with the "fragility of value" thesis from LessWrong. Roughly: cluelessness says our evidence about the long-run effects of our actions is swamped by unknowable ripple effects, so we cannot determine which of our options does the most good; fragility of value says human value is a small and complicated target in the space of possible value systems, such that even a near-optimal specification that drops a single component tends to produce a future with almost none of the value. Both threaten the action-guidingness of consequentialist reasoning, but the first locates the problem in our epistemic position while the second locates it in the structure of value itself. Before writing your answer, brainstorm the candidate points of contact and divergence. Then address: (i) is fragility of value just cluelessness restated, a special case of it, or a genuinely distinct problem?; (ii) does the distinction survive a thought experiment in which a morally ideal, fully informed agent — no epistemic limits whatsoever — is handed a slightly misspecified utility function?; and (iii) which of the two, if either, should actually worry an effective altruist deciding between near-term and long-term interventions, and why? Reason carefully and justify your verdicts rather than merely surveying positions.
