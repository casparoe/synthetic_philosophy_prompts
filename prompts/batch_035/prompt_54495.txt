I've come to think that some form of panpsychism — the view that experience exists at a very basic physical level, not only in brains like ours — is more plausible than I once did. What keeps me from going further is the combination problem: how could vast numbers of extremely simple experiences, presumably located in the micro-constituents of my brain, add up to the single, unified experience that is me, here, now? My credence that this problem has a coherent solution in principle — not that panpsychism is true, just that this particular obstacle can be overcome — sits at about 0.55. Here is the part I especially want your judgment on, and please be unsparing with it. My instinct, from forty years behind mixing consoles, is that combination seems possible in a way people may not have taken seriously: on a mixing desk, dozens of independent signals are routed through a bus and come out as a single stereo output. Nothing in any individual track contains the mix, the mix contains nothing over and above the tracks plus their routing and levels, and yet the output is genuinely one thing, not a heap of parallel things. Something similar happens when an orchestra plays: the audience doesn't hear forty separate musical lines side by side; they hear one sound with internal structure. I genuinely don't know whether this is a promising model of experiential combination or merely a seductive metaphor that works because sound waves superpose in a way experiences presumably don't. If it's a model, the obvious worry is that the routing and summing are done by non-experiential structure — the console, the air — and the panpsychist is supposed to be exactly the person who doesn't get to invoke non-experiential structure to do the interesting work.
