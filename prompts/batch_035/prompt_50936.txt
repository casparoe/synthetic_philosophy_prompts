I run a small behavioral coaching practice, and a fair portion of my business is commitment contracts: clients pay a refundable deposit that they forfeit if they miss agreed targets — gym sessions, writing pages, quit dates for vaping. Last night my oldest friend, who trained as an economist, and I argued about this until almost two in the morning. His position was that the whole offering is incoherent: a rational person would just do the thing, so anyone who signs a contract is either irrational (which the contract doesn't fix) or rational but weak-willed (in which case the contract is a tax on a character flaw). My position was half-formed — something about how signing changes what the later choice looks like from where the client stands now, and about cases like Kavka's toxin puzzle, where the reward attaches to the state you're in now rather than to anything you do later — but I couldn't turn it into anything crisp, and it occurred to me, somewhere around our third round, that I don't actually have a principled basis for steering particular clients toward or away from contracts. I do it on intuition.

Write me a decision procedure I can run with a new client during a single intake session (about forty-five minutes). It should consist of ordered questions to work through together, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and clear exits: where the procedure returns a firm "no contract," where it returns "sign, and structure it this way," and where it honestly hands the decision back to me and the client because the question is one of values the procedure can't settle. Ground it in the real decision theory here — precommitment versus mere resolution, the difference between changing your future incentives and merely betting against yourself, and the toxin-style case where what's being purchased is the intention itself rather than the later behavior — but it has to be usable by someone with no philosophy background, and it has to survive my economist friend reading it, so no hand-waving about willpower. Be explicit about where you've simplified and what the simplification costs.

Then run it on three clients. First: a woman who wants to quit vaping before her daughter's October wedding, with a forfeit for each day she's caught with a vape. Second: a man who wants to finish a novel and proposes a hundred-dollar penalty per missed writing day, though he has never once sustained a writing habit for more than a week. Third — and this is the one that keeps me up at night, and where I suspect my friend has the better of it — a client whose couples counselor has told him the counseling will only work if he genuinely intends to stay in the marriage, and who has proposed a heavy forfeit for leaving within two years, in the hope that signing will produce the sincere intention that the counseling requires. He would, I think, most prefer to have the intention and never face the choice at all.

For at least one of the three, the procedure should give an answer I will not like; flag which one and why. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do — the cases where running it would produce confident nonsense.
