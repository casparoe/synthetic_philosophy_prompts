For thirty-four years I worked as an analytical chemist, mostly in spectroscopy, where the daily problem was deciding whether a signal distinguishable from noise was really there at all. Since retiring I have been reading ethics seriously for the first time — Parfit, then the spectrum arguments of Stuart Rachels and, more recently, Temkin's Rethinking the Good — and my reading group has asked me to write up my position on whether "better than" is transitive so the others can scrutinize it. Before I embarrass myself in front of them, I want an audit of the package I have drifted into. It is this:

(a) "Better than" is transitive: if A is better than B and B better than C, then A is better than C. Whether one phrases the demand with "better than" or with "at least as good as," I take it to be the same requirement of coherence, and I hold it about as firmly as I hold that a quantity exceeding a second, which exceeds a third, exceeds the third. My career was built on relations of that shape.

(b) Indifference is not transitive. Sweeten a cup of tea one undetectable grain at a time: each cup is, as far as anyone could ever tell, exactly as good as its neighbor, yet the first and last cups are plainly not equal. In psychophysics we never treated this as a paradox — a discrimination threshold simply makes pairwise indistinguishability intransitive — and I see no reason evaluative comparisons should behave differently.

(c) Where two options differ only imperceptibly in well-being, one cannot go wrong in choosing either. A difference in how well a life goes that no one living it could ever notice is not something a choice can turn on.

(d) Imperceptible benefits aggregate across persons. If I can confer a benefit too small for any single beneficiary ever to detect on each of several million people, at modest cost to myself, I am obligated to do so; this is precisely the reasoning on which my charitable giving rests, and I do not intend to give it up lightly.

(e) Comparisons are complete. For any two outcomes, one is better than the other, or they are exactly equal. I have heard it suggested that some pairs are neither — that they are "on a par," in Ruth Chang's sense — but this strikes me as an evaluative relation that occasionally refuses to exist, and in the laboratory we never accepted "incomparable" as an outcome of a measurement; we built a better instrument.

(f) What is better is what one has most reason to choose. I see no daylight between the ranking of outcomes and the ranking that should govern choice; a gap between them looks to me like a place to hide from an argument.

Laid side by side, these feel to me as though they cannot all be true, though I confess I cannot locate exactly where the trouble is. My instinct says (a) and (b) are flatly contradictory, though wiser heads have hinted the real action lies elsewhere. Please go through the set as an auditor rather than a lecturer: identify which pairs, or larger groups, stand in genuine tension and which only appear to, distinguishing tensions that dissolve under a charitable re-reading of a commitment from those that survive every reading I could plausibly accept. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally fatal. For each, say what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most, and what I would be giving up with it. I would rather know precisely where I stand than be reassured.
