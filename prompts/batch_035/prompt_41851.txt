I co-organize a graduate reading group that has spent this term on assertion and testimony, working through the debate that grew up around Williamson's knowledge rule and its truth-norm and justification-norm rivals. At our last meeting we discussed a case of our own construction: a biology teacher asserts to her class that humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor. She has read the literature, understands the evidence, and testifies sincerely because she takes herself to have a professional duty to convey the science; yet, for reasons of religious upbringing, she cannot bring herself to believe the proposition, and so — on most views — does not know it. I'm told cases of roughly this shape circulate in the literature under the label of selfless assertion. One member of the group declared the example irrelevant to our debate because "knowledge is the norm of assertion": an assertion made without knowledge is thereby improper, and nothing more needed saying. Others protested that this merely restates the thesis under discussion rather than settling anything about the case, and the meeting ended without agreement even about what we were disagreeing about.

I would like a map of what the slogan could actually be claiming, so that we can argue about definite theses rather than a phrase. Please lay out the distinct readings you can find in it, from the weakest to the strongest — for example: that speakers are in fact disposed to treat knowledge as what they expect of asserters; that knowledge is sufficient for proper assertion; that it is necessary; that the rule is constitutive of the speech act, so that a putative assertion without knowledge is improper in something like the way a move made out of turn is improper in chess; and that "norm" here picks out a rule for criticism rather than for action, licensing hearers to object rather than instructing speakers in advance. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is substantive or a near-platitude, and — crucially — whether it actually settles the teacher case, or merely labels our reaction to it. I would also like a short comparison of how the rival norms (truth, justified belief, reasonable-to-believe) would treat the case, so the group can see whether the example discriminates among the views at all, or whether some non-knowledge norm accommodates our intuitions about it just as well.

We will circulate your answer before the next meeting, so please close by stating how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of — I would particularly want to know how firm a grip we have on the data about what ordinary speakers actually object to in assertions, as opposed to what philosophers have stipulated they should object to.
