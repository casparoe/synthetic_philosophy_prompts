I have just finished serving on the jury for a criminal trial, and the experience has unsettled what had been a casual interest in the epistemology of testimony. Nearly everything we weighed was second-hand: a detective summarizing interviews conducted out of our presence, an expert vouching for a lab procedure none of us could independently evaluate, a seven-year-old who described events with a confidence no juror had any track record to check, and — most disconcerting to me — an official transcript generated by speech-to-text software and certified by a court reporter who had merely corrected its obvious errors. Because the jury instructions forbade us from consulting outside sources about the witnesses, whatever background-checking epistemology might recommend was institutionally unavailable. Reading around afterward, I find I cannot decide between reductionism about testimony — the view, associated with Hume and Locke, that a hearer is justified in accepting testimony only via an inductive track record of the speaker's or of speakers in general — and anti-reductionism, the view, associated with Thomas Reid and defended more recently by writers like Tyler Burge, that testimony is a basic source of justification, requiring no positive corroboration and lost only to positive defeaters.

Please write a dialogue between two jurors — call them R (the reductionist) and A (the anti-reductionist) — who hold these opposing views and argue about them during a break in deliberations. The dialogue should observe the following constraints:

1. Both jurors must be equally competent: neither should be a foil, and neither should make the obvious mistakes (e.g., R should not claim testimony never yields knowledge; A should not claim it is never rational to distrust a speaker).

2. The argument must engage the following specific points rather than trading slogans:
   a. Whether reductionism is self-defeating, in that the historical and statistical evidence for the general reliability of testimony was itself acquired testimonially — the so-called credibility problem;
   b. Whether R can retreat to a local reductionism, on which the hearer needs only modest, defeasible background checking of each speaker rather than a global inductive case, and whether this retreat gives away the reductionist's distinctive claim;
   c. Whether the juror's predicament — barred from independent investigation — shows that even local background checking is an idealization real hearers cannot satisfy, or whether truncated checks and coarse stereotypes of speaker types suffice;
   d. Whether trusting the machine-generated transcript is testimonial trust at all, and if so in whom — the software's developers, the certifying reporter, the court as an institution — and what each view implies about a transcript with a small but nonzero word error rate;
   e. Whether the panel's effortless crediting of the seven-year-old witness, who had no track record at all, confirms anti-reductionism or merely displays a cognitive laziness that the epistemology should correct rather than ratify.

3. The exchange must be genuine: each juror should respond to the other's strongest formulation, not a weaker one. In particular, A should press the credibility problem against R's best response to it, and R should press the worry that anti-reductionism licenses gullibility against A's best response to that.

4. The dialogue should end unresolved: they disagree, the break ends, and they return to vote. A brief closing exchange in which each states what evidence at trial they would weigh differently because of their view would be welcome.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. Where you reference the historical positions, characterize them in your own words rather than attributing specific quotations.
