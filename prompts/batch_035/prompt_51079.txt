My metaethics reading group has hit a wall, and I want to try something structural about it. For the past year the two most vocal members -- one a quasi-realist in the Blackburn-and-Gibbard tradition, the other a straightforward cognitivist and moral realist -- have argued past each other about the Frege-Geach problem. The expressivist insists the problem is solved, or was never really a problem once simple Ayer-style emotivism was abandoned; the cognitivist insists the solutions are ad hoc repairs that work only because they quietly copy the structure of the truth-conditional account they were meant to replace. Neither has ever gotten the other to concede a single specific point, and the rest of us suspect that a fair amount of the dispute is verbal -- hinging on what deserves to be called a truth-condition, or an explanation of validity, rather than on any substantive disagreement about what follows from what.

Please draft the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this one issue, the Frege-Geach problem, setting aside every other metaethical dispute between them. I want the claims both can accept -- about the cat argument (tormenting the cat is wrong; if tormenting the cat is wrong, getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong; so getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong) and the validity of that inference, about why its invalid cousins fail, about the Frege point that a sentence's content in an unasserted context must be recoverable from its content in assertion, about what a compositional semantics owes us, and about where simple emotivism went wrong. Then the precise statements of their remaining disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording -- for instance, on whether the expressivist's story about embedded moral content (whether told via higher-order attitudes, plans, or some other device) explains the validity of the inference or merely reproduces its pattern, and on whether the cognitivist is entitled to treat truth-conditional compositionality as doing explanatory work that the expressivist's machinery cannot. Include the cruxes: which sub-questions, if settled, would actually move one or the other? One candidate is whether an expressivist treatment that handles conditionals can extend uniformly to negation, disjunction, and quantified contexts, or whether each connective demands a fresh patch; another is whether the cognitivist's own account of validity for moral arguments earns its keep or borrows everything from logic applied indifferently to any subject matter; a third is whether a deflationary truth predicate lets each side say everything the other says under its own label. For each crux, name the concrete argument, test case, or formal constraint both would agree bears on it, and record what each side predicts would happen.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table (one row per disagreement, with columns for the expressivist's phrasing, the cognitivist's phrasing, the underlying crux, the agreed test, and each side's recorded prediction). Keep the disagreement about the positions, not about scholarly attribution -- the two of them have never agreed on what any particular author really meant, so have them stipulate the positions at issue rather than litigate exegesis of specific texts. If you conclude that a stretch of their dispute is merely verbal, say which stretch and why, in terms both would sign. And be hard on both: the document should be one neither could dismiss as slanted, which means the cognitivist doesn't get to define explanation as truth-conditions by fiat, and the expressivist doesn't get to declare victory on the grounds that some formal system exists.
