I teach an undergraduate course in political philosophy — about 24 students, one 75-minute session, no background in political science or statistics assumed. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel a problem rather than hear about it: the left/right distinction is remarkably reliable as a sorting device — nearly anyone can file nearly any political statement on the axis — and yet nobody can say what single thing the axis measures, and some perfectly coherent positions sit on it only awkwardly, or refuse to sit on it at all.

The arc I have in mind, though you should improve on it: participants first sort a set of policy cards or political statements into left and right and discover how much agreement they have; then they compare their reasons and find that they placed the same cards for different, even incompatible reasons (equality, hierarchy, change, who-benefits); then they are dealt cards engineered to cross-cut the axis — pro-market environmentalism, socially conservative welfare statism, redistributive nationalism, a Marxism that despises progressive cosmopolitanism — and the sorting has to be renegotiated. The debrief should map what happened onto the substantive question: whether "left" and "right" name a real dimension of political opinion or the residue of where delegates happened to sit in the French National Assembly — a coalition that has survived not because it carves anything at its joints but because it does coordination work for parties and voters. Hold that genealogy back until the debrief; I want the puzzle to be felt before it is historicized.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (groupthink in the sorting stage, students quietly relabeling the cross-cutting cards as "centrist" rather than admitting they are unclassifiable, and whatever else you foresee), and be honest about which feature of the question a classroom cannot reproduce — for instance, that the axis does different work in different countries and eras, and no single session can simulate the way it anchors identity over a lifetime. Finally, if you think my framing smuggles in an answer — say, that high inter-rater agreement already settles that the axis is real, or that the cross-cutting cards reveal a defect in the axis rather than in the cards — say so plainly instead of designing the activity around it.
