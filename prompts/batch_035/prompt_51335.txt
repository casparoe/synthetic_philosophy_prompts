Something I've been chewing on: the big content platforms have rules against harmful content, and officially the story is binary — either the content violates the rules and comes down, or it doesn't and stays up, and if you disagree you appeal and a human answers the yes/no question. But in practice there's a third bucket the platforms quietly run: stuff that doesn't quite break the rules but gets demoted — it stays up, but the recommendation system stops pushing it, so it withers on the vine. Some platforms literally label this "borderline content." So the same platform operates two policies on the same predicate at once: on the removal side it behaves as if "violates the rules" has a sharp edge, and on the demotion side it behaves as if there's a band of genuinely in-between cases where the right response is a matter of degree, not a verdict.

What theory of vagueness, if any, does this two-tier setup presuppose? My instinct is that it fits supervaluationism nicely — on that view "violates the rules" is still determinately true or false of every video, it's just that the platform hedges on the *action* rather than on the truth-value, demoting in the penumbra as a kind of insurance. But I cant tell whether the arrangement actually *requires* that reading, or whether it's equally comfortable on views where the predicate itself is indeterminate in the band, and the demotion tier is just tracking that indeterminacy. And here's the part that really bugs me: when a creator in the band appeals, the appeal is still decided as a yes/no question — was this in the demotion band or not — which feels like it smuggles epistemicism back in, like there's a fact of the matter about where the band ends even though nobody can know it. Is that a real tension or am I overreading a bureaucratic habit?

So, three things: (1) reconstruct the implicit theory of vagueness the two-tier system is committed to, and be clear about what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with; (2) tell me where the whole thing is underdetermined, such that several rival theories of vagueness would license the exact same policy; and (3) if a platform had to actually take a stand — say it went full "indeterminate all the way down" — what would have to change in practice? Would appeals have to become shade judgments ("this was 60% in the band") instead of verdicts, or could the yes/no appeal process survive? And separately: is there any reading on which the demotion tier isn't about vagueness at all but just risk management about enforcement error, and if so does that change your answer to (1)?
