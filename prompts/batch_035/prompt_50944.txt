I'm on the committee of a small charity in Leeds that hands out roughly £40,000 a year in grants to struggling families in the borough -- help with school uniforms, boiler repairs, that sort of thing. We always have more applicants than money, and I've got into long arguments with the other trustees about what a "fair" split even means here. A couple of them basically want to give to whoever seems worst off emotionally (welfare, I take it?), others want a flat rule based on income bands (resources?), and I keep thinking the real question is what people are actually able to do -- whether a kid can go on the school trip, whether a mum can keep her job with the childcare costs she's got. From reading around a bit I gather this is an old argument philosophers have under the heading "equality of what", and that there are live puzzles underneath it: expensive tastes (if someone's genuinely miserable unless they get a foreign holiday, are they worse off than someone content with less?), people whose wants have shrunk to fit their circumstances (I think one article called these adaptive preferences), and whether you should treat bad luck differently from choices people made themselves.

I've got no philosophy training, just a stubborn streak, a decent chunk of committee time, and about a year before we rewrite our allocation guidelines. Which of the open questions in this area actually deserve my evenings, in what order, and why those rather than the others? I don't want a tour of the whole debate -- I want a defended pecking order. What makes one puzzle worth my time given that it's me and a spreadsheet, not a research institute? Some of this stuff (like whether tastes are "expensive" through anyone's fault) feels like it might matter loads for who gets a £200 grant, and some feels like it only matters for ideal societies we'll never live in -- tell me which is which. And what would change your ranking? For instance if we started means-testing properly, or if the council doubled our funding, or if a third of the applicants were asylum seekers with essentially zero income on paper.
