I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. Twice a year I run an optional half-day internal workshop for senior engineers at my company on estimation and forecasting, built around Kahneman's discussion of the inside and outside views and Tetlock's work on forecasting. This year I set a short take-home exercise afterwards, and I'm grading the submissions myself — I'm the only marker, and I'd like a second assessment of one particular response before I commit to a mark.

Here is the exercise exactly as I set it, worth 25 marks in total:

---
A monolith-to-services migration was estimated at eight months. It is now eleven months in, and the team's own tracking puts it at roughly 70% complete. Leadership wants a fixed completion date to anchor a customer announcement, and has asked you, as an adviser, whether to commit to one. In about 600 words, answer the following.

(a) Explain what the inside view and the outside view would each tell the team in this situation, and describe what a reference class for the decision would look like.
(b) Explain the reference class problem and say what, if anything, you would do about it here.
(c) Explain what epistemic modesty adds to the outside view, if anything, or explain why they come apart if you think they do.
(d) State, with reasons, whether the outside view should always override the inside view when the two conflict.
---

And here is the response I'm marking, reproduced verbatim:

---
The inside view is what our team uses by default: take the remaining work, break it into tasks, estimate each one, and add them up. It is reasoning from the particulars of our case. The outside view is the statistical one: set aside everything we know about our codebase and ask how long projects like this one actually take. The record on large software projects is fairly unambiguous — big migrations overrun their initial estimates the overwhelming majority of the time, and the overruns are usually large — so the prior from the class is already against us, and it is against committing to any date that assumes the current pace holds.

For the reference class, I would take all large enterprise software migrations. The reference class problem is that there is no principled way to choose which projects count as 'like this one': migrations by companies of our size, in our industry, in this decade, on our stack — each subdivision shrinks the sample. My resolution is to prefer the broadest class available, because statistics improve with sample size. 'All large software projects' gives far more data points than 'monolith-to-services migrations in our sector since 2015', and more data means a more reliable estimate, whereas a narrow class may contain only a handful of cases and mislead us badly.

Kahneman's advice is to start from the outside view and adjust from there, and Tetlock's forecasting research supports taking it seriously: foxes outperform hedgehogs precisely because foxes reason from statistics and precedents rather than from one big theory about the case at hand. So in this decision I would discount the team's '70% complete' figure heavily — it is inside-view information, produced by the same optimism that put us three months behind — and instead ask what fraction of comparable projects that reported being about 70% done actually finished within the next milestone. The honest advice to leadership is to withhold a fixed date until the class statistics make an on-time finish the likely outcome, and to delay the announcement until then.

On epistemic modesty: this is really the outside view under another label. Modesty says don't trust your own judgment over the aggregate; the outside view says don't trust your case-specific judgment over the aggregate of similar cases. Both are deference to base rates. So what does modesty add? Nothing, unless one wants a second word for deference.

Should the outside view always override? Yes. Inside-view reasoning is the very mechanism by which the planning fallacy operates, so every time we privilege it we are feeding the bias. The only defensible procedure is to set the estimate from the class and to refuse case-specific adjustments except where the class genuinely does not apply — which, for a project this ordinary, it does.
