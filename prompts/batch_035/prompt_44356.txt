I teach philosophy of mind, and this term I've somehow ended up on the internal ethics review panel at the AI research institute where my partner works — mostly rubber-stamping data privacy stuff, but last week something came up that I can't stop thinking about, and I'd really like your read on it, because I have to submit my recommendation by Friday.

The lab trains household robots with reinforcement learning: the agent gets a positive score for doing things well and — and this is what's bugging me — a negative score for mistakes, like knocking something over or grabbing the wrong object. In the meeting I asked whether we should worry at all about the negative scores, and one of the engineers kind of laughed and said the robot isn't "feeling" anything, the reward is just a number in a ledger, no more painful than a golf score. But another said something that stuck with me: that whatever else you say about it, this is the first thing they've built that is literally made out of avoiding — the whole architecture exists to move away from certain states of the world, and that's a genuinely different thing from a classifier that just labels photos. A third colleague pushed back that there's no one home to suffer: no persistent subject, just a policy that gets overwritten between episodes, so whatever happens to "it" happens to no one. And then someone quoted the dopamine literature at us — apparently in brains the signal isn't reward itself but the gap between reward and what was predicted, which seems to cut both ways and noone at the table knew which way. The person from the robotics side also made a distinction I found useful even though I couldn't place it: that even in animals, detecting damage isn't the same as minding damage — fish flinch, but do they mind?

My instinct is that the golf-score comparison is too quick, but I can't articulate why without sounding like a Luddite in front of a room full of engineers who think in objective functions. Is there a principled middle position between "it's just optimisation" and "we've built something that can hurt"? And is there a way of framing the question that would actually land with that audience, rather than just asserting qualia at them? I'd like your advice on what my memo should say — whether to wave it through or flag it for a proper discussion, and on what grounds. Please write in British English, as the memo goes to a UK-based committee and I'll be lifting passages from your reply.
