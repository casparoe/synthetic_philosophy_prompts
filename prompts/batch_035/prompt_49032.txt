I've become fairly convinced of the Christiano-ish view that approval-directed agents are the right basic architecture for safe AI: instead of handing the system a goal and letting it optimize over the long future, you have it choose actions (or evaluate options) on the basis of what an overseer would approve of, so the human judgment stays in the loop and you sidestep instrumental convergence, power-seeking, etc. What would actually count against this? I'm not looking for the standard "the overseer could be fooled by deceptive actions" — I take that as a live objection but a known one. What I want is the deeper stuff: e.g., whether approval-direction just quietly reintroduces goal-directedness at one remove, since the approval function is itself a learned thing that the system can pressure at the margins, or whether "what I'd approve of on reflection" collapses into a full-blown coherent goal anyway and the safety is only the safety of a delay. Also, if the answer is that you need the overseer to scale with the system, is the position still doing any work, or has it been qualified into something unfalsifiable — "approval-direction is safe whenever the overseer is good enough to approve safely"? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed approval-direction person should concede and which they can shrug off. For context: what would move me most is either a concrete story where approval-direction does worse than a plain corrigible goal-directed system, or an argument that the two architectures converge in the limit and the distinction does no real safety work.
