I've recently left a permanent lectureship in philosophy of science to take up a two-year fellowship at a small institute that funds conceptual work on AI risk, and I now have to decide — within the next few months, so that the time isn't frittered away — what I should actually spend those two years on. My training is in formal epistemology and decision theory; I can read the ML literature but I will not be running experiments. What I want from you is a dependency-aware prioritisation of the open conceptual questions in the debate over existential risk from AI, together with a defence of the ordering: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone with my background, and which are overrated.

To make the task concrete, here are the candidates as I see them, though I would welcome additions or subtractions. First, the question of what exactly the disvalue of an AI catastrophe consists in: extinction of currently living people, versus a permanent curtailment of humanity's potential, versus a long lock-in of some flawed but survivable arrangement — since these pull apart, and the choice among them matters for whether 'existential' risk is even the right organising category. Second, whether goal-directed agency is something that sufficiently capable systems will exhibit as a matter of course, or a contingent design choice — which bears directly on how much weight the standard loss-of-control arguments deserve. Third, whether the loss of control, when it comes, is better modelled as a sharp, discrete event or as gradual disempowerment spread across many systems and many decisions that individually look fine, and what turns on that distinction methodologically. Fourth, what an appropriate epistemology of model evaluation would look like: what would justify confidence that a system is safe, given that the absence of dangerous behaviour in tests is only weak evidence about behaviour in novel conditions. Fifth, whether the alignment problem is well-posed at all when the thing being aligned to is human values under deep disagreement and moral uncertainty. Sixth, the incentive structure of the organisations building these systems, and whether racing dynamics are a problem of coordination that institutions can in principle solve or a prisoner's dilemma written into the technology itself.

For each question you take seriously, tell me what depends on it downstream, whether philosophers rather than ML practitioners have any comparative advantage on it, what evidence arriving in the next two years would move it up or down your list, and — importantly — which of these are pseudo-questions, where conceptual work merely restates empirical bets in philosophical vocabulary. I am less interested in a survey than in a defended agenda for one person with finite time. Please write your answer in British English.
