In my world history class, our textbook seems to treat famines in two inconsistent ways, and I'm trying to figure out what each treatment presupposes. The Irish famine of the 1840s and the Bengal famine of 1943 appear in a unit on natural disasters, alongside hurricanes and earthquakes, described in terms of the blight and the failure of the monsoon — as if the famine happened to people the way a flood does. Yet in the chapter on the Great Leap Forward, the same book calls the resulting famine man-made and treats it as something officials did to the population. I've also been reading about Amartya Sen's work on Bengal, where he argues that total food supply in 1943 was not dramatically lower than in neighboring years, and that what collapsed was particular groups' ability to command food through wages, land, or trade — what he calls their entitlements — which suggests the disaster was in the distribution, not the harvest. That made me wonder what my textbook's split presupposes. What theory of famine causation does placing a famine in the natural-disasters chapter require: that famines are events of food scarcity rather than failures of distribution, or could it be defended some other way, for instance by pointing to who had the power to intervene? Is the book's harsher treatment of the Great Leap Forward actually consistent with the rest, given that in Ireland and Bengal there were also policy choices — grain continuing to be exported, relief being refused — that governments made and could have made differently? And if we rebuilt the practice on the entitlement view instead, what would have to change: how textbooks classify these events, how charity appeals frame their requests, and who, if anyone, ends up looking blameworthy? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind the current practice, say where it is genuinely underdetermined so that several views of famine could license it, and then tell me what a textbook or a relief agency would have to look like if the entitlement view were built in from the start.
