I teach a big intro ethics course, and the trolley unit is where I either win them over or lose them for good, so lately I've been running it more like a seminar question than a history lecture. Here's what I want from you, and please don't give me the encyclopedia entry on double effect — I've read it, assigned it, I could recite it in my sleep.

Give me your actual credence — a number, not "it depends on how you formulate the doctrine" — that the doctrine of double effect is doing genuine explanatory work on ordinary trolley intuitions. To be precise about the proposition: in the standard cases (Foot's original tram, the bystander at the switch, the fat man), the pattern of permissibility judgments is better explained by the intended/foreseen distinction than by its simpler rivals. Note I'm asking about the psychology, not the normative question — I don't care here whether DDE is true as a matter of ethics, only whether it's tracking what's actually going on in people's heads.

Then decompose the number. At minimum I'd want terms for: (1) the probability that intuitions distinguish at all between the two causal structures, roughly "harm redirected off its existing path" vs. "harm to the one brought about as part of the plan"; (2) conditional on that, the probability that the intended/foreseen distinction is doing the work rather than the deflection story — and here's the pressure point I actually care about. Thomson's loop case was built to break the deflection explanation: the side track loops back, so if the fat man weren't there the trolley would swing around and kill the five anyway, meaning diverting it looks less like redirecting a threat and more like using him to stop it. And yet people's intuitions mostly still say permissible, which suggests they just aren't tracking the loop at all. So is that evidence FOR the doctrine (people aren't reasoning about causal structure, so something DDE-shaped must be driving) or evidence AGAINST it (people aren't reasoning, period, and whatever's driving is about as deep as a puddle)? Give me your take on that fork as its own term. (3) A term for whether the proportionality condition does independant work or is just decoration philosophers insist on to keep the counterexamples out.

Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence would move it most — and when I say evidence, think about what could actually discriminate the hypotheses: some new case design that cleanly seperates the intended/foreseen distinction from the deflection structure, or population-level stuff. For what it's worth, I've played with telling students that the one on the side track is the person who let the trolley loose in the first place, and I honestly cannot tell whether their intuitions shift because of the doctrine or just because they want the guilty one to suffer.

For reference: my own number has drifted from about 0.5 down to 0.35 over a decade of teaching this, though I don't fully trust it — I may just be worn down by undergraduates. Tell me if you land higher, and more importantly, which of my three terms you think I've got wrong.
