A graduate student in my philosophy-of-mathematics study group has an argument, running through the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, that concludes there is no fact of the matter whether the real numbers are countable. Every step looks defensible to me; the conclusion does not. Here is the argument as he gives it.

(1) If first-order ZFC is consistent, then by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a countable model M. (2) Since M satisfies ZFC, M satisfies Cantor's theorem: within M there is no function from the natural numbers onto the reals. (3) Yet M itself is countable: externally, there is a bijection between the natural numbers and the objects M takes to be its reals. (4) So ZFC, even when perfectly sound, does not secure the uncountability of the reals: its assertion that no bijection exists is restricted to bijections among M's own elements, which is not what "uncountable" was supposed to mean. (5) Nothing in first-order ZFC distinguishes M from the intended universe: any first-order statement true in one is true in the other. (6) So the statement that the reals are uncountable has no model-independent content; from a wider standpoint the reals of any given model are countable, and no standpoint can be first-order certified as the right one. (7) The same applies to us: if our own set-theoretic universe satisfies ZFC, then the very same theory has countable models, and by (5) no first-order fact distinguishes our situation from theirs; we possess no first-order means of certifying that our own reals are not, from some wider standpoint, countable. (8) Therefore there is no fact of the matter whether the reals are countable — only facts relative to standpoints, none of which is privileged.

I cannot accept (8), but I also cannot find the exit. My own hunch is that the trouble sits in (4): the external bijection is not one of M's elements, so M is not wrong about anything, and "uncountable," as used inside a structure, simply quantifies over that structure's own functions. My friend concedes that this handles M, but insists the reply cannot remain internal to a model: at (7) the argument re-runs for the intended universe itself, where there is no model, no membership relation held fixed from outside, and no "outside" to point to — and my response then has nothing left to appeal to. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does the familiar point that the external bijection is absent from the model actually stop the argument, or does it merely blunt (4) while leaving the slide from (5) through (7) intact, so that the real cost has to be paid somewhere later — at the move from indistinguishability to relativity of content, or at the "wider standpoint" invoked for the universe itself? If forced, I would rather keep (1), (2), and (5), which look like ordinary model theory to me, than keep (8); but I would like to know exactly what that preference commits me to.
