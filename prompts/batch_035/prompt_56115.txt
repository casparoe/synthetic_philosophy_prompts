I direct the research staff of a citizen-participation office in a mid-sized city, and we are perpetually in the business of creating small, ad-hoc voting bodies: neighborhood councils with advisory power, special-purpose districts (a watershed authority, a business improvement district, a library taxing district), and the occasional referendum, such as an upcoming vote on whether an industrial neighborhood may secede from the city and govern itself. Nearly every time we stand one of these bodies up, we get the same fight: who counts as a member of the demos for this decision? My staff keep improvising answers, and I would like to replace the improvisation with something defensible. The philosophical tension I want the procedure built around is the familiar one between two principles: that everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in it, and that the demos is properly constituted by those subject to the decision's authority or coercion rather than merely touched by its effects. There is also a third consideration my staff keep running into: some people have relevant local knowledge that the decision needs (the people who actually maintain the watershed, work at the refinery, use the school), and a fourth, practical worry: if every remotely affected party gets a vote, some of these bodies become too large and too heterogeneous to make any decision at all.

Please convert this tangle into a decision procedure my staff can actually run in under an hour per decision, with no philosophy background, producing a written record that would survive a hostile question at a city council meeting. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, default rules for when the answers are murky (for instance: when in doubt, does the procedure default to residents of the legal boundary, to property owners, to some residency-plus-stake hybrid, or to an explicitly provisional franchise?), and the points where the procedure must stop and hand the choice to elected officials because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure systematically cannot do — I am particularly interested in whether it can say anything principled about parties who are affected but cannot vote at all: future residents, children, and, in one live dispute, a downwind suburban municipality in another jurisdiction entirely that will bear the air-quality consequences of the refinery neighborhood's secession vote.

Then run the procedure on three cases we actually face. First, the refinery secession referendum itself: the seceding neighborhood's 9,000 residents would gain zoning and emissions authority whose effects fall mostly on 60,000 people downwind, in a different city. Second, a school district vote on consolidating and closing two schools, where roughly a fifth of the enrolled children live outside the district lines because of an open-enrollment agreement the district now wants to factor into the decision. Third, the watershed authority's vote on reallocating irrigation water, which affects farmers inside the district, downstream recreational users outside it, and a city that draws its drinking water from the river but has never had a seat on the authority. For at least one of these, I expect the procedure to give an answer I will find politically unwelcome, and I would rather see that outcome worked honestly than smoothed over.
