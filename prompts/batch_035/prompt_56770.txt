I'm grading a take-home exam for a graduate course I teach on formal epistemology and the foundations of machine intelligence, and I'd appreciate a careful second read on one of the answers. The exam question read:

"Suppose an agent reasons in a formal system T (think of PA or ZFC), and let Pr_T(⌜φ⌝) abbreviate the arithmetized claim that there exists a T-proof of the sentence φ. The agent would like to construct a successor that reasons in the very same system T, while verifying in advance — by a proof in T — that the successor will perform only actions whose safety is provable in T. (a) State Löb's theorem and explain why it appears to stand in the way: what exactly is the agent unable to prove, and why? (b) Now suppose we try to route around the obstacle by giving the successor a strictly stronger system, T + Con(T), where Con(T) says that T is consistent. Does this improve the agent's position, and can the construction be iterated so that each generation can verify its own successor?"

The student wrote:

"Löb's theorem: if T proves 'Pr_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ,' then φ is true. This is the source of the trouble. To trust my successor, I would need to establish something like 'whatever my successor proves is true,' or at least 'if my successor proves that action a is safe, then a is safe.' But my successor reasons in T, so this amounts to proving reflection for T, and by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem I cannot prove that T is consistent. Since a system that is not even provably consistent certainly cannot be proven sound, there is no way for me to verify the successor in advance. The Löbian obstacle is thus the second incompleteness theorem in slightly different dress: my inability to certify my successor is just my inability to certify my own consistency, projected onto a copy of myself.

For (b), the escape works. The successor reasons in T + Con(T), which proves everything T proves, plus Con(T). Now, Con(T) is a claim I accept — I believe T is consistent, I simply cannot prove it within T. So when I check one of the successor's proofs, either it never uses the extra axiom, in which case it is already a T-proof I can verify outright, or it uses Con(T), an axiom I regard as true. Either way I can certify the proof. And the construction iterates: the next successor reasons in T + Con(T) + Con(T + Con(T)), and each generation can verify the next in just the same way, yielding an indefinitely long chain of self-certifying agents."
