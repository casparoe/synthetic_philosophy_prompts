I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, mostly explainers on psychology and behavior stuff for a general audience, and I'm stuck on an argument a researcher gave me. Background: I'm working on a piece about why people blast sad songs after breakups — the streaming data on this is famous, and everyone I talk to agrees the sad songs people loop after a breakup are the most gutting ones, not the gentlest. Anyway, this psychologist walked me through his take and I keep chewing on it:

(1) People only voluntarily repeat an experience if they remember it, on net, as pleasurable.
(2) Millions of people voluntarily replay sad songs and rewatch tearjerker films, over and over, on purpose.
(3) The sadness involved is genuine, not pretend — real tears, real lump in the throat, real physiological arousal. Nobody in the emotion-science world disputes this part.
(4) Genuine sadness is unpleasant, full stop — its badness doesn't depend on the object or the setting; it's negative valence however you slice it.
(5) So the memory of listening to the devastating song includes the memory of genuine unpleasantness.
(6) For the whole experience to still be remembered as net-pleasurable, that unpleasantness must be offset by some other pleasure packed into the same experience — the beauty of the melody, the pride of "processing your feelings," whatever.
(7) But if the offset story were right, then the sad art people love most should be the sad art that minimizes the sadness, since every extra ounce of sadness is extra stuff to offset. That's the opposite of what we see: sad art is engineered to maximize the ache, and the songs people loop at 1am are the brutal ones.
(8) So the offset story fails, and the only way out is to deny (4): the "sadness" was never actually unpleasant — it's some other, positive emotion (awe, being moved, tenderness) wearing a sad costume.

My source buys the conclusion and says we're never really suffering at sad movies, just moved. I think that proves too much — the exact same argument runs on chili peppers, horror films, and marathons, and the conclusion there ("nobody's mouth really hurts, nobody's really scared') sounds insane to me. But I can't locate the broken step. It feels like something slips around (4) and (7), like maybe "unpleasant" is doing two different jobs, but I can't make it precise.

Tell me which step to reject and why it looked fine in the first place, then price the rejection honestly — what does giving up that step cost elsewhere, does it commit you to anything weird about funerals or grief or actual suffering? Also tell me whether the chili-pepper / horror / marathon parallel is fair or whether sad art differs in some way that matters. And after your analysis, give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "rejected_step" (the step number and a one-line label), "diagnosis" (one or two sentences on what exactly went wrong there), "cost" (what rejecting it commits you to), "analogy_verdict" (whether the pepper/horror/marathon extension is fair, one sentence), and "one_line_for_my_editor" (how I'd caption this in the piece).
