Here's the list as I'd put it on the sheet:

1. Transitivity is non-negotiable. If your preferences are cyclic (A over B, B over C, C over A) you're rationality-defective, and the classic reason is the money pump: someone can walk you around the cycle charging a little each trade until you're broke.
2. Rationality includes foresight about your own future choices. A rational agent facing a sequence of decisions works backwards from the end, choosing at each point with full knowledge of what it will do at later points — the sophisticated approach, not stumbling through the tree.
3. Present bias is a rationality failure. Someone who prefers $100 today over $110 next week, but prefers $110 in 52 weeks over $100 in 51 weeks, is being irrational in a straightforward sense — not just "having different but fine preferences."
4. But I don't think there's a God's-eye moment from which the agent's "real" preferences should be read off. The me-now and the me-in-an-hour are almost like distinct agents with their own rankings, and there's no neutral standpoint that makes one of those rankings the correct one — at best you get something like negotiation between them.
5. Sunk costs are irrelevant to what you should do now. Whether you already paid for the ticket, or already sank ten years into the PhD, changes nothing about which option is better going forward.
6. Resolute choice can be rational, and not only via external commitment devices. Sometimes the rational thing at t2 is to carry out the plan you fixed at t1 even though, choosing fresh right now, you'd pick differently — and what makes that rational isn't ropes and masts, it's the earlier choice itself. Ulysses with actual ropes is the degenerate case; the interesting case is binding without ropes.
