So I'm in a historiography seminar this semester and we've been going back and forth on the same question for like three weeks now and it's going nowhere. The question is basically: would there have been something like Nazi Germany even if Hitler had died in, say, 1918, or never gone into politics at all? Half the seminar is basically on team "great man" -- they say no Hitler, no Holocaust, no world war, because the specific blend of ideological obsession, personal charisma, and willingness to take insane gambles (like remilitarizing the Rhineland) doesn't come along twice, and the people who would plausibly have taken over the nationalist right in Germany, like a Strasser-type figure or some military-backed authoritarian, would have produced something like Franco's Spain or a nastier Weimar-era military dictatorship -- bad, but not world-historically catastrophic. The other half says look at the structural stuff: a humiliated country with a ruined economy, elites who wanted to destroy the parliamentary system anyway, widespread revanchism, a deep antisemitism that predated Hitler by decades, a conservative establishment that was handing power to authoritarian solutions on a plate. On that view Hitler is more like the person who happened to be standing at the front of a wave that was going to break one way or another, and the counterfactual where Germany ends up in roughly the same place is at least as plausible.

Here's what frustrates me though. Everyone in the seminar is actually arguing in good faith, they all know the same facts, nobody is being a hack. But we're completely stuck, and I've started to suspect the disagreement isn't really about Germany at all -- it's about something else the two teams are bringing to the table. My working theory, and I want you to tell me if it holds up, is that it's a disagreement about the semantics/epistemology of counterfactuals: the structural people implicitly hold something like "you don't get to cite an individual as the cause of a macro-outcome unless the background conditions couldn't have produced someone functionally equivalent," while the great-man people implicitly hold something like "the counterfactual test should ask about this specific bundle of traits and acts, and if the bundle is rare enough, it counts as decisive regardless of how much 'pressure' was in the system." So they're applying different thresholds for how much of a coincidence you're allowed to wave away as "it would have happened anyway." But I'm honestly not sure, because there also seems to be a genuine empirical dispute buried in there about how much slack there was in German politics -- like, how many plausible leaders there really were in that pond, which none of us actually knows.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it a verbal dispute about how to individuate causes, a substantive empirical one about the actual menu of alternative leaders, a difference in explanatory values (do you want an explanation of why it happened or of why it happened *then* and *that way*)? And whatever your diagnosis is, tell me what would actually count as progress here -- is there some methodological move, some way of sharpening the question, some kind of evidence (memoirs? contemporaneous assessments of the alternatives? comparison cases like Italy or Hungary?) -- that could in principle move reasonable people off their positions, or is this just going to be people voting on their priors forever? I'd rather have a real diagnosis with reasons than a both-sides survey, and its fine to tell me my own theory is wrong if you think it is.
