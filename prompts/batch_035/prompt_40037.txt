I run a small forecasting team at a consultancy (4 analysts, mostly generalists, we do short-turnaround questions for clients -- geopolitical stuff, tech timelines, occasional market calls). I've been reading about epistemic akrasia and higher-order evidence and I think there's something there my team needs but I can't figure out how to make it operational. Like, the literature says you shouldn't end up believing "P, but I shouldn't believe P", and that certain kinds of evidence about *your own reasoning* (you're tired, the expert disagrees with you, you were wrong the last five times on this question type) are supposed to matter somehow, but it's all pretty abstract. Meanwhile my analysts either ignore that stuff entirely or they overcorrect -- one guy found out he'd been badly calibrated on China questions for a year and basically threw out all his priors about the region, which was worse.

So here's the ask: write a procedure my team can actually run when they notice higher-order evidence bearing on a forecast they're about to submit. Something that fits on a page, runs in maybe 15 minutes before a deadline, and answers in order: (1) which higher-order signals are worth taking seriously vs noise (does it matter that you feel rushed? that a domain expert disagrees? that your client has an obvious interest in one answer?), (2) how to actually adjust the credence without doing something dumb like jumping to 50/50 or abandoning your prior entirely, and (3) when to just flag the forecast as low-confidence instead of adjusting at all. It should say where the procedure has to hand things back to the analyst as a judgment call, and where following it will give you an answer that feels wrong but should be followed anyway.

Then run it on three cases from our actual workflow:

- An analyst whose forecast on a coup question disagrees with the country specialists at a big think tank, and she genuinely can't tell if they know something she doesn't or if they're just biased toward stability.
- An analyst who's been up for 20 hours and is about to submit a forecast he's confident in, knows sleep deprivation degrades judgment, but has no idea by how much or in which direction.
- An analyst who just got a bad calibration score on a whole category (central bank decisions) and now wants to hedge every interest rate forecast toward the base rate even though his actual object-level reasoning hasn't changed at all.

Also be upfront about what this procedure can't do -- I'd rather know where it breaks than have a fake sense that we've solved it. And if there are places where the philosophy genuinely doesn't settle what to do (I get the sense the akrasia literature has actual live disputes here), tell me that too, so we can pick a house policy rather than pretend the answer falls out of the theory.
