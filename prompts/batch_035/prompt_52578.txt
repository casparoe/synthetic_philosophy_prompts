Convene, in your imagination, a year-long working group between two philosophers of mind who have argued past each other for a decade: a committed higher-order theorist in the Rosenthal/Lycan tradition, for whom a mental state is conscious only when the subject has a suitable higher-order representation (a thought or inner perception) of being in it; and a committed first-order representationalist in the Tye/Dretske tradition, for whom a perceptual state's being conscious requires only that its content be poised and available for reasoning and report, with no monitoring state needed. Their exchanges have mostly consisted of trading putative counterexamples the other side refuses to recognize as counterexamples. Write the joint statement they should be able to produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign -- about what the explananda of a theory of consciousness are, about how both read the standard dissociations between conscious and unconscious perception (masking, blindsight, inattentional blindness), and about which objections both regard as confused or question-begging; (2) a table of residual disagreements, with each statement phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording as faithful to its own view; (3) the cruxes -- subquestions such that settling one would move at least one side. I would expect at least one crux about whether the empirical metacognition literature (confidence ratings, type-2 signal detection, blindsighters' poor introspective judgments) measures the higher-order relation the higher-order theorist needs, and one about the misrepresentation worry: what happens, on the higher-order view, when a higher-order thought makes a state conscious but misrepresents it -- say, by representing an experience of orange as an experience of red -- and whether first-order accounts escape an analogous problem. Add other cruxes as you see fit. Finally, (4) for each crux, a concrete experiment, thought experiment, or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If you conclude that some portion of their dispute is merely verbal -- for instance, over whether "the state is conscious" means the same thing on both sides -- identify which portion and why. Do not adjudicate who is right; the deliverable is a document both could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved.
