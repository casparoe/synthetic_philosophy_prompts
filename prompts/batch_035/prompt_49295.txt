My philosophy of language course spent last week on Hofstadter's satirical "Person Paper" — the one written in the voice of a pompous fictional columnist, describing a parallel English where race-specific terms do the work that gendered terms do in ours: pronouns split by race instead of sex, generics like "whitekind" standing in for "mankind," and so on, with the columnist huffing about the activists who want to "clean up" the language. Our professor leaned on it hard: the satire lands, she said, precisely because the analogy is basically exact — the absurdity you feel reading the fictional English is what generic "he" looks like from outside the status quo — so anyone revolted by the racial version but relaxed about the gendered one is just displaying status quo bias, and the case for pronoun reform is the same kind of case as the one against racist usage. My roommate, a linguistics major, thinks this smuggles in way too much. His line is that English grammar forces a gender choice on every third-person singular pronoun but doesn't force a race choice anywhere; in the fictional world the racial marking would have to have been deliberately installed, whereas gendered pronouns are an inherited grammatical feature that English shares with half the languages in Europe, centuries older than any deliberate policy of exclusion. So, he says, a speaker using generic "he" isn't doing the same thing a speaker of that other English would be doing, and the moral reading has to come apart too. im honestly not sure how much of this is vibes.

Can you audit the analogy properly instead of letting either of them win on rhetorical force? I want the correspondence table first — what maps to what between the two Englishes: the pronouns, the generics, the marked groups' social position, the history of the terms, who is defending the convention and why. Then the disanalogies, sorted into ones that actually matter for the moral conclusion and ones that don't (my guess is the roommate's forced-choice point cuts both ways, since the fictional world could have inherited its racial grammar too — but work that out rather than assuming). Then the verdict: does the professor's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — is being bothered by one convention but not the other really just status quo bias, or does something genuine block the transfer? And if the analogy can't carry all that weight by itself, tell me what it would need to carry it, or whether there's a better comparison that makes the moral point without the shaky mapping.
