I'm writing a term paper for a graduate epistemology seminar on Timothy Williamson's argument against luminosity in Knowledge and Its Limits (2000), and I'd like you to draft an essay on it. By way of setup: Williamson calls a condition luminous if, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know that it obtains, and he pairs this with the KK principle, according to which whenever one knows something, one is in a position to know that one knows it. His argument against both proceeds via a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge: because one's discriminations are fallible, knowing that a condition obtains in a given case requires that it also obtains in relevantly similar nearby cases. Then consider a sorites-like series of cases in which, say, one's feeling of cold fades imperceptibly from one moment to the next; near the boundary where the condition ceases to hold, the margin requirement makes it unknowable that the condition holds, even though it does hold. And since one did know that it held a short while earlier, there is a case in which one knows the condition obtains without being in a position to know that one knows it — a failure of KK. Iterating the reasoning pushes the unknowability further and further back from the boundary, which is meant to show that no non-trivial condition is luminous.

The essay should do three things. First, reconstruct the argument carefully enough that a reader who hasn't encountered it can follow it, and explain why the sorites-style structure matters (why gradualness across a series of cases, rather than mere fallibility, does the work). Second, weigh the main objections and replies: that the argument smuggles in an externalist, safety-style conception of knowledge that a Cartesian about introspection could simply reject, so it begs the question against the luminosity of mental states; that the argument proves too much, since on plausible assumptions even very simple cases of perceptual or introspective knowledge would be destroyed; and Williamson's own concession that trivially luminous conditions exist, which raises the question of whether the interesting battle is only over non-trivial ones. Third, I'd like you to take seriously a somewhat less-discussed wrinkle: whether the argument's force is uniform across conditions with different internal structure — for instance, whether a condition with a natural, psychologically sharp boundary (a well-defined pain being present or absent, an occurrent thought occurring) resists the margin-for-error reasoning better than a vague, graded condition like feeling cold, or whether Williamson's argument generalises to our knowledge of our own occurrent thoughts and thereby threatens a deeper level of self-knowledge than the coldness case suggests.

Please reach and defend a definite conclusion — something like 'luminosity fails for graded conditions but the Cartesian can retreat to a restricted class of sharp-boundary mental states' or 'the argument generalises to all non-trivial conditions, but the KK principle can be independently motivated anyway' — rather than ending on an agnostic note. Please write the essay in British English.
