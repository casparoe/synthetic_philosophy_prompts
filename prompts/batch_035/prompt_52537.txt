I work on mechanistic anomaly detection for language models — flagging inputs that get processed by different *mechanisms* than training inputs, rather than inputs that merely produce bad outputs — and a colleague has an argument that our whole program rests on an ill-defined notion. I want to know which step to reject, but every time I try, I seem to give up something I need.

His argument:

1. Call two inputs mechanistically alike when the same components, doing the same causal jobs, are responsible for how the network handles each of them.
2. Mechanisms are individuated at some granularity: individual parameters, features, heads, layers, or the network as a whole.
3. At the finest granularity, no two distinct inputs are mechanistically alike. Because of superposition, each input leaves a distinct activation trace across most of the network, so there is always some component whose behavior differs between the two inputs.
4. At the coarsest granularity, all inputs are mechanistically alike. Causal responsibility is distributed enough that no small ablation reliably destroys typical behavior, so every input is handled by the same mechanism, namely the network.
5. So whether two inputs use the same mechanism is not a fact about the network alone; it is a fact about the network *plus* the granularity at which we chose to describe it.
6. But our detectors claim to detect a property of the input, not of our descriptions; and by (5), any input can be made to count as anomalous or normal by redescribing the network at a different granularity.
7. Therefore "mechanistically anomalous" does not track anything objective, and the research program is confused at its foundation.

He is not claiming the detectors are useless — he compares their verdicts to saying a coastline has one bay or three, which is a fact about our conventions as much as about the coast. When I answer that the network itself must privilege some granularity, he asks which one, and how we could know without first choosing a granularity to describe the network in. I would like to keep the thought that our detectors track something real rather than our descriptive conventions. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And is the coastline comparison quietly doing some illicit work?
