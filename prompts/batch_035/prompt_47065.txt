Heres my package of views on decision theory with infinite or undefined expectations, and I want to know if it hangs together before I present it to my reading group:

(1) Expected utility maximization is correct whenever the expectation is well-defined, and that covers essentially every real-world gamble, so the theory is basicly fine as is.
(2) St. Petersburg isn't a real problem. No actual casino has an infinite bankroll, so you cap the payoffs at what the house can actually pay out, and the value comes out modest and finite. The paradox only exists for a game that can't literally be offered.
(3) The Pasadena game is different — rational choice theory is just silent about it. (In case you haven't seen it, the version I learned: flip a fair coin until the first heads. If the first heads is on flip n, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n dollars — so you win $2 if heads comes on flip 1, lose $2 if the first heads is on flip 2, win $8/3 if it's on flip 3, lose $4 if it's on flip 4, and so on. Multiply payoffs by probabilities and the expectation is the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which only converges conditionally, so rearranging the terms can make it sum to anything you like.) My view is that there's no fact of the matter about what Pasadena is worth, so no choice involving it is irrational.
(4) Dominance is close to rock bottom for me: if option A is at least as good as B in every state of the world and strictly better in some, you have to take A.
(5) The value of a gamble can't depend on the order you happen to sum its outcomes in.
(6) Rational choice theory shouldn't have holes — between any two options there should be a fact about which is at least as chooseworthy.

I have a general feeling that (2) and (3) can't both survive, since whatever cap dissolves St. Petersburg would seem to give Pasadena a definite value too, and that something about (4) and (6) rubs against (3), but I honestly can't see exactly which commitment has to go, if any. Someone also told me the rearrangement facts put (1) and (5) in direct conflict, though I don't follow why. And a friend sketched a fix I only half understood: instead of assigning each game its own value, compare two games directly by looking at whether the series of payoff-differences between them is well behaved, even when neither game's own series is. Would adopting that let me keep (5) and (6) while dropping (3), or does it quietly cost me something else? Go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Please be upfront about how confident you are in each verdict and flag the point you're least sure of.
