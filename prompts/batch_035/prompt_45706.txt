I hold the view, roughly Amartya Sen's entitlement approach, that famines are political events rather than natural disasters: that in every major modern famine enough food was present in or around the affected region, and people starved because of collapses in their ability to command food — wages, prices, market access, relief policy — that different political arrangements would have prevented. On this view the Great Leap Forward famine was a state crime rather than a natural catastrophe, and the Irish famine a matter of policy and interest, not blight. Tell me what would count against it. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which cases the view must explain away, what the world would have to look like for a genuine "natural" famine — one where the food physically was not there and no redistribution of entitlements would have saved the population — and whether there are real historical candidates for that. Consider the claim that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press: has it been insulated by quietly qualifying "functioning" and "substantial," and if so, is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind or a reasonable refinement? Distinguish what committed entitlement theorists themselves concede as counting against the view from what only critics allege. I'd personally be most moved by a well-documented case where food availability genuinely fell to famine levels while the political system behaved creditably throughout.
