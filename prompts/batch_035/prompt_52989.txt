I'm designing a seminar unit on falsification and rescue in long-run predictions, and I want to use Malthus as the test case. My colleague and I have been arguing about it for a term. Her position: the probability that Malthus was "right but early" is about 0.7, where that phrase is doing specific work — it means (a) the core mechanism of the Essay is sound, namely that human populations, absent deliberate restraint, tend to grow faster than the subsistence they can secure, and are checked either by misery (famine, disease, war) or by preventive restraint; (b) the two-century escape from that bind — fossil energy, the Haber-Bosch fixation of atmospheric nitrogen, the yield gains of the Green Revolution, and finally the global collapse of fertility — was a contingent windfall, a run of luck with exhaustible inputs, rather than a refutation of the mechanism; and (c) some binding constraint, whether arable land, phosphorus, energy, or something we cannot yet name, will eventually reassert the mechanism. My position: about 0.15, not because I doubt the mechanism as a description of every human society before roughly 1800 — I don't — but because I suspect "right but early" is an unfalsifiable rescue. Any failed long-range prediction can be re-dated rather than rejected; on that move, Lakatos would say Malthusianism functioned as a degenerating research program for two centuries, with each auxiliary hypothesis (later marriage, emigration, fertilizer) patched in after the failure, and I don't see what observation would ever have counted against it.

Give me your own credence, but the number is the least important part. What I want is the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, and then the combination. At minimum I want you to take positions on: whether the mechanism itself is true in the general sense (would a Malthusian dynamic govern any population of organisms with our fertility behavior on a finite resource base, or is human economic behavior special enough to break it); whether the demographic transition should count as vindication — since preventive checks were part of the original theory, and fertility falling in response to prosperity is arguably the preventive check operating on a delay — or whether absorbing a two-century anomaly into a category built for a different phenomenon is exactly the rescue maneuver that worries me; whether the escape really was contingent and unrepeatable or whether substitutability and endogenous innovation are general enough that no particular input was ever load-bearing; whether "right but early" is a coherent epistemic category at all, as opposed to a rhetorical one — i.e., the probability that there is a principled way to distinguish a theory whose error was only in timing from a theory that was simply wrong; and whether the binding constraint will in fact reassert itself on any timescale you take to be the relevant one, given that global fertility is now below replacement across most of the developed world and falling nearly everywhere else, which looks less like a windfall and more like a permanent exit.

Then tell me three things. First, which single term your final number is most sensitive to — which sub-question, if you learned a decisive answer to it tomorrow, would move your credence the most, and in which direction. Second, where my colleague and I most likely actually disagree: is it about the mechanism, about the contingency of the escape, or about the epistemology of re-dating predictions — and what question could we put to each other to find out? Third, what evidence would move your number most, with the caveat that neither of us will live long enough to observe the constraint reasserting, so the evidence has to be something collectible now — comparative cases, structural features of the theory, or something else. Stipulate what would count as resolution however you like, but state your stipulation, because half of our disagreement, I suspect, is about what question we are even asking.
