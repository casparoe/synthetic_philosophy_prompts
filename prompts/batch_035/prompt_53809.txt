I'm a metaethics PhD student and somehow I keep getting voluntold to run sessions in our department's "philosophy for non-philosophers" series — next month it's about 45 minutes, whiteboard, maybe fifteen grad students mostly from econ, linguistics, and psych, and the organizer handed me the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism as my topic. Plan the session for me. Don't explain the problem to me — I know it cold, I need the pedagogy.

The audience is comfortable with basic logic (econ students eat modus ponens for breakfast) but knows zero metaethics, so the job isn't to make moral language seem weird to them — it's to make them see that the weirdness creates a specific technical problem for one specific view. So: the order the pieces need to go in, and why the sequence breaks if I reorder it — I'm actually tempted to open with negated moral sentences since they're easier than embedded conditionals, tell me whether that's a trap or a genuine on-ramp. For the carrying example, I don't want the standard getting-your-little-brother-to-torment-the-cat setup; give me something the econ folks won't find cartoonish, ideally with premises they'd actually endorse, and tell me what the example has to do and what it must NOT do (e.g. I suspect anything with mixed moral/descriptive premises imports baggage I don't want — am I right or just being precious?). Misconceptions I can already predict someone raising: that expressivism should just bite the bullet and call embedded moral sentences false or meaningless, that the whole thing is ambiguity like bank/bank, that this proves expressivism is dead and we can all go home, and the one where they think the punchline is that you can't infer attitudes into existence. Map where in the sequence each of these will surface and where exactly I should head it off. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to explain in 45 minutes and just flag as unfinished business — my guess is hybrid expressivism, but I worry the deflationist "who needs truth-conditions anyway" move is actually worse with this crowd since they've all seen a formal semantics. And end me with one question I can put to the room that would show me whether they got the real point, which as I understand it is that the expressivist needs one and the same occurrence of a moral term to make the same contribution in asserted and unasserted contexts, otherwise the inference loses its validity and with it the explanation of why the conclusion follows from the premises. Be honest about what a 45-minute version of this just can't deliver — my goal is that they leave seeing why the problem is about logic and not about people yelling their feelings at eachother, not that they leave with a verdict on whether expressivism survives.
