I've been trying to work out what I really believe about whether my individual acts make a moral difference, and my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously add up. I think a single flight I take to New York makes no difference to climate harms, yet I think a lifetime of such flights does amount to doing something wrong. I think one chicken purchase doesn't matter — the supermarket reorders on a smooth demand curve — yet I'd think less of a lifelong meat-eater who cites exactly that in his defence. I vote in every election even in a constituency where one party has a five-figure majority, and I think I'm right to, although my vote has no realistic chance of deciding anything; but I don't think I'm obliged to attend a demonstration that is certain to be enormous either way, and I would skip it in the rain without guilt. I feel fine declining to be the 999,997th signer of a petition, yet if my signature were the millionth — the one that triggers a promised donation — it would plainly matter. And I think the neighbour who enjoys cleaner streets thanks to everyone else's recycling, while not bothering himself, is doing something wrong, even though his own recycling would change nothing. Is there a single principle, or a small set of rivals, that captures these verdicts? What does it say about the case I can't settle: buying a second-hand petrol car for trips I could take by bus? Please tell me which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and be candid about where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail or what consideration would push you to the opposite conclusion.
