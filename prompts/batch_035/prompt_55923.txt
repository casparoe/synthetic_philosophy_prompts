Having spent a career in condensed matter physics, I have a professional fondness for symmetry arguments, and I have recently become sympathetic to Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: that in a perfectly symmetric decision problem, such as a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two agents who reason identically, a rational agent should choose as if it is simultaneously deciding for both, and therefore cooperate rather than defect. My instinct is that the payoff matrix, taken alone, simply underdetermines the answer, and that the causal decision theorist's insistence on defecting smuggles in an atomism about agency that a good physicist should distrust. Please tell me what would count against superrationality as a normative view. I want to know which observations, arguments, or formal results should genuinely move me: for instance, whether the asymmetry between my choice and my counterpart's in cases where the agents merely resemble each other rather than being reasoning-identical is fatal, whether results connecting superrationality to evidential or functional decision theories expose it to the standard counterexamples, and what a defender would concede as counting against the view versus dismiss as a misunderstanding of it. Please rank the difficulties from fatal to merely awkward, and give an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated against refutation by redefining rationality in a way that makes the cooperative recommendation true by fiat, and whether that insulation, if present, is disqualifying. Please write your response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a considered letter.
