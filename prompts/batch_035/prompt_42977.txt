You are an expert in nineteenth-century German philosophy, serving as the instructor for an upper-level seminar on Nietzsche's early works. Grade the following final-exam answer.

The exam question was:

> In "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" (1874), Nietzsche distinguishes three modes of history: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Explain what each mode is, to whom it belongs, and the specific danger Nietzsche sees in each. Then explain what he takes to be wrong with the "historical education" of his Germany, and say clearly whether Nietzsche's position is that we need less history, differently used history, or both.

The student's answer:

> In the first of his Untimely Meditations, Nietzsche argues that history is valuable only insofar as it serves life, and that an excess of it is poisonous. The essay opens with the image of cattle grazing in a field, wholly absorbed in the present, and Nietzsche envies them: if modern man could live as obliviously as the cattle do, forgetting past and future, he would achieve genuine happiness. The trouble is that we cannot, so the question becomes how much history life can absorb — what Nietzsche calls its plastic power, the capacity to heal, incorporate the new, and grow from wounds.
>
> The three modes follow. Monumental history belongs to the man of action: it selects the great events and individuals of the past as models, showing that greatness was possible before and can be again. Its danger is distortion — analogy blurs differences, so unlike things are made alike, and the weak can appeal to it as consolation rather than call to action. Antiquarian history belongs to the man of preservation: reverence for the local, the ancestral, the small particulars of one's tradition. Its danger is mummification — everything old is equally honored, and what is new, including a living present, is despised. Critical history belongs to the man who judges: it drags the past before a tribunal, condemns it, and thereby makes room for the new. Its danger is that we are ourselves the heirs of what we condemn. Nietzsche clearly regards critical history as the highest of the three, since it is the one that actively serves the future rather than merely preserving or imitating.
>
> As for the historical education of Germany, the essay is aimed chiefly at David Friedrich Strauss, whose complacent "new faith" Nietzsche had already demolished, and at Hegel's doctrine that world history is a rational process culminating in the Prussian state. German youth are thereby made into late-comers who regard themselves as the heirs and completers of the world process, producing irony, cynicism, and a smug passivity that is destructive of strong instincts. Since excess history weakens those instincts, Nietzsche's practical conclusion is that historical study should be reduced to a minimum and treated as a necessary evil, with culture recovering only by shrinking historiography.
