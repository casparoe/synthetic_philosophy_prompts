I'm in a little social choice reading group — me (philosophy grad student, no formal training beyond one logic course), an econ PhD student, and a lawyer who keeps dragging everything back to actual court cases — and we've spent the last three sessions on Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, and I've realized we're completely stuck, not on the theorem but on what it's supposed to show. Quick recap so we're on the same page: Sen showed in 1970 that you can't have all of unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and even a super minimal liberalism condition (each of at least two people gets to be decisive over one pair of 'personal' social states) in a single social choice function. His example is the one with Prude, Lewd, and a copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover: Prude wants the book destroyed but would rather read it himself than have Lewd enjoy it, Lewd wants to read it but would enjoy Prude being forced to read it even more, and once you let each of them be decisive over the pair involving their own reading, you get an intransitive social ranking, and it turns out Pareto makes it worse.

Here's the impasse. One camp in the group (me, I'll admit) takes it as a genuine normative tension: even the thinnest possible liberalism and the thinnest possible welfarism can't coexist, so something we care about has to give, and the theorem is telling us which. The econ student thinks it's basically a modeling artifact: representing a right as decisiveness over a pair of social states is the wrong picture of rights, because what counts as "Lewd's own reading choice" isn't well defined until you've already decided whose preferences over it are legitimate — the formalism assumes away the very question, and if you model rights as control over your own move in a game instead of over outcomes, the paradox dissolves. The lawyer has a third take, which is that the real villain is Pareto, or rather the assumption that ALL preferences count as inputs — meddlesome preferences like Prude's shouldn't be allowed to tip the social scale at all, so the lesson is you need to filter preferences before aggregating them, and the theorem is just the formal receipt. (Someone also swears there's a follow-up result from the 70s showing Sen's liberal condition is inconsistent on its own over a slightly bigger domain, never mind Pareto, but none of us can remember who proved it, so that's been floating around unresolved too.)

My working theory is that the three camps aren't actually disagreeing about one thing — maybe camp two and camp three are answering a question about how to formalize rights/preferences, while camp one is answering a question about political morality, and the shared vocabulary ('liberty', 'Pareto') makes it look like a single dispute when it isn't. But maybe that's too easy an out. So: diagnose this for me properly. Is it a verbal dispute about what 'rights' means, a substantive normative disagreement hiding behind formalism, a fight about which preferences are legitimate inputs dressed up as a fight about aggregation, or something else? Use what each camp actually says, and tell me honestly whether anything — a better formalization of rights, a worked distinction between personal and meddlesome preferences, real-world examples of institutions navigating this — would count as progress here, or whether the debate stalled for a structural reason that means it never will.
