I spent my working life in population genetics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science — Popper in particular. Something now puzzles me about the practice I spent decades participating in, and I would like someone to reconstruct what that practice implicitly presupposes.

Here is the practice. When a biologist proposes an adaptive explanation of a trait — this enzyme's optimum shifted because the climate cooled; this gene was driven to fixation by selection for pesticide resistance — the discipline no longer accepts the story on plausibility alone. We require that it be tested against a null alternative in which the pattern arose by drift, and the tests are familiar: compare the rate of substitutions that alter the encoded protein against the rate of silent ones that do not; ask whether the pattern of variation bears the signature a selective sweep would leave rather than the signature drift would; simulate the neutral process and ask whether the observation is extreme under it. The rhetoric surrounding all this is Popperian. We say we are trying to refute the adaptationist story, and we dismiss an untested one as a just-so tale until the neutral alternative has been excluded. Yet the inferential machinery we actually use is not Popperian at all. We compute how probable the data are under competing hypotheses and prefer the hypothesis under which they are less surprising; we never deduce a prediction and consign a theory to the scrap-heap upon a single contrary observation, but accumulate comparative support.

Popper himself, I gather, spent some years doubting whether the theory of natural selection was testable at all — on the ground that, formulated carelessly, it threatens to collapse into the tautology that the fittest are those who survive and leave more offspring — and he later withdrew much of that doubt, allowing that the theory, properly formulated, is testable and has in fact survived severe tests. So my question is this: what view of scientific testing does the practice I have described presuppose? Does it vindicate Popper's withdrawal of the doubt, by presupposing that fitness can be measured independently of survival, so that the claim that the fittest leave more offspring is a risky, refutable assertion rather than a definition? Or does it show that his original worry was aimed at the wrong target? And does the practice hang together at all, given that its rhetoric is deductivist while its machinery is probabilist? Which rival views of testing does it quietly rule out — and is it in fact compatible with several of them, so that the Popperian gloss is decoration rather than commitment? Finally, if we took the presupposed view seriously and replaced the rhetoric with it, what would visibly change in how papers are written, reviewed, and defended?

I would like one subsidiary puzzle folded into the answer. The same practice treats the neutral alternative as the null hypothesis to be rejected, rather than as an equal competitor to be compared symmetrically. Does that presuppose a substantive commitment — that drift is the default condition of populations and selection the exception requiring special evidence — and, if so, is that a commitment about how the world mostly is, or merely a convention about who bears the burden of proof?
