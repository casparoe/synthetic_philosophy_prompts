I've been in a decision-theory reading group (we met off the back of a LessWrong-adjacent meetup, mostly working through the classic stuff on precommitments and updatelessness) and one of the guys has an argument that's been bugging me for a week. It starts from commitment races — the idea that in bargaining situations, credibly burning your boats first wins you the surplus, so rational agents are incentivised to commit earlier and harder than each other, until some of them end up stuck in terrible equilibria they'd have paid a lot to avoid. Fine, standard, we've all seen that discussion. But then he connects it to updatelessness and I can't tell if the result is profound or just broken. His argument:

(1) A commitment is only advantageous if it's made before the information arrives that would make you regret it. (2) So the optimal commitment is made as early as possible — before you know which games you'll even face. (3) Updateless agents do exactly this: behind a veil of ignorance about their observations, they pick one policy for all worlds and stick to it. (4) So updatelessness is the terminal stage of the commitment race — the race everyone "wins" by pre-committing pre-natally to everything at once. (5) Since the updateless agent has already made every commitment it will ever make, it can never be beaten to the punch in any bargaining situation, so it never needs to worry about commitment races again. (6) But by the same token it's permanently locked into whatever bargaining posture that pre-natally chosen policy encodes, including against agents whose existence it couldn't have anticipated. (7) And a theory of rationality that requires you to permanently surrender the ability to respond to the actual world — including the ability to make new commitments! — in order to win commitment races has proven too much: it isn't solving the race, it's declaring the racers finished before they're born.

He says either you accept the whole thing and updatelessness eats commitment and then eats itself, or you deny (2), in which case you have to say exactly when the commitment window closes, which nobody can do non-arbitrarily. My instinct is that the slide happens between (2) and (3) — treating "commit early" as "commit once and for all" — but every time I try to say it out loud it sounds like I'm just re-asserting that fresh information should matter, which is precisely the thing updatelessness denies, so I talk myself into a corner. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather not give up either updatelessness or the claim that some late, informed commitments are rational, but I suspect I can't keep both. Also, if you think the whole thing diffuse into two different senses of "commit" and that's doing all the work, I'd want to see exactly where.
