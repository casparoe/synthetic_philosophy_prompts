My friend made a promise to her grandma on her deathbed — something pretty arbitrary tbh, scattering the ashes at a lake the grandma loved but that everyone else in the family hates and that's a genuine pain to get to (like 9 hours of driving plus a ferry). When my friend started hedging about whether it really had to be THAT lake, her mom shut the whole thing down with "a promise is a promise," said like it ends the discussion. And it's been bugging me since that this little tautology is doing alot of work in everyday arguments, and I genuinely can't tell what work it's doing. Can you unpack the different precise claims "a promise is a promise" could be making? I can think of a few candidate readings but I bet there are more. Like: (a) the literal reading, which is just "this promise is a promise" — a nothing-burger, but maybe rhetorically doing something like "stop treating this promise as a maybe-promise"; (b) that the obligation is internal to promising itself, so it doesn't get weighed against consequences the way other obligations do — this seems to be the one people actually mean?; (c) something stronger, that promises bind absolutely with no exceptions at all, which seems false (everyone accepts promissory excuses, and there's the classic thing about promises extracted at gunpoint or under duress, or promises to do something wrong); (d) a character thing — not really about the promise having some magic binding force but about what breaking it says about you, like the keeping matters to who you are rather than to the grandma; (e) a practice/institution thing, where the point is that promising as a whole only works if people can't renege when it's costly, so the individual case rests on the general practice rather than anything about this particular promise; (f) maybe something about promises being exercizes of a special normative power — like how consenting or commanding seems to just MAKE an obligation exist rather than track one, and "a promise is a promise" gestures at that weird fact; and (g) something about the promisee's stake, where what matters is that the grandma relied on it or was owed it, which seems like it could cut either way here since she's dead now and I've seen people argue obligations to the dead are a weird special case (who exactly is wronged if it's not done? the family? no one?). For each reading, say whether it's basically trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or flat-out false, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it supports the mom's use of it, which wasn't just "you should do it anyway" but "no weighing of costs permitted, end of discussion." Because it seems to me like several of the readings support "you should probably still do it" without supporting "it's not up for debate," and at least one might even let my friend off the hook, or not, I honestly can't tell.
