I'm a PhD student in psych and I somehow got volunteered onto the committee in our program that hands out a small pot of pilot funding every quarter (~$15k) for students to run studies before applying for real grants. This semester I got sucked into a decision theory reading group and we just did Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, and it seems almost suspiciously well-suited to our problem: we obviously can't pretend we're risk-neutral with a fixed pot and students' careers on the line, and REU at least gives the committee's gut risk attitudes a principled place to live, as an explicit risk function over cumulative probabilities rather than vibes that shift depending on who argued loudest.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the committee can actually run, quarter after quarter, in a two-hour meeting over a stack of ~8 proposals. Concretely: how do we fix the risk function r once (elicit it from committee members? offer a few candidates and vote?) and then treat it as settled rather than relitigating it on every proposal; how do we turn a proposal into a gamble in the first place -- defining outcomes, ordering them worst to best, and assigning probabilities when most of our probabilities are basically made up; how to compute REU on a whiteboard in ten minutes (I imagine we bin outcomes and weight by the r-transformed cumulative probabilities but I want the exact recipe); a quick check for when risk attitude can actually flip the ranking versus when it's just expected value anyway so we don't waste time; and how to handle the portfolio problem, since REU is a theory of single choices but we're slicing one pot across multiple projects, and I genuinely don't know if that breaks the whole approach or if there's a sane way to allocate sequentially.

Please be explicit about where the procedure is secretly a judgment call -- like, how coarsely we bin outcomes can probably flip the answer, right? -- and include a fallback for when the committee can't agree on r at all. Then run it on three hypothetical proposals: (a) a adequately-powered direct replication with near-certain modest payoff, (b) a cheap online study testing a long-shot theory that, if it pans out, unlocks a whole literature, and (c) two coin-flip-ish sub-projects that could be funded together or separately, to show what the procedure does with correlations. For at least one of these the procedure should give an answer I won't like -- I'll admit I'm a moonshot person and I suspect a concave-ish r will kill my favorites, so show me exactly where that happens, so I can't pretend the theory is secretly on my side.
