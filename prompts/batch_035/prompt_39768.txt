I'm a PhD student who mostly works on the epistemology of testimony, but I'm in a norms-of-assertion reading group this term and I've been keeping a private list of my verdicts on when it's proper to assert something, trying to see if they hang together. Here they are. Telling my friend, before the draw even happens, "you're going to lose the lottery" — a million tickets, hers is one of them — feels improper in a way that "it's a million-ticket draw, so probably not" doesn't, even though the flat assertion is basically certain to be true. Someone looking at what is in fact a dog in a field, in good light, and saying "there's a sheep in the field" — when there IS a sheep, but it's the one hidden behind the hill that the dog is standing in front of — seems completely fine to me, even though they don't know it. There's a teacher case floating around the literature where someone thinks the evidence for evolution is a hoax but asserts it to her class anyway because that's what the evidence supports — that also seems fine to me, or at least not improper the way the lottery assertion is. "It's raining but I don't believe it" is obviously a paradigm of impropriety. And when I imagine challenging someone's assertion, "how do you know?" feels like the natural, cutting challenge, whereas "is it true?" feels somehow off-target.

The obvious problem is that the lottery case and the challenge data pull hard toward "assert only what you know," but the sheep case and the teacher case seem to violate that norm while being fine. My tentative repair is something like: proper to assert when your evidence is as good as it could reasonably be, locally, for that kind of claim — but I can't get this to condemn the lottery assertion without also condemning the sheep one, since the looker's evidence is about as good as evidence for 'there's a sheep over there' ever gets. So maybe the verdicts just don't cohere, or maybe my repair is just badly stated. There's also a wrinkle I keep going back and forth on: asserting a diagnosis you're 90% sure of to a patient who directly asks "is that certain?" — I genuinely can't settle whether my feeling is that this is fine, or that the doctor is required to say "probably" rather than "yes".

So: is there a principle that captures all my verdicts, or something close, or do some of them have to go? Please give me exactly five numbered items, each only a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your best candidate principle, stated precisely; (2) what it says about each of my five verdicts from the first paragraph; (3) which verdict, if any, it has to overturn and why that one is the right one to sacrifice; (4) its verdict on the doctor case; and (5) an honest note on what's actually settled in this debate versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on it — don't present one side's take as if it were the consensus, because I genuinely can't tell from the reading group how much of this is agreed vs. load-bearing assumptions.
