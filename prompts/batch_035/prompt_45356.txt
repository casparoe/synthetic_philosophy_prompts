Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Bernard Williams's integrity objection to consequentialism. Her version of it: consequentialism requires an agent to be always ready to abandon her deepest commitments whenever the world would go even slightly better if she did, and a person who internalizes that requirement is alienated from her own life — there is no longer anyone whose projects those were, so the theory destroys the very agents whose good it claims to promote. I resisted by invoking the companion point Williams is famous for, usually called the one-thought-too-many point: when someone does the decent thing only after checking that it maximizes overall good, the calculation itself, quite apart from the outcome, strikes us as defective — a thought the agent could not fittingly act from. Her comeback was that this concession dissolves her own objection rather than supporting it: if what is wrong with the consequentialist agent is a matter of which thoughts it is admirable or appropriate to act from, then Williams has at best given us a theory of character, not a refutation of claims about what ought to be done, and the consequentialist can simply agree that agents ought not to calculate while insisting that the theory, not the agent, is where the maximizing belongs. We broke off there, each suspecting the other of silently shifting between 'ought' claims about acts and 'ought' claims about agents, and neither of us could say how the shift is supposed to be blocked.

Next month I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends the integrity objection in roughly Williams's form, and I want to use the occasion to get past the point where my friend and I stalled. Write exactly five interview questions, numbered 1 to 5, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. At least one question must press the tension I described — whether the objection survives once it is reformulated as a claim about the agent's standpoint rather than about what ought to be done, and if so what work the word 'objection' is still doing. At least one must take on the sophisticated consequentialist's reply that the theory can itself direct agents not to calculate, and ask what is supposed to remain wrong with an agent who never calculates but whose ultimate justification for her projects is external to them. At least one should press on what the objection presupposes about the unity of a life — whether 'integrity' names a psychological fact that any theory of right action must accommodate, and what would count as evidence for that. The remaining questions are up to you, but for each one the second sentence should be a follow-up keyed to the most likely reply, so that no question can be answered with a restatement of the view.
