For some years now I have held, without examining it very hard, a hybrid view about the moral status of animals that I first encountered in Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, in the section of his third chapter titled Constraints and Animals. Stated in my own terms: persons are protected by moral side constraints — they may not be used, harmed, or sacrificed merely as means to aggregate benefits, however large those benefits are — while nonhuman animals receive no comparable protections; their pains, pleasures, and lives count only as inputs to an aggregate, to be weighed against the interests of other animals and of persons. Nozick, as I read him, gestured at something like this combination — a utilitarianism governing what may be done to animals running alongside a Kantianism governing what may be done to persons — without ever committing to it. I have drifted into committing to it. It accounts, neatly, for my firm judgment that one may not kill an innocent person to harvest organs for five, alongside my judgment that great human benefits can justify serious animal costs. What I want now is not a defense but a map of the view's exposure: what would count against it.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what a committed defender of the hybrid would concede as genuine counterevidence from what they would dismiss, and address at least the following specifically:

1. The line-drawing problem. If the person/animal boundary is drawn by species membership, the view is open to the charge of speciesism: why should membership in a biological category, rather than the capacities that plausibly ground constraints, do the moral work? If it is drawn by capacities — self-consciousness, rationality, the ability to value one's own life — then some animals plausibly qualify for side constraints and some humans (infants, the severely cognitively impaired) plausibly do not, and the hybrid must either accept those verdicts or attach constraints to something other than the very capacities that were supposed to distinguish animals from persons, which looks ad hoc.

2. The trivial-benefits problem. Nozick's own examples haunt me here. He asks whether it is permissible to snap one's fingers, for the pleasure of snapping along to music, if one knows that the snapping would cause ten thousand cattle to die in agony; he asks whether one may kill a cow merely to obtain the best material for a baseball bat whose slight superiority yields extra pleasure. My view says the cattle deaths, spread over the aggregate, could be outweighed; but it also says that the marginal gustatory pleasure of eating animals rather than plants, accumulated across billions of eaters, could outweigh the suffering of intensive farming. I believe intensive farming is wrong. Can the hybrid say so without smuggling a constraint back in for animals?

3. The lexicality problem. Is there any case in which an animal's major interest outweighs a person's minor one? If I say the person's interest always wins, the view is not really a utilitarianism for animals at all — it contains a hidden lexical priority for persons, and the hybrid collapses toward Kantianism for everyone or plain speciesism. If I say aggregation runs across the species boundary, then enough toenail-level human conveniences can apparently justify enormous animal suffering, which also seems wrong.

4. The predation problem. If animal welfare is governed by an aggregate, why does the aggregate only matter when humans tip it? Does the view commit me to obligations regarding wild animal suffering — predator control, habitat intervention — of a demandingness I am not prepared to accept? And if not, what stops the slide?

5. Underdetermination. What, if anything, does the hybrid predict that its rivals — a pure utilitarianism that grants side constraints to no one, and an animal-rights view that grants them to at least some animals — do not? If two or more of these views agree on every case either of us can construct, I want to know whether that is a problem for the hybrid or whether it is the kind of view whose exposure must be theoretical rather than observational.

Finally, I would find most persuasive a demonstration that the hybrid, consistently applied, either licenses something I regard as clearly impermissible or forbids something I regard as clearly permissible; I cannot currently see which way such a demonstration would run, and I would rather you ran it in whichever direction the arguments actually point than flattered my prior verdicts.
