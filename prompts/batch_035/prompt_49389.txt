On September 26, 1983, the Soviet early-warning system reported first one and then five American missile launches. The duty officer, Stanislav Petrov, judged the report to be a false alarm — his reasoning, on the standard account, being that a genuine American first strike would not begin with five missiles — and declined to pass the alert up the chain of command. The report was later attributed to sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds; no missiles had been launched. I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, with attention to epistemic closure. Work through the mapping carefully: identify the candidate knowledge ascriptions (did Petrov *know* the report was a false alarm? did he *know* no missiles were incoming? did he *know* the system had malfunctioned?), and ask for each whether the belief satisfies the sensitivity condition — in particular, ask whether, in the closest worlds where missiles really were inbound, Petrov would still have disbelieved the alarm, and what that implies about whether his "no launch" belief tracks the truth. Then consider the closure question: whether tracking theory can allow that Petrov knew the alarm was unreliable while denying that he knew, of some specific proposition the alarm's unreliability is supposed to rule out, that it obtained — and whether that combination is an embarrassment for the theory or a virtue. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about tracking theory itself: does it vindicate Nozick's refusal to let knowledge of a disjunction decompose into knowledge of its disjuncts, or does Petrov's situation reveal a structural problem that a safety-based account handles better? Be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and keep the analysis disciplined about which facts of the episode do the philosophical work. Please write in American English.
