My dad died about six weeks ago, and I've been seeing a therapist since. At the last session she kind of awkwardly told me that under the current diagnostic manual I already meet the criteria for major depressive disorder — apparently there used to be a rule that you couldn't diagnose depression during the first couple of months after losing someone, but they got rid of it when the manual was revised, so now the same symptoms count as a disorder within two weeks of the death. She clearly didn't love telling me that, and I didn't love hearing it.

It's been bugging me since, but not in the way you'd expect. My symptoms are honestly a textbook response to my dad dying — can't sleep, no interest in anything, the whole checklist. Everything in me seems to be doing exactly what you'd expect a human to do when their father dies. So in what sense is there a "dysfunction" here? The manual frames disorders as some psychological mechanism not doing what it's supposed to do, and I keep snagging on that "supposed to."

I've read enough Wikipedia to know there are basically two ways people cash out biological function. One says the function of a trait is whatever it was selected for over evolutionary history. The other says function is just the role a part plays in keeping the larger system going right now. If grief is a response that selection actually shaped — the crying, the withdrawing, the constant thinking about him — then on the first view my mind is functioning exactly as designed, and labeling it a disorder starts to look like calling a fever during an infection an organ failure. But on the second view I'm not sure what the relevant "system" even is. Me as an organism that keeps myself alive and gets things done? And is grief even the kind of thing that functions apply to, or does function talk only work for mechanisms and organs and not for states like sadness?

And here's the part that really bothers me. From what I can tell, the decision to drop the grief exemption wasn't driven by anyone settling which theory of function is correct — it was driven by worries that people with genuinely treatable depression were being denied care, plus insurance coding and all that. So does the diagnostic practice actually presuppose an account of function at all, or is it compatible with several of them? Or maybe what it really presupposes is something about the line between being harmed and malfunctioning — the idea that you can be harmed and still be working fine, versus harmed because something broke.

So that's what I want to know: what does the practice of diagnosing grief as depression actually commit us to? Which account of function does it strictly need, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it underdetermined so that a bunch of theories would license the same diagnosis? And what would a clinician who took the other view seriously have to change in practice — would they wait longer, use different symptoms, refuse the label altogether? Last thing, and be honest with me: is the two-week thing as philosophically weird as it feels right now, or am I just being defensive because it's my grief they're talking about? For context I'm 41, functionally getting through my days, and I definately don't feel "disordered", just sad in a way that seems like the correct amount.
