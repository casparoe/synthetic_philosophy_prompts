ok, background: I'm a retired geochemist, spent 35 years dating rocks and arguing about error bars, and since retiring I've gotten obsessed with the question of when a legend turns into history. I've read a fair bit now (pop stuff mostly, some of the drier academic things) and I've converged on a set of views. My daughter says some of them contradict each other and I honestly can't see it, so I want an actual audit. Here's the package:

1. Oral tradition is a lot sturdier than the cynics think. Basically every long-remembered tradition preserves a real kernel of events; the distortion is real but the kernel survives. (This got reinforced for me by Schliemann actually digging up Troy after everyone said Homer was pure fairy tale.)

2. So: there was a real war, or several conflated into one, at Hisarlik, and the Iliad preserves a genuinely old, badly garbled memory of Mycenaean palace society.

3. King Arthur, on the other hand, is basically fiction. Folklore motifs welded onto maybe a name. I don't believe there's a recoverable historical figure there at all.

4. The Exodus as described also didn't happen. No Egyptian record of anything like it, no trace in Sinai archaeology. At most a small group left Egypt and the memory got inflated later, or the whole thing was constructed long after.

5. That said, I hold that absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence, especially for nomadic peoples who leave nothing, and especially for losers — the victors don't write down their embarrassments. You of all people should respect that not finding something is often just a statement about your detection limit, not about the deposit.

6. Here's my own contribution, the one I actually think is novel (my physicist brain couldn't help itself): fidelity of oral transmission should decay roughly exponentialy, like a radioactive decay curve. There's a "half-life" of historical memory. My back-of-envelope guess is that past ~500 years of transmission you're mostly reading noise. Related point: two sources only count as independent if their errors aren't correlated, and most supposedly independent sources about the deep past are actually copies or branches of a common earlier telling.

7. The contamination principle: the more gods and marvels woven through a story, the less real history survives in it. Marvels push out memory, the way bioturbation destroys laminations (sorry, sediment joke).

Also, for what it's worth: I accept the Vikings reached Newfoundland, and my wife says that shows I'm credulous about legends, same as with Troy. And I think the Trojan Horse itself is obviously invented, and Achilles and Helen are probably as real as Paul Bunyan, even while point 2 stands.

So: go through all of this and tell me which of my commitments actually pull against each other, which only look like they conflict, and how bad each tension is. Rank them by severity instead of listing everything as equally damning, and for each one say what would have to give and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'm not going to tell you which one I suspect is the weak spot, because I genuinely don't know — if I did I wouldn't be asking. Please be specific about the mechanisms, don't just wave at "oral tradition is complicated." And don't let me off the hook on point 6 — if my little half-life model is doing real work anywhere, or causing damage anywhere, I want to know.
