I serve a rotating term as the ethics member on my university's animal care and use committee — the slot traditionally filled by a philosopher — and I review roughly a dozen protocols a month: studies involving mice, rats, zebrafish, and occasional work with dogs and pigs. The veterinarians and scientists on the committee want operational answers before each monthly meeting, and the committee's votes are audited by federal inspectors, so our written reasoning has to be defensible. What they usually get from me instead is an unhelpful tour of the moral theories. I want to find out whether Kant's treatment of animals can actually be turned into something a committee could run.

Set aside for now whether Kant is right. His position, as I understand it from his ethics lectures and the Doctrine of Virtue, is that we have no duties *to* animals, only duties *regarding* them: cruelty toward animals is wrong because of what it does to the agent — it dulls the sympathy on which our duties to humanity depend — and because animals, as sentient fellow laborers of a kind, furnish an instructive analogy to humanity, as in his example of the old working dog who ought not to be cast off once he can no longer serve. Everything hinges on that rationale. Please write a decision procedure that my non-philosopher colleagues could actually apply: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which question the procedure terminates in a no, and — this is the part I most want to see — where it terminates in a handoff because the remaining question is one of values or contested empirical fact rather than something the Kantian apparatus can settle. In making the translation you will have to decide which distinctions must survive the simplification (direct versus indirect duties, the character-cultivation rationale, the analogy to humanity) and which can be dropped (Kant's teleological claim that nature's purpose for animals is human use, perhaps). Be explicit about those choices, and be equally explicit about which steps of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three protocols of the kind we actually see: (1) a sepsis study in which mice are permitted to become gravely ill and die without analgesia, on the grounds that analgesics confound the data, conducted by experienced staff who handle the animals daily; (2) the routine killing of surplus animals from a breeding colony — entirely painless, performed by trained personnel with no animus and, often, minimal contact with the animals; and (3) a toxicology study farmed out to a commercial contract facility, so that the suffering is real but the moral agents closest to it are strangers to our institution. The third case is where I suspect the Kantian rationale goes strange — if the wrongness runs through the agent's own character, harm that is outsourced and performed by people who are merely doing a job seems oddly insulated — and I would like you to be honest about that. I expect at least one of the three to yield a verdict the committee, which is also bound by regulations premised on animal suffering mattering in its own right, will find unpalatable — the painless-surplus case, I suspect — and I want you to state plainly what the procedure cannot do, where it misfires, and whether the misfires are repairable within Kant's framework or only by smuggling in premises Kant himself would reject.

The procedure must be runnable in under an hour per protocol by an intelligent non-philosopher. Do not trouble yourself with citations or literature — I know that literature — what I need is the operational artifact itself.
