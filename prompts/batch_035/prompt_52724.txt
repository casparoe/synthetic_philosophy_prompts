My friend is a nurse who reads a lot of popular science books, and she's been pressing an argument on me for weeks that I can't answer. She starts with a story about a scientist raised from birth in a black-and-white room, where she learns absolutely everything there is to know about the physics of light, the wiring of the eye and brain, and how people use color words — every physical fact about color and color vision, full stop. The day she finally walks out and sees a red tomato, it seems obvious that she learns something: she finds out what red looks like. So my friend lays it out like this. (1) Before leaving the room, the scientist knows every physical fact. (2) After seeing red for the first time, she knows something she didn't know before. (3) Anything you come to know is either a physical fact or it isn't. (4) Since she already had all the physical facts, whatever she gains must be a non-physical fact. (5) So there are facts about the world that are not physical facts, and the claim that everything is ultimately physical is false.

On its own this just strikes me as strong, honestly. But here's what bothers me: when I pushed her, she agreed the same reasoning works in other places. A chemist who knows every physical fact about hangovers but has never been drunk seems to learn something the first morning after. A man who has memorized every physical fact about Rome but has never left Paris seems to learn something the first time he walks out into one of its piazzas. Run those through her steps and you get non-physical facts about hangovers and about the city of Rome — and she doesn't want to bite that bullet. She only wants the conclusion for colors and feelings, not for cities and headaches. So something in the argument seems to prove too much, and yet when I read each step on its own, it still looks fine to me.

Which step should we reject, and why did it look okay when I first accepted it? I'd rather keep step (2) if that's possible — it really does seem like the scientist learns something when she sees red — but I want to understand exactly what keeping it costs, and what the alternatives charge for giving it up instead.
