I teach philosophy of language at a small department, and at last week's curriculum meeting I watched two colleagues of mine — call them Dr. Ahn and Dr. Boyer — argue at length about the contemporary standing of Russell's theory of descriptions. I have been unable to settle for myself who came out ahead, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

Some background. The exchange began when we had to decide whether to teach the theory in our introductory unit as refuted, superseded, or still live. Dr. Ahn defended the Russellian analysis of sentences like "The present King of France is bald" as a quantified conjunction — that there is exactly one present King of France, and that whatever is the present King of France is bald — noting that the paraphrase dissolves the classic puzzles: negative existentials no longer require Meinong's shadowy objects, excluded middle is preserved once negation is allowed wide scope, and George IV can wonder whether Scott is the author of Waverley without thereby wondering whether Scott is Scott.

Dr. Boyer replied that as an account of English the analysis is simply falsified. Strawson observed that we do not judge the sentence about the King of France to be false; rather, the question of its truth or falsity fails to arise. And Donnellan showed that speakers routinely use descriptions to pick out a particular individual even when the description fits nothing or fits the wrong thing — as when someone at a party says of a man whose glass in fact contains only water that the man drinking a martini is going to be ill, and thereby says something true about that man. On strict Russellian truth conditions the utterance is false, so a theory that gets competent usage this wrong has been refuted, not refined.

Dr. Ahn answered that Boyer was conflating the semantic content of the sentence with what speakers do with it: Strawson mistook a precondition on making an assertion for a gap in truth conditions, and Donnellan's referential uses can be treated along Gricean lines, as Stephen Neale has argued — what is literally said is the quantificational content, and the referential intention explains what is meant. The Gricean apparatus of two levels was invented precisely for cases where literal content and communicated content come apart, and no one supposes that conversational implicature falsifies the semantics of "and" or "some."

Dr. Boyer then pressed the cost of that rescue: it requires saying that the martini speaker said something false while communicating something true, which strains the notion of what is said; and Kripke's distinction between speaker's reference and semantic reference makes the divergence look principled and systematic rather than incidental, which suggests that the referential use reflects the semantics of descriptions rather than a pragmatic overlay on them. He added that Russell's own motivations — knowledge by acquaintance, the elimination of Meinongian entities — are now museum pieces, so what survives is at best an idealization, like the frictionless plane in mechanics: indispensable for certain inferential purposes, wrong if read as a description of how English actually works.

Dr. Ahn closed by insisting that inferential structure is no minor purpose: the validity of our arguments, including Boyer's own, depends on the logical form the quantificational paraphrase exposes, and an idealization that underwrites logic is doing exactly what semantic theory is for. Boyer rejoined that semantics is not only for logic, and that a theory which reclassifies its apparent counterexamples as pragmatic whenever they arise has smuggled the contested cases outside its domain rather than accounted for them.

They finally tested their positions on a fresh case that came up in our own department: our newsletter, prepared in advance, announced that the holder of a newly endowed visiting lectureship would present her research on Tuesday — in a week when, as it happened, the bequest fell through and no lecturer was ever appointed. Ahn said the newsletter sentence is simply false on the Russellian reading and the department owed its readers a correction. Boyer said that nobody would naturally call the sentence false; it misfires, and the appropriate correction is that there is no such lecturer — which, he said, is Strawson's point exactly.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Taking on the role of an expert in the philosophy of language, begin by briefly reconstructing the strongest considerations on each side before assigning verdicts, and then say who is right about which specific claims — not merely who prevails overall — and identify anything both parties are missing, including whether the test case they settled on genuinely discriminates between their positions or was ill-chosen. Throughout, please separate what is settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested; for instance, if the Gricean treatment of referential use, or the Strawsonian presuppositional treatment, has not carried the day by consensus, say so explicitly rather than presenting either as the received view. And where you are genuinely uncertain in your own adjudication, flag that as well.
