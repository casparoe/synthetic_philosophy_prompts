For the past three years I have been reading about the French Revolution on my own — I have no training in history, just whatever books I worked through and the arguments I follow (and join) in online history forums. Over that time I have noticed that one argument never moves: what the Revolution was really about. One camp says class struggle — a rising commercial class pushing out a privileged order, with the Terror as that conflict eventually turning on itself. A second camp says ideas: a new political language of the people and the general will took hold, and the violence came from the inner logic of that language, not from anyone's material position. A third camp shrugs: it was a chain of contingent choices — war, harvest failures, factional panic — and things could easily have gone otherwise at a dozen points.

As far as I can tell, professional historians largely stopped taking the first camp seriously decades ago; the class picture apparently does not fit what is known about who stood where (plenty of nobles and lawyers on the revolutionary side, and the bourgeoisie and the aristocracy not lined up against each other the way the story requires). Yet among people like me the argument never ends, and I have come to suspect it is not really one argument at all. Listening closely, I notice that people's answers to two different questions always move together: what caused the Revolution, and whether the Terror was already implied in the project from the start — whether the Revolution, as people like to put it, devoured its own children. Those who say class struggle tend to say the violence was incidental, a product of circumstance; those who say it was about ideology tend to say the violence simply was the ideology arriving at its conclusion. Almost nobody's view on one question shifts without their view on the other shifting too, which makes me doubt that either question is being answered on the evidence alone.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the standoff substantive — a genuine disagreement about facts that more archival work could in principle settle — or verbal, with the camps using the word cause to mean different things (some meaning the deep background conditions without which nothing, others meaning the thing that actually tipped the situation)? Are the camps answering different questions under one name — why it started versus why it turned murderous? Or is the real disagreement about what counts as an explanation at all: is naming a structural condition an explanation, or only a description of the room in which the actual explaining still has to be done? Base the diagnosis on what each camp actually says, not on a survey of the Revolution, and be explicit about which parts of the disagreement you think no further evidence could settle and why. Then, if your diagnosis holds, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction people could adopt, a splitting of the question in two, or an honest argument that this particular argument should be expected to run forever.
