For most of my working life I've been a live sound engineer — twenty-odd years of club gigs, festivals, and the occasional corporate job — and my native technical vocabulary is the one you learn at a mixing desk: signal path, gain staging, buses, aux sends and returns, monitor feeds, latency, feedback loops, grounding. I have no academic background in anything, but for the past year I've been chewing on a position I first heard laid out on a podcast interview with a philosopher, and I'd like it restated in the only technical language I actually command, because right now it lives in my head as pure fog.

The position, as I took it in: some philosophers hold that conscious experiences — the felt redness of red, the ache of a stubbed toe — are produced by brain activity but cause nothing in their turn. The brain states generate both the experiences and everything I say and do; the experiences themselves sit off to the side, causally idle. That already sounded strange, but the part I can't shake is the follow-up. I plainly make judgments about my experiences — I can say the toothache is duller today than yesterday, or that two shades of paint look identical under this light — and on this view those sayings are physical events produced by brain states with no help from the experiences themselves. So my sentence about the toothache has the toothache nowhere in its causal ancestry, yet it tends to be right about the toothache. The presenter called this the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and the hand-wave I remember was that the report and the experience are like two console outputs fed from the same bus: they track each other because they share an upstream source, not because one drives the other. I understood the words, but not the machinery.

So here is the job. Translate epiphenomenalism and this paradox into the vocabulary of a mixing desk, as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in extra assumptions just to make the translation come out: treat the brain and the wider world as the main signal path, the experiences as some kind of send, the verbal reports as outputs, and say precisely what corresponds to what. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which pieces of the position map cleanly onto sends, buses, and returns; which pieces had to be bent or distorted to fit; and which pieces cannot be said in my vocabulary at all — and for that last group, tell me whether the failure is on my side, meaning my desk vocabulary is missing something a more expressive framework would have, or on the philosophers' side, meaning their claim was less clear than it sounded. I have a standing hunch that the whole thing is just a send with no return — a bus routed out to nowhere — and that the so-called paradox is nothing but the surprise of realizing the talker is downstream of the same source as the send. I would like to know whether that hunch of mine is a genuine translation, or a way of not thinking about it.
