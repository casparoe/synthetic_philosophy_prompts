I'm taking a population ethics course this term, and in the tutorial a guy presented an argument for something like negative utilitarianism that ends up somewhere I really don't want to follow him, but I can't find the bad step. It goes like this: (1) suffering is bad and its badness can't be offset by happiness — no amount of bliss cancels out even a single severe torment, which is why it would be obscene to tell someone dying in agony that it's fine, all things considered, because the rest of their life had plenty of pleasure in it; (2) since happiness can't offset anything, the only thing that can counterbalance a suffering is its relief or its ending; (3) so a world containing suffering that will never be relieved is worse, all else equal, than any arrangement in which that suffering isn't occuring — including an arrangement in which no one exists at all; (4) every world with conscious life in it contains some suffering that will in fact never be relieved (people die in pain, animals get eaten alive, etc.); (5) a world with no conscious life contains no suffering; (6) therefore a world without conscious life is better than any world with it, and painlessly preventing conscious life from ever arising — or ending it — would be an improvement. He then tacked on a corollary that bothered me even more: by the same premises, putting every conscious being into a permanent dreamless anesthesia would be exactly as good as annihilation, since neither arrangement contains any suffering and neither one loses anything that happiness could have offset.

Here's my problem. I find both conclusions horrifying, but I genuinely can't see which premise is the rotten one, and worse, I can see how each rejection gets expensive. Denying (1) seems to let people buy torture with pleasure, which sounds monstrous. Denying (4) looks like wishful thinking about how much suffering is in principle relievable. Denying (3) or (6) looks like I'm just refusing an inference because I dislike where it points, which is not a reason. If I had to rank them, I'd most want to keep (1) — it feels like the moral core of the whole view, the thing that makes suffering-focused ethics attractive in the first place — but I suspect that's exactly the one that has to go, and I'd like to know what I'm actually paying if I give it up. So walk me through it: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine when I first read it, and what does rejecting it cost? Then tell me whether there's any principled difference between the annihilation conclusion and the anesthesia corollary, or whether they stand and fall together — and be honest with me if you think the conclusion isn't as absurd as I'm assuming it is.
