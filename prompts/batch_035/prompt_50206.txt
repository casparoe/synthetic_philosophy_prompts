I run a small team at a county council that administers a discretionary grant pot for disabled residents — a few thousand pounds per grant, maybe a dozen requests a month, most turned around in under a week. The requests are all over the place: a stairlift, a powered wheelchair, funding a personal assistant so someone can leave the house, broadband so they can video-call family, and the occasional request for straight cash. Everyone on the team has their own gut rule and they contradict each other. One social worker basically asks "will it make them happy", another will only fund "practical equipment", a third is always trying to stretch the pot over as many recipients as possible. I gather there's a whole debate in political philosophy about equality of what — welfare versus resources versus capabilities — and it feels like our three gut rules are just the three theories in casework clothes. Can you turn that debate into a decision procedure my team can actually follow: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and says no, and where it punts to a panel because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to fit on one side of A4 and be usable by social workers with no philosophy background. Then run it on three real-ish cases: (a) a powered wheelchair for a man with severe MS who says he mainly wants it to get to his garden, (b) a games console plus a year of broadband for an isolated young man whose care review says his main problem is loneliness, and (c) cash instead of any equipment, for a woman who says she knows better than we do what she needs. The console case is the real test — half my team thinks it's obviously no and I'm not so sure. And be upfront about what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire, including which of the three cases it can't really settle.
