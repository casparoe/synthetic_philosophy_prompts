There's something about carbon offsets I keep chewing on and can't resolve, and since you folks have thought way harder about collective action stuff than me, here goes. When you book a flight, the checkout page gives you a checkbox: offset this flight's emissions for $6 or whatever. Same deal at some gas pumps, car rentals, even concert ticket sites. And separately there's the whole footprint-calculator ecosystem ranking personal actions, fly less, eat less beef, etc. But here's what bugs me: the offset money never touches your flight. It goes into a pooled pot funding tree planting or cookstove projects, and whether any project happens depends on whether the pot crosses some funding threshold, probably months later. So your individual $6 has the exact same structure as the standard inefficacy cases — one fewer burger not changing how many animals are raised, one vote not flipping an election. In fact it's basically Parfit's thing about how harms get produced by large sets of acts where no single act makes a difference, just bundled into a retail product. And yet it's marketed to you as a 1:1 transaction: *your* flight, *your* offset, "your flight will be carbon neutral."

So what I want teased out is what the practice actually presupposes. Does selling offsets at the individual level only make sense under something like a fairness or participation view — you extracted the benefit, so you ought to contribute, regardless of whether your contribution tilts any outcome — rather than the outcome-based story the marketing language implicitly tells? Does the "neutralize your flight" framing quietly rule out the very consequentialism it seems to invoke, since a strict consequentialist could shrug and say your $6 has ~zero marginal effect on the pot? Or is the whole thing philosophically underdetermined, with several rival theories of individual responsibility all licensing the same checkbox, so there's no single implicit theory to reconstruct? And is there a tension between the two halves of the practice, the calculator scolding you for personal choices versus the offset absolving you for a price — or do those actually rest on the same underlying view?

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion. And for each point, flag where it's most likely to fail — what argument or consideration would flip you on that point if I pushed back. I'd rather have your honest confidence levels than a confident-sounding essay; I've definately been burned before by tidy answers on collective harms that fell apart under one good counter.
