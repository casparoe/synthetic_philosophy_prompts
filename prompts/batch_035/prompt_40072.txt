Some context first. A couple of years ago I stopped speaking to my father after a long history of stuff I won't go into, and for a long time I simply did what my older sister told me was right — she'd been through the same upbringing and I trusted her judgement completely. Recently I've noticed I keep facing smaller versions of the same decision on my own (whether to reply to his texts, whether to tell him about a family funeral), and I feel unequipped, like I borrowed a verdict but never got the ability that generates verdicts. That's what set me writing this, and it's made me actually read the moral testimony literature for the first time.

Here are the notes. The usual debate is about whether we can get moral knowledge from testimony the way we get empirical knowledge — some say deferring on moral matters is fine, others say it's suspect because testimony can transmit knowledge but not understanding, or because moral judgement is something you're supposed to exercise for yourself. My thought is that both camps treat all cases of moral deference as a single kind, and that's the mistake. The cases where deferring goes wrong, I want to say, share a structural feature: the decision is not one-off but iterated — you will have to keep re-applying it, enforce it at family dinners, explain it to relatives — and deferring outsources precisely the part of the deliberation that would have formed the dispositions you need for the iterations. The sister's verdict covered the big decision but left me with no policy for the small ones. By contrast, moral testimony seems unproblematic in cases where the recipient won't have to keep inhabiting the decision, or where the reasons can be re-derived later if needed. So the problem with moral deference isn't epistemic (missing understanding) but practical: it's a failure to build the standing you'll need. I'm aware there's an obvious objection — we defer to doctors about diagnoses and then have to manage an illness for years, and nobody thinks that's corrupt — and my answer is that with empirical testimony you can go back and re-consult, whereas the moral consultant in intimate cases either won't be there or the situation will be too particular for their earlier verdict to transfer. I'm honestly not sure that answer carries the weight.

What I'd like from you: (1) an honest assessment of whether the "iterated vs one-off" distinction does any real work or collapses back into the understanding-based view once you push on it, (2) whether the doctor analogy reply is as weak as I suspect, and (3) which parts of the notes are the most original vs most likely to have been said before in some form — I'd rather know now than at a reading group. Please be candid rather than encouraging, and where you make claims about how the debate usually goes, say how confident you actually are in them and flag the single point you'd be least sure about.
