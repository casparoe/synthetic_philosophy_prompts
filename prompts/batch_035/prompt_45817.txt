I've spent the past year thinking about pacifism on my own, without any formal background in philosophy, and I've reached the point where I'd rather see my confusion laid out as a set of numbers than left as a mood. The question I keep circling is this: what is the probability that absolute pacifism is true — the view that one may never intentionally kill another human being, even in defense of oneself or others, even to stop an ongoing atrocity? Not whether it's admirable, and not whether most people could live up to it; whether it is correct.

I've tried to break the question into parts, and I would like your own number for each, with a sentence of justification: first, the probability that there is an absolute constraint against intentionally killing an innocent person, one that no consequences can override; second, the probability that combatants and aggressors fall outside the protection of such a constraint, so that killing in defense is in principle permissible even if killing the innocent never is; third, the probability that, even where killing in defense is in principle permissible, the conditions for it are so rarely satisfied in practice that a personal blanket rule against ever fighting is the rational choice.

The third part is where I've done most of my own thinking, and I want to know whether the thought holds up. Here it is. Nearly everyone who has ever fought in or supported a war believed their side met the conditions for justified killing, and most of them were wrong by their own later lights. So even if, say, two percent of wars really are justified, a person who decides in advance never to fight is not betting that those exceptions don't exist — they're betting only that they couldn't reliably recognize one if it arrived, and the historical record seems to make that a fairly safe bet. If self-assessment in wartime is systematically unreliable, a blanket rule can be rational for an individual even when the doctrine behind it is false. As I understand it, this means absolute pacifism as a personal rule can be rational even if absolute pacifism as a claim about what is permissible is false. But my aunt, who holds the doctrine in full, tells me this argument quietly assumes that only consequences matter, since it justifies the rule by its results for the agent's own track record. Has she caught a real flaw, or is she misreading the argument?

For reference, my own overall number for the doctrine being true is about 0.15, while my brother, who accepts defensive wars, would put it closer to 0.05. Please give me your overall number, the decomposition, and the combination; tell me which term you are least confident in and what would move it most; and finally tell me where the disagreement among the three of us most likely lives — in the absolute constraint itself, in the reliability question, or in something I haven't put on the list, such as whether a moral risk argument is even capable of generating a duty rather than merely excusing a personal choice.
