I'm a novelist three chapters into a book whose characters use slurs, and my editor's queries have made me notice that my judgments about what each use does are a mess. My verdicts on my own cases: a character who belongs to the group a slur targets using it affectionately with a friend who also belongs — fine, it barely registers as the same word. The same word in the mouth of a bigoted character, in quoted dialogue — fine, and doing its job. The same word again in narration, in free indirect style, presented unmistakably as that bigot's inner voice — I flinch, though nothing occurs but ink on a page and the thought is attributed to him. A chapter set in a period when the term was a neutral label: I cannot make it read as neutral on the page, whatever the characters would have felt. Translating a milder derogatory term from another language into this one: I strengthen it, and think I'm right to. A critic mentioning the word between scare quotes in a review of an older novel: fine. An audiobook narrator voicing the bigot's lines aloud: I hesitate in a way I never do for the printed page. I would like a principle — or a small set of candidates — stating what a slur's offensiveness is actually a property of: the word itself, the act of using it, or the standing of the one who wields it. Please return your answer as a JSON object with these fields: principle (the statement, as precise as you can make it); clauses (an array of objects, each with the clause itself, the case that motivates it, and the verdicts it saves); overturned_verdicts (an array of my cases the principle cannot accommodate, each with the reason it fails); and predictions (an array of new cases, with the verdict the principle yields for each). If my verdicts are jointly unsatisfiable, I want to know which to abandon and what I pay for abandoning it.
