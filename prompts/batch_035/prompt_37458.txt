Bit of context first: I got into a long argument with a friend (economics PhD, extremely smug about it) about Buridan's ass — the donkey that starves to death between two identical haystacks because it has no reason to go to one rather than the other. His view is that it's a solved problem: "you flip a coin, obviously," and anyone who thinks there's more to say is just confused. I think there's something genuinely strange in the neighborhood, but I can't pin down exactly what, so I'd like an essay that actually takes the puzzle seriously instead of waving it off.

Write an essay on whether Buridan's ass points to a real gap in how we think about rational choice. Some threads I'd love to see woven in (use them or discard them as you see fit, but address at least a couple): (a) the donkey's reasoning is supposed to go "no reason to prefer left, no reason to prefer right, therefore no reason to move at all" — if that's the story, is the error in the premises or in the "therefore"? (b) when a person just grabs one of two identical sandwiches, is that an act of choice, or are they outsourcing it to whatever their hand happened to be near — and does the difference matter? (c) my friend's coin flip: is choosing at random a way of choosing, or an admission that choice has run out? And here's the bit that keeps bugging me: if rationality demanded a reason for every act, wouldn't the coin flip itself be irrational, since no reason favors heads over tails? (d) does the puzzle change when the tie isn't exact — the options are clearly different but neither is better, versus literally identical haystacks? If those are genuinely different situations, the essay should say in what way.

Whatever you do, don't end on a wishy-washy "it depends what we mean by rationality". Take a definite position: either the paradox exposes a real defect in the theory of rational choice (in which case say what should be patched — drop the assumption that rational agents have complete preferences? add tie-breaking conventions, and if so are those norms of rationality or just practical expedients like driving on the right?), or it's basicaly a joke at the expense of a theory of rationality nobody ever held. Defend the verdict, and be upfront about where your position is doing real philosophical work rather than just reporting consensus. Also happy to be told my friend is right and I'm chasing a ghost — but you'd have to earn that conclusion, not just assert it.
