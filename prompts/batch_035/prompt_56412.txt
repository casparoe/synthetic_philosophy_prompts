My friend works in animal welfare certification for egg and chicken brands, and we had a massive arguement last night about what her job actually assumes philosophically. The audits mostly check things like cage bans, access to outdoor range, perches and nesting boxes, no beak trimming — basically whether the birds can perform natural behavior. But when I pointed out that cage-free and free-range systems often have higher mortality, more keel bone fractures, and more cannibalism than well-run furnished cages, she admitted the standards only treat those harms as dealbreakers up to a point — a farm with somewhat worse injury and mortality numbers can still pass as long as the enrichment boxes are ticked. So what theory of animal welfare is this practice actually presupposing? Is it that natural behavior is constitutive of welfare (the bird living according to its nature), with physical harms treated as mere indicators? Or does it presuppose hedonic welfare, in which case the scheme looks incoherent, since it knowingly trades more pain for more naturalness? Or is there a preference-satisfaction reading where the hen's drive to dustbathe and nest counts even at some cost in suffering? I honestly can't tell whether certification strictly commits to one of these or is underdetermined such that several theories could rationalize it — and I'd also like to know what the standards would have to look like if you rebuilt them from a strictly hedonic view. Would we end up licensing furnished cages again, the very thing the welfare movement spent two decades shutting down?
