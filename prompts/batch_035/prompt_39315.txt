I've gotten really into Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, the one built around a tiny 1537 poem Marot left for a sick girl -- 28 lines, three syllables each, rhyming couplets -- and the dozens of English translations it spawned, and I've basically adopted the form-first view: when you translate a poem you should preserve the form as exactly as you can (line count, syllables, rhyme), and semantic loss is just the price of admission. A version that carries all the meaning but none of the music is, on my view, a failed translation of a poem -- maybe a nice gloss, but not a translation. What would actually count against this? I'm not looking for the obvious "some poems are loosely structured" caveat -- I want the cases and considerations that should genuinely move me, including ones where keeping the form arguably betrays the poem rather than serves it (say, when the source culture reads that form very differently than we do, or when the rhymes you're forced into drag in connotations the original never had). Also tell me what the view predicts that rival views about translation don't -- is there any translation outcome where form-first people and meaning-first people would give different verdicts in advance, or does the disagreement only show up after the fact? And an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by moves like "that translator just wasn't skillful enough," and if so whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just how translation talk has to work.
