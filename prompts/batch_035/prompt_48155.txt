(a) The question (worth 30 of the 100 exam points):

"Explain Searle's Chinese Room argument, from his 1980 paper 'Minds, Brains, and Programs.' Your essay must: (i) state the thesis about minds and computers that the argument is directed against; (ii) describe the thought experiment and the conclusion Searle draws from it; (iii) explain the systems reply and the robot reply, together with Searle's answers to each; and (iv) assess whether Searle's answer to the systems reply succeeds."

(b) The student's answer:

"Searle's Chinese Room argument is an attack on the Turing test. Turing proposed in 1950 that if a machine's conversational behavior was indistinguishable from a human's, we should count it as thinking. Searle thinks this can't be right when it comes to actually understanding a language.

He asks us to imagine a man sealed in a room. The man understands no Chinese at all. Through a slot come batches of Chinese symbols, and he has a rulebook, written in English, that tells him, for any batch that comes in, exactly which batch to send back out. The rules are purely formal: he matches up shapes and never knows what any of them mean. If the rulebook is good enough, the people outside can't tell they aren't corresponding with a native speaker. Yet the man understands no Chinese, and, Searle says, he is doing everything a computer running a program does. So running the right program is not enough for understanding: syntax by itself doesn't give you semantics. Since the man does everything a program can do and never understands, no computer, no matter how good its output, could ever genuinely understand anything, and the Turing test is exposed as a test of mimicry rather than understanding.

The systems reply: the real problem is that the room is shut off from the world. Give the room cameras, microphones, maybe wheels, so the symbols coming in are grounded in perception and action instead of arriving through a slot, and the resulting system will genuinely understand. Searle's answer is that this only piles on more uninterpreted symbols for the man to shuffle.

The robot reply: the understanding belongs to the whole system — the man plus the rulebook plus the paper — even though the man alone doesn't understand, rather in the way no single member of a translation team may grasp a whole text but the team still produces a good translation. Searle answers by having the man memorize the rulebook and do all the work in his head: then there is nothing in the room but the man, and he still doesn't understand Chinese, so the systems reply fails.

On (iv): I think Searle's answer succeeds. Given that the only thing in the room that could plausibly understand anything is the man, and he doesn't, it follows that nothing in the room understands — and that settles the systems reply, which would otherwise have to say the understanding exists without being located anywhere in particular, which sounds like a category mistake. Notice also that Searle's own view, that understanding is a biological phenomenon caused by the brain, is basically the same point as the systems reply's claim that understanding emerges at the level of the whole organized system. So the two sides are closer than they look — the real disagreement is just about which whole does the understanding."
