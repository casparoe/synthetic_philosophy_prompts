Is there a name for this? I'm a novelist — domestic realist stuff mostly, four books nobody's aunt has read — and the one I'm writing now has a minor character who's a cable-news foreign policy pundit, so I've been doing research the lazy way: reading op-eds like a scholar and arguing with my brother-in-law until he leaves the table. And I keep hitting the same move, in print and at dinner. Nobody argues about the actual situation. They argue about which earlier situation it secretly is. Back when Syria was hot, it was "another Vietnam" for the people who wanted out and "another Munich" for the people who wanted in, and I've watched the same person extract opposite lessons from the same event: Vietnam taught him you don't fight wars you can't win, and also that we could've won if the politicians hadn't held back — whichever one the current arguement needs. The thing I can't get over is that the analogy seems to do all the work. Once you grant that this is Munich, you've granted everything, because the lesson of Munich is so vivid that disagreeing makes you Chamberlain. Nobody ever shows the analogy is apt, they just get you to nod at it. My brother-in-law does the same thing with China — someone at a party said we're in a "Thucydides trap" and he repeated it to me last week like it settled the question of whether war is likely.

So, does this have an established name? I know Godwin's law, but that's just about Hitler comparisons showing up eventually, it doesn't capture the analogy doing the reasoning. I also know the flip side, the old line about how those who don't learn history are doomed to repeat it — that seems to be the license everyone is operating under, as though history comes with the lessons pre-attached and citing one is as good as demonstrating it. And I don't want just the label (though I'd take it, my pundit character could really use it in dialogue). What I want is the actual carving: what is the move, exactly? Is it just a weak argument from analogy, or is something specific happening when the precedent comes with a one-line moral pre-installed? Is there a defensible version of this — is it how experienced people reason from past cases, only going wrong when the case is cherry-picked — and where's the line between that and what you'd just call judgment? And one more thing I can't put my finger on: it feels unfalsifiable, becuase whatever happens afterwards gets counted as the lesson confirming itself. Lose the war and the analogy was right; win it and the lesson was still right, we just finally listened. Is there a concept for that part specifically, or does the whole cluster of things I'm describing turn out to be one phenomenon with a name I should already know?
