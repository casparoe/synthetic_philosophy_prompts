Next month I'm giving a talk at a longstanding rationalist meetup defending the thesis that Aumann's agreement theorem has been systematically overread by the very community that canonized it: the theorem concerns idealized agents with a common prior whose posterior probabilities are common knowledge, and every real-world application quietly smuggles in either the common prior (which we can never establish between two actual humans) or the common knowledge condition (which, once iterated, leaves no disagreement left to explain — the result explains too much, not too little). The audience will be roughly half longtime LessWrong-style readers who treat disagreement between apparent epistemic peers as prima facie evidence of someone's irrationality and who practice so-called Aumann updates in their forecasting, a couple of Bayesian statisticians who know the formalism cold and will pounce on any sloppiness about priors versus posteriors, and some newer members who only know the theorem as a slogan. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this crowd, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — likely the one about what my own inability to reach agreement with the room that evening says about me, on my own argument.
