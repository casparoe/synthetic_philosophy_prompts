Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk to a philosophy department that is mostly analytic philosophers of science — roughly half Humeans of the best-system stripe, two committed powers/dispositions theorists, a couple of neo-Aristotelians — plus one specialist in post-Avicennan kalām, and no one else with any background in Islamic philosophy. My thesis: al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, properly read, is not the crude view that causation is an illusion to be tolerated, but a serious explanatory program in which God's habit (ʿāda) does the same structural work a Humean assigns to the best systematization of regularities — and that on the specific question of how miracles are epistemically identified, the occasionalist has a genuine advantage, because a criterion built from materials al-Ghazālī himself uses (the prophet's public challenge, the community's demonstrated inability to produce its like, and mass-transmitted attestation) identifies a miracle without ever needing the concept of a law-violation, which is exactly the concept that gives the Humean trouble. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones a generalist audience would — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way its likely asker would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I want the Humeans' "this is just best-system analysis with God bolted on" objection in there, and the powers theorists' complaint that habit without necessity explains nothing about why like produces like. Make sure the kalām specialist's objections appear too and are phrased with their specificity rather than diluted for the room: the worry that I'm flattening a tradition in which later Ash'arites softened the anti-causal line, and the reading controversy over whether al-Ghazālī denies causal necessity itself or only our knowledge of that necessity, which matters for whether my best-system analogy is even faithful to the fire-and-cotton discussion. Since almost nobody in the room will have read the primary texts, replies cannot lean on shared bibliography or on winning textual citations — they have to work on their own terms in analytic vocabulary. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, clearly flagged: if God's habits are themselves contingent, what underwrites expecting them to persist tomorrow — so that either the habit's stability is brute and I've gained nothing over the Humean, or it's necessary and I've quietly abandoned occasionalism for a modal realism about regularities.
