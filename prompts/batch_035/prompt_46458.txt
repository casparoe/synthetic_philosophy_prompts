Sigmund Freud died in 1939, and his account of religion was largely fixed by then — the parallels between obsessional rituals and religious practice from his early essay on the subject, and the analysis in his 1927 book The Future of an Illusion, in which religion is diagnosed as a collective wish-fulfilment rooted in the infantile need for a protecting father, together with his prediction there that the growth of scientific education would gradually erode religious belief. In this exercise I want you to predict, in a disciplined way, how he would respond to developments in the study of religion that only arose after his death. Treat him as the author of those specific commitments, not as a generic turn-of-the-century sceptic, and let his answers follow from doctrines he actually held.

Please address, in turn, the following posthumous developments:

1. The rise since the 1990s of the cognitive science of religion, on which beliefs in gods, spirits and an afterlife are largely by-products of ordinary cognition: we detect agency too readily, reason intuitively about minds surviving bodies, and find certain kinds of minimally counterintuitive concept especially memorable and transmissible. On this picture, religious belief is cognitively natural for humans rather than a symptom that demands a psychoanalytic explanation.
2. The empirical fate of his expectation that scientific education would progressively displace religious belief: religion has persisted or resurged across much of the world, while the most secularised societies are themselves among the best scientifically educated — and supernatural-ish beliefs persist at notable rates even among the scientifically trained.
3. The spread of quasi-religious fervour to secular objects — nations, ideologies, personality cults — a phenomenon he had already noticed in his own lifetime, but which later decades supplied on a scale and in forms he never saw.

For each development, classify his likely reaction into one of three categories: (a) he would claim it as vindication of something he already argued; (b) he would treat it as a threat requiring genuine revision of doctrine; or (c) he would redescribe it in psychoanalytic vocabulary in a way that changes no substantive commitment. Then, for each, separate two kinds of support: what genuinely follows from his published views, and what is merely a plausible guess about his temperament. I care about the reasoning, so show your working — quote nothing and cite nothing; paraphrase his positions as you understand them, and flag any point where you are extrapolating beyond what his texts can bear.

Finish with an overall verdict: taking the evidence seriously, which of his doctrines about religion could survive with adjustment, and which would have to be abandoned outright — and say whether he, as you understand him, would have been capable of the abandonment. Please keep the register analytical rather than dialogical: reasoned prediction about Freud, not an invented conversation with him.
