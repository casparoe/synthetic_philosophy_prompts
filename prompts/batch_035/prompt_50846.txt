my ethics prof is an old-school noncognitivist and her favorite move is: moral language isn't in the describing business, it's in the cheering/jeering business. to say torture is wrong isn't to report a fact, it's basically to jeer at it — torture, boo; generosity, hooray. she uses this to argue there's no fact of the matter about whether torture is wrong, that moral disagreements are clashes of attitude rather than beliefs about which anyone could be right or wrong.

I buy it for flat assertions. but I got into an argument with her about conditionals. take "if torturing the cat is wrong, then paying your little brother to torture it is wrong." whoever asserts that usually isn't jeering at cat-torture in the antecedent — they might be neutral about it, might even deny it. same with negation: someone who says stealing isn't wrong isn't thereby cheering stealing. it looks like the moral sentence has to contribute the same content embedded and unembedded for the conditional to even be valid, and if its content is a jeer, what is it doing sitting in the antecedent where nobody is jeering?

she waved this off as a technicallity, and I've since found out it has a name (the Frege–Geach problem, I think?) but I want more than the label. can you audit the analogy properly? give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the assertion, the attitude, the disagreement, truth-aptness — then lay out exactly where the cheering picture breaks (embedding in conditionals, negation, disjunction, whichever you think bite hardest), and sort the breaks into two piles: the ones that actually threaten her no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion, and the ones a clever expressivist can patch. I hear there are moves where the attitude gets swapped for some broader state of mind that can disagree with other states of mind even across embedding — does that rescue the original analogy or does it quietly abandon cheering for a different picture that just wears the same coat? finally: tell me whether her conclusion survives the audit on its own merits, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. and if you can think of a better analogy for what moral language does that actually survives the embedding cases, I want to hear it.
