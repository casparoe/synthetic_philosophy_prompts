I'm a software engineer who sits on the committee that decides engineering promotions at my company, and a decision from our last cycle is still bothering me. Two colleagues on the same team were both considered. One had led a greenfield project that leadership had already flagged as strategic; it shipped, he presented it at the all-hands, and promoting him was an easy call under our rubric. The other had spent the year maintaining a critical but unglamorous system, absorbed most of the team's on-call load, and quietly unblocked a dozen other engineers. Almost none of that maps onto the rubric's "scope" and "impact" categories, so she wasn't promoted. When she pushed back, our director defended the process: same rubric for everyone, written criteria, no politics, so outcomes track merit as closely as anything could. Her reply has stuck with me. She said the rubric doesn't just measure contribution, it decides what counts as contribution — and that her teammate's supposedly superior "impact" was largely that he happened to be assigned the project leadership already cared about, while she happened to be assigned the work everyone depends on but nobody watches. I don't have a philosophy background, so I may be missing something basic here, but I genuinely can't tell whether her complaint is the modest one that our rubric is badly calibrated and could be fixed by crediting maintenance and support work, or the deeper one that no rubric-based process can deliver what "we promote on merit" promises, because what we're calling merit is manufactured by the very system that claims to detect it. Which is it, and is there a principled way for the committee to respond to her argument rather than just tweaking categories or feeling bad?
