Background: I spent thirty-five years as an industrial polymer chemist (mostly polyolefins, if that means anything to you), and since retiring I've been attending a philosophy of science reading group at the university here in town. We just did the Putnam/Kripke material on natural kind terms and I got somewhat obsessed with the question of whether the sciences really carve nature at it's joints. I put together an argument that I thought settled it, showed it to my friend Ruth who spent her career as an entomologist, and she says it proves far too much. Now I genuinely can't decide which of us is right. The argument: (1) A grouping is a natural kind only if membership is fixed by a single underlying property that all and only its members share, and that explains the other properties we use to recognize them. (2) The chemical elements are like that — nuclear charge fixes membership, all and only, and nuclear charge explains the bonding behavior, the spectral lines, the works. (3) Biological species are not like that: no gene, no trait, no biochemical feature is shared by all and only the members of a species; ring species and hybid zones break the "only" side, and the typical properties drift over the lineage's history so even the "typical" part is unstable. (4) The obvious repairs fail — if you say common descent is the shared property, then an ancestor and all its descendants form one kind, which nobody wants, and if you say gene flow is, the asexual lineages and those same ring species break it. (5) So species aren't natural kinds at all; they're historical things, more like one family tree than like gold. (6) So biology doesn't carve at the joints the way chemistry does, and "tiger" groups things the way "weed" does, by our convenience.

Ruth's counterattack is that if I run my own test honestly it eats the elements too. She points out that hydrogen and deuterium have the same nuclear charge but differ chemically enough that heavy water will kill the cells she used to culture (at high enough concentration, granted), and organic chemists exploit isotope effects all the time — so if "gold" is still one kind when its members differ in behavior at the margins, that's exactly the shape of a species: a cluster held together by something, with exceptions at the edges. And she enjoys pointing out that my own polymers wreck the criterion from the other side — no two samples of polyethylene ever made are microscopically alike, yet we wrote thousands of papers as if polyethylene were a thing. So either the criterion is strict and chemistry fails it too, or it's loose and biology passes it too, and the conclusion I wanted (chemistry carves, biology doesn't) slips away either way.

What I want to know: which premise should I give up, and what do I pay for it? For what it's worth I'd rather keep (2) than (1) — I can't stomach the thought that gold isn't real in the way "weed" isn't — but I also suspect there's a slide somewhere between (1) and (6) that I can't name, something about what "explains" is doing in the two cases. And if the right answer is that Ruth wins outright, tell me what the least painful version of losing looks like — does anything survive of my intuition that chemistry is the paradigm case?
