Sharp Lines, Bad Memory: A Defence of Epistemicism About Vagueness

The sorites paradox feels like a trick played on us by our own vocabulary. One grain is not a heap, and adding a single grain can never make the difference between a heap and a non-heap, yet a hundred thousand grains arranged normally is a heap. Something in that chain of reasoning is wrong, and the question is what. The supervaluationist says our concepts are intrinsically imprecise: there is no fact of the matter about where heaphood begins, only many ways of making the concept precise, and a sentence is true only if it comes out true on all of them. The epistemicist says something much stranger: there is a perfectly determinate fact about which grain-count makes a heap, and we simply cannot know it. In this essay I defend the strange view.

My first point is that arbitrary-but-real boundaries are everywhere and nobody finds them metaphysically scandalous. Time zones have sharp edges; a county line runs through some farmer's field; you become eligible to vote at midnight on your eighteenth birthday, though nothing in you changes. These lines are entirely conventional and yet perfectly real — there is a fact about which side of the line you stand on. Language is a human artefact through and through, so why should a sharp boundary in the extension of "heap" be any more miraculous than the sharp boundary of the county? The incredulous stare that epistemicism attracts seems to me borrowed from a picture of language as something discovered rather than built.

Second, I want to argue that supervaluationism presupposes the very sharp boundaries it denies. Each admissible precisification of "heap" draws an exact line in grain-count space. So lines exist — lots of them. All the supervaluationist has done is swap one sharp line for a sharp set of sharp lines. And the set itself must have a boundary: either a precisification is admissible or it isn't, in which case there is a sharp boundary to admissibility (and the epistemicist's mystery just recurs one level up), or admissibility is itself vague, in which case we need precisifications of admissibility, and the regress never bottoms out. The machinery for avoiding sharp boundaries is constructed out of sharp boundaries.

Third, I offer a diagnosis of why the boundary is unknowable, which I take to be the real explanatory burden on the epistemicist. Vague predicates are lossy compression. If speakers had to store, for every context, the exact cutoff for "tall" in centimetres, the costs of using language would swamp the benefits; a compressed code deliberately discards that information. On this picture the boundary is real — the uncompressed facts settle it — but the linguistic system is engineered so that no participant ever needs to represent it. Our ignorance is not an embarrassment for epistemicism; it is the point of the design. We are like users of a file format who insist there is no such thing as the 1024th byte because the documentation never mentions it.

A final worry, which I have not resolved. The compression story may fit supervaluationism better than epistemicism. A lossy code is naturally modelled as a family of admissible reconstructions, which sounds a lot like precisifications, and it is not obvious that compression yields one privileged cutoff rather than many tolerated ones. If that is right, my third section argues against my second.
