i teach philosophy of mind, and every spring we hit animal consciousness and every spring a student asks me "so which animals are conscious?" and I change the subject, because my honest views on this turn out to be a pile of gut reactions with no visible principle behind them. over the break I finally wrote out my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know whether there's one principle that captures them, or whether I have to start over.

here's the list. chimp: obviously conscious, full stop, there's something it's like to be one. fish: I now think they feel pain — I resisted this for years, was raised on 'fish nociception is just reflex', but the stuff about them trading off pain against other goods, and giving up the guarding of a preferred shelter when you give them morphine, moved me, and I'm not going to demand more of a trout than of a toddler. crabs: here's where it gets embarrassing. the behavioral evidence is basically the same — injured crabs guard a wound, trade off protection against other needs, and yet I can't quite bring myself to say there's something it's like to be a crab. bees: I'm fine saying a bee smells something and likes it, but when a student tells me about the result where bees seem more willing to try things after an unexpected sweet surprise, I say that's just arousal, not anything like optimism — and then can't explain why the same argument doesn't apply to a dog wagging its tail. nematode worms, 302 neurons: clearly nothing it's like. and the case I genuinely go back and forth on: the octopus. I want to say it's conscious, maybe more vividly so than most fish. but the arms do a lot of their own thing — a severed arm still withdraws from stimuli, and an intact one seems to reach and taste semi-independently of the central animal. so do I think the arm itself feels anything, or does only the animal-as-a-whole feel things through the arm? and is that even a question with an answer for a creature whose nervous system is built like that?

my tentative principle is something like 'consciousness needs integrated central control of behavior' — that would explain the nematode, maybe the bee, and the octopus as a whole. but I suspect it costs me either the crab verdict or the fish one, and I don't know which I'd give up. so: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets all or most of these? what does it say about crabs, and about octopus arms specifically? which of my verdicts can't it accommodate, and would you overturn them or complicate the principle? and tell me what it predicts for something I haven't listed — say, a cuttlefish, or a human patient in a persistent vegetative state, or a robot that matches all the behavior. I'd rather hear an honest accounting of what gets sacrificed than a principle contorted to save everything.
