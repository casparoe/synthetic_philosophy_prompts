So here's my situation. I spent about forty years in materials science before I retired, mostly doing failure analysis on alloys and composites, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been working my way through philosophy books like my life depends on it. The Ship of Theseus has completely gotten under my skin, and I think it's because of something from my old working life: we'd routinely have a sample that we re-polished, re-etched, annealed, and re-tested so many times that nothing of the original surface remained, and no one ever agonised over whether it was "the same sample." It was the same sample because we wrote it down as the same sample in the logbook. My hunch is that identity over time for artifacts is like that — a bookkeeping convention we impose, not a fact about the world — but I honestly can't tell whether that's a profound observation or just me smuggling lab habits into metaphysics, and a philosopher friend of mine insists I'm badly wrong, that there's a real fact of the matter about whether the repaired ship is the original one.

So I want a probability estimate, and not a waffly one. Suppose we do the classic thing: a ship has every plank replaced one at a time over several years, keeping the same hull shape, same name, same crew, continuously afloat throughout. Then, separately, someone gathers up all the discarded original planks and reassembles them into a ship as well. Give me your credence that the continuously-repaired ship is the same ship as the original — an actual number — and decompose it properly: roughly how much rides on whether artifact identity over time is a substantive, mind-independent fact at all versus a partly conventional one (my bookkeeping suspicion), how much on which criterion is correct if it is substantive (continuity of form and function, versus original matter, versus some "best candidate" idea where both ships compete), and any other sub-questions you think genuinely belong in the calculation. Then do the same treatment, even if just briefly, for the reassembled-from-original-planks ship. For each term give the number and a line of justification, show how you combine them, and tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, because that's what I want to argue about with my friend.

One more thing to make it harder, beause the pure textbook version feels too easy: imagine a variant where each replacement plank isn't identical to the old one but slightly longer, so the ship grows a few percent each year and after decades it's noticeably a different size and proportion, still one continuous vessel with no sharp jump anywhere. Does your credence in "same ship" drop in that case, and which term in your decomposition is doing the work when it drops? I'd find that really clarifying.
