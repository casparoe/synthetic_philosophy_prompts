I sit on the clinical review panel at my hospital, where we adjudicate disputed comparisons — which drug to keep on formulary, which protocol performed better, who gets credit in our internal quality metrics. We've developed a track record on cases where a stratified analysis reverses the aggregate result, and next month we face a new one that has us split down the middle. I don't need a lecture on the arithmetic of the reversal; we all understand how the weighting produces it. What I need is a recommendation consistent with our own precedents, an identification of the closest analog, and a candid account of what principle we'd be committing ourselves to either way.

Our files first. In Case 1, we had to choose a default between two stone-removal procedures: the newer procedure had the better overall success rate, but within both the small-stone group and the large-stone group the older procedure did better, because the newer one had mostly been tried on small stones. We chose the older procedure, reasoning that stone size is fixed before treatment and the aggregate was just mixing patients of different difficulty. In Case 2, our annual award between two surgeons, the junior surgeon had lower mortality within every pre-operative risk stratum, but the senior surgeon had lower raw mortality overall because he disproportionately took the easy cases. We gave the award to the senior surgeon, on the ground that the question was who had the better year with the case mix he actually had, not who we'd refer a family member to. In Case 3, an observational comparison of a cholesterol drug, the drug looked worse within both the smokers and the non-smokers but better overall, because patients on the drug were disproportionately non-smokers; we discounted the aggregate and judged the drug not shown to help.

The new case: Drug N versus Drug O for treatment-resistant depression, using pooled observational data from our network, with no randomized head-to-head available. Within the patients who stayed on their assigned medication through the six-month endpoint, and within those who dropped out before it, O shows better symptom scores; overall, N shows better scores. The reason is that N is far better tolerated — many more patients assigned O quit within the first month — and, crucially, whether a patient stays on the medication is itself caused by which medication she got. One colleague insists that our Case 1 precedent settles it: when the strata reverse the aggregate, we trust the strata, so we recommend O. Another says the stratification variable is downstream of treatment, which is exactly what made the aggregate untrustworthy in Cases 1 and 3, so the stratified comparison here is the artifact and we should recommend N. A third notes that Case 2 shows we're perfectly willing to go with the aggregate when the question is how things actually turned out rather than what would have happened otherwise. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is genuinely the closest analog, and where does the analogy break? If our track record underdetermines the verdict, name the rules that would each fit every case we've decided so far, say what each recommends here, and describe the further case — realistic enough to land on our docket — that would best force us to choose between them.
