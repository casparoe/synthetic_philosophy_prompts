You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory and the epistemology of markets, and I would like your careful adjudication of an argument that ran for most of last night.

My friend Sam, who follows football far more closely than I do, proposed a £20 bet with me on this weekend's match. He chose the odds himself, he initiated the whole thing, and when I hesitated he pressed me, which unsettled me further. My refusal rested on a feeling I could not properly defend: he is the one at the table who reads everything about these teams, and if he is so keen to offer me those particular odds, the offer itself is telling me something I cannot otherwise see. His reply had two prongs. First, the odds are fair by his own calculations, so the bet is close to a coin flip and nobody is being exploited. Second — and this is the part that really got me — if I genuinely believe his price is wrong, the rational response is to take the other side, not to walk away, so refusing to bet at all is mere timidity dressed up as wisdom. He closed by ridiculing the idea that his eagerness could count as evidence: you cannot extract information from my wanting to bet, he said, since you already know I enjoy betting.

So: was my refusal rational, or had he won the exchange by the end of the evening? Please think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming about what a person's willingness to bet at particular odds reveals about their beliefs, and what two parties' mutual acceptance of the same odds would reveal. Then settle the question, explain under what conditions betting between friends is rational anyway, and give me concrete advice for the next time he tries this. Please write your answer in British English.
