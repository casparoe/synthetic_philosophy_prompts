I'm taking a course on Greek political history, and a running theme is that the three canonical failures of Athenian democracy — the Mytilenean debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates — read almost like a scripted vindication of Plato's ship-of-state analogy from the Republic. You probably remember the picture: the city is a ship on which the sailors brawl over the helm without ever having learned to steer, insisting that navigation can't be taught anyway; they butter up the shipowner with wine and flattery until he hands them the tiller, while the one true pilot, who actually studies the seasons, the winds, and the stars, is dismissed as a useless stargazer. My professor argues that the historical record fills in the analogy almost point by point. In the Mytilenean debate, the assembly votes in fury to execute every adult male on Lesbos and enslave the rest, then reverses itself the next day — a storm the crew nearly sailed into and barely escaped. In the Sicilian debate, Nicias, the cautious general who warns that the assembly is underestimating the scale of everything, gets shouted down and shipped off anyway, and the whole expedition is annihilated. And in 399 the jury, by a small majority, condemns the one Athenian who claimed to know how little he knew — the stargazer, disposed of at last. The professor draws the obvious moral: these episodes show that the analogy isn't just rhetoric; it's a diagnosis, and it points toward rule by navigators.

I want a careful audit of how far this actually holds, because I suspect it's doing more work than it can carry. First, the mapping: who exactly is the true pilot in each episode? Socrates maybe, but Nicias is a strange stargazer — his advice was arguably bad in its own way, and Alcibiades was neither ignorant of Sicily nor without expertise as a commander. Who is the shipowner — the assembly, or the demagogues who court it? What are the winds and stars supposed to be — and is there anything in the Sicilian case that anyone in Athens, expert or not, could have reliably known? Second, the disanalogies: the Mytilene episode involves the assembly reversing itself within a day, which looks less like a drunken crew and more like an error-correcting mechanism, and the second trireme catching the first in time is a fact about institutions that a ship can't replicate at all. The trial of Socrates, meanwhile, happened in calm seas, after a war Athens lost, under legal procedures the analogy says nothing about. So the same analogy is being asked to cover what look like three different kinds of failure — a motivational failure of passion, an epistemic failure about distant facts, and a procedural or legal failure — and I worry it blurs them together. Third, the question the whole thing turns on: the analogy presupposes that navigation exists as a craft in politics, that there are people who can read the stars. Does the record show that, or does it show that the stargazers (Nicias, Socrates) were also wrong, self-defeating, or simply outranked in expertise by people with worse characters?

Give me the correspondence table, then the places it breaks, and be explicit about which disanalogies matter for the professor's conclusion and which are just noise. Then tell me whether the conclusion — that the episodes argue for rule by the navigators rather than the crew — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was resting on the analogy the entire time. And if you think the analogy is salvageable in some restricted form, say what the restricted form is, because I have to write a paper on exactly this and I'd rather not discover after submitting that I was auditing a version of the argument nobody actually holds.
