I'm a novelist, which mostly means I write arguments I can't finish. My current book is about a German engineer, and there's a dinner scene at the center of it that I can't get out of my head because I watched a version of it happen: my cousin, who works at a utility (loathes coal, not a nuclear romantic by any stretch) spent an entire Christmas laying the following on me, and now my protagonist is going to inherit it from her brother, and I need to know whether it actually holds up before I let her respond.

His argument, as clean as I can make it:

1. A kilowatt-hour is a kilowatt-hour. The grid meets demand from whatever mix it has, so if you close plants, the missing generation comes from somewhere.
2. In 2011 Germany shut its eight oldest reactors basically overnight after Fukushima, and renewables were already being built as fast as the country physically could manage — grid connections, permitting, supply chains were the bottleneck, not will. So in the short run the missing terawatt-hours came from coal and gas.
3. The epidemiology people put routine coal air pollution at something like 25 deaths per terawatt-hour in Europe, and nuclear — even averaged over Chernobyl and Fukushima — at a tiny fraction of that. Every coal TWh that exists because a nuclear TWh was shuttered is a statistical killing, done for feelgoods.
4. So the phase-out predictably cost thousands of lives plus a big slug of CO2, in exchange for protection against a risk that was already vanishingly small.
5. Whatever explains the German decision — panic, media, politicians reading polls — the mechanism is general: risks you can picture get regulated, risks you can't picture get ignored, and the unpicturable ones kill far more people. Vaccines, planes, GMOs, waste repositories.
6. Therefore decisions about large-scale risk should be taken out of democratic politics entirely and handed to agencies that count every death the same, regardless of how photogenic it is.
7. And anyone who accepts 1 through 4 but refuses 6 — like me, at the table — is just admitting they'd trade thousands of lives for the feeling of being the sort of person who trusts democracy.

He swears he's not doing a bit. He also concedes, when pressed, that the conclusion applies to basically all vivid-risk regulation everywhere, and he's fine with that, which is exactly where I lose the thread: it feels like he's proven too much, but I can't find the loose screw. My instinct says it's step 2, but honestly the short-run replacement claim sounds right to me. It could also be that something sneaky happens in 5 or 7, because "this mechanism killed people in Germany in 2011" and "this mechanism kills more than it saves everywhere" don't obviously say the same thing, but I can't name the difference and he can smell that I can't. There's also a wrinkle he waves off: the exit was already law before Fukushima (Schroeder's government passed it in 2002, Merkel extended the plant lifetimes in 2010, then reversed course after the accident), so the "overnight panic" framing is itself a narration choice — which, ha, as a novelist I'm professionally obligated to notice.

I have to write her counterargument, and I refuse to let her win just because the plot wants her to. Structure your answer as:

1. Diagnosis in one breath — a few sentences, the one thing she should say at the table.
2. The step that fails — about 200 words. Name the step, and point at the exact sentence where it slips.
3. Why it looked fine — about 150 words on why an honest, numerate engineer would still believe the bad step.
4. What he gets to keep — about 100 words on which parts of his argument survive intact, because I want him to lose the fight without looking like a fool.
5. For the scene — a short paragraph on how the dinner actually ends: who concedes what, and what stays unresolved between them.
