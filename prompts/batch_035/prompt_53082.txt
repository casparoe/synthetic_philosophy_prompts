I'm auditing my old lecture notes on genocide as a legal and moral category, and I've realized they present Raphael Lemkin's conception as if the 1948 Genocide Convention simply codified it, when the relationship between the two is actually where all the interesting philosophy lives. Lemkin's account, developed in his 1944 work on the Nazi occupation of Europe, understood genocide as the coordinated destruction of the foundations of life of a national group — political institutions, culture, language, religion, economic existence, and physical survival together — so that a group could be annihilated as such without every member being killed. The Convention, by contrast, narrows the protected groups to national, ethnical, racial, and religious ones, lists five concrete acts (killing, serious bodily or mental harm, destructive conditions of life, birth-prevention measures, forced transfer of children), and requires a specific intent to destroy the group in whole or in part as such. Here is my question: analyze the drafting of the Convention as a case study in the politics of naming atrocities, run in both directions. What did the legal codification do to Lemkin's broader conception — which parts of it survived, which were deliberately dropped (I'm thinking especially of the omission of political and cultural groups, and of cultural destruction generally), and what pressures explain the losses, from states' worries about their own treatment of political dissidents and minorities to the perceived need for a definition a court could actually apply? And what does the episode show about Lemkin's conception itself — was the legal narrowing a betrayal of the idea, or the price of its existence, or does the very fact that a moral concept had to be deformed to be made legally actionable suggest the moral and legal categories were never tracking the same thing?

I want a careful, disciplined analysis, not a rhetorical one. Please map the two conceptions element by element (protected groups, prohibited acts, the role of intent, physical versus cultural destruction, individual versus group as the victim), so it's clear exactly where the Convention follows Lemkin and where it departs. Be honest about the limits of what is common knowledge here: where you're relying on the general scholarly narrative about the drafting rather than on specific negotiating history, flag that, and where the historical record would be needed to settle a point, say so rather than guessing. I'd also like you to end by assessing whether the famous later controversies — the arguments over whether atrocities that fall outside the Convention's categories are thereby lesser or merely differently named, and the recurring pressure to coin new terms like politicide, ethnocide, and democide — should be read as evidence that Lemkin's original conception was the sounder one, or as evidence that no single concept could carry the weight he wanted it to carry.
