I co-teach a seminar with a colleague from the econ department, and after ten years I've noticed the debate over interpersonal utility comparisons never actually moves, it just gets restated with new formalism. On one side there's the Robbins-style thought: comparing one person's utility with another's isn't measurable or observable, so claims like "a dollar does more good in a poor person's hands" aren't scientific, they're just expressions of sentiment. On the other side are the people who build machinery to license the comparisons anyway — Harsanyi-style aggregation theorems, extended preference accounts, veil-of-ignorance setups where you average over uncertainty about who you'll turn out to be. My economist colleague thinks the ordinalists won in the 30s and everything since is an attempt to smuggle back in what was kicked out; the philosophers I know think the ordinalists won a point about measurement and then illegitimately drew a conclusion about moral and policy reasoning, which is a different thing entirely. And thats where it sits — every year a new paper, nobody budges.

My hunch is this isnt an empirical or even a semantic disagreement at bottom — its two groups with different attitudes about what gets to count as data: positivist scruples about verification on one side, and on the other the plain observation that we make these comparisons constantly in ordinary life (a parent weighing medical decisions for two children, a nurse triaging patients) and nobody thinks that talk is meaningless. But I'm not confident, and I'd like a real diagnosis rather than my grumbling. Is the dispute verbal, methodological, or are the sides answering different questions under one heading — one about measurement, one about what moral reasoning is permitted to assume? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — or is this a case where we should expect permanent stalemate, and why?
