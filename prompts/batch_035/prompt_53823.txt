I write historical fiction, and each novel confronts me with a decision I make perhaps a dozen times per chapter with no method behind it: when the sources disagree, fall silent, or wear their partisanship openly, what may I assert on the page as fact, what must I hedge as a narrator's guess, and what am I free to invent outright? My training is in the craft of prose, not in history, and my constraints are fixed: I work from translations and whatever secondary literature I can obtain, I have no archival access, and a consulting historian reads my manuscript exactly once, after the draft is done — by which point the wrong choices are expensive to unmake. What I need is a decision procedure I can run myself, in a few minutes per instance, before I commit a sentence to the page.

Please build it out of the actual tradition of source criticism, but compressed for a practitioner. The distinctions I suspect matter most, and which I'd like you to keep or discard as the design requires: the difference between a source that errs at random and one that errs in a direction, since the second can be corrected for and the first only averaged; the discipline of not treating a source's silence as knowledge that a thing did not happen, which means asking what would have had to be true for the writer to mention it; the gap between a source that was present and one that merely heard from someone; and the gap between what a source says happened and what it says the happening meant, which is where partisanship usually hides. The procedure should preserve what these distinctions actually claim while making them usable by someone without the theory, and it should say plainly which of them you decided to drop in the simplification.

Then run it on three cases I actually face in my current manuscript: a scene where the only narrative source is a chronicle written to flatter the ruler it describes; an episode where two otherwise credible sources flatly contradict each other about who was in the room; and a matter where the literary sources say one thing and the material evidence — coins, in this instance — says another. I want at least one of these to come out in a way I will not like, and I want you to tell me which one that is rather than softening the verdict to spare me.

Finally, mark for me the point at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision back: I assume there are moments where the question stops being what the evidence supports and becomes what a novelist may do with a gap the evidence cannot fill, and I would rather have that boundary drawn honestly than have the procedure pretend to settle questions of license in the dress of questions of fact. And since I will be trusting this procedure for years: tell me where you think it is most likely to misfire, and what would change your mind about it — which argument or consideration would lead you to redesign the steps or return different verdicts on the three cases.
