I'm a graduate student straddling philosophy and behavioral science, and a seminar discussion that has run for most of a term has now completely stalled, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of the arguments themselves. The dispute is about whether the behavioral-economics treatment of akrasia explains the phenomenon or explains it away. On one side are the philosophers, who take the classic puzzle — running from Socrates' claim in the Protagoras that akrasia is impossible, through Aristotle's picture of the person who knows the better course but is defeated by appetite, to Davidson's question of how an intentional action can go against the agent's own better judgment — to be about a genuine evaluative failure: the agent judges, at the moment of action, that one course is better and yet chooses the other. On the other side are the economists and the Ainslie-style picoeconomists, who model weakness of will as ordinary preference reversal under hyperbolic discounting: from a distance the larger-later reward dominates, as the smaller-sooner option approaches it comes to dominate instead, and there is no mysterious failure of evaluative rationality at all — just an agent with consistent, if non-exponential, discounting, whose preferences happen to be indexed to when the choice is made. The economists' view is that this dissolves the ancient puzzle; the philosophers' rejoinder, in the spirit of Richard Holton's work on intention, is that discounting captures, at best, a shift in what the agent wants, whereas the akratic agent is supposed to still endorse the judgment she betrays — she sincerely reports that the larger-later option is better even while reaching for the immediate one, and no curve through her revealed preferences obviously captures that.

The stall has a fixed shape, and I've now seen it three times. The philosophers grant that hyperbolic discounting is a real mechanism and cheerfully accept it as the causal story, while insisting that it leaves the interesting question untouched: the normative question of what makes akratic action criticizable, given that the agent's contemporaneous preference favored it. The economists regard this as a refusal to accept the explanation: if your preferences flip and you act on your current preference, there is nothing left to explain, and the philosophers are clinging to a folk-psychological notion of 'better judgment' that revealed-preference methods cannot find. Neither side budges, and each accuses the other of changing the subject. My working hypothesis is that the whole thing is verbal in a specific way — that 'weakness of will' is being used by one camp to name a choice-pattern (reversals over time, overcome by precommitment à la Ulysses and the Sirens) and by the other to name a structural relation between judgment, intention, and action at a single moment — so that each side is answering a different question under one label. But I can also see a competing diagnosis: that there is a hidden substantive question doing the work, namely whether evaluative judgments just are context-indexed preferences, in which case the discounting model either vindicates the Socratic denial of akrasia or forces us to admit the akratic agent has no judgment to betray.

Please tell me which diagnosis you actually think is right, using what each side concretely says rather than a survey of the literature. If it's verbal, say precisely where the equivocation sits — is 'better judgment' the culprit, or 'acting against'? If it's substantive, say whether it's empirical (something about how valuation and judgment are actually realized, something experiments on delay discounting or precommitment could in principle settle) or conceptual (something about whether a preference ordering can carry the normative force a judgment is supposed to have). And in either case, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that splits the phenomenon into two, a specific kind of experimental result, a formal result about what discounting models can and cannot represent, or a reason to think the impasse is permanent because one side is in error about its own question. I'd rather hear that the seminar should dissolve into two separate seminars than sit through another semester of talking past each other.
