I serve on the board of my neighborhood association in a 1920s streetcar suburb, mostly duplexes and fourplexes that were legal when they were built but were downzoned to single-family in the 1970s, so nearly every existing owner lives in what the code now calls a legal nonconforming use. Next month the board must take a position on a proposal to replace a shuttered nursing home at the end of our street with a five-story, seventy-unit apartment building, a quarter of the units rent-restricted, made possible by a recent state law that overrides the local zoning cap. I have to decide how to vote, and I keep going back and forth, so I would like your help thinking it through.

The opposition is led by a neighbor who, before he retired, helped write the 1970s downzoning. His argument is not about architecture. He says that he and everyone else bought their homes under a set of rules, that a home purchase is a bet on the surrounding rules staying put, and that changing the rules after people have staked their savings on them is a kind of confiscation, even if the change is lawful. He also notes that he has never objected to the fourplexes themselves — the rules in force when they went up allowed them.

But he is being attacked from another side. Several of our most active board members are renters, one of whom has organized tenants in the neighborhood for years. Their view is that the meeting's assumption that the people in the room are the neighborhood is itself the problem: the seventy households who would live in that building have no voice at the meeting, and neither do the families priced out of the neighborhood over the past decade, and if we get to vote on whether they exist, then the vote itself is doing the harm.

And there is a complication of my own. The developer, knowing the board's position carries weight at the hearing, has offered to sign a community benefits agreement — a small pocket park, local hiring preferences, and a funded repair program for the street's sidewalks — if the association supports rather than merely abstains. Two of my friends on the board think this is a bribe dressed up; two think it is the only concrete good anyone will extract from this process, and that turning it down is moral preening at other people's expense.

Three questions, then. First, does the argument that property was bought under the rules create any real moral claim against changing them — especially when the rules being changed were themselves rewritten fifty years ago, in a way that quietly transferred rights from future builders and renters to existing owners? Second, whose interests should the board actually be weighing — its dues-paying members, the people who live here now regardless of tenure, or the people who would live here if the building existed — and is there any principled way to decide who counts, or only a fight about it? Third, does taking the benefits agreement corrupt our vote, or is it simply the neighborhood negotiating, through the only channel it has, for a share of the value the new rules create?

Please begin with your bottom line — how I should vote and why, in the first sentence — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my questions.
