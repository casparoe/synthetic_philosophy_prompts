Working on my KK chapter and I've got an argument that feels like it proves too much. (1) Knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning: treat p as a reason for action only if you know p. (2) A rational agent can act on a norm while knowing she's conforming to it — when I hand over the cash because I know I owe you, I don't merely conform, I can tell that I'm conforming. (3) If I know p, then by (1) I may treat p as a reason; by (2), in so treating it I know I'm conforming to the norm; and knowing I'm conforming to a norm that demands knowledge of p requires knowing that I know p. So KK falls out of two norm platitudes, no reflection or ascent needed. But here's the too-much part: run the same reasoning on "I know that I know p" and you get knowledge at every level of the iteration, all for free, from a single act of knowing. I'm a bounded agent who struggles to remember why i walked into a room. And if I swap in "belief aims at knowledge" for (1), I seem to get that I always know what I believe, which is just luminosity of belief with extra steps — the exact thing the margin-for-error machinery was supposed to kill. So which step do I reject? I'd like to keep (1), it's doing real work elsewhere in the diss. Tell me what rejecting (2) costs, whether there's a principled way to stop the ascent that doesn't just cripple the original argument for KK, and whether the luminosity spinoff is a genuine consequence or me sliding between norms.
