I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and our reading group has spent this term on Douglas Hofstadter's work on fluid concepts — the Copycat letter-string microdomain, conceptual slippage, and the architecture built around it. A recurring move, both in the discussion and in the texts themselves, is the slogan that analogy is the core of cognition — sometimes put more strongly, as analogy-making being what cognition basically is. Most recently the slogan was used to wave away a question I raised about external validity: I asked whether the slippages Copycat discovers in a world of letter strings — successorship, sameness, right-ness, group-ness — tell us anything about how people bend real, bulky concepts like money, marriage, or species under pressure, and I was told, in effect, that the question dissolves because it is analogy all the way down: the microdomain just is cognition in miniature.

I would like to be able to respond to that move precisely rather than simply bristle at it. Could you lay out, as an ordered list I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making? Please order the readings from least to most demanding, and for each one say: what exactly it asserts, whether it is plausible, what would count as evidence for or against it, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it actually supports the dismissal of my external-validity question, or whether it instead makes that question more pressing. To give you a sense of the range I have in mind: there seems to be a reading on which the slogan is close to a truism about frequency (a great deal of human thought proceeds by comparison); a developmental reading (concepts are first acquired, and later restructured, through analogies); a computational reading on which a single kind of mechanism — pressure-mediated slippage over a self-modifying representational network — underlies perception, categorization, and reasoning alike, so that modeling it in any domain, however small, is modeling cognition itself; and a methodological reading about where research effort is best spent, e.g., on carefully designed microdomains rather than on systems loaded with encyclopedic knowledge.

For each reading, please also note whether it is hostage to facts about Copycat in particular — for instance, that the concepts in its network were hand-built for one microdomain, that its pressures were tuned by its designers rather than learned, and that its notion of temperature modulating randomness was engineered rather than emergent. If any of the readings is false as stated, or unfalsifiable, say so plainly rather than giving it a polite pass. And if none of the defensible readings supports the dismissal, please spell out what the most charitable version of my interlocutor's point would be, so that I can engage with the strongest form of it rather than with the slogan.
