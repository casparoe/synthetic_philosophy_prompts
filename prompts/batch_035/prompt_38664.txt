Something I can't get straight: it seems like modern states run several population policies at once that quietly point in different directions, and I want to know what they jointly presuppose about the value of people existing. Here's the package:

1. Lots of governments — think France's long pro-natalist tradition, Hungary's family policy, South Korea's response to its fertility collapse — spend serious money trying to raise the birthrate: baby bonuses, subsidized childcare, long parental leave, housing perks for families.

2. The very same kinds of states, often literally the same ones, sharply restrict immigration — even though immigrants are grown-up workers who could fill exactly the demographic hole the birthrate subsidies are aimed at, no eighteen-year wait and no bonus required. A baby bonus and a border fence, paid out of the same treasury.

3. Meanwhile, in a different department of the same state, population growth tends to show up as a cost: traffic models, water planning, and climate accounting all treat extra people as a burden, and shrinking towns are sometimes quietly relieved.

4. And the same officials who fund new babies will often say, when pressed, that the point is really to support parents — helping families that already exist — not to create people per se.
