I have followed the population ethics debates on LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the effective altruism Forum since the late 2000s — long enough to watch the repugnant conclusion evolve from a seminar-room curiosity into a load-bearing premise in arguments about the long-term future. I now want a serious essay on a question I have never properly forced myself to answer: does the repugnant conclusion refute totalism?

The essay should weigh, in particular:

1. Parfit's own observation that a life barely worth living need not be barely tolerable — the inhabitants of his Z-world can have friendship, love, and achievement, just at a much lower level than the ten billion inhabitants of the A-world. Does the conclusion still repel once the comparison is described that way, rather than as teeming masses of drab lives?

2. The price of escape. Averagism implies that one ecstatic hermit outperforms 7.7 billion moderately flourishing people. Views that forbid trading quality of life for quantity at any exchange rate can be driven to the so-called sadistic conclusion — that it can be better to add a smaller number of lives not worth living than a larger number of lives barely worth living — or to the very repugnant conclusion, in which a vast population barely worth living, alongside a significant number of terribly suffering lives, ranks above a smaller population of excellent lives. Arrhenius's impossibility theorems suggest that some such cost is unavoidable.

3. A hybrid of my own, which I would like assessed rather than flattered: total welfare is what matters, but the marginal value of an additional life declines with population size — the hundredth person added to the world counts far more than the hundred-trillionth. Does this actually block the slide from A to Z, or does the marginal value merely get smeared so thin that trillions of barely positive lives eventually win anyway? And what does it cost — does the goodness of adding a child come to depend on how many strangers exist elsewhere, and is that violation of separability a price worth paying?

Reach a verdict — bite the bullet or reject totalism — and do not end on a non-committal note. Throughout, please distinguish what is settled (the formal results: which axioms jointly entail which conclusions) from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature (for example, whether the repugnant conclusion is acceptable at all — Tännsjö and Huemer accept it, while many others do not), and do not present a contested judgment as the consensus.
