hey, so I'm a novelist and I've been writing in first person for like fifteen years, and at some point I noticed I don't trust my own mind any more than I trust my narrators, which is to say not at all. I've got notes for a little essay about it and I want someone to tell me if the central argument is actually any good or just cute. here are the notes:

1. when I write "she felt a flicker of contempt," I'm doing exactly what my narrator does — reporting an inner state on the basis of pretty thin evidence. the sentence feels true as I type it, same as when I tell a friend why I was short with the waiter.

2. the bit I actually care about: fiction might be the only place where introspection can be checked against ground truth. in a novel there IS a fact of the matter about why the door got slammed — because I, the author, decided it. if my character says "I slammed it because I was angry," but I know I wrote that scene because she's nursing a grievance from three chapters ago that she won't admit to herself, then her introspective report is confabulation that I designed, and I know it's confabulation because I wrote both layers.

3. so fiction is a controlled experiment: we can make introspective reports that are demonstrably false while feeling right, and watch exactly what it takes for them to go wrong (a motive the character can't own, a label borrowed from her culture, etc.). maybe this tells us something about how loose the fit between feeling and report is in real people too.

4. my worry: the comparison is rigged. my god's-eye facts are reasons I invented, and a real mind doesn't come with an author standing behind it. is that a fatal disanalogy or just a difference in how the facts are grounded?

can you tell me whether the argument survives point 4, and whether there's enough here for an essay a philosophy-literate reader wouldn't find embarrassing? be blunt, I can take it. keep the whole reply under 350 words.
