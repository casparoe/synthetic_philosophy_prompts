For roughly two years I've been chewing on a problem in the philosophy of mind that I've never seen anyone state quite this way, and I'd like you to argue a specific position about it. My own reading has convinced me that the appeal of functionalism is that a mental state is defined by what it does — by its causal relations to sensory input, other internal states, and behavior — rather than by what it is made of. That is what allows the same pain to be realized in a human brain, an octopus's nervous system, and perhaps someday a machine. But here is what bothers me: every time a new kind of realizer is admitted, the list of properties shared by all realizers shrinks. A human brain, an octopus brain, and a hypothetical silicon system have less and less in common, so what remains common to the entire class — the thing functionalism says the mental state *is* — gets thinner and thinner. In the limit, the common part may approach something so abstract that it carries almost no causal information, while the heavy lifting is always done by whichever particular realizer happens to be running on that occasion. I've come to call this "causal dilution": the mental state seems to borrow its causal powers from its hardware, and the more kinds of hardware can run it, the less it seems to have going for itself. The specific position I want you to argue is that this dilution, far from refuting functionalism, is exactly what we should expect once we stop treating a mental state as a single underlying property and start treating it as a repeatable role whose causal contribution is the same in every case: a structured sensitivity linking situation, internal adjustment, and action, whose specific manner varies from realizer to realizer. What is shared across realizers is a pattern of organization, not a bundle of powers. My own image is that a mental state relates to its realizers the way a melody relates to performances of it — nothing material in common between one performance and the next, yet recognizably the same thing going on. If that analogy embarrasses you, drop it and argue the point straight.

Please structure your answer in the following order, with these sections and roughly these lengths: (1) "The thesis in one paragraph," about 100 words, stating the position flatly and without the analogy; (2) "The dilution problem at full strength," about 200 words, giving the worry its best run, including the thought that the disjunction of all realizers may not amount to a genuine property at all, since the members need share nothing more than a job description; (3) "Why organization, not powers, is what is shared," the heart of the argument, about 350 words; (4) "What must be conceded," about 150 words, stating honestly where the position is weakest — in particular whether it can answer someone who insists that a pattern of organization with no powers of its own is a way of *describing* a cause rather than being one; and (5) "Where this leaves functionalism," about 100 words. Argue, don't survey: take the position and defend it, and if you think it is indefensible somewhere, say so in section 4 rather than quietly softening the thesis earlier on.
