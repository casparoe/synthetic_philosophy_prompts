I convene a decision-theory reading group at my university — twelve postgraduates in philosophy and economics, meeting for a single ninety-minute session. We have just finished a slow pass through Newcomb's problem and the one-boxing debate, and for the following session I want to stage something rather than discuss it: a game in the lineage of Hofstadter's superrationality experiments from his 1980s Scientific American columns — the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, in which a prize is structured so that individually rational pursuit of it drains the very thing being pursued.

What I want is an in-class lottery with a specific strategic property, and I need you to work out the exact payoff formula that delivers it. Each participant secretly submits a nonnegative integer, the number of entries they buy into the draw; one entry is then drawn uniformly at random from the pool and its owner takes the prize; but the prize shrinks as the total pool grows, in such a way that (a) whatever the others do, each player's expected value is maximised by submitting roughly the total everyone else submits — so that dominance reasoning pulls everyone's numbers upwards — while (b) if everyone submits a single entry, everyone's expected value is far higher than in any outcome that arms race can reach. Give me the formula, a worked numerical example the group can verify on a whiteboard, and a defence of the parameters — group size, stakes paid in bookshop vouchers rather than cash, real enough to sting but small enough to clear with the department.

Structure the session in stages, with no lecture beforehand and no mention of superrationality, Hofstadter, or the historical lotteries until the final debrief. Stage one: the cold round, submitted in silence. Stage two: reveal of the aggregate — not who submitted what — followed by a timed period of open, unenforceable discussion, and then a second secret submission. Stage three: something that gives the symmetry premise a grip in the rules themselves rather than in the facilitator's patter — perhaps a round in which the draw pays out only if the room's total matches a sealed total the same twelve people recorded in a written-reasoning exercise a week earlier, or some cleaner mechanism of your own devising that makes each participant's best evidence about the others' choices run through their own choice. Stage four: a vote on a binding rule change for a final round — say, capping entries — with the question I want the debrief to isolate being whether each member's reason for voting as they did was fear of defection or the thought that their reason was, in the relevant sense, everyone's reason.

Then the debrief: a script mapping specific moments in what actually happened onto the formal distinctions — dominance reasoning, Nash equilibrium versus Pareto-inferior outcomes, common knowledge of rationality as it operates and fails to operate in a room of friends, and the stipulated symmetry that stage three merely pretends to. The group should leave having felt, in their own submitted numbers, both the pull of the arms race and the different, vertiginous character of reasoning that treats one's own choice as evidence about identical reasoners. Include honest notes on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — someone submitting an absurdly large number, abstentions breaking the payoff maths, the discussion round collapsing into open collusion — with handling instructions for each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: a reading group is not a population of symmetric strangers, and no classroom mechanism can make the correlation that superrational reasoning presupposes anything more than stipulated. Say clearly what the facilitator must supply by fiat and what the participants can genuinely discover for themselves.
