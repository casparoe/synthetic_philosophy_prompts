Write a dialogue between a tranquilist and a classical hedonist. The tranquilist holds that only the absence of craving and suffering has final value: pleasant excitement, thrills, the "high" of getting what you want are at best instrumentally useful, and at worst they just set up the next craving. The hedonist holds that positive experiences matter in their own right, not merely as the switching-off of bad ones.

Give them a concrete case to fight over: a pain clinic that can allocate one remaining course of treatment to one of two patients. Patient A would come out with no suffering at all, but also nothing you'd call joy — a flat, mild contentment, no projects that excite her. Patient B would get back ordinary ups and downs: real delight sometimes, real grief sometimes, plus a lingering background ache the treatment can't touch. The tranquilist says A's outcome is at least as good; the hedonist thinks that's crazy.

Both should be sharp and actually respond to each other. In particular let the hedonist press on whether the felt relief that follows suffering isn't itself a positive experience (in which case absence of suffering and presence of pleasure come apart less than the tranquilist wants), and let the tranquilist press on whether the hedonist's attachment to highs is itself just one more craving that keeps the whole treadmill running — so that a life organized around maximizing highs is a life organized around manufacturing appetite. No cop-out ending where they just agree to disagree; but each should land one genuine hit on the other. Don't have either one be a pushover.
