I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'd like an essay written for someone in roughly my position: conversant with the concepts but not with the literature.

Here's the situation that prompted my question. I maintain, as a volunteer, a mid-sized open-source library that a lot of infrastructure quietly depends on. Its original author — call him D., since the details don't matter — was by far the most talented contributor the project ever had, and a few years ago it came out that in his private life he had done things most of us would call seriously wrong, the kind that ended his career and his standing in the community. The codebase is still there. It's genuinely elegant: the parts he wrote are the ones that have needed the least maintenance in a decade. Every time I work in those parts I notice it. We've had recurring, never-quite-settled arguments in the project about what to do — whether to fork and rewrite his modules on principle, whether to keep his name in the history and documentation, whether it's strange that we describe his design decisions with admiration in our contributor guide.

When I've tried to articulate what bothers me about these arguments, I realize the group keeps talking past each other because we're assuming different answers to an older question, the one about art: whether a work made by a bad person can be a good work, or whether its maker's immorality somehow gets into the work and counts against its value. My instinct as an engineer is to say the code's quality is a fact about the code — the design either scales or it doesn't — but I've also noticed that I no longer recommend one of his other projects to people, which suggests my "the artifact is independent of the author" view isn't as stable as I thought, since I don't actually believe the other project got worse.

Write an essay that works through this. Specifically:

1. Lay out the strongest versions of the positions people in my situation keep collapsing into each other: that the artifact's merits are entirely independent of the maker's character; that the maker's immorality genuinely diminishes the artifact's value; and any middle positions you find worth taking seriously (for instance, ones that distinguish our assessment of the artifact from our practices around it — what we celebrate, preserve, or recommend).

2. Address the analogy I keep drawing, between code and art, and tell me where it holds and where it breaks. My code is not just contemplated; it's executed, depended on, and extended by people who must understand its author's design intentions to work in it. Whether that makes the moral question sharper or just different, I'd like to know.

3. Reach a verdict. Not a compromise that restates the dilemma — an actual position on whether D.'s modules are good code, and on what our project should do about the history and the contributor guide. I'm the one who has to close these recurring arguments, so give me something I can act on. You're welcome to make the verdict conditional on facts (e.g., if the wrongdoing relates to the project's domain versus if it doesn't), but the conditions should be spelled out.

One constraint on how you write it: I want to be able to tell what's settled and what isn't. Where something you say reflects genuine disagreement among philosophers or critics who work on this, say so plainly rather than presenting it as the consensus view; where something is near-uncontroversial, I'd like to know that too, so I don't hedge unnecessarily in front of my co-maintainers. I don't need citations or a reading list — I just need the reasoning to be honest about which parts of it a knowledgeable person would contest.
