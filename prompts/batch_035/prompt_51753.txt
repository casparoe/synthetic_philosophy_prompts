I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar this semester on public memory and the political philosophy of commemoration, and I'm writing my term paper on the track record of my hometown's Commemorative Works Commission, the body that decides what happens to contested monuments, building names, and the like. I've been going through its decisions from the past two decades, and I've noticed something I want help thinking through: the outcomes all feel consistent with each other, but when I try to state the principle behind them, more than one principle seems to fit. The record, as best I can reconstruct it: the commission removed a 1924 bronze of a Confederate officer after historians established that the dedication ceremony explicitly celebrated the cause he fought for; it retained but recontextualized with a plaque a street named for an early mayor who drafted the city charter but also held people enslaved; it removed the name of a physician from a middle school that had been named in the late 1950s specifically to honor his direction of the state's eugenic sterilization program; it retained, again with a plaque, a fountain honoring a regional novelist for her novels after her private letters revealed deep antisemitism; and it renamed a library branch honoring the benefactress whose endowment built the reading rooms, after archival work showed she had insisted that Black patrons be barred from those very rooms.

The new case coming before the commission, which I'm using as the centerpiece of my paper, is a bronze in the lobby of our science museum honoring a mid-century biochemist for the nutrition trials that led to the elimination of a regional deficiency disease. Recent historical scholarship has established that those trials were conducted on institutionalized children, without anything resembling consent, and that this coercive design was not incidental to the work but constitutive of it. The plaque, notably, doesn't just honor the man or the public-health outcome; it celebrates the trials themselves as his crowning achievement. I've argued in my draft that this case is where the candidate principles behind the commission's record come apart, but I want a second opinion on whether I've got the candidate principles right and on what each of them predicts here.

Please act as an expert in applied ethics and political philosophy who has advised cities on commemoration controversies, and think carefully before answering; I'd actually like you to start with a short brainstorm of every plausible rule that could rationalize the five earlier decisions, then impose some discipline. Specifically: lay out the strongest two or three candidate principles that jointly fit the track record, say what each one predicts for the biochemist statue, and tell me which verdict you'd bet on and why, including which earlier decision is the closest analog. Then do two more things: tell me what principle the commission would be adopting with each possible outcome, since either way it's committing itself to something; and construct the further hypothetical case — one I could add to my paper as a thought experiment — that would best discriminate between the candidate principles, meaning a case where they give cleanly different verdicts. I care less about the final answer than about seeing the logical geography clearly, since that's what my paper is supposed to demonstrate.
