I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and this semester I'm running a 75-minute discussion section (about 18 students, mixed philosophy and CS backgrounds) attached to a course on formal systems and the philosophy of mind. The unit is on Hofstadter's notion of "jumping out of the system" (JOOTSing), using the MIU system as the anchor. I want the students to feel the difference between grinding away inside a formal system and stepping outside it — not to be told about it.

In case it's useful context: the MIU system has four rules, applied to strings built from the letters M, I, and U, starting from the axiom MI. Rule 1: if a string ends in I, you can append U (xI → xIU). Rule 2: if you have Mx, you can form Mxx (doubling everything after the M). Rule 3: if III occurs anywhere in a string, you can replace it with U. Rule 4: if UU occurs anywhere, you can delete it. The famous question is whether MU is derivable from MI.

I've sketched the skeleton of a game but want a full, runnable design. Teams earn points for each distinct string they derive, with a large bonus reserved for deriving MU. I've also been toying with a mechanic I'd like you to develop: every fifteen minutes or so, teams may spend accumulated derivation points to propose one new rule, which I will accept only if it is statable in the same string-rewriting format. My hunch is that this lets teams try to "jump out" by changing the system, and lets the room discover which extensions help, which don't, and why the decisive insight (something about counting letters) lives at a different level than any of the rewriting rules. But I may be wrong about how the mechanic plays out, so feel free to modify or replace it, and tell me why.

Please provide: complete rules and materials; a payoff scheme requiring no real money; role assignments (including whether to have one or more "meta-level" observers and what they're told to do); a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the object-level/meta-level distinction and onto what JOOTSing is supposed to be. Also include a candid section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (spoilage by students who know Hofstadter, the invariant being noticed in the first five minutes, rule-confusion, teams gaming the point system) and how to handle each, plus one honest paragraph on what feature of JOOTSing no classroom activity can reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.

End your response with a final line of the form "Verdict: X", where X is either "inside" or "outside", giving your considered judgment on whether the move that settles the MU question is available to anything that operates strictly within the system — including any of the rule extensions the game permits — or only to something standing outside it.
