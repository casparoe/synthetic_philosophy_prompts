I'd like you to adjudicate a dispute between two friends of mine, Katya and Marcus, over a puzzle from the philosophy of probability. They spent an entire dinner arguing and asked me to referee; I honestly couldn't, so I'm passing it along.

The setup (a version of the so-called Judy Benjamin problem): a soldier, Judy, is dropped at night into terrain she knows only from a map. The map is divided into two equal halves, Red territory and Blue territory, and the part of Blue territory nearest the front line is occupied by Red forces. Her prior is uniform: credence 1/2 that she's in Red territory, 1/2 that she's in Blue territory; and conditional on being in Blue territory, 1/2 each that she's in the Red-occupied or the Blue-occupied zone. Headquarters radios exactly one message: if you are in Blue territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that you are in the Red-occupied zone. Nothing else. Both friends agree on one thing: her credence, given that she's in Blue territory, that she's in the Red-occupied zone should move from 1/2 to 3/4. The fight is over her credence that she is in Red territory proper.

(1) Katya argued that a conditional carries no information about its own antecedent. The message is entirely about what to think *if* she's in Blue territory, and it can be fully accommodated by adjusting her opinions within Blue territory: her new credences become 3/8 that she's in the Red-occupied zone of Blue territory, 1/8 that she's in the Blue-occupied zone, and exactly 1/2 that she's in Red territory. She did the arithmetic on a napkin and said the case was closed.

(2) Marcus said the general principle is false. If a pundit declares that if a certain party wins the election he will eat his copy of the manifesto on live television, learning this should *lower* your credence that the party wins — so conditionals can clearly be evidence about their antecedents. And in Judy's case, he insisted, the message should raise her credence in Red territory: headquarters volunteered a detailed report about the Red-occupied share of Blue territory, which would be a strange thing to radio unless Blue territory were a live and worrying possibility. He added an extremity test: if headquarters had said the odds were a million to one, Katya's rule would still leave Judy frozen at exactly 1/2 on Red territory, which he found absurd.

(3) Katya replied that the pundit case works through assumptions about the *speaker* — what he wants, what he knows, why he chose to speak — and not through the conditional's content. And if Judy is allowed the same assumptions about headquarters, they cut in unknown directions: the radio operator might be briefing her on Blue territory precisely because she's probably in Red territory and he's covering the alternative. The content of the message, on its own, underdetermines any change to the antecedent's probability, so the only principled update is the minimal one: change exactly what the message speaks to and nothing else.

(4) Marcus countered that "minimal" is doing sneaky work, since minimal by what measure? On his telling, the natural way to make the message true is that Red forces have pushed into Blue territory; in the nearest picture of the world in which the conditional holds, the front line has moved, so Judy's prior — calibrated to the old front line — is stale, and her credence in Red territory proper should creep up too. Katya answered that this is an invented backstory rather than evidence, and that if backstories count, rival backstories cut every which way, so updating should be a function of the message actually received.

They deadlocked on two questions: whether the message should move Judy's credence in Red territory at all, and whether Katya's stubborn 1/2 is defensible or a technicality.

Please write your adjudication in American English, with these sections in this order:

1. **The undisputed arithmetic** — briefly confirm the numbers both friends accept and why (roughly 100 words).
2. **Move-by-move adjudication** — take moves (1) through (4) in order, rating each as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with your justification (450–550 words).
3. **What both are missing** — any consideration absent from their exchange that bears on the questions they deadlocked on (roughly 150 words).
4. **Overall verdict** — your answers to their two questions, stated plainly (roughly 100 words).
