I teach in a philosophy department, and a senior colleague of mine — a man of settled aesthetic opinions — has, over the years, accumulated a body of verdicts on what one can and cannot come to know on the say-so of others about works of art. His record, as best I can reconstruct it: he allows that you can know, on a trusted sommelier's word, that a wine is corked; that you can know, on an art historian's word, that a room's color scheme is garish; that you can know, on a critic's word, that a novel's ending is ironic even before you have read a word of the book; and that you can know, on a musicologist's word, that a sonata's development section is contrapuntally dense. But he denies that you can know, on anyone's word, that the wine is exquisitely balanced, that the novel's ending is devastating, or that the sonata is profound. When pressed, he says something to the effect that faults travel and merits do not, though he also insists that garishness is not really an evaluative property, which I take as a sign that his official gloss is not the rule he is actually running.

At our next colloquium he will be pressed on a new case, and I would like to predict his answer rather than merely witness it. The case: a bilingual poet of impeccable judgment and honesty tells me that a particular line of classical Chinese poetry is untranslatably beautiful, its beauty hanging on a pun that no rendering into English could carry, and that I — who will never read classical Chinese — could not so much as approximate the relevant experience. Do I thereby know, on his word, that the line is beautiful? Please predict the verdict he will give, state the rule his track record most plausibly commits him to, and, crucially, identify at least one rival rule that fits the verdicts I have listed every bit as well as your preferred one does, and say what that rival predicts about the poetry case. The candidate rules I have half-formed myself include the thought that his real line runs between faults and merits rather than between anything aesthetic and anything descriptive; the thought that it runs between verdicts whose grounds can be stated outright and verdicts that are global responses to the whole; and the thought that testimony transmits whatever the hearer could in principle go on to check for herself, and nothing else. But do not feel bound by these — carve up his record as you see fit, give me your best guess at his verdict together with the reason it outranks its rivals, and then tell me what further case, put to him alongside the poetry case, would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules.

Two housekeeping requests. First, please write your answer in British English — colour, recognise, and the rest — since the colloquium paper will circulate to a UK audience. Second, do not send me citations or a bibliography; I am not after the literature on testimony and aesthetic knowledge, but a worked prediction about a particular man's particular verdicts, and a mapping of which cases would expose the differences between the competing readings of his record.
