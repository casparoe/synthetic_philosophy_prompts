I organize a monthly philosophy session for longtime readers of the rationalist blogosphere — about a dozen people, one 90-minute slot, and a crowd with zero patience for whiteboard lectures. Design an in-class activity that makes the identity of indiscernibles fall apart as an experience rather than as a claim. The core mechanic I want you to build out: some participants play inhabitants of a variant of Black's universe — the one containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres a mile apart in otherwise empty space — while the rest play the scientific community trying to settle, by observation alone, how many spheres there are and which is which. The catch is a strict language constraint: every question and every report must be purely qualitative — no proper names, no indexicals like "this sphere" or "the one closer to me," and no coordinate systems that smuggle haecceitist facts in through an external reference frame. Spell out the rules, the materials, the scientists' win conditions, and a debrief that maps the moments of failure onto the actual dialectic: why the totality of qualitative truths comes out invariant under swapping the spheres, what a Leibnizian can still salvage by moving to impure predicates or by pointing out that "there exist exactly two spheres" is itself a qualitative truth, and where the structuralist would say the participants were never entitled to ask the which-is-which question in the first place. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I already expect someone to try to break the game with relational tricks like "the sphere I kicked" — and be honest about which part of the metaphysics no staged activity can reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
