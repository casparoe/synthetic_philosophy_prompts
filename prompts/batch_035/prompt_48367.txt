For as long as there has been modern mathematics, the debate over Zeno's paradoxes of motion has been stalled in a peculiar way. On one side stand those who hold that the paradoxes were dissolved by the theory of convergent infinite series: the runner's path can be partitioned into infinitely many sub-intervals whose lengths sum to a finite number, and the mistaken assumption that any infinite collection of completed sub-journeys must amount to something infinite was exactly Zeno's error. On the other side stand those who reply that summing the series merely labels the destination without explaining how a process can complete infinitely many distinct parts — that a point-by-point account of motion tells us where the runner is at each instant but never how the runner gets from one instant to the next, so the formal result restates the fact Zeno called into question rather than answering him. The supertask literature (Thomson's lamp, Black's transfer machines) seems only to have sharpened the deadlock. My working hypothesis is that each side takes the other to have changed the subject: the formalists say the skeptics demand an explanation where none is owed, while the skeptics say the formalists answer a mathematical question Zeno never asked. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, turning on what counts as solving a paradox; substantive, turning on whether space and time are actually infinitely divisible, about which physics is arguably silent below the Planck scale; or two different projects — demonstrating consistency versus explaining completion — pursued under a single heading? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a new conceptual distinction, a result from physics, or an argument that the impasse is permanent because one side is asking for something that is in principle unexplainable.
