I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, and one recurring thread that never quite gets resolved is whether a future that abolishes suffering must abolish pain itself or only pain's badness — the David Pearce-style abolitionism question of whether a civilization that keeps intact nociception, danger learning, and injury-avoidance, but strips out all aversion to it, has actually achieved anything worth wanting. In a recent discussion of this, someone brought up pain asymbolia, the odd condition (usually from insular cortex damage) where patients report that they feel pain, can rate its intensity and point to its location, but simply don't mind it — they say they'd accept more of it, or even ask whether the stimulus is meant to be unpleasant. My friends Dana and Felix have been arguing about what this shows, and I want a written record of their strongest exchange. Dana holds that the badness of pain is intrinsic to its phenomenal character: there is something it is like to feel pain, and that feel is itself pro tanto bad, so if asymbolics genuinely have the feel, something bad is happening to them whether they mind it or not — and a suffering-free civilization that keeps the feel keeps the bad. Felix holds a two-component view: pain is a sensory representation of bodily damage plus an aversive, imperative-like distress component, and only the second is bad; on his view asymbolia is a clean dissociation that vindicates him, and abolition should target aversion, not sensation. Dana's comeback is that Felix's view makes the badness of pain hostage to a contingent reaction, which seems to get the order of explanation backwards.

To keep the dialogue honest rather than a recital of familiar positions, please work in a thought experiment I raised during the argument, which I'll call reverse asymbolia: a patient with congenital insensitivity to pain who feels nothing at all from injuries, yet develops intense hypervigilant dread — checking her body for damage she cannot sense, suffering acutely about the unfelt burns and fractures accumulating on her limbs. Dana has to say whether this patient's suffering is bad in the same way ordinary pain is bad, and what that costs her story; Felix has to say what exactly is bad for her, given that on his account the bad component is missing and the sensory component never fires. I'd also like Dana to press the familiar objection that the reports of asymbolics — intensity ratings that stay high while unpleasantness ratings flatline — show a real change in the feel itself, not just a calm reaction to an unchanged feel, and Felix to press back on whether "unpleasantness" can be pried apart from the feel the way Dana needs.

Please write the dialogue with exactly five exchanges, where one exchange is Dana speaking and Felix replying; each speaker should say at most two or three sentences per turn, so the whole thing stays tight, and there should be no introduction, scene-setting, or concluding commentary outside the dialogue itself. Both characters should be equally competent — no strawmen, no one talking past the other, each responding to the actual point just made. Critically, I want a committed ending, not a diplomatic draw: by the end of the fifth exchange, one of them should genuinely concede, or the two should explicitly locate the precise crux that settles it and state which side that crux favors — make the choice and show the reasoning, rather than leaving me to weigh the two positions myself.
