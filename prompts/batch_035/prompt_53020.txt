Consider the two-envelope paradox in its standard form. One of two envelopes contains twice as much money as the other; you are handed one at random and, before opening it, are offered the chance to swap. If X denotes the amount in your envelope, then the other envelope appears to contain either 2X or X/2, each with probability one half, so its expected contents appear to be (5/4)X — strictly more than X — and you should swap. Yet the identical argument, run from the perspective of the other envelope, recommends swapping back, and iterating the reasoning suggests you ought to keep trading in circles.

A colleague of mine collects resolutions to this puzzle and currently holds the following five in her files:

(a) The symmetry argument: swapping gains you nothing in expectation, since the situation is symmetric between the envelopes; the 5/4 calculation must therefore contain an error somewhere, and locating that error is an optional exercise rather than a requirement for the verdict.

(b) The conditioning argument: the fallacy lies in treating "the other envelope contains 2X with probability 1/2 and X/2 with probability 1/2" as holding for every possible value of X. Once the amounts are drawn from some prior distribution over possible sums, the conditional probabilities of doubling versus halving shift with the observed amount, and no coherent prior makes the 5/4 calculation come out right at every value.

(c) The improper-prior diagnosis: the paradox implicitly assumes something like a uniform distribution over all positive amounts, which is not a probability distribution; once this is noticed, the expectation computation is exposed as resting on a non-entity.

(d) The infinite-expectation diagnosis: the paradox's setup is quietly inconsistent, since the symmetry plus the 5/4 reasoning force the expected value of the amount in an envelope to be infinite; familiar problems with comparing infinite expectations (as in the St. Petersburg lottery) show that "you should switch" is not licensed here.

(e) The exchangeability argument: one should distinguish between the ex ante choice between the two envelopes (a symmetric coin flip, worth nothing) and conditional reasoning given a particular observed amount, where switching genuinely is favorable for some observed values and unfavorable for others; the paradox conflates the two perspectives, and the average gain over the pair, weighted properly, is zero.

My colleague also sketched a sixth candidate of her own: that the real error is an illicit application of the law of total expectation — summing conditional expectations over events on which they are not integrable — so that the 5/4 result is a numerical artifact of an operation that fails to converge rather than a mistaken probability assignment at any particular value.

Please rank these six candidates in tiers according to how completely you think they dissolve the paradox, from those that fully settle it down to those that merely gesture at where the trouble lies. For each, give a sentence or two explaining its placement, including any residual worry you have that the resolution leaves unaddressed. Then say whether you believe there is, among these six or elsewhere, a fully satisfying resolution — or whether some residue of paradox remains even once the best of them is granted.
