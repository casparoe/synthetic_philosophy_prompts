My two roommates got into a huge argument on a long car ride and eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Some background: one of them, Priya, took a religion course last semester and got really into Jainism — especially the doctrine that truth is many-sided, anekantavada I think she calls it (probably misspelling that), the idea that any single claim only captures one facet of reality, and that you should really be prefixing everything with something like "in some respect, this is the case." The trigger was a fight between Priya and her brother about whether their mom is controlling, which Priya tried to end by declaring that from the brother's standpoint the mom IS controlling, from the mom's own standpoint she's being caring, both are true in their respective respects, and so nobody has to lose.

My other roommate Marco thought this was nonsense, and it escalated from there. (1) He said if "in some respect, X is true" is the most you can ever say, the doctrine applies to itself, and it doesn't seem to rule anything out — "in some respect, the mom is NOT controlling" is just as licensed, so all you've done is said nothing. (2) Priya said that's exactly the point: real questions get settled by specifying the respect, not by a bald yes or no, and it's the bald yes/no answers that start family fights in the first place. (3) Marco replied that sure, context matters everywhere, but then the doctrine collapses into ordinary "it depends" — no exotic metaphysics needed — unless it makes the stronger claim that genuinely contradictory views can each be true, which he doesn't think even the blind-men-and-elephant story supports: each blind man is wrong about the elephant as a whole, they just each hold a true bit about a part, and someone who'd seen the whole animal could simply correct them. (4) Priya countered that the blind men aren't wrong at all on their own piece — the one holding the trunk really is touching something thick and snakey — and that Marco's "whole elephant" perspective is itself just another partial standpoint, since nobody actually has a God's-eye view; she says the Jains themselves treat full omniscience as basically out of reach for ordinary knowers. (5) Marco's last word was that if no standpoint can ever be corrected from outside, the doctrine can't do the work Priya wants it to do in family disputes — it can't tell her brother and her mom anything about how to actually move forward, it just makes everyone feel heard while the same fight recurs next Thanksgiving.

They turned to me and I had nothing. Can you go through their five moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification, and then say who comes out ahead overall? I'd especially like to know whether the self-reference point in (1) is a genuine problem for the doctrine or just a cheap trick, and whether (3) or (4) is right about the elephant.
