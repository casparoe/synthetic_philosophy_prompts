Quick context: I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training (closest I've come is a philosophy podcast I listen to while debugging), and I've been having a running argument with my flatmate about the brain-in-a-vat thing. He's fond of this view he calls the tracking theory of knowledge, from Nozick I think he said: you know p only if (a) were p false, you wouldn't believe p, and (b) were p true, you would believe it. He likes it because it hands us everyday knowledge but blocks the sceptic: I know I have hands, but I *don't* know I'm not a handless brain in a vat deceived into believing I have hands, because if I were one, I'd still believe I wasn't (the belief isn't "sensitive"). Fine, whatever. But last week he ran this on me and it's been bugging me since, because it feels like it proves too much:

(1) I know I have hands — the tracking theory itself says so. (2) I know that having hands entails not being a handless deceived brain in a vat. (3) If you competently deduce q from something you know, you come to know q — deduction is like the one inference rule that never loses information; it's basically my whole job. (4) So after thirty seconds of reasoning I know I'm not a handless deceived brain in a vat. (5) But the tracking theory says I can't know that, per the sensitivity point above. (6) So the theory has to say that running valid logic on your own knowledge can fail to extend it — my flatmate happily bites this bullet, but it seems to me like a type system where && of two verified values comes back unverified. And here's the extra bit that really gets me: he says take the conjunction of (1) and (2) — "I have hands, and if I have hands I'm not a vat-brain" — and he admits I know each conjunct separately but claims I don't know the conjunction, because if the conjunction were false (i.e. I'm a vat-brain), I'd still believe it. So I can know p, know q, but not know p-and-q?! He also muttered something about how lots of tiny reasoning steps might be fine while the one big step to the sceptical conclusion isn't, which sounded awfully convenient to me. Is the conjunction thing a genuinely separate problem or just the same problem wearing a hat?

I'd much rather keep (3) — doing logic on what you know should be safe, that feels non-negotiable to me — and honestly I'd like to keep knowledge of my hands too. Tell me which step you'd reject and why, why that step looked fine in the first place, and what it actually costs to give it up (both for the tracking theory and for whatever you'd put in its place). And keep the jargon to a minimum or define it as you go, I'm a programmer not a philosophy student.
