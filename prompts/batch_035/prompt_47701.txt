My roommate has this argument against utilitarianism, riffing on the separateness of persons objection, and I genuinely can't find the clean way out of it:

(1) Pain is bad because it's bad for the one who feels it. Badness is always badness-for a particular subject, there's no free-floating badness floating around.
(2) Any moral reason has to bottom out in goods or bads for particular people — there's no extra vantage point from which things are good "for the world."
(3) Utilitarianism tells me to impose pain on A whenever it produces a larger benefit for B and C.
(4) To justify that, I need a single scale that contains both A's pain and B's and C's benefits.
(5) But there is no subject for whom A's pain and B's benefits are both given. Nobody experiences the sum.
(6) So the scale in (4) is a fiction, and utilitarianism is prescribing tradeoffs that no one is ever in a position to actually make. It's not just wrong, it's incoherent.

The obvious counter, I thought, was: fine, but the same reasoning applies within a single life. My Tuesday headache isn't co-experienced with Thursday's relief, so by her logic prudence is a fiction too, and I can never justify taking an unpleasant pill now for a benefit later. She says no — within a life there IS a single subject over time, and that's exactly what separateness of *persons* is supposed to mark, it doesn't apply to the parts of one person. But I've read enough Parfit to think the "single subject over time" is itself pretty shaky, and if she rests her whole anti-utilitarian case on it, she's taking on a metaphysics of personal identity she really doesn't want to defend in front of me.

So where does this thing actually break? Is it step (5) — is the sum really a fiction, or is that an equivocation between "nobody experiences the total" and "the total can't be a reason for anyone"? Is the slide from (2) to (4) doing hidden work? Or is my instinct right that the real cost lands on her: if she holds the line at lives rather than experiences, she owes me a story about why the life is the right unit, and every story on offer makes the unit fuzzy in ways that leak back toward the utilitarian side? Price out the exits for me. For what it's worth I'd like to keep something like (1) — I think pain really is bad for the one in it — I just don't want to concede that this buys her (6).
