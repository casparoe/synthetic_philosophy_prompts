At our last rationalist meetup (video call, the usual half-dozen regulars) two of the guys got into it over the old torture vs. dust specks question, and it went somewhere way more interesting than usual. Dave took the standard LessWrong line: when you've got 3^^^3 people each getting a dust speck in the eye, the aggregate is obviously worse than one person being tortured for fifty years, and refusing to multiply is just scope insensitivity — the exact bias we've all supposedly trained ourselves out of. Maya was adament that this is different: some harms are on a different level entirely, and no amount of trivial annoyance can ever add up to torture; the units just don't convert, no matter the exponent. Dave then said fine, but that's what makes it interesting — if you take her line, "worse than" can't be transitive. A year of torture plus one extra dust speck is surely worse than the year alone, and you can chain zillions of such steps from one speck all the way up to fifty years of torture; so if each tiny step makes things worse, transitivity forces you to concede the specks beat the torture after all, and if some steps make no difference at all, you get a sorites-style pile where a thousand identical specks count for nothing but a trillion somehow count for something. Maya said this is just the spectrum-argument trick, and those arguments famously prove too much: if you can hear the difference between any two tones that are close enough in wavelength, chaining gets you that you can distinguish any tone from any other, which is absurd — so non-transitive perceptual indifference is a thing we already live with, and maybe comparative betterness can be like that too. Dave answered that indiscriminability isn't a value relation — your eyesight's limits don't impose structure on the moral universe — whereas "worse than" is supposed to be the thing aggregation and decision theory actually run on, and if it's non-transitive you're open to being money-pumped by your own ethics. Maya's last word was that the money pump only works if you're forced to trade at every step, and someone with lexical priorities can just refuse the trades that would cross a level, so the dutch book never completes.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — who's right about which specific points, not just who wins overall. In particular: does the chaining argument actually force Maya's hand the way Dave says, or does her perceptual-indifference analogy do real work in blocking it? Is the money-pump threat real here, or does refusing trades genuinely save you from it (and at what cost)? And say what, if anything, both of them are missing — my vague suspicion is they're partly talking past each other about what "worse than" is even for (a report of what to choose, versus a fact about outcomes), but I can't pin it down well enough to say.
