My friend and I spent the better part of a dinner arguing about insect welfare, and the whole argument kept collapsing back onto a single analogy she was leaning on: sentience is a dimmer switch, not an on/off light. The bulb is fully lit for a chimpanzee, noticeably dim for a chicken, barely glowing for a bee, and dark for a thermostat — and moral status, she said, scales with the setting: the bee's suffering counts, but it counts a tiny amount, and this is exactly the picture that makes the whole program of worrying about the trillions of insects in the wild and in agricultural systems intelligible in the first place. She even used the analogy to defuse the thermostat worry: sure, maybe there's some vanishingly faint glimmer in any system that detects and responds to its environment, but the reading is so close to zero it rounds to nothing, and we shouldn't be paralyzed by it. I objected that the picture felt too easy, but I couldn't say precisely where it breaks, and she accused me of defending human exceptionalism by another name.

I'd like you to audit the analogy properly. First, build the correspondence table: what plays the role of the current, the switch, the bulb, and above all the setting that the analogy assumes we can read off a dial. Then show where the mapping fails, and pay special attention to the things I half-noticed but couldn't articulate. A dimmer varies along exactly one dimension that we know how to measure — watts — whereas a bee seems brightly lit on some capacities (detecting and escaping noxious stimuli) and completely dark on others (anything like a persisting self, or felt states in anything like Nagel's what-it-is-like-to-be-a-bat sense). So perhaps consciousness is less like one dimmer and more like a whole floor of rooms in which different lights are on and off independently, and if there is no single dial, there may be no fact of the matter about which animal is "more sentient" than which — no common currency in which a bee's 0.001 and a chimp's 1.0 live. That matters for the question the argument actually turned on: her conclusion wasn't just that sentience is graded, but that moral status attaches in proportion to the degree. Does that proportional conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy quietly carrying it the whole way — importing, for instance, the idea that because a dimmer's setting is a single ordered magnitude, "degree of sentience" must be too, and that moral weight can therefore be multiplied out like the numbers on the spreadsheets where people assign moral weights to species? If the floor-of-rooms picture is better, what becomes of the view that insect suffering matters, but only a little — and is there some third analogy that preserves what's genuinely right in gradualism without smuggling in a single scale?

Whatever the answer, please be even-handed: she may be wrong about the dimmer but right that my resistance was really a refusal to accept that anything could matter only slightly, and I want to know if that's what was actually happening to me.
