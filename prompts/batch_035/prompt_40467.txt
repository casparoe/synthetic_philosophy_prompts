My reading group has been working through Hofstadter's strange-loop material, and the careenium thought experiment — the billiard table full of small magnetic balls (symms) that clump into larger, stable formations (symmballs), with the table's walls passing in information about the outside world by changing their bounciness — was supposed to be the dessert session. Instead it detonated a two-hour argument between two members that the rest of us watched without being able to referee, and I'd like someone to sort it out.

For context: Hofstadter uses the careenium as a cartoon of the cranium. The symms are the neural substrate; the simmballs are symbols or concepts, whose comings-together and falling-apart mirror, in a coarse way, what's going on outside the table. His famous question about the setup is, roughly, who pushes whom around inside the careenium — do the little balls do all the pushing, with the simmballs just being our name for certain fuzzy clumps of them, or do the simmballs themselves do the pushing?

The argument went like this. (1) Marcus said the answer is trivially the symms: the simmballs are made of nothing but symms, every collision is a symm-on-symm collision, and calling a simmball an agent that "dances with" other simmballs is a convenience of description, not a fact about the physics. (2) Iris said that's too quick: at the symm level, the events that matter are astronomically complex and effectively unstatable, whereas at the simmball level there are real regularities — simmballs that mirror outside-world things trigger the simmballs they've been associated with, and this accounts for the table's behavior in a way no symm-level description could. Explanations at the clump level aren't going away, so the clumps aren't nothing. (3) Marcus replied that explanatory convenience is not causation: the fastest-moving molecule in a gas might be useful for explaining a room's temperature fluctuations, but we don't say it steers anything, and Iris was conflating levels of description with levels of causal power. (4) Iris then brought in Hofstadter's further twist — the self-symbol, a simmball that represents the whole system including its own patterns of clumping — and argued that once a structure like that exists, it genuinely steers: a decision made "at the symbol level" sets off a coordinated cascade of symms, and the cascade wouldn't have happened without it. (5) Marcus answered that the "decision" is just our blurry name for a symm-cascade that was already underway — a press release about a coup, not the coup — so the self-symbol's causal role is a flattering illusion. (6) Iris closed by saying that if Hofstadter is right that the two levels are descriptions of one and the same process, then Marcus's insistence that only the substrate is "really doing the pushing" is itself just a preference for one level of description, no more privileged than hers, and his reductionism is doing no work that his physics doesn't already do.

I noticed the two of them seemed to be using the word "cause" to mean different things, but I couldn't get beyond that. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, half-right, or mistaken, and why. Then address the specific question the setup raises: on Hofstadter's own terms, is there any substantive sense in which the symbols push the substrate around, or is that a façon de parler that the physics fully cashes out? Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them missed — in particular whether the dispute really turns on causation at all, or on something else they never separated out.
