"In a short essay, (a) explain what the 'responsibility gap' is supposed to be, and why it is often claimed to arise especially with learning autonomous systems rather than with ordinary machines; (b) explain one prominent strategy for closing or dissolving the gap; (c) briefly defend a view on whether it is a genuinely new problem or an old problem in new clothes."

Here is one student's answer, typed out as written (typos and all):

"The responsibility gap arises when an autonomous system causes harm and there is no human who satisfies the usual conditions for holding someone responsible. With ordinary machines responsibility falls on the user or the manufacturer, because they could foresee what the machine would do: a toaster behaves the same way every time, so if it injures someone the maker is liable. But a learning system keeps changing its behaviour after deployment. A military drone that adapts its targetting in response to what it encounters may do something none of its programmers predicted, so the programmer cannot be blamed for an outcome they could not foresee, the commander did not control the specific strike because the drone selected the target itself, and the drone is not a moral agent. So nobody is responsible. This is essentially the problem of many hands in a new setting: when lots of people each contribute a small part of a process, each contribution is too small for any one person to bear full responsibility, and the drone case just adds more hands (programmers, commanders, maintainers, regulators).

One proposed answer is meaningful human control, which requires that a human stays in the loop and approves or vetoes each decision before the system acts. If the operator signs off on the strike, the operator is responsible, and the gap is closed because someone had the final say every time. But this does not really help. P. F. Strawson's four-case argument shows that when an agent's decisions are the product of a chain of causes they did not originate — as when neuroscientists manipulate someone's choices — we do not hold them responsible even though they act on their own desires. The operator rubber-stamping a recommendation produced by a system they cannot inspect is in the same position.

Finally, since no individual is responsible in gap cases, no wrong is done to the victims. The harm is a misfortune, like being struck by lightning, not an injustice, so the victims are owed charity but not compensation as a matter of right. This also shows the gap is not really new: it is the old many-hands problem plus ordinary products liability, which the law already handles."
