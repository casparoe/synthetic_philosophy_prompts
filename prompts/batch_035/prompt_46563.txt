I sit on the pastoral care commission of the publicly funded hospital network in our region. Our current arrangement funds chaplaincy posts — Anglican, Roman Catholic, Muslim, and Jewish, allocated roughly in line with patient demand — out of the network's own budget, following the cooperative settlement found in parts of continental Europe rather than the stricter separation associated with French laïcité. A local humanist association has now petitioned us to fund accredited non-religious pastoral carers on the same basis, for patients who want someone to talk to about dying, meaning, and loneliness, but not within a religious framework. Our survey work suggests that roughly a tenth of patients who want such a conversation would prefer a non-religious visitor: about the level of demand that justified one of our smaller existing posts.

The commission is split, and the split is not merely practical. One side holds that chaplaincy is by definition a religious office, so the petition rests on a confusion: the state discharges its duty of neutrality by staying out of spiritual care altogether, and the principled response to our current arrangement is not to extend it to humanists but to shrink it, transferring provision to the religious communities themselves. The other side holds that neutrality means neutrality between religion and non-religion, not between the state and one side only: if we pay for a rabbi to sit with a dying patient but would not pay for a humanist carer to do the same thing, we are not neutral but quietly confessional.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting in three weeks' time. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund the posts, decline the petition, or approve it under conditions — with a time frame for implementation; set out the reasoning in terms the commissioners can actually debate, engaging honestly with the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, rather than restating our equalities obligations; and make explicit which general settlement between church and state the recommendation quietly endorses — strict separation, cooperation with recognised religious bodies, or even-handed support for religion and non-religion alike — together with a view on whether that settlement is defensible for a healthcare system at all, or whether the network should confine itself to clinical care and leave questions of meaning to patients and their own communities.
