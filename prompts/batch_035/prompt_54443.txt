I direct a small community-dispute resolution program attached to our county government. Our caseload is dominated by fights in which the parties disagree about values rather than facts or interests: a neighborhood association that opposes a supervised injection site because it sees enabling drug use as wrong in itself, against a public-health coalition that treats preventable overdose deaths as the only consideration that could matter; a historic-preservation board and a transit agency locked over demolishing a row of 1920s storefronts for a bus lane; a school committee split over how a health curriculum should present contraception. These disputes arrive with lawyers, deadlines, and a county board that expects us to produce a signed agreement, and over the years we have mostly settled them by splitting the difference — shave the building's height, save two of the four facades, teach both framings in one unit. Lately I have come to suspect that splitting the difference is the one thing we never actually reason about; it is just the number we reach when everyone is too tired to keep arguing.

Years ago I took a seminar on the ethics of compromise, and the framing that stayed with me is that a good compromise is supposed to resemble gains from trade: each side gives up something it values less in exchange for something it values more, so that both do better under the deal than under the winner-take-all alternative. That framing carries a lot of luggage I have never learned to unpack in practice. It implies compromise is not consensus — the parties never have to converge on reasons, only on terms — and that a settlement reached by exhaustion is not the same thing as one reached by trade. It suggests that some compromises genuinely expand what is available to both sides (log-rolls, in-kind side payments, sequencing one project after another) while others merely launder one side's defeat so it can be announced as mutual victory. It raises the problem of values held as absolute or lexicographic, which cannot be traded at any price — and the harder practical problem that a party's claim of non-negotiability may be sincere conviction or a bargaining posture, and these look identical from across the table. And it leaves me with the notion, half-remembered, that even a legitimate compromise can leave a moral remainder — a real cost to one side's deepest commitments that should be acknowledged afterward rather than re-described as everyone having won.

Please write a decision procedure my staff can actually run. Concretely: two mediators, a ninety-minute intake meeting with each party separately, then a single day-long joint session, and a county board that signs off on any agreement. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it terminates with a finding that no mutually improving trade exists and the parties should go to litigation or a council vote instead, and the points at which it must hand the question back to a human because it is a question of values the procedure cannot settle. It needs concrete tests for distinguishing genuine absolutes from strategic absolutism, and for detecting pie-expanding packages as opposed to capitulation dressed as a deal. It should also say something about repeated dealings — most of these parties will be back at our table within two years, and I do not know how much that should change what we count as a fair outcome the first time. Then run the procedure on three cases: the injection site, the storefronts versus the bus lane, and a recent dispute over a modest bequest, where the family wanted a memorial statue and the town wanted scholarships, both sides refused every hybrid we proposed, and I privately thought they were being childish about it. On that last one I would like to know whether the procedure vindicates their refusal or exposes it as posturing — I suspect the answer will not be the one I want, and I would rather know that now. Finally, be candid about where the procedure will misfire: which of its steps are judgment calls wearing a checklist's clothes, and what it is likely to get wrong that an experienced mediator would catch.
