I'm in a book club and we're rereading Of Mice and Men next month, and I keep getting stuck on the dog scene in a way the usual discussion questions never touch. Early in the book, Carlson takes Candy's old dog out back and shoots it — he says it's for the dog's own good, since the dog is old and miserable — and Candy immediately regrets it. His line to George afterwards is roughly that he ought to have shot the dog himself rather than let a stranger do it. And then the ending repeats the pattern: with the lynch mob coming for Lennie, George doesn't stand aside, he kills Lennie himself, painlessly, with Carlsons gun, while telling him the farm story one last time.

Read the novel through the doing/allowing, killing/letting-die lens. The standard cases in that literature — Foot's surgeon, Rachels' bathtub pair where pushing a kid under and standing by while he drowns come out looking the same — all set the choice up as: do the harmful thing, or just don't act. But Steinbeck's two deaths have a different shape. George's real alternative to killing Lennie isn't standing by while Lennie dies of some independent cause, it's letting other people kill him badly. So the choice is less killing vs. letting die and more doing a death yourself versus delegating it. And Candy's regret suggests the moral weight isn't in the act/omission line at all but in who does it — as if the person who loves you shouldn't outsource your death to a stranger. Does the Foot/Rachels debate, and the people who later tried to save the distinction, have the resources for that, or do they only model cases where pure omission is a live alternative to action? Does the consent asymmetry matter — Lennie half-asks George for it, while the dog obviously can't consent, and Candy the proxy says no and gets talked over? Please honestly tell me if the doing/allowing lens is just the wrong lens here and something like a duty-of-care story fits better, and flag the two or three scenes we should anchor our discussion on. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.
