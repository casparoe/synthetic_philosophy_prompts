I write long-form pieces for a science and ideas section, and my editor wants something past the usual "is philosophy dead?" round-up — the Hawking line, Feynman's grumbling that philosophy of science is about as much use to a scientist as ornithology is to birds, Weinberg's old essay about how little philosophy ever did for him, versus the philosophers replying that physicists do philosophy badly when they do it unawares. I've decided to build the piece around a concrete scenario instead of the soundbites: a small foundation (think a couple of million a year, enough for maybe a dozen fellowships) wants to fund philosophy-adjacent work aimed at actually helping working scientists — not AI ethics, which is already swimming in money, and not history of science either. Concretely: philosophers embedded in physics, biology, or cognitive science departments, working on questions the scientists themselves bump into.

So what I need from you: which open questions or lines of work at the philosophy–science interface would you actually prioritise for that kind of money, and in what order? I'm much less interested in a survey than in the reasoning — for each item, why it's upstream of other work, whether it's tractable for philosophers rather than for the scientists themselves, and what would make you move it up or down the list in a few years. And please be frank about the other side: which of philosophy's claimed contributions to science are overrated or don't follow at all (measurement problem? scientific realism? the whole "conceptual engineering" thing?). If your honest answer is that most of it doesn't cash out, say so and tell me which two or three bits would still survive that verdict — those are what the piece will hang on.

One formatting thing: please write in British English throughout (prioritise, colour, that sort of thing), since that's our house style and it saves my poor sub-editor a headache. typos in my questions are my own fault, not hers.
