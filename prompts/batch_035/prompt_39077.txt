In online arguments about deplatforming, I keep encountering the so-called paradox of tolerance — the line, usually traced to a footnote in Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies, that a society practicing unlimited tolerance will end up having its tolerance seized by the intolerant, and so must be prepared not to tolerate intolerance. It gets invoked to support everything from moderating fringe voices on social media, to denying a legal existence to parties that would abolish the democracy they run in, down to punching street agitators. I want to know what precise theses that slogan could actually be expressing, laid out as a menu rather than one interpretation. The readings seem to me to vary along several dimensions: what tolerance is (an individual virtue, an institutional policy, a reciprocity arrangement where you tolerate only those who tolerate you back?); who counts as intolerant (anyone holding bigoted or offensive views, or only those prepared to use force or fraud to suppress others?); and what refusing to tolerate amounts to (mere public criticism, exclusion from platforms and institutions, or state coercion up to criminalization?). For each distinct reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and — crucially — whether it actually licenses the specific uses the slogan is being put to. In particular, does any reading license suppressing views that are merely offensive rather than aimed at abolishing tolerance itself? I'd also like you to address the standard riposte that once intolerance of the intolerant is licensed, every side will declare its opponents the intolerant ones: which readings of the slogan are most vulnerable to that abuse, and which have a built-in structural answer to it?
