I'm a second-year law student and we just did the Hart–Fuller debate in jurisprudence, which I thought would be a dry historical thing but it actually caused a proper argument at my flat between my two coursemates, Priya and Tom, that went on for like an hour. It started from the grudge informer problem — the postwar German cases where the courts had to deal with people who'd reported their spouses or neighbours to the Nazi authorities for "defaming the regime", sometimes just to get rid of them, and the question was whether the Nazi statutes the informers relied on counted as law at all. I mostly watched and kept out of it, and honestly I couldn't tell who was winning, so I said I'd ask someone neutral. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each point, and who's more right overall?

It went roughly like this. (1) Priya said Hart's positivism is a moral hazard: if you say law and morals are conceptually seperated, then whatever the state calls law is law, which is exactly the attitude that let officials off the hook after the regime fell — they could say they were only following valid law. She said Fuller was right that law has an inner morality (generality, publicity, prospectivity, congruence between what's announced and what's enforced), and a system that systematically tramples these isn't just bad law, it stops being law, which is why the German courts could call the Nazi decrees "lawless law" rather than having to pretend a valid statute happened to be wicked. (2) Tom said that was all backwards: the German courts' real complaint, as Hart pointed out, was that punishing the informers required making the law retroactive, and dressing that awkward choice up as "the statute was never law" just hides the moral question instead of answering it — the positivist at least keeps it in the open, where you have to defend retroactive punishment honestly or let the informer go. (3) Priya answered that keeping the question open isn't neutral either — the label "valid law" carries real weight in a courtroom, and a judge told a statute is law feels bound to apply it; Fuller's point is that a legal system whose rules are secret, constantly changed, and applied retroactively already fails at being a system of rules, so "is it law?" is not purely descriptive. (4) Tom said that proved less than she thought: a state can be public, prospective and consistent while doing atrocious things with the content of its statutes — those procedural virtues are compatible with wicked law, so even if Fuller wins on the edge cases (the truly chaotic regime), Priya still doesn't get her headline conclusion that unjust law isn't law; at most she gets that some extreme regimes produce a grey zone where "law" gets indeterminate. (5) Priya's last word was that the grey zone is exactly where all the interesting cases live, so the conceptual dispute isn't pedantic — whether you call it law determines whether the courts owe it any deference at all, and that's a difference with real consequences, whatever Tom says about labels.

Neither of them would concede after that, and I couldn't referee because I honestly found both sides convincing at different points. Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and say what (if anything) both of them are missing.
