So MMO's like WoW have this item restoration thing where if you get scammed out of your sword, or accidentally vendor it, or a hacker deletes your gear, the GMs will give it back to you -- and they mean the *same* item, enchants and gems still attached, not an equivalent. You only get a few restorations per year, so the wiki warnings treat it like something precious and non-fungible. But then look at what happens when there's a duplication exploit and duped copies of a rare mount spread through the auction house: the devs just mass-delete every copy, including ones innocent players paid real gold for in good faith, and at best you get some gold or a generic make-good package as compensation. So the same studio, in one case, insists nothing but the original will do, and in the other case destroys your original and calls the debt settled with a fungible payout. What view of what a virtual item even *is* makes both of these coherent? Does the restoration side presuppose that the item is a particular with a history that can survive deletion and re-creation (which is kind of wild when you remember it's literally a database row that gets deleted and re-inserted), while the dupe-deletion side presupposes items are just interchangeable tokens of a type? Or maybe the difference isn't metaphysical at all -- it's about deterring exploiters vs. keeping paying customers happy, and the "your same item, restored!" language is basically marketing. Can you sort out which parts of the practice actually require taking a stand on virtual object identity and which parts would work under several different theories? Also curious whether the fact that it's all database rows under the hood makes the "same item" talk literally false, or whether that's like saying a restored painting isn't the same painting because atoms got swapped.
