I am a retired analytical chemist. For the better part of thirty years I measured trace contaminants at concentrations close to the edge of detectability, and the vocabulary of that work is the one I still think in: calibration curves, signal-to-noise ratios, limits of detection, the distinction between precision and accuracy, random versus systematic error, replicate measurements, and — above all — the distinction between the measurand (the quantity that has a value) and the measurement result (our best, always imperfect, attempt to fix that value). I mention this because ISO guidance makes a distinction that I suspect is doing a lot of quiet work in what follows: uncertainty arising from the measurement process versus uncertainty about what is even meant by the quantity — but I do not want to lean on that; I want your translation judged in the plain terms of the bench.

In retirement I have begun reading philosophy seriously. I have worked through a good deal of the literature on vagueness — the sorites paradox with heaps of sand and heads of hair, and the two positions I cannot decide between: epistemicism, associated with Timothy Williamson, on which vague predicates have perfectly sharp boundaries and our ignorance of them is no different in kind from any other ignorance; and supervaluationism, on which a vague term admits many admissible precisifications, each of which draws a sharp line somewhere, with no one of them being what the term means — so that a borderline case makes 'the last grain that makes a heap' neither true nor false, whilst 'either there is a last grain or there is not' comes out true. I am fluent in my own vocabulary and a relative novice in yours: roughly two years of reading, no formal training.

Here is the task. Restate both positions, each in turn, in the vocabulary I actually used at the bench — as faithfully as you can. Treat it as a translation exercise with a strict rule: do not smuggle in the assumptions of my framework and present them as part of the translation. In particular, do not quietly assume that every predicate or quantity has a true value because that is how measurands behave; if that assumption is doing the work in your rendering of epistemicism, say so explicitly, because that is precisely what I want to find out. Likewise, when you render supervaluationism, do not let it collapse into a familiar story about reporting conventions unless the collapse is really there in the position.

Then give me the residue report, in three parts: what translates cleanly; what translates only with distortion, and what the distortion consists in; and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I want you to be as interested in the third part as in the first — I am not asking you to flatter my framework.

Finally, answer the question I actually care about. My working instinct is this: epistemicism is just the claim that the measurand has a true value even when our procedure cannot resolve it — which every working scientist accepts without ceremony, since the alternative is that nothing exists below the limit of detection — and supervaluationism is a conventionalist position about how results should be reported when no single convention is privileged, dressed up in semantic language. If that instinct is right, the debate is less substantive than it appears. But two things nag at me. First, Williamson seems to claim there is a fact of the matter about which particular grain turns the pile into a heap, not merely that some boundary exists below our resolving power — and I cannot tell whether that is the true-value claim writ small, or something my vocabulary genuinely cannot express. Second, the sorites inference — the step-by-step slide from 'a heap minus one grain is still a heap' to absurdity — seems unlike anything in error propagation, and I suspect it is exactly where my analogy runs out.

So tell me, on the basis of the residue: does the residue show that my measurement vocabulary is missing something the philosophers need, or that their disagreement is less clear than it looked? If it is my vocabulary that is missing something, say what, in terms I could act on. And if the honest answer is that the translation cannot decide between the two positions — that both renderings are equally faithful — say that too, rather than manufacturing a verdict.
