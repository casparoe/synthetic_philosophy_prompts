I'm a novelist working on a long essay on Styron's Sophie's Choice for a collection about moral extremity in fiction, and I want to set it against Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling — specifically the teleological suspension of the ethical. I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of religion who reads both the novel and Kierkegaard with equal seriousness, and to work through the comparison scene by scene before committing to an overall reading.

The obvious line is that Sophie is an anti-Abraham: her choice of which child to give up is commanded by a camp officer, not by God, and Kierkegaard's whole point is that the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, not to a man wielding arbitrary power. But I suspect the comparison is more interesting than a simple inversion. Consider a few threads: first, the Nazis deliberately construct her choice so that whichever way she acts she is wrong by every ordinary ethical measure — is this something like the ethical suspended from below, a parody rather than a suspension, and can Kierkegaard's framework actually help us mark that difference? Second, Sophie's lifelong silence and guilt versus Abraham's silence and joy: she confesses her choice to Stingo, the narrator, across a series of late-night retellings — is that confession what it would take for a knight of faith to become visible to others, or is it rather proof that she was never anything but a broken knight of resignation, still trapped inside the universal? Third, and most important to me as a novelist: does Styron dramatize something Kierkegaard arguably underplays — the perspective of the sacrificed child and of the one who survives, that is, the ethical's claim on the victims rather than on the agent?

Please say plainly where the novel resists the Kierkegaardian reading, and don't reduce Sophie to a counterexample in a debate about Fear and Trembling; she deserves better than being a position. Take a clear stance on whether the novel dramatizes something the philosophy misses. End with a final line of the form Bottom line: ... giving your overall verdict in one sentence.
