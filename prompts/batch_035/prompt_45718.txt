I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working slowly through game theory and the philosophy of rationality with a small reading group. For our final session of the year we are covering iterated elimination of dominated strategies, including the subtler case where a pure strategy is dominated not by any single rival but by a mixture of two others, and I would like to close with a poem in which the form does the philosophical work rather than merely name the idea.

The subject should be the p-beauty contest: players each choose a number from 0 to 100, and the winner is whoever is closest to, say, two-thirds of the average of all guesses. Iterated reasoning strips away the dominated guesses round by round — first anything above 67, then anything above 44, and so on — until only one number survives. I would like the poem to be voiced by a contestant reasoning through this in real time, feeling cleverer than the room and then less clever than the game.

The formal constraint: six stanzas of diminishing length — six lines, then five, then four, then three, then two, then one — each stanza one round of deletion. Fix a pool of six end-words in the opening stanza, treated as the six surviving strategies; each subsequent stanza may use only a proper subset of them, so that the available vocabulary of endings visibly shrinks, and the final single line must end on the one survivor, the number to which the reasoning converges. At least one deletion along the way should be a word or line that no single remaining line outperforms on its own but that a blend of two remaining lines does — and the poem should let the reader see that this deletion works only through the mixture, not through any one rival.

A colleague in the group remarked that with strict dominance the order of deletion does not matter: whatever route you take, the same strategy survives. I would like the poem to convey that inevitability — that the shrinking feels like discovery rather than choice. Beyond the mechanics, I want it to keep hold of the human unease underneath: the winner is the one who reasons all the way to zero, and the poem should make clear both that this is the unique rational answer and that a room of people who actually wrote down zero would have been better off if none of them had.

Please begin your response with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary; a short prose note of no more than 150 words explaining your order of deletions and which deletion depended on a mixture may follow the poem. Someone who knows iterated dominance should find the poem exactly right, and someone who does not should still find it a good poem about outsmarting a room that is outsmarting you.
