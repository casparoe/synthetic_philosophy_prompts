For a while I've been trying to settle, by gut alone, when an autonomous system opens a genuine responsibility gap — a harm no person is responsible for, because the system itself, rather than any human, produced it — and when the "gap" is just a fiction people hide behind. My verdicts so far:

A self-driving car injures a pedestrian because of a sensor misclassification no amount of competent testing would have caught. The engineers are blameless for the misclassification itself, but the manufacturer is responsible for the injury, since it chose to put a system of that reliability on public roads.

A hospital's dosing algorithm has a bug a competent code review would have caught; a patient is harmed. This is not a gap at all: the programmers are responsible, and calling it one is spin.

An autonomous trading system loses pension money in market conditions no reasonable designer could have anticipated. The fund is still responsible to the pensioners, even though every human involved was careful — "the algorithm did it" is no defense.

A military drone with a targeting decision no operator had time to veto misclassifies and kills civilians. The commander who fielded it with a veto window that short is responsible, even though he neither made the decision nor could have foreseen that specific error.

A company deploys a learning system behind a safety regime that everyone at the time, acting in good faith, agreed was reasonable; after deployment it develops a harmful behavior that no one specified, intended, or could have predicted. My gut says the company is still responsible.

But here is what I can't reconcile: if a friend lends her car to a careful driver who then has a genuinely freak accident, she is not responsible, even though she put the car in a position to cause harm. And when a person does unforeseeable harm through no negligence, I don't think anyone is responsible at all. So why does the deploying company feel different? My tentative principle is that if you delegate the deciding itself — you chose the system precisely because it would act without you — you own whatever it does, and the gap only opens for systems nobody chose to deploy or risks those affected knowingly accepted. But this seems to make an employer responsible for a careful human employee's freak accident in exactly the way I don't want, unless there's some real difference between deploying a human and deploying a system.

Can you find a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or close to it? Please state it precisely, motivate each clause from the case that demands it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save. I'd also like to know what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a hobbyist releases a free, open-source autopilot, a stranger modifies it, and the modified version injures someone — is the hobbyist inside the gap or outside it?
