I've been trying to sort out, case by case, when I think a dispute is merely verbal, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. The dispute over whether a tomato is a fruit or a vegetable I count as purely verbal: the botanist and the cook are disagreeing about a word, not about the world. The same goes for arguing over whether a hot dog is a sandwich. But when a Platonist and a nominalist disagree about whether the number seven exists, I think that dispute is substantive, even though I can't point to any observation that could decide it. Meanwhile, when two people both accept that electrons and quarks exist but disagree about whether there is such a thing as the mereological sum of my table and my shoe — the single entity composed of both — I find myself calling that dispute verbal, which puzzles me, because the parties seem to be using their quantifier the same way in the sum case as in the electron case. Stranger still, a dispute about whether a couple's union counts as marriage, in which the two parties agree about every legal and practical consequence, strikes me as not merely verbal even though it has exactly the shape of the tomato case: all the facts are common ground and only a word appears to be at issue. And I take the dispute over whether fish feel pain to be substantive even though part of it is plainly about how to use the word "pain." My tentative principle is something like: a dispute is merely verbal when both parties' claims can be true at once, once we let each party's terms mean what they mean by them, so that the world itself is indifferent between them — but this principle makes the marriage case verbal and makes the Platonist case verbal as well, and I am unwilling to swallow both of those. Can you state a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures my verdicts or most of them, say which clause is doing the work in each case, tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed (say, a disagreement over whether a locked-in patient is conscious), and be explicit about which of my verdicts I ought to give up? I'd also like to know whether something like Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance is the right tool here, or whether Theodore Sider's resistance to it — the thought that there is a joint-carving meaning of "there is" — better explains my reluctance to call the mereological dispute verbal. Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a letter.
