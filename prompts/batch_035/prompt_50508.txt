I am one of five trustees of a charitable trust founded by a local mill owner in the 1890s. His will charges us with applying the trust's income for the relief of the poor of the parish, in perpetuity, and the endowment now stands at roughly £2.4 million. After decades in which we simply made modest grants, we are debating, for the first time in living memory, a long-term policy for the fund, and three positions have emerged.

One trustee argues that we should spend far more generously now. The poor of the parish today are real, identifiable and badly off, and we know exactly what they need: heating grants, debt advice, repairs to the almshouses. The poor of 2150, by contrast, are a cloud of merely possible people about whose needs we can only guess, and there may be no parish left to be poor in.

Our treasurer takes the opposite view. The hydrological assessments for our valley point to serious and worsening flooding over the coming century, and he thinks we should hold current spending down and build reserves for flood defence and adaptation work that will mostly benefit parishioners not yet born. He puts it bluntly: a cold pensioner in 2150 matters no less than a cold pensioner this winter.

A third trustee, an accountant, agrees that the future counts, but proposes to discount it. Any benefit arriving in a given year, he says, should be treated as worth about 3 per cent less for every year we must wait, partly because resources are worth less when they arrive late, and partly because we cannot be confident that the trust, the parish, or for that matter anything recognisable as either will still exist to receive them. On his method, a benefit in 2150 counts for roughly a fortieth of the same benefit today, and the trust's duties to the poor of, say, 2250 are as good as nil — a consequence he accepts with a shrug.

Two matters trouble me, and I would welcome your thoughts on both.

First, the treasurer's stance seems to conceal a difficulty that neither he nor the spend-now trustee will address squarely. Which young families stay in, move to, or leave the parish over the coming decades will depend in part on what we choose to fund now, so the poor of 2150 will not merely be strangers to us; they will be different people depending on our decision. The spend-now trustee says this clinches his case: the will cannot have meant people who exist only if we are frugal, and we cannot wrong someone by bringing them into existence in the first place. The treasurer calls this sophistry, but offers no argument against it. I cannot work out who is right — or, more importantly, what follows even if the treasurer is. If the far future counts fully, does that commit us to holding spending down now? And if so, how is that not the first step of a policy in which, since there is always a later generation, nobody's sake is ever finally served?

Second, the accountant's method. It presents itself as the sober, book-keeping way of taking a clause about perpetual benefit seriously. Yet if benefits decay at 3 per cent a year regardless of when the fund was established, the word 'in perpetuity' has, on his own figures, effectively expired — and the founder plainly did not mean by it 'for about a hundred and twenty years, give or take'. Is the discounting an honest rendering of the founder's intent, or a quiet repeal of it? If it is a repeal, what should replace it — counting the welfare of the distant poor the same as our own, or something in between — and on what basis could a trustee defend that choice when the accounts are inspected?

I have no stake in any of the three positions beyond wanting to be able to look the founder's memorial bust in the eye. Please set out how you think we ought to reason, and be as hard as you like on the arguments; the parish has waited this long, and it can wait for a considered reply.
