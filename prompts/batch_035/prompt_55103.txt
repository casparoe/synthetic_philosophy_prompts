A chapter of a popular economics book I'm reading summarizes decision theory's trouble with Maurice Allais like this: "The independence axiom is the load-bearing wall of expected utility theory: it says that if you prefer one gamble to another, you should go on preferring it when both are mixed with the same chance of receiving a third, common outcome. In 1952 the French economist Maurice Allais showed that practically everyone violates this. Offered a choice between a sure million and a gamble with a small chance of getting nothing, people grab the sure thing; yet when both options are converted into gambles carrying the same small chance of getting nothing, they flip and prefer the riskier one. Because the independence axiom is what keeps preferences transitive, Allias effectively proved that ordinary people are irrational, and by the 1980s expected utility theory had been abandoned as a description of how people choose. The lesson is that the axiom has never had a real defense — it survives only because economists find it mathematically convenient."

I know enough to suspect this isn't entirely fair, but not enough to say exactly where it goes wrong. Go through it piece by piece: what would a working decision theorist accept, what would they wince at, and what is flatly false? In particular I'd like to know whether the link the chapter draws between the independence axiom and transitivity is right, whether Allias's own take on what his example showed is being misrepresented, and whether expected utility theory really was abandoned. Finally: setting aside the errors, does the chapter's concluding lesson follow from the accurate parts, or does the argument for it depend on the mistakes?
