I'm a retired physicist who's spent the last few years reading philosophy and decision theory for fun, and I keep noticing a pattern in real disputes that I don't have a name for. Two parties are locked in a conflict — two siblings fighting over who inherits a house, two firms in a patent dispute, whatever — and each side is confident it would win if it went to court. Because each side is confident, no ordinary settlement looks good to either of them: if I'm 90% sure I'd win the lawsuit, why would I accept half the house? So it looks like there's no deal both sides prefer to fighting, and the usual "just find the Pareto improvement" advice goes nowhere. And yet there are deals both sides genuinely prefer to fighting, and here's the part I find strange: each side accepts the deal because of its own confidence. My brother is sure he'd win, so he's happy to agree to "whoever the court would rule for gets the house, but we skip the trial and give the other side a token payment instead" — by his lights he's getting the house plus a payment. I'm sure *I'd* win, so by my lights the same deal is great for me. We shake hands for opposite reasons, both believing we just took the other side to the cleaners. You can build variants with a coin weighted by each side's stated confidence, or with cheap arbitration standing in for the court. It works every time there are real costs of fighting (lawyers, time, a burned relationship) that the deal avoids — those saved costs seem to be what "funds" the whole thing, since the loser of the simulated fight gets paid out of them.

So my questions: does this exact configuration have an established name in game theory, economics, or philosophy? It's clearly a cousin of a Pareto improvement, but it can't be one in the ordinary sense — the two sides' beliefs can't both be right, so the "improvement" seems to exist only relative to beliefs that are jointly inconsistent, and I don't know whether the literature treats that as a bug or as the whole point. Is it related to betting and speculation on disagreements, to the Coase theorem, to insurance, to Dutch book arguments? Where are the edges — what happens when one side is bluffing about its confidence, when there are three parties instead of two, when the fight has no costs to save, or when the "trial" whose outcome we're simulating is something as messy as a war?
