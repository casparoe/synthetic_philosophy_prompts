hey so quick backstory: I'm a junior and this year I got voted onto our student council, which mostly means I sit in on the "shared resources committee" (yes our school really calls it that). The situation we have to rule on by next month: last year the school bought 60 graphing calculators that anyone can borrow from the library instead of making kids buy their own, since the math teachers assign homework that needs them and some families can't just drop $100. By december half of them were gone or broken, the ones left are definately grimy, and now the librarian wants to kill the whole program. One math teacher is pushing for a $20 deposit and a sign-out sheet, the vice principle wants a hard quota like "one checkout per student per month," and another student rep says if we just publish a list of the people who never returned them last year (basically shaming) it'll fix itself. The principal asked each of us for a short written opinion she can circulate before the vote, and I got assigned to write mine on the "big picture," because I'm the one who kept bringing up the tragedy of the commons in meetings.

Here's where I need help. I sort of know the basic Hardin story (everyone overgrazes the common pasture because each herder gets the full benefit of one more cow while the cost is spread over everyone), but when I actually try to apply it I get confused about which cases it even fits. Like, is the calculator cart really a commons? There's a fixed number so me taking one means someone else can't, that part seems clear. But people on the committee also keep calling the school wifi and the free tutoring center "the commons," and those feel different — nobody's wifi gets slower because more people are on it (or does it?), and tutoring seems more like there's just enough to go around. So one thing I want in the opinion is a sort-out of which parts of the overgrazing story actually apply to the calculators and where the analogy to wifi and tutoring breaks down, because right now we're using "tragedy of the commons" as a magic phrase for anything shared and I don't think that's right. Also apparently there's a whole literature, I think tied to a political scientist named Elinor Ostrom (Nobel prize?), saying communities sometimes govern shared stuff fine without privatizing it or writing strict rules — which sounds like the shaming-list proposal, but I can't tell if her work actually supports that or if the student rep is misusing it. Oh and at one meeting someone said the deposit idea could backfire into an "anticommons" where nobody can use them at all, which I honestly could not follow.

So the written opinion should do three things: recommend one concrete decision (kill it, deposits, quota, some self-governance thing, or a mix) with reasoning the other members could actually respond to; do that sorting-out of where the commons framework genuinely applies versus where it's being stretched; and treat the strongest version of the losing side fairly — the librarian isn't just grumpy, she watched the program die once already. And since the math teacher has a PhD and loves catching me out on reply-all: please be clear about which parts of this are actually settled among people who study it and which are genuinely contested, instead of presenting one school of thought as the consensus. Write it as continuous prose like an email or a letter, no headings or bullet points or bold anything, since that's the format our written opinions are supposed to follow, and keep it at a level I can defend out loud in a meeting — I'm not trying to sound like a textbook.
