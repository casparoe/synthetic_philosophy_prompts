Two members of my department have argued about the doomsday argument for a decade without either budging: one maintains that reasoning as a random sample from the class of all observers — the self-sampling assumption — gives finding ourselves among the first hundred-odd billion humans genuine evidential weight for an early end to the human story, since a vastly larger future population would have diluted our typical sampling position; the other maintains that the self-indication assumption cancels that update by boosting the prior probability of hypotheses on which more observers exist, while conceding that this move inherits the presumptuous-philosopher problem — apparently committing us to prefer universe-theories with enormously more observers before consulting any ordinary evidence at all. Write the joint statement a well-run adversarial collaboration between these two should produce. Include: the claims both can sign about the shared machinery — reference classes and how to choose among them, observers versus observer-moments, and what Bayes's rule does and does not settle about evidence indexed to one's own existence; the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording of its view, including whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal and, if so, which part and why; the cruxes — I suspect one is how to handle hypotheses that entail zero observers, and another is whether anthropic principles are constraints on rational credence or just bookkeeping devices for choosing reference classes; and, for each crux, a thought experiment or model case both would agree bears on it — the presumptuous philosopher, Bostrom-style Adam and Eve scenarios, or a case they construct themselves — with each side's prediction about the case's upshot recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
