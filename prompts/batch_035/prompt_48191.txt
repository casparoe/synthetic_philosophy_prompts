I run a sci-fi book club that's mostly philosophy grad students plus a couple of spouses who are very patient with us, and next month is Ender's Game. Everyone always wants to do the just-war/military-ethics angle on it, and I'm bored of that, so I want to take it in a different direction this time: the philosophy of mind material about group minds and levels of description. The pairing I have in mind is Hofstadter's dialogue with the conscious ant colony, Aunt Hillary — the one where the colony is a genuine person with beliefs and a personality, no individual ant is conscious in any interesting sense, and her best friend is the anteater who eats a few of her ants every visit, which is supposed to be fine, more like a blood draw than a murder, since the mind lives at the colony level and not the ant level. The core thought: whether you count one mind or trillions of organisms depends entirely on which level of description you're at, and moral status has to follow the minds, not the bodies.

It seems to me Ender's Game is secretly the same thought experiment, but weaponized. The formics have queens who are (as far as the book ever suggests) the only real minds, with worker drones that are basically limbs — so the species is a small population of persons with an astronomical number of bodies, not a population of trillions of persons. Which raises the question I actually want the club to chew on: when Ender wipes out the queens and the homeworld, has he committed xenocide of trillions, the way the novel's own vocabulary ("xenocide") keeps insisting, or a much smaller massacre of hive-minds, each of which dies once and then loses its enormous body? Is the book's moral arithmetic — the crushing weight of Ender's guilt, which the whole ending is built on — actually consistent with the biology Card invented, or is that guilt only intelligible if you count bodies instead of hives? And there's a mirror image that I suspect is the real philosophical core but can't quite articulate: the formics apparently never grasped that individual humans were each whole minds rather than drones — from their side, killing a few humans was like clipping someone's nails, no real loss — and by the time they understood what they'd done it was too late. Both species, in other words, may have been fighting at the wrong level of description, and it cost all of them everything. I half-remember the details of how the formics finally catch on, so don't lean too hard on my memory there, treat it as a question for the reading rather than a settled fact.

What I'd like from you: an actual reading of the novel through this lens, anchored in specific scenes and episodes I can point the group to — the final "simulation" where Ender burns the homeworld, the queen reaching him at the end, the egg he finds and promises to carry — with an honest account of where the novel resists the Aunt Hillary framing (Ender's empathy with the queen, and the way she's presented as a mother of her people, seem to cut both ways: does that support one-person-per-hive or complicate it?). Also tell me whether the novel ends up committed to a view, or whether it wants the guilt of trillions of deaths and the biology of a few thousand minds at the same time and never notices the tension. Since nobody will have the text open, work from plot and paraphrase rather than quotes. Audience is mixed, so gloss any jargon (levels of description, supervenience, etc.) in plain language. And end by telling me what the novel dramatizes about group minds that the philosophy alone doesn't, and whether it's worth assigning or whether I'm forcing the reading.
