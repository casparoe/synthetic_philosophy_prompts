I am a retired physicist who has spent the last two years working through philosophy of mind and personal identity, and I have become captivated by Derek Parfit's teletransporter cases in Reasons and Persons — the device on Earth that scans, destroys, and replicates you on Mars, and Parfit's unsettling conclusion that personal identity is simply not what matters. I recently rewatched Christopher Nolan's film The Prestige, and it struck me as an unusually rich dramatization of exactly this problem. In case some details need refreshing: the magician Angier obtains a machine built by Tesla which, when activated, does not teleport him but produces a duplicate some distance away while the original falls through a trapdoor into a water tank below the stage and drowns. Angier performs this "trick" night after night, and near the end of the film he remarks that it took courage to climb into the machine each evening, never knowing whether he would be the man who drowned or the man who took the bow. The famous earlier scene with the duplicated top hats, and the many tanks in the basement, are worth bringing in as well.

I would like a careful critical reading of the film through Parfit's discussion of what matters in survival. Some questions to wrestle with: Does Angier's nightly dread make sense on a Parfitian view, where psychological continuity and connectedness are preserved no matter which "copy" he ends up as, such that nothing of importance is lost in the tank? If the dread is nonetheless intelligible — and the film clearly wants us to feel its force — does that undercut Parfit, or does it merely reveal an ineliminable bias toward the future self one happens to be, which Parfit himself allows for in his discussion of self-interest and what he calls the asymmetry between our attitudes to our own continued existence? Note too that the film differs from the classic teletransporter in one respect worth taking seriously: the duplicate is created simultaneously with, and a short distance from, the drowning original, which makes this closer to Parfit's "branching" cases where identity plainly fails even if what matters might survive. Does the film exploit that closeness to make the drowning seem like murder when the pure teleporter version might not?

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who knows the personal identity literature thoroughly and also takes cinema seriously as a philosophical medium — do not reduce the film to an illustrated thought experiment, and do not bend Parfit to fit the film. I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming section laying out the possible readings before committing to one, then give the substantive analysis, and I would appreciate honesty about where the film resists the Parfitian reading (the grief, the cruelty, and the framing of the tanks all seem to push the other way). For my notes, please end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence stating whether the film ultimately vindicates Parfit's claim that identity is not what matters, undermines it, or leaves the question deliberately suspended.
