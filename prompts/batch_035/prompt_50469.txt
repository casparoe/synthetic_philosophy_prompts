For my extended essay in the IB Diploma Programme, I am writing about the chapter of Douglas Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* titled "The Location of Meaning." I drafted an argument for my analysis section that seemed solid to me at the time, but my supervisor wrote in the margin that it proves far too much, and I cannot see where it breaks.

The background: Hofstadter splits any message into three layers. The frame message is the announcement, in effect, that these marks are a decodable message at all. The outer message is the implicit information about *how* to decode — the recognition that these strokes are Chinese characters, or that this groove pattern encodes music. The inner message is the content that survives decoding. His conclusion is that meaning is not located in the object: a recording of a Bach piece drifting on a spaceship is not intrinsically music; it becomes music only when some intelligence with the right decoding machinery encounters it.

Here is my argument, numbered:

(1) Meaning is not intrinsic to a message; it exists only relative to a decoding mechanism.

(2) A decoding mechanism must grasp the outer message — how to decode — before it can extract the inner message.

(3) But the outer message is itself a message, and by (1) no message carries meaning intrinsically. So it, too, must be understood by something that already understands it.

(4) Whatever understands that outer message must, by the same reasoning, have acquired that understanding from a prior outer message, and so on. So understanding is either grounded in something understood without any decoding, or it never gets started.

(5) If some understanding is decoding-free, then decoding is not necessary for meaning after all, and (1) is false: things can mean intrinsically.

(6) If instead all understanding rests on prior decoding, the chain never begins, and no one has ever understood anything.

(7) Therefore either (1) is false or no one has ever understood anything. Either way, the framework destroys itself within a page, which is obviously not what Hofstadter intended.

I refuse to accept that nobody has ever understood anything, so (6) is out. But I would much rather keep (1), because if I give it up, then it looks as though any object could mean anything — that with a sufficiently weird mapping, the arrangement of atoms in a rock could be read as a novel, which is exactly the absurdity Hofstadter's framework was built to avoid. Please tell me which step I should reject, why that step seemed so natural when I wrote it, and what rejecting it costs me for the rest of the essay. And if you think the argument is actually valid rather than broken somewhere, say which numbered claim simply has to go, and what I pay for giving it up.
