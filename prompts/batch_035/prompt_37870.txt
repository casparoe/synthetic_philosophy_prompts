I've been turning over an argument for mereological nihilism and I can't decide whether it's sound or whether it cheats somewhere in the middle. Here is the argument as I have it:

(1) Take any collection of things that might compose a further object — say, the quarks bound together in a proton. Either they compose a further object or they don't.

(2) If composition ever occurs, then it is sometimes indeterminate whether it occurs. For we can construct a sorites-style sequence of cases running from clear composition (the quarks tightly bound in a proton) to clear non-composition (two quarks at opposite ends of the galaxy), varying the arrangement by tiny steps, with no sharp cutoff anywhere in the sequence.

(3) If it is indeterminate whether some things compose a further object, then it is indeterminate how many concrete objects exist: there is one more object on the "composition occurs" branch than on the other.

(4) But how many concrete objects exist cannot be indeterminate. This is not like asking how many grains make a heap, where the fuzziness is plainly a feature of our words. Objects are individuated by the world, not by us, and the world cannot have a fuzzy census.

(5) Therefore composition never occurs: there are no composite objects, only simples arranged proton-wise, arranged table-wise, arranged person-wise.

I confess I find the conclusion's audacity appealing, but I've noticed something that worries me. Exactly parallel reasoning seems to apply to the simples themselves:

(6) Every fundamental particle has a vague spatial location — an electron's position is a spread-out wavefunction, and for many regions it is indeterminate whether the electron is in that region.

(7) If it is indeterminate whether a region contains an electron, it is indeterminate whether there is an object located there, and hence indeterminate how many electrons exist.

(8) But by (4), indeterminacy in the census of concrete objects is impossible. So there are no electrons either.

The nihilist was supposed to leave us the simples; the argument seems to have taken those too, which suggests to me that something has gone wrong earlier in the chain.

A friend who holds the nihilist line responds that the two cases are disanalogous: electron vagueness is vagueness in where an object is, not in whether there is an object, and only the latter kind of indeterminacy is banned by (4). But I don't see why that distinction does any work. If it is indeterminate whether the region contains the electron, it is indeterminate whether an object exists there, and that appears to be precisely the form of indeterminacy (4) was invoked to rule out.

So here is my question. Which step of the original argument is weakest, and does the generalization to simples actually go through, or does the friend's location/existence distinction somehow block it? I am inclined to keep (4) — I share the intuition that the world cannot have a fuzzy census — and to attack (2) or (3) instead. But if I do that, I owe an account of where the equivocation lies, and I cannot find it: "indeterminate whether composition occurs" and "indeterminate how many objects there are" strike me as two descriptions of one and the same situation. Tell me which premise to reject, why that premise looked fine in the first place, and what the standard ways of rejecting it each end up paying for.
