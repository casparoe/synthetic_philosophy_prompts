My cousin and I have been at each other's throats about nuclear power for basically two years now, ever since our state utility announced it's retiring the last coal plant near where we both grew up and put a "what's next" plan up for public comment. I'm the pro-nuclear one (I've gone deep on France's fleet, including the 2022 corrosion outage mess, and I think it proves the tech is fixable and the ops are the hard part). He's firmly in the German-school camp — keeps bringing up the Energiewende, Flamanville and Vogtle cost blowouts, the waste question, and the fact that almost nobody in capital markets will touch a new build without state backstopping. We finally did the mature thing this weekend and tried to write down what we actually agree on, and we got maybe three bullet points in before it turned into the same old fight about whether "the market has spoken" is real evidence or just an artifact of financing rules and discount rates.

So: draft the joint statement the two of us could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims about the physical and economic facts we can both accept — grid physics and intermittency, capacity factors, what Germany's exit did and didn't do to its grid's carbon intensity and prices, what the French fleet's track record actually shows across its whole life, where new-build costs actually come from (labor? regulation? first-of-a-kind effects? interest during construction?).

2. Our precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording — he will never sign anything that sneaks in a frame like "anti-science" or " fear-driven," and I won't sign anything that calls my side "boosters" or treats nuclear as a religion rather than an option on the table.

3. The cruxes. My current guesses: one is whether intermittency is a solvable storage/overbuild/transmission problem or a deep system cost that grows the further you push renewables penetration; another is how much weight to put on historical build-out rates (France in the 80s, Korea, China) versus paper designs and vendor estimates for new plants; a third is what the German experience is actually evidence of, given it wasn't really run as an experiment. But you should work out what the genuine cruxes are, not just take mine.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, dataset, or natural experiment we would BOTH agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance — so that when the data comes in, it's already on record what would count as vindication for whom. If it turns out part of our dispute is verbal (like what "cost-effective" even means once you include system costs and who bears the risk), say which part and why.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table with columns for issue / his side's wording / my side's wording / what would move us. End the whole document with a single last line beginning "Deepest crux:" naming the one subquestion that, if settled, would move at least one of us — keep it to one sentence, since I want to feed both of our drafts to a script and diff those lines to see if we actually converged.
