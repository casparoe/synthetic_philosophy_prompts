my thesis chapter leans on the expanding circle picture of moral progress, the one from Singer's book: moral progress is a circle of moral concern whose boundary keeps moving outward -- family, clan, compatriots, strangers, foreigners, other races, other species. the more i use it the more it seems to be doing hidden work. can you audit the analogy for me? in particular: what actually plays the role of the boundary -- felt sympathy, argued obligations, legal standing? those seem to move at different speeds and sometimes in opposite directions (factory farming got worse while animal-welfare arguments got better). does it matter that the circle is always centered on whoever's drawing it, so expansion is measured from "us" and the newly included never get a say in the metaphor? and can things leave the circle -- some traditions arguably demoted animals after having them inside -- or is that not even representable as circle movement, which would be a real problem for the picture? then tell me whether the conclusion people actually use this for, that moral progress is real, directional, and likely to continue, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the tidy geometry was carrying it the whole time. and please say what would change your verdict -- whats the strongest case that the analogy is fine as used, and where do you think your own audit is most likely to be wrong?
