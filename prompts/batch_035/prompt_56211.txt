I head the product design review board at a mid-sized consumer fintech company — we build a savings and budgeting app with a few million users, and every week we sign off on new interface features: onboarding flows, notification campaigns, cancellation paths, promotional timers. Our compliance team checks whether features are legal; nobody checks whether they are manipulative, and after a long and rather testy dinner argument with a friend of mine who did graduate work in philosophy, I've become convinced that "not illegal" is not the same as "not manipulative" and that my board needs an actual test, not vibes. The argument ran on her contention that the mark of manipulation is influence that works by bypassing a person's rational capacities rather than engaging them — you don't give someone worse reasons, you arrange things so their reasons never get a hearing. I resisted, partly on her behalf of my own product: it seemed to me that if we never lie, never fabricate urgency, and give users every option in writing, nothing we do could count as manipulation.

The features we fought about most were these: an auto-save enrollment screen where the opt-in is pre-selected; a cancellation flow whose decline button reads something like "No thanks, I'd rather keep losing money to overdraft fees"; streak-based notifications that tell users they're "about to break their 34-day streak" and frame turning off notifications as losing something; and limited-time bonus offers with countdown timers that, in at least one version we shipped, reset when the user reopened the screen. She was willing to bite a bullet I didn't expect: she said the pre-selected default is probably the *least* manipulative of these, since defaults work through the same laziness whatever they're set to, and we use them paternalistically — for the user's own good — whereas the streak notifications weaponize loss aversion in a way that engages no capacity I'd want engaged. I think intent has to matter, she thinks she doesn't need intent at all as long as the influence operates the wrong way, and we deadlocked on whether truthful information, presented selectively, can manipulate. What I took away is that I don't trust my own judgment to be consistent feature-by-feature, and neither should the board.

So I'd like you to write a decision procedure my design review board can actually run — a checklist with questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clean "yes, manipulative, redesign" or "no, fine to ship," and crucially where it stops and flags the thing as a judgment call for a human, because I suspect the intent-versus-mechanism dispute she and I couldn't settle is exactly the sort the procedure can't settle either. Ground it in whatever is actually defensible in the philosophy here: the bypass-versus-engage idea, the difference between persuasion, coercion, and manipulation, the question of whether manipulation requires deception, and whether paternalistic manipulation for the user's own benefit should get different treatment from self-serving manipulation. Then run the procedure on the four features above, including at least one where it comes back with an answer I won't like — I'd particularly like to know whether the streak notifications really are worse than the default, or whether she was just being contrarian. Constrain it to something a non-philosopher reviewer can apply in about thirty minutes per feature, and be explicit about the cases where the procedure will misfire or where two honest reviewers applying it will diverge.
