So for the past year and a half I've been in a reading group on vagueness that has completely stalled, and I'm trying to figure out whether the stall itself is telling us something. The setup: three of us came in as epistemicists (there's a sharp cutoff in the extension of 'heap', we just can't know where it is), two are supervaluationists (no fact of the matter about any single borderline case, but the sentence 'there is a cutoff' still comes out true because it's true on every precisification), and one person holds a degree-based view where borderline sentences take intermediate truth values. The weird part is that we all agree on the actual data. None of us can point to a specific number of grains where 'heap' flips. When one of us ran a forced-march variant on us at a session — judging each case one at a time in sequence, so you can't just say 'it's vague' forever — everyone did draw a line under the pressure, but never the same line twice, and nobody's line was anywhere they'd have predicted. And we all hit the same wall at higher-order vagueness: the boundary of the boundary is vague too, and every side's story about that looks ad hoc when we scrutinize it, including our own. Nobody has budged an inch in eighteen months. It's not that we ran out of things to say, it's that we ran out of ways to say them that the others would even accept as being about the same thing.

My working hypothesis, and please treat it as a hypothesis that could be wrong: the disagreement isn't really about English. It's about what a semantic theory is for. The epistemicists in the group talk as though semantics is descriptive linguistics — go find the facts about the language, including the inaccessible ones — while the supervaluationists talk as though it's closer to a rational reconstruction of how a community ought to manage words that aren't fully determined. If that's right, then a lot of the standard arguement over whether vagueness is a form of ignorance, which we've basically re-enacted in miniature from the published literature, is two projects sharing a vocabulary.

So here's what I want from you: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two research programs answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually says — pay special attention to how the camps treat the forced-march data and penumbral connections (like the fact that no single case is both borderline and not borderline, or that if something is a borderline heap then nearby cases are too), because that's where our group fights hardest and where I suspect the real fault line is. Then tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, experimental work on how competent speakers actually draw lines, a formal result about which treatments of higher-order vagueness are even coherent, or should we just expect a permanent pluralism, and why. And if your diagnosis is that one of the three positions secretly rests on an empirical assumption about speaker behavior — say that outright, because at least that would give us something to go test.
