You are an epistemologist who works on testimony and the philosophy of evidence and who has a side practice helping non-academic institutions translate philosophical arguments into working procedures. I need exactly that from you: a decision procedure, not an essay.

I am the standards editor at a mid-sized general-interest magazine that publishes first-person essays. Roughly twice a month we receive a submission that turns, in whole or in part, on a claim of something miraculous or extraordinary: a terminal diagnosis that vanished, a dream that anticipated a death, a weeping statue, a voice heard simultaneously by a whole family. Our current practice is ad hoc and, frankly, embarrassed — fact-checkers verify what can be verified (documents, dates, corroborating witnesses) and then we improvise. What I need is a procedure the desk can actually run in about thirty minutes per submission, usable by fact-checkers with no philosophy training, that yields one of three outcomes — run as written, run with a specific framing or caveat, or decline — and that leaves documented reasoning for the file.

The philosophical anchor should be Hume's argument on miracles, treated as a serious starting point rather than a slogan. As I understand it, the core of the argument is that testimony is only as strong as the balance between the evidence for the witness's reliability and the intrinsic improbability of the event reported, so that for a putative violation of a law of nature the witness's being mistaken or deceptive would itself have to be more improbable than the event. Our desk has informally been applying what we call the denominator rule: you cannot say how strong a piece of testimony is without knowing the rate at which similar testimony turns out to be false, and for miracle claims we almost never know that denominator. Tell me whether to keep this rule, fix it, or drop it.

There is also a second-order problem I want the procedure to confront rather than dodge. Our decision is not a belief; it is an act of publication, and publishing testimony makes us a link in a chain of testimony. Hume's question is what a rational hearer should believe given testimony; ours is what we should help a heterogeneous readership believe, many of whose priors — shaped by religious commitment or its absence — are unlike ours. The procedure has to say explicitly whether to apply our own priors, try to estimate the readers', or refuse the question, and that choice should be a step in the procedure, not an afterthought.

Before drafting, brainstorm: map which parts of Hume's argument genuinely transfer to an editorial decision (witness reliability, the antecedent improbability of the event, the balancing structure itself), which transfer only with modification (Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature; a spontaneous remission is not that — it is rare but lawful — and the procedure must draw and use this distinction), and which do not transfer at all. Then write the procedure: an ordered set of at most eight steps, each specifying what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a decline, where it mandates reframing rather than declining, and which questions are questions of values that must be handed up to an editor instead of settled by the checklist. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Then run the procedure, showing its work, on these three submissions:

1. An essayist reports that her mother's inoperable pancreatic cancer vanished between scans after a pilgrimage. We have the before-and-after imaging and the oncologist's confirmation of remission, though the oncologist calls it a documented if unexplained spontaneous regression. The essayist frames it as a miracle; the mother does not.

2. A weeping statue in a small town: roughly forty witnesses, a blurry video, a diocese that declined to investigate, and a chemist acquaintance of the essayist saying condensation is consistent with the footage. The submission is really about what the town's belief did to the town, and the statue is almost incidental to it.

3. A reader claims to have dreamed a plane crash in detail three days before it happened. Her sister confirms the reader described the dream to her the morning after, the crash occurred, and the dream matched in one specific detail that was not publicly reported until a week later.

Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I will not like — I want its independence demonstrated, so say plainly which answer that is and why it follows anyway. Add a closing section on how the procedure misfires: the recurring situations in which it gives the wrong answer or no answer, with an example of each. Please give me no reading list and no citations; everything needed is in the argument itself. And if you must simplify, preserve above all the structure that makes Hume's point something better than a blanket prejudice against the miraculous — testimony really can, in principle, be good enough, and the procedure should say what that would take.
