I've been down a bit of a Nuremberg rabbit hole lately (a documentary, then a couple of podcast series) and there's something I can't shake about the whole setup. The London Charter was written by the four victors in mid-1945, only months before the trial, and it invented charges like crimes against peace — planning and waging aggressive war — even though no statute anywhere had ever said that a head of state commits a crime by starting a war. The defense lawyers moved to dismiss precisely on retroactivity grounds, and the tribunal said no: the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928 had already outlawed war, so punishing it wasn't retroactive. The tribunal also threw out the act-of-state defense (you can't hide behind "my state ordered it"), allowed superior orders only as mitigation rather than an excuse, and applied crimes against humanity only to atrocities connected to the war — the German state's persecution of its own Jewish citizens in the 1930s, before the war began, basically couldn't be charged as such. And of course the whole thing ran one direction: nobody prosecuted the area bombing of German and Japanese cities, and at Tokyo the Indian judge Pal dissented on basically these grounds — that it was the winners judging the losers with the rules made up after the fact.

But here's the thing that gets me: the very same institution was obsessively positivist in how it ran itself. Detailed indictments, defense counsel with real rights of cross-examination, evidence rules, careful judgments going count by count. So what view of law does this practice actually presuppose? Some natural-law view where certain acts are so self-evidently criminal that prior enactment isn't needed to punish them? A softer middle view where custom crystallizes into law before anyone writes it down and the Charter was just declaring what already existed? Or could even a strict legal positivist make the whole thing kosher by saying the Charter itself WAS the law, applied prospectively-ish to conduct after August 1945? My friend says the tribunal presupposes nothing but power — victors' justice all the way down — and I half agree, but it can't be that simple given how much effort the tribunal spent on the legality argument and the procedure. So what I'd want from you: reconstruct the implicit theory of law the practice needs to be defensible; then distinguish what it strictly rules out (does it rule out positivism outright, or just a crude might-makes-right view?) from what it's merely compatible with; say whether the asymmetry — no Allied defendants ever — is itself a philosophical commitment of the practice or just a political fact layered on top of it; and tell me whether the parts are even consistent with each other, because demanding written charges for the defendents while running your own substantive law off an unwritten norm feels tension-y in a way I can't quite articulate. And then the flip side: what would have had to change about how those trials were conducted if the Allies had instead committed to a strictly positivist view of legality — just walk away? get treaties in place and accept no trials for WWII at all? something in between? I'm after the philosophy more than the history, though obviously don't mangle the history.
