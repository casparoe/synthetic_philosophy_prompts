My housemate has gone full selfish-gene mode and keeps trotting out an argument he thinks settles the whole units-of-selection debate, and the annoying part is i can't find where it goes wrong even though im pretty sure something does. It goes like this. (1) For something to be a unit of selection, selection has to discriminate between rival versions of it — lineages of the thing have to out-proliferate other lineages. (2) Whenever selection does anything at all, whether we'd normally describe it as acting on organisms, groups, whatever, the result is that some alleles went up and others went down, and this genic ledger is complete: nothing about the evolutionary change is left undescribed by it. (3) A complete description at the genic level leaves nothing for higher levels to do, so "selection at the level of the organism" and "group selection" are just summaries of lots of little genic selections. (4) Therefore the gene is the only genuine unit of selection, and talk of a trait evolving "for the good of the group" is always a mistake — whats really going on is just alleles for the trait beating alleles against it. (5) So group selection, where it seems to happen, is really genes doing their thing with extra steps.

Here's what's eating me though. You can run the same trick one level up: whenever allele frequencies change, it's equally true that organisms carrying allele A out-reproduced organisms carrying allele a, for every allele, and this organismic description is just as complete as the genic one. If completeness of the description at level X makes X the real unit of selection, my housemate has proven that organisms are the only real unit just as soundly as that genes are — the argument proves too much, in both directions, which makes me suspect some premise is quietly doing two different jobs (something about "complete description" sliding into "does the causal work", maybe?). But when I try to spell that out I get tangeled, and there's a residual worry: maybe the gene really is special because gene lineages are literal copies whereas organisms aren't copies of anything, so maybe the genic description IS privileged in a way the organismic one isn't. For what it's worth, I want to keep premise (2) — I genuinely beleive the genic ledger is complete — and I'd happily lose (3) if I understood exactly what rejecting it costs, since it feels like rejecting it just lets my housemate come back with "fine, maybe description doesn't privilege the gene, but causation still happens there".
