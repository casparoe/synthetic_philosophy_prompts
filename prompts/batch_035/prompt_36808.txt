been retired from physiology for a few years now and finally catching up on philosophy of biology, and something is bugging me. in the lab we always said the function of the kidney is to filter blood, the function of the heart is to pump, etc — meaning the job the organ does in keeping the organism running, with zero interest in its evolutionary past. now my philosophy reading group keeps pushing the selected-effect view, and the analogy they lean on is the artifact one: the heart's function is to pump blood for the same sort of reason a carburetor's function is to mix fuel — its function is why it's there, what it was put in / kept around for, and natural selection is what plays designer even with no designer around. audit this artifact analogy for me. what maps onto what (designer, intention, blueprint, malfunction, the user who repurposes a screwdriver as a paperweight)? where does it break — organisms have parts that drifted into new uses (feathers that started as insulation and only later got coopted for flight), vestigial parts, and crucially no one who *meant* anything, which the artifact side quietly imports. and then tell me whether the historical conclusion survives: on this picture, a structure that pumps blood perfectly but arose by one freak mutation a generation ago, with no selection history yet, would have *no* function at all. that seems like a reductio to me, but maybe my physiologist's usage is just tracking something else entirely. if the analogy can't carry it, whats a better one?
