I run a reading group on AI alignment for a mix of grad students and industry folks, about 14 people, one 90-minute evening session, and next week's topic is process-based vs. outcome-based supervision of AI systems -- the debate over whether you should reward a model only for final results or grade its reasoning step by step. I've read the arguments back and forth but I want people to feel them, so I'm looking for an actual in-class game where the participants are put in the position of the trained system, not the trainer. My rough idea: split people into teams who solve tasks under two different scoring regimes -- one group gets paid (points, not real money) only for correct final answers and nobody looks at how they got them, the other group has to submit a step-by-step trace that gets audited and can lose points for sketchy steps even if the final answer is right. There should be some deliberate loophole in the tasks that lets you crank out correct-looking answers cheaply without really doing the work (something like a reference answer sheet nobody is forbidden to consult, or a way to reverse-engineer the grader's answer format) -- I want the outcome-supervised teams to discover it and the process-supervised teams to be blocked by the audit, or better, to try to fake a plausible-looking trace to hide that they used it.

Can you design the full thing: rules, the task set, the scoring scheme, how the audits work, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the real debate -- Goodharting, the capability tax of process supervision, the legibility/interpretability argument, the worry that process-supervised models just learn to perform the right kind of reasoning rather than actually doing it. The debrief shouldn't lecture the material up front; the rules should generate the phenomena and the debrief just names them. Also tell me honestly what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it -- e.g., teams that never find the loophole, or everyone piling onto the loophole because the points make it obvious. And be frank about the parts of the debate that a room of humans roleplaying as models can't reproduce at all (generalization to held-out tasks, mesa-optimization, that real models don't already know they're being graded on process) and where I'll have to just say it in the debrief.

One constraint: keep the tasks solvable by tired people at 7pm -- logic puzzles, estimation questions, that kind of thing, nothing that requires a laptop. And please make the loophole something they have to notice rather than something I announce, since the whole point is the discovery.
