AI safety practice at the frontier labs, as best I can tell from the outside, rests on two prongs. The first is behavioral evaluation: before deployment, a model is shown a large battery of adversarial and capability-related prompts — requests for uplift assistance, sycophancy probes, cases designed to elicit sleeper-agent-style behavior that looks benign in training and pursues a hidden objective in deployment — and if the answers look fine, the model ships. The second is mechanistic interpretability: researchers extract internal activations, find directions or sparse-autoencoder features that light up on particular inputs, and validate a feature by showing humans a list of examples that trigger it, where success just is the humans recognizing a concept they already had — a feature counts as "understood" when it corresponds to something for which we already have a word. Both prongs, notice, reward the same thing: agreement with our existing ontology. Behavioral evals can only probe for failures we already know how to describe; interpretability can only certify structure that maps onto our existing vocabulary.

Here is what bothers me. It seems entirely possible that a model trained on human-generated data has rich internal structure about the world that never surfaces as human-legible behavior — that it knows things it never has occasion to say, and makes distinctions we don't make. If the model's internal ontology is even partly incommensurable with ours — if it tracks things we lack concepts for, or fails to carve the world where we carve it — then the behavioral prong is querying the model only along dimensions where we already know what to ask, and the interpretability prong is only ever finding the projection of the model's representations onto concepts we brought to the table ourselves. Nothing about either practice would detect the residue.

So my question: what view of minds, or of knowledge, does this two-pronged evaluation regime actually presuppose? A translation view, on which model concepts are just human concepts under some permutation, so an ontology-matching map always exists and the only problem is computing it? An externalist or behaviorist view, on which it simply doesn't matter what's inside so long as the input-output function is right, so internal inaccessibility is a non-issue? Or a training-pressure view, on which gradient descent against human-labeled data forcibly imposes human-ontology structure on the model, so the incommensurable residue is small in some specifiable way? Reconstruct whichever theory the practice needs, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is underdetermined — several different views might license it as things stand. I'd also like you to address the tension I think I see between the prongs: the behavioral prong is comfortable with something like behaviorism, while the interpretability prong seems to presuppose the negation — that inner structure matters and is at least partially legible to us. Can one institution coherently rely on both? And finally: suppose we took the possibility of genuinely inaccessible information seriously rather than as a philosophical party trick. What would deployment-gating evaluation have to look like instead — or is there no coherent alternative, on the ground that "everything we can measure looks fine but something important is going on" is a claim that no evidence could ever support or undermine, and hence one a responsible lab is entitled to ignore?
