For a few years now I've been arguing with friends about whether the brain is a computer, and I've noticed we never get anywhere because we mean different things by the claim. One of us means the brain literally runs programs the way software runs on hardware; one means only that it processes information somehow; and one treats computation as a metaphor that can be dropped whenever it becomes awkward. I'd like to find out what I actually think by answering questions I could answer before knowing any of the positions' names.

Please build a decision tree for this. The root should be a commitment almost everyone accepts, and each branch node a question about a concrete commitment rather than a label -- for instance, whether a suitably described wall or the solar system computes anything at all (that is, whether computation is in the eye of the beholder), whether a perfect computer simulation of a storm would make anything wet, whether a system could think without representing anything as being any way, and whether the body and the environment are parts of the thinking machine or merely the inputs and outputs to it. The leaves should carry the names these positions actually go by in the literature -- at minimum the classical view that thought is symbol manipulation over internal representations, the connectionist view, embodied or enactive cognition, and the outside-view that ascribes computation to anything from the standpoint of an observer -- plus any others you think belong there. Give each leaf one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, and for any combination of answers that no one seems to defend, note it and say whether you think that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the difference, and if a node could only be answered by someone who already knows the jargon, replace it with a concrete case.
