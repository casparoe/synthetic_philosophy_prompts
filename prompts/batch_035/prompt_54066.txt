Two of my colleagues in decision theory got into it at a workshop coffee break and asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't — I teach intro ethics, this is above my pay grade. The topic was the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads, and if heads first lands on toss n, you get 2^n dollars. The exchange went like this. (1) Marcus said the expected value is infinite — each term is 2^n times 1/2^n, which is 1, summed forever — so any finite ticket price is a bargain and refusing one is flat-out irrational. (2) Dana said no casino has infinite money. Cap the payoff at the house's bankroll and the expectation collapses: against a house that can pay at most 2^30 dollars, the fair price is about $30, so declining a $100 ticket isn't a paradox, it's just correct. (3) Marcus granted the cap but said Dana had discovered a price rather than dissolved anything: as the bankroll grows the fair price grows like its logarithm, without bound, so all Dana has shown is that a rational agent keeps upgrading what it will pay as houses get richer — and that the intuition Dana was leaning on now hangs entirely on facts about solvency, which is a weird place for a decision-theoretic principle to live, especially since tiny probabilities of astronomical payouts are now doing all the work. (4) Dana retreated to Bernoulli: with diminishing marginal utility (log utility, say) the game's value is a couple of dollars, bankroll aside. (5) Marcus brought up Menger's old point that any unbounded utility function generates its own St. Petersburg — just make the payoffs climb fast enough in utility terms — so Bernoulli only buys finiteness by begging the question. (6) Dana's last stand was that an option with infinite expectation may simply be non-comparable with the alternatives, and walking away from a non-comparable bet is permitted. Marcus answered that this permits walking away from anything with infinite expected value, and maybe that's a reductio — or maybe it's fine, he admitted he wasn't sure.
