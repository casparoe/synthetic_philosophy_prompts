"The lottery paradox, which Kyburg introduced, shows that if you accept that a sufficiently high probability suffices for rational belief, then a ticket holder in a million-ticket lottery is rationally required to believe his ticket will lose. Running the same reasoning for every ticket, he is rationally required to believe that all tickets will lose, while also rationally believing that some ticket will win. So rationality demands outright inconsistent beliefs, and the only way out is to deny that justified belief is closed under conjunction. Makinson's preface paradox is just the same paradox in a different costume: an author believes each claim in his book on independent grounds, and separately believes at least one of them is false, so his rational beliefs contradict each other too. Since the two cases are structurally identical, whatever dissolves one dissolves the other, and since no solution to the lottery paradox has ever commanded consensus, we should just accept the conclusion: rational belief isn't closed under conjunction, and perfectly rational people can knowingly hold beliefs they know to be jointly inconsistent."
