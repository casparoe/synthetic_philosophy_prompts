I'm a novelist, four years into a book about a wounded republic sliding into dictatorship, and the same question keeps ambushing the narrative one: is the man driving events or riding them? My editor wants the demagogue to be the engine of the plot; my instincts say the movement would have found another face. To think this through properly I've gone back to Tolstoy, who is the great novelist-historian on exactly this problem, and I'd like a reading of War and Peace through the Great Man debate.

Specifically: read Tolstoy's portraits of Kutuzov and Napoleon — the council at Fili, the abandonment of Moscow, Napoleon wandering Borodino convinced he is directing a battle Tolstoy insists no one directs — together with the essayistic passages, especially the second epilogue, where Tolstoy argues that leaders are pushed along by forces made of countless small wills, and that taking the leader as a cause is like taking the hilltop as the cause of the valley. Treat this as a philosophical position, not just an authorial tic. Then put it to work on the historian's counterfactual that my novel keeps circling: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? Where does Tolstoy's picture genuinely illuminate that question — the structural argument that Weimar's collapse was overdetermined, that the anti-Semitic mass movement, the veterans' networks, the economic catastrophe, and the elite scheming were all in place — and where does it fail? I suspect Tolstoy's view leans on his peculiar fatalism and his conviction that consciousness makes history look willed when it is really a resultant of infinitesimal actions, and I'd like to know whether the anti-Great-Man conclusion survives if you strip that metaphysics away. And tell me what the novel dramatizes that structural historiography cannot: does showing Kutuzov's wise passivity from inside — asleep at the council, letting Moscow go — give me something a historian's account of overdetermination doesn't?

Finally, since I'm going to steal whatever you give me: can a novel actually be structurally anti-Great-Man while narratively committed to individuals, or does prose fiction have a built-in bias toward the great-man form that Tolstoy was fighting?

One request on how you frame the answer: please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. I gather the intentionalist-versus-functionalist debate over the Holocaust, the question of how overdetermined Weimar's collapse really was, and even whether counterfactual history can yield knowledge at all are live disputes, not settled matters — flag them as such rather than presenting one side as the consensus, and tell me where your own reading of Tolstoy is a defensible interpretation rather than the standard one.
