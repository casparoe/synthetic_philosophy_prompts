I'm TA-ing a seminar called minds and machines, mostly second-years, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We just covered Dennett's four-creature taxonomy from the tower of generate-and-test stuff (Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian) and honestly the discussion was dead on arrival, everyone just nodded along like it was obvious that a creature that can test hypotheses in its head does better than one that has to act and find out. I want them to actually FEEL each rung of the ladder, and feel it as a difference in how much dying has to happen before the right answer is found, if that makes sense.

So design me an in-class activity/game. My vague idea: the same little game played four times under four different rule sets, where the rules force the students to be each kind of creature — round one they're stuck with a hardwired strategy they're not allowed to change and improvement only happens when bad strategies get culled and replaced by copies of winners (population learning, not individual learning), round two they can only learn by trial-and-error reinforcement from their own payoff history with no lookahead allowed, round three they get to run hypotheses offline before acting, round four they get some kind of cultural mind-tool like notes left by previous players. But I'm open to a totally different mechanic if you have something better — a foraging game, a maze, a card game, whatever works with stuff I can print out and run with no app and no real money (candy or points is fine).

Please give me: full rules for each round, what materials to prep, a scoring/payoff scheme where it actually pays to climb the tower, and enough environmental structure that a dumb hardwired strategy doesn't accidentally win by luck. Then the debrief — map each mechanic onto the part of Dennett's story it's supposed to be, including the bit about a Popperian creature getting to let its hypotheses die in its place, and how Gregorian creatures import thinking tools rather than growing them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I already suspect half the class will cheat in the Skinnerian round by quietly planning ahead, since you can't really stop someone from thinking). And be honest about what the activity just cannot reproduce — like whether the Darwinian round can show anything true about real selection given that it happens in 15 minutes with 18 people, and whatever else has to be papered over with facilitator hand-waving. Don't tell them which round is supposed to be "better" in advance — I want them to notice the ladder themselves and only get the vocabulary at the end.
