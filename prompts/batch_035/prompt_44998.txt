So this started because I dropped my phone on concrete last week and the screen didn't crack — I'd just put one of those bulky cases on it. Somehow that spiraled into an argument about dispositions that I now can't get out of, and it seems to prove way too much:

(1) To say the glass is fragile is just to say it would break if dropped or struck — a simple conditional, nothing more. That's what I was always taught: dispositions just are conditionals.
(2) With the case on, the phone would not break if dropped.
(3) So with the case on, the glass isn't fragile; take the case off, and it is.
(4) Whether the case is on is a fact about the glass's surroundings, not about the glass itself.
(5) So fragility isn't an intrinsic property of the glass at all — the very same intrinsic state counts as fragile or not depending on what happens to be wrapped around it.
(6) This generalizes to every disposition, because for any disposition you can always find some interfering factor — bubble wrap, an anger-management coach, a guard dog — that keeps the manifestation from happening when the stimulus occurs.
(7) So all dispositions are extrinsic, relational facts.
(8) Character traits like kindness, courage, and honesty are dispositions.
(9) So whether I'm a kind person isn't determined by anything inside me — it depends on how the world happens to be arranged around me, and I'm no more genuinely kind than the cased-up phone is genuinely unbreakable.

The problem is that (9) is basically the end of my thesis, which is on virtue ethics and kind of needs people to have characters. I'd love to reject (3) but I can't see how, given (1). My professor says the fink/mask literature is exactly about this but I've read a bit of it and it feels like everyone just agrees the conditional analysis fails without spelling out what the alternative costs. Which step should I give up, and what am I actually paying for it? I want to keep (8) if I possibly can.
