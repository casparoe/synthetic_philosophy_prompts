So last night my friend and I got into a huge fight about the demandingness objection to consequentialism (she's a philosophy grad student, I'm just a guy who read too much Peter Singer in college), and we realized halfway through that we were talking past each other because "consequentialism demands too much" is doing like three different jobs. One complaint is about absolute amount of sacrifice (nobody should have to give until it hurts, full stop), one is about fairness (why is *me* on the hook when a million free-riders give nothing), and one is more about alienation (a life where every dentist appointment is shadowed by "this money could deworm a village" is a life you can't live from the inside). My friend's hot take is that almost nobody in the demandingness literature separates these cleanly, and that if you actually sort people by cases rather than by which famous name they like, the standard positions scramble around in interesting ways. I want to test that take.

Could you build me a decision tree / sorting instrument for this? Start from the generic "consequentialism is too demanding" complaint and branch on concrete cases until you land on named positions. The branch nodes should be cases people can answer without knowing any labels, stuff like: would it be wrong to skip a friend's birthday dinner to take an extra shift whose wages you'll donate; is the morality of keeping your savings account different in a world where everyone else gives 10% versus one where nobody gives anything; is there some threshold of your own flourishing below which you're never required to go even for enormous gains to others; would an ideal observer who felt no pull toward her own projects still be admirable or defective; does it change anything whether the sacrifices are small-and-constant (daily coffee money) versus large-and-once (selling the house). You choose the cases, but aim for maybe 6-8 nodes and 6-9 leaves, with leaves including at least: plain unlimited act consequentialism (demandingness as a bug we should fix in ourselves, not the theory), satisficing or scalar variants, some agent-centered prerogative view, a rule or institutional-level move where what's required depends on what others are doing, and a hybrid/sophisticated consequentialism that lets you care about your own stuff as an indirect means. Please also do two things most taxonomy handouts skip: mark which leaf combinations are unoccupied in the actual debate and say why you think that is (incoherent? just unattractive? nobody got around to it?), and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I suspect the prerogative view and the "what others are doing" view produce identical answers on almost every case you'd realistically write, and if so I'd rather know that than pretend the tree is cleaner than it is. Audience is an intro-level study group, so keep the nodes self-contained and don't assume anyone has read the literature.
