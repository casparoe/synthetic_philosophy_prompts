Every ten years since 1985, on the anniversary of the April day American troops reached our small town near Heidelberg, my family holds a private gathering we half-jokingly call our "Jahrzeit for the living." A designated family member must deliver a short address answering one question: does our family's obligation to the German past still bind us, and if so, in what form? My grandfather gave the first address in 1985 and argued, in essence, that with most of the responsible generation dying off, the question was closing itself. My grandmother gave the second in 1995 and dismantled his position using Karl Jaspers's fourfold distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt — she kept a battered copy of The Question of German Guilt on her desk for decades and knew the typology cold. She died last year, and the fourth address, this spring, is mine.

The audience will be about twenty-five people, and the speech has to navigate all of them:

- Several grandchildren and great-grandchildren, some born after 1990, who consider the whole ritual an exercise in guilt-tripping people who weren't alive for any of it, and who have said they'll skip unless this year's address actually speaks to them rather than at them.
- A cousin's husband, a Heidelberg law professor, who thinks the ritual is philosophically confused and politically unhealthy — he calls it "a secular original-sin service" and will be listening for sloppiness.
- My great-aunt, now ninety-seven, who as a sixteen-year-old typed transport lists in the town administration in 1944 and has never once spoken at or about these gatherings. She will be in the room.

Please write the address. It should be six to eight minutes when spoken (roughly 900 to 1100 words) and satisfy the following constraints:

1. It must take a definite position on whether the obligation still binds and what form it takes now — not a balanced survey of views.
2. It must be structured around Jaspers's fourfold distinction, and it must use that distinction to draw the line my grandmother's address arguably blurred: between guilt, which on Jaspers's own account cannot be inherited like blood, and liability or responsibility, which can pass to successors. The sharp problem I want the speech to confront honestly is that the teenagers are strictly right on Jaspers's terms — nobody in that room is guilty of anything that happened before their birth or in their childhood — and yet the ritual persists. So the speech has to explain what the gathering is for if it is not for guilt, and it should not dodge the fact that metaphysical guilt, as Jaspers describes it, rests on coexistence and solidarity rather than on actions, which makes it the one category that time alone seems incapable of dissolving.
3. Do not quote Jaspers verbatim. Paraphrase him; nobody at the gathering will have the text open, and a misremembered quote would be worse than none. You may freely invent brief remembered fragments of my grandmother's 1995 address, since only family heard it.
4. Keep a strict jargon budget: at most three terms of art, each explained in one plain sentence inside the speech itself. Beyond those, write so that the teenagers stay with you.
5. Decide deliberately whether and how to acknowledge my great-aunt — silently, obliquely, or directly — and make the speech carry the consequences of that choice either way. Do not turn the address into a trial of one woman in her tenth decade.

After the speech itself, append a structured summary in the form of a single JSON object with the following fields (plain strings unless noted):

- "thesis": the address's central claim, in one or two sentences;
- "binding_guilt_types": an array of the Jaspers categories (from "criminal", "political", "moral", "metaphysical") the address treats as still in force for the family in any sense, each with a one-sentence reason;
- "guilt_vs_liability": how the speech distinguishes inherited guilt from inherited liability or responsibility, in one or two sentences;
- "terms_of_art": an array of the terms used under the jargon budget, each paired with its in-speech gloss;
- "address_to_the_young": how the speech answers the complaint that it guilt-trips those born after the events;
- "address_to_the_great_aunt": whether she was acknowledged, and how;
- "departures_from_1995": where the address departs from the grandmother's framing, if it does;
- "proposed_obligations": an array of the concrete forms of obligation the speech proposes, if any;
- "anticipated_objection": the law professor's most likely objection to the address, in one sentence, and the address's implicit or explicit response, in one more.
