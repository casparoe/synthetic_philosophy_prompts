I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, so my default instinct in any memory-versus-record dispute is "the archive wins, update your map." But a fight between two friends of mine over my hometown's founding story has me genuinely unsure, and I'd like a careful adjudication of it.

The town I grew up in, Millhaven, has always told a story about the flood of 1907: the founder, Josiah Harlan, rowed a dory through the streets at night and pulled a dozen families out of the water, and the plaque in the square commemorates exactly that. Last year a volunteer group digitized the local newspaper archive, and the fragments complicate the story badly. The flood coverage never mentions Harlan rowing anything — he appears two days later, at the cleanup, in a photograph. What it does contain is one line crediting a "schoolteacher, A. Rinez" with the boat rescues. Meanwhile the Reyes family, descendants of the woman in question, have been telling a version with her name in it for a century, and the town mostly shrugged them off. The council now has to decide whether to revise the plaque, and my two friends, Dana and Marcus, argued about it for three hours at dinner.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Dana said it's straightforward: the archive is evidence; community memory is produced by a process we know confabulates and flatters; the town should revise the plaque and update, and anything else is belief retained for social utility — a noble lie with better PR. (2) Marcus answered that the archive is not a view from nowhere; it's a record of what a 1907 editor found notable, and "A. Rinez" appears exactly once, misspelled, precisely because of who counted as recordable then. The oral tradition preserved her name for a century. So "memory versus record" is really one curatorial process against another, and the record's silence isn't evidence of absence. (3) Dana granted the archival bias but said it proves incompleteness, not reliability: the Reyes tradition could itself be self-serving family lore, descendant communities have reasons to remember a certain way too, and you can't use the archive's known flaws to license preferring an even less reliable channel — the convergence of the two sources is what made the case interesting, not memory's independent authority. (4) Marcus replied that "which channel has better error properties" is the wrong standard entirely: on the Halbwachs view, memory's function isn't fact storage but selection of what matters, and a community that lets an archive dictate what it remembers loses something memory is for. History as a discipline can hold the correction without the town reorganizing its identity around it; the two operate at different levels, so "which should give way" is a category error. (5) Dana called that too convenient: if memory's job is selection rather than accuracy, then a public factual claim on a plaque is squarely on history's turf, and "different functions" becomes a permanent license to keep whatever story flatters you. When pressed to name a case where community memory beat the archive in a straight factual contest, Marcus could only name this one — and only because the archive happened to contain the confirming fragment, which looks like a concession of Dana's standard.

Please adjudicate this move by move — who's right at each step and why, rating the moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right — and then give me your own answer to the general question they were circling: when a community's memory and the record conflict, which should give way, and does the answer change depending on what's at stake (a plaque stating facts versus a ritual sustaining identity)? I'd like a committed response: take definite positions on each move and on the general question, don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me, and tell me plainly what both of them are missing if anything. It's fine to conclude that the dialectic turns on a distinction neither of them draws explicitly — but say which one, and defend it.
