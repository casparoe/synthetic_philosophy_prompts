A friend from my decision theory seminar walked me into an argument built on the old Death in Damascus story, and I can't find the exit. The setup: you meet Death in the Aleppo market, and he tells you he expects to find you in Damascus tomorrow. You know Death is never wrong about such things. My friend's argument ran as follows. (1) Given his perfect track record, his announcement gives you knowledge: you now know that tomorrow you will be in Damascus. (2) If you know you'll be in Damascus, then hiring the caravan to Aleppo is a futile expense — a pointless act, one that cannot change the outcome, is irrational even when cheap, and refusing futile means is what rationality asks. (3) So you should stay put, or in any case you should not flee. (4) Here he generalized, and this is where I got uneasy: the reasoning so far, he said, had nothing to do with Death's being supernatural. Any predictor with a reliable enough read on you fixes your future the same way, and you predict yourself constantly — you know you'll pay rent on the first of the month, know you'll take your usual train home. So whenever you're confident about what you'll do, deliberating about it is pointless, and rationality requires settling into your own forecast rather than resisting it. (5) Since we are confident about most of our own future behavior, deliberation is pointless almost all of the time. He accepts this cheerfully and calls it fatalism vindicated.

That conclusion seems to me to prove far too much — I deliberated about how to phrase this very question, and I don't believe that was wasted effort. But here's my problem: taken alone, each step still strikes me as plausible. Even (2), which I distrust most, seems right in ordinary cases: if I know the raffle results are already sealed, buying more tickets really is futile, and not because of anything causal about the drawing. And step (4) has something to it too, since my own confident self-forecasts do seem to constrain my planning in some way I can't articulate. My friend also added a remark that might or might not matter: he said anyone who takes both boxes in Newcomb's problem should want to flee to Aleppo on the same grounds, which confuses me, because he himself is a one-boxer and still thinks staying put in Damascus is obviously right.

Please tell me which step or inference I should reject, explain why that step looked fine, and say what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether rejecting it commits me to fleeing to Aleppo being rational after all, or to denying that I know I'll pay my rent, or to something else I should notice. If more than one step is troubled, tell me which is least costly to give up, and whether the real damage is in a slide between two readings of a single step rather than in any premise as stated. I would most like to keep (1), and I'd rather give up only one of the later steps. Please answer in American English, say how confident you are in your overall diagnosis, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
