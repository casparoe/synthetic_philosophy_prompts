I am presenting at a departmental colloquium a paper defending the central arguments of Hume's essay "Of Suicide" — that suicide can violate no duty to God, no duty to our neighbors, and no duty to ourselves — while conceding at the outset that Hume's analogy between ending one's life and diverting a river or administering medicine, on the ground that both equally disturb the appointed course of nature, will not move a theist who distinguishes ordinary from extraordinary exercises of providence. My paper's constructive claim is that Hume's strongest answer to the Kantian, autonomy-based prohibition is nowhere in the essay itself but in his bundle theory of personal identity: if there is no persisting self over and above a bundle of perceptions, then there is no substantive bearer to whom a duty of self-preservation could be owed, and the duty-to-self objection collapses. The audience is a mid-sized department: two committed Kantians, one of whom has written on the Groundwork's treatment of suicide as a violation of perfect duty to oneself; a Thomist natural-law scholar who takes self-preservation to be a basic inclination of the sort Aquinas's second way trades on; a Hume specialist who regards the essay as minor occasional rhetoric rather than serious philosophy; and a clinical ethicist who serves on a hospital suicide-prevention committee and has little patience for abstractions that float free of actual cases of treatable depression. Please prepare my question-period sheet as a numbered list of exactly seven items, ordered by how much damage each objection could do if handled badly, each item consisting of the objection phrased in the vocabulary of the particular questioner most likely to raise it, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and one sentence identifying where that reply is soft. The seventh item should be the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. Give me just the seven items, with no introduction or conclusion.
