I'm on the budget working group for a shared research facility that's jointly run by our linguistics department and the cognitive science program. Each year we split a fixed operations pot between the two units, and historically it's been done by horse-trading in a way everyone is sick of. This year a colleague with a game theory background proposed we formalize it: each unit submits valuations over the candidate projects the money could fund, there's a mandated default split that kicks in if we can't agree (which acts as the disagreement point), and then we apply a bargaining solution to the resulting problem instead of arguing. Two solutions got proposed and the group split down the middle. Half want the Nash solution — pick the split that maximizes the product of the two units' utility gains over the default. The other half want Kalai–Smorodinsky — find each unit's best achievable payoff given the other unit gets its own best (the utopia point), and pick the split where each unit gets the same fraction of its utopia-relative gain. Both sound fair in the abstract, and the math-y people assure us they genuinely come apart in our setting.

The thing that's really got us stuck is the year-to-year dynamics, since the feasible set isn't fixed — projects get proposed and dropped all through the spring. Our dress rehearsal last year was embarrassing: the cog sci unit had a big industrial-matching option added late in the process, which improved cog sci's best-case payoffs across the board, and under the product-maximization rule they ended up with a smaller share than before the option existed. The story in the hallway was "they found better things to do with the money and got punished for it," and one member nearly quit the working group over it. The colleague says the equal-fractions rule can't do that to you (she called it monotonicity), but the price seems to be that deleting some fantasy option nobody would ever have chosen can still move the split, because it drags down one unit's utopia point — which our Nash faction says is irrational, since a rejected alternative shouldn't matter. We have to pick one rule and write it into the facility's charter for next cycle, so re-litigating this every spring is off the table.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: recomend one of the two solutions for the charter, with the reasoning spelled out for non-specialists, and give an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side — including whether the year-to-year dynamics (things being added and dropped from the feasible set) should drive the choice more than the one-shot fairness properties do. Assume the submitted valuations are reasonably truthful but strategically shaded, if that matters to the argument. It should be something the actual committee can act on: a clear bottom line, the reasons, and a line or two on what to tell people who will inevitably claim the chosen rule is rigged.
