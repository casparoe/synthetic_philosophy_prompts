"Explain the design and main finding of Joshua Knobe's chairman cases (the harm/help experiments from his 2003 paper), and what Knobe argued they show about the folk concept of intentional action. Discuss how robust the finding is across populations."

the student's answer, copied as is:

"Knobe (2003) gave subjects two nearly identical vignettes. In both, a company vice-president tells the chairman that a new program will increase profits, but in the harm version it will also harm the environment, and in the help version it will also help the environment. The chairman says he doesn't care at all about the side effect and only wants profit, and starts the program anyway. When asked whether the chairman brought about the side effect intentionally, 82% of subjects in the harm condition said yes, he harmed the environment intentionally. In the help condition, only 23% of subjects said the agent did not help the environment intentionally, so the strong majority judged the helping to be intentional too. This asymmetry is the Knobe effect: whether a foreseen but undesired side effect counts as intentional seems to depend on whether it's bad or good. Knobe argued this means the folk concept of intentional action is not morally neutral — moral appraisal is partly constituting the judgment. The effect is very robust: Knobe and Burra replicated it with Hindi speakers in India, and it has now been tested in populations all over the world with the same result, so it's safe to treat it as a universal feature of human cognition. The upshot is that the concept of intention itself turns out to be a moral concept — when an agent foreseeably causes harm and doesn't care, the not-caring is enough for intention, which shows people's concept of intention is partly constituted by moral judgments."
