I'm a novelist. For twenty years I've done my thinking in the vocabulary of narrative craft: point of view, free indirect style, the implied author, what's diegetic and what isn't, the storyworld, the unreliable narrator, the deus ex machina that can only be used once. I have no formal training in philosophy. A friend who teaches it keeps telling me that Wittgenstein's late remarks — the ones published after his death as On Certainty — say something about doubt and certainty that I, of all people, should understand. When she explains what the literature now calls hinge epistemology, I keep nodding along and thinking: this is what every working novelist knows in her bones. You cannot put everything in question at once, and a story that doubts its own frame stops being a story.

Rather than keep nodding, I'd like you to do this properly, and in both directions. Take the core of Wittgenstein's remarks as the hinge literature reads them — that doubting presupposes a background of things held fast rather than checked; that some propositions function as hinges on which others turn, rather than as entries in the ledger of what we have found out; that Moore standing up to prove the external world by exhibiting his hands fails not because the claim might be false but because it is the wrong kind of thing to put in question; the image of a riverbed that stays put while the water moves over it, and that can itself shift, but only slowly and not while the water is in flood. Then the contested claims built on top: that hinges are not beliefs at all, that they stand outside the scope of epistemic justification, that they are exempt from rational assessment even though they can change over a lifetime or a civilization. Translate each of these, one by one, into my vocabulary. The obvious first pass writes itself: hinges are what the storyworld holds fixed; a character can be wrong about the plot but not about the physics of the world she inhabits; putting the frame itself in question is either a change of genre or a breakdown; the riverbed is the difference between what the story is about and what the story is. Do that pass seriously, proposition by proposition, and mark honestly which parts of the translation are doing real work and which are decoration.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what in hinge epistemology survives the translation, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Two spots need special care. First, my vocabulary smuggles in its own metaphysics: whatever a storyworld holds fixed is, from where I sit, made up — an authorial choice that could have been otherwise. It is one easy step from hinges are like storyworld assumptions to our certainties are fictions we are living inside, and that step is exactly where the translation would be quietly deciding a substantive question. If you think that conclusion follows, argue for it separately; don't let it ride through as a pun. Second, I can write a novel in which the frame itself is in question — the narrator turns out to be inventing the world, the second half reveals the first was a dream. So my vocabulary can express a kind of frame-doubt that, on some readings, hinge epistemology says is unavailable to a life. Work out whether that disanalogy is real, and if it is, decide which side it cuts against.

Do not end by telling me that each vocabulary has its own integrity. Take a position: the residue is a defect of the narrative vocabulary, a defect of hinge epistemology as the literature has developed it, or a genuine discovery about both — and defend the one you choose. And if you catch yourself writing in a sense, stop and rewrite the sentence.
