I'm a retired field ecologist and lately I sit on the board of a small land trust. We have a chronic problem with volunteer trail stewards: people sign up all enthusiastic, then vanish after a month, and every onboarding costs us real training hours. Our chair wants new stewards to go through a six-month probation with mandatory early-morning work parties before they get keys to the toolshed and any say in decisions. Another board member says this is just making people jump through pointless hoops — the hours are wasted, and we'd get more total work done by letting everyone show up whenever they feel like it. After forty years of watching peacocks drag those tails around, I have this itch that the chair is onto something real — that the cost might be the point rather than a bug — but I can't quite spell out when making something costly actually filters for the right people and when it just wastefully scares off good ones. Help me think it through: is there a principled way to decide when the hoop-jumping is doing genuine work and when we should scrap it?
