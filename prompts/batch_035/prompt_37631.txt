I've been going to a study group on Buddhist philosophy and someone there walked me into an argument about anatta that I can't get out of. It goes: (1) The Buddha's basic point is that when you search for your self among the components of experience — body, feelings, perceptions, volitions, consciousness — you find only the components, never an owner behind them. (2) Anything that can't be found when you look for it carefully doesn't exist. (3) So the self doesn't exist. (4) What doesn't exist can't be an agent, can't be responsible for anything, and can't be the one who benefits from compassion or liberation — at most there's a causal stream, and streams aren't anyone. (5) So karma is incoherent (nobody acts, nobody reaps), and the Buddha's whole ethical teaching is aimed at beings who, by his own analysis, aren't there. (6) A doctrine that dissolves the audience it's addressed to has refuted the teaching that carries it, so either the no-self doctrine is wrong or the rest of the teaching is. The guy who pushed this on me is leaning hard on (2), and I'll admit the whole thing smells like the chariot analogy turned against itself — you can't find the chariot among the parts either, but it would be weird to conclude you can't ride one to the neighboring town. But if I give up (2) I worry I've just admitted that things exist precisely when careful analysis can't find them, which sounds like the opposite of what the analysis was for. Which step do I reject, and what am I paying for it? I'd honestly like to keep both (1) and the claim that karma and compassion still make sense, so tell me if that combination is available or if I have to choose.
