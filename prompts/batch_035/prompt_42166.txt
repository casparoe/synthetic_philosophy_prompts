I run a small blog about everyday decision-making, mostly read by people who have never heard of Herbert Simon but will happily spend three weekends comparing laptops. I want a "what kind of decider are you actually?" quiz that sorts readers by how they handle concrete cases, not by which label they'd pick for themselves — so no question can just be "are you a maximizer?" or "do you think more choice is better?"

Eight questions, each one a case with two or three plain-language options, then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves I want:

1. Straightforward maximizer — take the best option, full stop.
2. Simon-style bounded optimizer — optimizing is still the ideal, but search costs count, so "good enough" is really just maximizing with the cost of looking folded in.
3. Intrinsic satisficer — the aspiration level isn't a concession to costs; once the bar is cleared, stopping is the whole point, and an obviously better option sitting on the same shelf doesn't obligate you to reach for it.
4. Ecological-rationality type — the interesting question is which heuristic fits which environment, not whether heuristics are a regrettable fallback.
5. Bar-adjuster — quietly lowers the aspiration level when the world disappoints, and thinks that's wisdom rather than settling.

Two things I care about:

- One question has to involve paying to expand the choice set (a paid search service, an extra broker fee, one more round of interviews) — I want the key to say whether willingness to pay for more options tracks the maximizer/satisficer split or cuts across it, and if it cuts across, which pairs it actually seperates.
- One question has to be about stopping early on epistemic grounds: you have a candidate that clears the bar, and one more check might reveal a hidden flaw. I suspect "check anyway" answers could come from maximizers but also from a distinct worry-about-error type, and I dont think my cases can pull those apart — if so, flag it in the key rather than pretending the quiz handles it.

Also: no two leaves should rest on the same pattern of answers, and if two of the five genuinely can't be distinguished by these cases, say so in the key instead of fudging it. The audience has no philosophy background, so every option must describe what the reader would actually do — never a term of art, never a school name.

Whole thing — questions plus key — under 700 words. Tight, please.
