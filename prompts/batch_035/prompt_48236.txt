I'm giving a talk at a bioethics conference next month making the case for what David Pearce calls the hedonistic imperative: the idea that we should use biotechnology — eventually genetic engineering of our reward circuitry, not just better pills — to abolish suffering outright, not merely treat it when it goes clinically wrong, and replace it with engineered gradients of wellbeing (lows and highs that keep motivation and discernment intact) rather than some flat uniform bliss. Sketch of my argument, so you can aim the prep at it: suffering is an artifact of blind selection, not a metaphysical necessity, so "its just life" was never an argument; the badness of pain gives anyone who feels it a reason to want it gone, and the rest of us a reason to help; we already accept mood engineering whenever it relieves illness — anesthesia, SSRIs, deep brain stimulation for depression — so the treat/enhance boundary is a habit of the last century, not a principle; and the line that suffering is necessary for depth or meaning smells like an adaptive-story rationalization of whatever the status quo happens to be. One piece of ammo I intend to deploy: pain asymbolia — people who register the pain signal but it simply doesn't bother them — which suggests the sensory alarm and the distress are separable, so in principle you could keep the alarm and delete the agony. I fully expect someone to counter with kids born without pain sensation who die young from unnoticed injuries, and honestly my reply to that is half-formed, so tell me if it's any good.

The room: mostly bioethicists of the Kass school, who take deep repugnance to be a form of moral knowledge and are protective of "given human nature" as a normative anchor; a vocal contingent of disability-studies scholars who are going to hear "abolish suffering" as one more genteel euphemism for selecting against lives they consider worth living; and two or three utilitarian philosophers who are friendly in principle but will come at me on wireheading, on whether engineered contentment is a counterfeit of the real thing, and on what happens to motivation and moral seriousness if the worst experience anyone can have is mildly annoying. Prepare me like a speaker's prep sheet: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each could do, each phrased the way that particular subgroup would phrase it (where the bioconservative, disability-rights, and utilitarian versions of the same worry differ, give me all versions), with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front versus what to hold, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Lead with the bottom line in the very first sentence — the single objection most likely to sink the talk if I fumble it — then the reasoning and the sheet, no preamble and dont restate my question back at me.
