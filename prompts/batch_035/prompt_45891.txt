My roommate Marco, who took one decision theory seminar and has been insufferable ever since, walked me through an argument at dinner last week that I can't stop chewing on. (1) Take the coffee at his lab: with 8 grains of sugar it tastes identical to him as with 9 grains, and that holds all the way up -- any single grain is imperceptible, even if you gave him two cups side by side and all afternoon. (2) If you can't tell two options apart even under the best conditions, neither is better than the other for you. (3) So each cup in the chain is no better than the next one. (4) And "no better than" is transitive -- a tie doesn't turn into a loss just because you string enough ties together. (5) So the 8-grain cup is no better than the 800-grain cup, which is basically syrup no human would drink. (6) But the 8-grain cup clearly IS better than the 800-grain one. (7) Conclusion: the culprit is transitivity. "Better than" just isn't a transitive relation, and rational preference can have this chain structure without anything being wrong with you.

He's been milking this practically ever since. When his girlfriend wanted to chip in for an upgraded pair of noise-cancelling headphones, he refused, saying any upgrade is just a stack of imperceptible steps, and if no single step is worth anything, the whole thing isn't worth paying for. He's also started saying his preferences can straight-up cycle (he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, all out loud) and that this is fine and we're all just brainwashed by decision theory textbooks. Something about the move from (7) to the headphones bugs me in a way I can't articulate -- isn't he using the chain to drag the "tie" verdict all the way from 9 grains to 800, about ten minutes after arguing that chains don't carry evaluative facts intact? But I also can't see which premise in the main argument is the bad one. My instinct is that "can't tell them apart" and "indifferent between them" and "no better than" are quietly swapping places somewhere between (2) and (4), but every time I try to pin it down he says I'm just refusing to accept his conclusion.

So, help me out: where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise should he give up, and what does rejecting it cost -- does he have to admit you can taste the difference after all, or that imperceptible things can still be better, or what? And separately, does the headphones thing actually follow from whatever he's established, or is he double-dipping? I'd honestly rather keep transitivity of "better than" if there's a principled way to do it, and I'd also like to know what to say about the cycles thing (it seems like someone with cyclic preferences can get money-pumped, but he'd just say that's more textbook brainwashing). Be blunt about which of us is right where, and what each of us is missing.
