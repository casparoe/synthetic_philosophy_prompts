I have been cataloguing my gut verdicts about when someone has a reason to do something, in the neighborhood of Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons, and I would like to know whether my verdicts trace to one coherent principle or whether I am conflating two different notions of "has a reason." The cases and my verdicts: (1) A rational, well-informed, unwavering man who wants to be rid of his wife and would inherit from her death: I want to say he has no internal reason not to kill her, but he does have a reason not to, full stop. (2) A teenager who genuinely cares nothing for his sixty-year-old self: he has a reason to save for a pension, though it does not feel like the same kind of "reason" as in (1), and I cannot say why. (3) A woman terrified of flying, who knows flying is the only way to reach her dying mother: she has a reason to fly, and the reason is somehow already hers even though the fear blocks it from moving her. (4) A contented lifelong smoker with no desire whatsoever to quit: before any desire to quit exists, I am inclined to say he has no reason to stop — yet I said the teenager has a reason to save, and I cannot locate the difference. (5) A fully informed amoralist who tortures for pleasure and cares about nothing else: he has a reason to stop, and I do not think it is a matter of what he could be brought to want. (6) A woman raised in a closed sect who wants to stay, though her desires would transform if she ever deliberated freely: I flip on this case weekly. Can a single principle deliver all six — say, internal reasons as what survives a sound deliberative route through the agent's actual motivations, external reasons as those that hold regardless — or must I choose between giving up my verdict in (1) and giving up the one in (4), since they seem to pull in opposite directions on whether reasons can float free of motivation? If any principle that saves most of my verdicts forces me to drop one, please say which verdict should go and why, and be honest about the cost of the more complex principle that saves everything. Then tell me what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not decided: someone in severe depression who currently cares about nothing, including getting well — do they have reasons to do the things that might make them well, and if so, are those reasons internal, external, or some third thing my case list has been hiding from me?
