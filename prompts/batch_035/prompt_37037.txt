I'm a novelist, and for the past year I've been at work on a book whose protagonist is a physicist losing her religious faith and finding a strangely liturgical replacement for it in the many-worlds interpretation — every prayer answered somewhere, every sin also committed elsewhere. To keep the physics honest, I periodically have dinner with my technical consultant, a retired quantum theorist named Otto, and my editor, Marta, who did a physics degree before defecting to literature. Last week I made the mistake of asking them, over dessert, how my protagonist should talk about probability: if every outcome of a measurement happens in some branch, what could it possibly mean to say one outcome is more probable than another?

Otto's position was that this is the theory's open wound. He grants that the formalism spits out the Born rule's squared-amplitude weights, but he insists those weights are not yet probabilities: probability, he says, is a measure of uncertainty, and in many-worlds nothing is uncertain — the "or" of quantum mechanics is really an "and." A theory in which everything happens with certainty, he argued, has smuggled the word "probability" in without earning it, and the weights are just numbers until some interpretive work turns them into chances. Marta answered that this is looking for probability in the wrong drawer: the right move, she said, is to notice that before the measurement, the agent doesn't know which of the future selves she will be — call it self-locating uncertainty, or if Otto dislikes that framing, a decision-theoretic one: the question isn't "what will happen?" but "how much should I care about each of the futures that will contain a copy of me?" and the answer has to be some weighting or other, so the weightings can be assessed for rationality rather than derived from nothing.

Otto was unmoved on two counts. First, he said the caring proposal quietly assumes that caring proportionally to the squared amplitudes is the rational choice, whereas the natural, democratic proposal — give each resulting world equal weight — gets the wrong answer; so whatever delivers the Born weights, it isn't an obvious rationality constraint, which suggests it was read off the formalism after the fact and dressed up as a principle. Second, he complained that even counting branches, let alone weighting them, is undefined: the splitting isn't into a tidy finite set of worlds but into a continuous smear, decoherence being a process rather than an event, so "how many futures" has no answer and any weighting scheme inherits that vagueness. Marta's last word was that vagueness is common to all idealizations — the very notion of a "single measurement outcome" is vague even in classical physics — and that it doesn't stop the weights from doing their job in the limit; the demand for a crisp branch count seemed to her a demand for precision no physical theory anywhere supplies.

I confess I couldn't referee, and it matters for the book: my protagonist should be losing an argument to the right person. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific point, where each of them concedes or should concede something, and what, if anything, both are missing (I'd especially like to know whether Marta's two framings, the uncertainty one and the caring one, are actually the same move or whether one survives Otto's objections better than the other). Please write your answer in American English.
