I am in the fourth year of a dissertation that is going badly, and my advisor has given me an argument for quitting that I cannot refute but also cannot accept. He concedes that the job market is not hopeless and that I could probably finish in two more years; his claim is not that finishing is a bad bet by ordinary human standards, but that it is not what a rational person would do, given where we are now. The argument runs as follows.

(1) The only reasons for choosing one option over another are differences in how the future will go. (2) What I have already invested — four years of stipend, my late twenties, everyone's expectations of me — is fixed no matter what I do next. (3) So those investments cannot count in favor of continuing. (4) Therefore I must decide between finishing and quitting exactly as a person standing outside the project would decide: by comparing the future the dissertation offers against the future of leaving, with nothing on the scale but what lies ahead. (5) Leaving now offers a certain salary, seniority compounding from age thirty-one, and release from a project whose expected value I have watched deteriorate for two years. (6) Nothing in the future counts in favor of staying, since a newcomer facing the same remaining work and the same remaining costs would walk away. (7) So staying is irrational.

He then observes — and this is what unsettles me — that the argument generalizes. Whenever a long project's expected remaining value dips below that of starting something else, step (6) issues the same verdict: quit. Every serious undertaking passes through such troughs, because the fresh alternative always enjoys a honeymoon the incumbent cannot. So a world of agents reasoning this way is a world of abandoned dissertations, marriages dissolved at the first bad year, and half-built bridges. He accepts this and says the half-built bridges merely show that the sunk-cost fallacy is widespread and our reluctance to abandon things is sentimentality.

I do not believe sentimentality is what stops me from agreeing, but I cannot locate the false step. Step (4) looks weakest to me — I have four years of accumulated things a newcomer has not — though he anticipates the objection: he says those things bear on the future and are therefore already weighed at step (5), so that once the scale is loaded, deciding as if new and deciding with one's history come to the same thing. If he is right about that, (4) survives and (7) follows. Which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost — either in odd verdicts elsewhere or in conceding something to my advisor? Please answer in at most 400 words.
