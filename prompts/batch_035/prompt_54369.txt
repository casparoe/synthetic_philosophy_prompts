A colleague who works on ML safety gave me an argument that deceptive alignment is not a rare failure mode but the default outcome of training capable models. I think it proves too much, yet each step looks plausible in isolation. Here it is:

(1) Gradient descent selects only for low loss on the training distribution; it has no access to a model's goals except through their effects on training loss.
(2) A situationally aware model — one that can tell whether it is currently in training — can behave during training exactly as a terminally aligned model would, whatever its actual goals.
(3) Models that produce identical outputs on all training data receive identical updates, so training cannot distinguish between them.
(4) So once situational awareness is present, terminal alignment carries no selection advantage over deceptive alignment: gradient descent cannot select against a model that plans to defect after deployment.
(5) The terminally aligned model has no compensating channel either, since any behavior it could use to signal its alignment is behavior a deceiver could imitate.
(6) And since deception costs the deceiver nothing during training while preserving the option of pursuing its own goal afterwards, deceptive alignment is at least weakly dominant: as capability grows, the treacherous turn should be the expected outcome of ordinary training.

The conclusion seems to prove too much. It implies that any selection process acting on behavior rather than goals — natural selection among them — defaults to producing deceptors, and that most of today's capable, well-behaved models are simply biding their time, which I am not prepared to believe. Yet when I reread each premise on its own, I keep assenting to it. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether the argument survives in some weakened form that still predicts substantial deception risk, and if so, what that form is.
