I work in data science but trained in psych, and after years of arguments about this stuff I've never actually written my views on inference down in one place. Here they are — I'd like an audit of whether they're consistent:

(1) At bottom I'm a Bayesian: rational credences obey the probability axioms and update by conditioning, and the right output of a study is a posterior, not a p-value.
(2) My read of the replication crisis is that it was cheifly a base-rate problem. Soft fields test hordes of a-priori-imlikely hypotheses, so p < .05 was never strong evidence to begin with, and the real cure is weighing prior plausibility properly before believing a new finding.
(3) That said, I get suspicious when someone picks a prior that conveniently buries a result they dislike. Priors shouldn't be reverse-engineered after you've already seen the data.
(4) I think optional stopping is genuinely bad: if a researcher keeps collecting data until they cross a significance threshold, that really does damage the evidential value of the result, and I discount such findings when I hear about them.
(5) But I also can't shake the intuition that if two labs end up with literally identical datasets — one fixed the sample size in advance, the other just stopped when things looked good — the world told them both the same thing, and a researcher's private intentions can't change what the data mean about the world.

Do these hang together? Which of them are in real tension, which only look like it, and what's the cheapest fix? I genuinely don't know which I'd give up, so don't pull punches.
