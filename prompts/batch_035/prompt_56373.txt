I spent thirty-six years as a physical chemist — mostly spectroscopy, the last decade in polymer characterization — and since retiring I have joined a fortnightly philosophy reading group that has worked through Kripke's Naming and Necessity and Putnam's Twin Earth material, with a few members now pressing David Chalmers's two-dimensional semantics on the rest of us. I follow perhaps two-thirds of it. Our most decisive member is Miriam, a retired circuit court judge who treats every example the group proposes as a case to be ruled on, delivering verdicts with great confidence and, I am fairly sure, no underlying theory she would own up to. She insists she is just reporting how the words work. I have kept minutes of her rulings, and I would like your help predicting her rulings on the cases I plan to bring next meeting.

Her verdicts so far, in the order she issued them:

1. "Water is H₂O" — necessary; and only empirical investigation could have established it.
2. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — same ruling: necessary, a posteriori.
3. "Water is the watery stuff" — by which she meant the clear, potable liquid that fills our lakes and rivers, whatever its underlying chemistry might turn out to be — knowable a priori, simply by grasping the concept.
4. On the hypothetical that the clear, potable liquid had turned out to be XYZ rather than H₂O: it might indeed have turned out that way, and in that scenario, she ruled, the liquid would still have been water. This is the ruling that made me sit up.
5. Stick S, the standard meter in Paris: "Stick S is one meter long" is contingent, yet a priori.
6. Jade — after I pointed out that the term covers two chemically distinct minerals, jadeite and nephrite — genuinely names a disjunctive kind; it is not a term that half-fails to refer.
7. "Phlogiston" never referred to anything; when I pressed her, invoking jade, she said that nothing even superficially played the phlogiston role, whereas the two jades each fully play theirs.

The three cases I intend to bring next meeting:

A. The discovery scenario: suppose it turned out that our lakes, rain, and tap water are all XYZ — macro-indistinguishable from H₂O by any test available to us — while H₂O exists only as crystalline deposits several kilometers underground, never encountered by anyone. Has it turned out that water is XYZ rather than H₂O? Did the word "water" refer to the lakes' liquid all along, to the hidden deposits, to both, or to neither?

B. The counterfactual twin: setting aside what would have "turned out," consider as a mere counterfactual a world whose lakes are XYZ and whose deep deposits are H₂O. Given that in our world water is H₂O, does that world contain water — and if so, in its lakes or in its deposits?

C. Caloric: did the word "caloric," as used by the physicists who posited a self-repelling fluid flowing from hot bodies to cold, ever refer to anything at all? To heat? To molecular motion? To nothing?

Please predict Miriam's verdict on each case, and, more importantly, tell me what rule she seems to be running. I can see at least two ways to systematize her record — two-dimensional semantics, with its distinction between what is settled by how the world seems to us and what is settled by how it in fact is; and some retooled descriptivism on which "water" just means the watery stuff and the identity with H₂O was a discovery about its referent — and members of the group have argued for a version of Putnam's externalism as well, though I suspect that one strains against her fourth ruling. Which of the new cases best discriminates between the candidate rules? Where do the rules converge, and where do they come apart on A, B, and C — and if her verdicts on A and B come out differently from each other, what exactly is doing that work? Finally, give me the single further question I should put to her that would settle, as cleanly as anything in this territory can be settled, which rule she is implicitly running — and tell me frankly whether any of her seven rulings to date sits uneasily with the rest, so I know whether to press her on it. Please write in American English.
