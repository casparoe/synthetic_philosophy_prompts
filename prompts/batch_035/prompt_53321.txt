My friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, the UK law that put animal sentience into domestic legislation for the first time, and I have not been able to let it go since. To be concrete about what the Act actually does: it declares that animals are sentient beings, covers all vertebrates by default, and was amended during its passage to extend coverage to decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, shrimp) and cephalopod molluscs (octopuses, cuttlefish, squid), following a government-commissioned scientific review that concluded there was strong evidence of sentience for at least the cephalopods. The Act's machinery is an Animal Sentience Committee whose job is to scrutinize whether government policies have had, or will have, adverse effects on animal welfare. Insects, snails, and everything else with a nervous system were left outside, and as far as we could tell the ordinary welfare and cruelty statutes still apply only to vertebrates — so the practical upshot for a lobster, which one act now recognizes as sentient and which can, apparently, still be boiled alive without legal consequence, seemed to us close to nil.

What we could not resolve is what boundary the law has actually drawn, and what theory of moral patienthood that boundary presupposes. Three features of the Act strike me as odd when taken together. First, the line is drawn by biological group: every member of the covered taxa is in regardless of its individual capacities, and every member of the excluded taxa is out regardless of theirs — a bee that learns, solves problems, and seems to play gets no standing, while a much less impressive decapod is covered. Second, the evidential burden is asymmetric: vertebrates were included without any fresh review, while the invertebrate groups had to earn their way in through a scientific case strong enough to persuade Parliament. Third, the consequences are toothless relative to the declaration — sentience is recognized as a fact, but the duty it generates seems to be only that policymakers pay it some regard.

So my questions are these. What view of the boundaries of sentience does this legislative pattern presuppose? Is it committed to sentience tracking lineage — to clades rather than computations being the right units of moral concern — or is the clade-based line better read as an epistemic and administrative proxy, a way of parceling out our uncertainty about which animals feel rather than a claim about what feeling consists in? If moral patienthood supervenes on the functional or computational organization of a nervous system rather than on ancestry, is the Act's way of drawing lines merely a tractable approximation a functionalist could live with, or does it misclassify in a way that matters — protecting some animals that lack the relevant capacities and excluding others that may have them? What does the asymmetry between vertebrates and invertebrates presuppose: that prior law had already settled the question for vertebrates, or that evidence is only demanded of animals alien to us? And if the legislature took the computational criterion seriously — assessing animals by what their nervous systems do rather than by which family they belong to — what would have to change? Would the Act need to extend to bees, narrow itself to particular species within the covered groups, or abandon group-level lines altogether for something more like case-by-case assessment, and could any such approach remain administrable? I would also like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several quite different theories of which creatures matter morally would all license the same statute.
