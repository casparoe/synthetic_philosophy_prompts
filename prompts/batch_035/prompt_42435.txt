I'm a journalist who writes mostly about science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on whether death is bad for the person who dies. It started as a fun sidebar on Epicurus but the more I dig the less settled it looks. My own working credence that death (ordinary death, no afterlife, permanent end of experience) is bad *for the person who dies* is about 0.7. The philosopher I sound ideas off with is closer to 0.25 — she leans Epicurean: if death is just the absence of all experience, there's no subject left around to be harmed, so nothing is bad *for you*; it can be bad for the people who love you, but that's a different claim.

Give me your own number, but a bare number is useless to me — what I want is the decomposition. Roughly: the probability that being harmed requires you to exist at the time of the harm (the "no subject" premise), the probability that the deprivation account — death is bad for you because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had — is coherent and can do the work without smuggling a subject back in at the time of the loss, the probability that Lucretius's symmetry point (we don't pity the endless stretch before our birth, so why dread the endless stretch after it) can genuinely be broken rather than explained away, and any other terms you think belong in the chain. One line of justification for each, then the combination, and then tell me which single term the final answer is most sensitive to — I suspect that's where my 0.7 and her 0.25 actually diverge, and I'd like to claim that in the piece with some confidence.

One more thing, if it helps: I've been probing readers' intuitions with a case I cooked up for the article — you undergo suspended animation, no dreams, no experiences at all, for two hundred years, and are then revived perfectly healthy. Nearly everyone says the gap wasn't bad *for you* while it lasted (nobody home to be harmed), yet the same people insist dying is worse than the gap, even though both rob you of the exact same stretch of life. Feel free to tell me what that case does and doesn't show, or whether it's just muddying things. And please write in British English, and don't pad it with a tour of every named philosopher who ever wrote on this — I need the reasoning out in the open, not a reading list.
