novelist here, mostly mid-list literary fiction, no philosophy training beyond whatever leaks in. i keep running into what historians call whig history -- telling the past as one long buildup to the present, where every episode becomes a step toward how things turned out and the present makes the whole story feel inevitable. and honestly it's incredibly familar, because it reads like a plotting problem. can you restate the whig history idea, and the arguments against it, in the vocabulary i actually work in -- setups and payoffs, chekhov's gun, foreshadowing, character arcs, the way an ending retroactively decides which earlier details "count" -- and then give me the residue report. my suspicion is the whole thing turns on there being an author: in a novel things are foreshadowed because somebody planted them with the ending in mind, and the ending makes earlier events necessary because i wrote it that way, on purpose. history has nobody doing the planting, and no ending at all, just a present that happens to be where the excerpt stops. so: which parts of the whig historians move are a legit borrowing from narrative craft, which parts only work if you smuggle in an author who knows how it all comes out, and is what's left over a limit of my vocabulary or a diagnosis that the whig historian was just confused? and since you're at it -- is there a way to narrate the past that's honest the way a good novel is honest, where the ending feels earned without ever feeling preordained? that's basically the craft question of my life, so don't skip it.
