I'm TA-ing an intermediate game theory course and I have to write the final-exam question on iterated elimination of dominated strategies, specifically the mixed-strategy wrinkle. My professor wants a question that can't be answered by reciting the textbook chapter, and there's a specific confusion I want to target because half the class fell into it on the midterm: students seem to think that once you let a pure strategy be eliminated because it's dominated by a *mixture* of other strategies, everything they learned about iterated elimination breaks — that the order of elimination suddenly matters, or that you can accidentally delete a Nash equilibrium, or that the whole procedure becomes circular because the dominating mixture might itself get eliminated later. One student actually wrote that mixed domination is a "different kind" of domination that has to be handled with different rules than pure domination, which I think is wrong but I can see why it feels right to them.

So here's the setup I'd like the question built around. Give the students this row-player payoff matrix (columns L, C, R; only row's payoffs shown, since the point is about row's reasoning):

- T: 0, 0, 0
- M: 3, 3, −1
- B: −1, −1, 3

Part of the question should have them verify that T is strictly dominated by a 50-50 mix of M and B but by no pure strategy, so that pure-strategy-only elimination and mixed-inclusive elimination actually deliver different outcomes on this game — that's the hook. Then the main essay part should present a claim from a hypothetical classmate, something like: "allowing mixed domination makes iterated elimination order-dependent and can destroy Nash equilibria, since the dominating mixture can itself be pruned later," and ask students to evaluate it precisely, which means they have to say what survives: whether iterated elimination of *strictly* dominated strategies (with mixed dominators allowed) is order-independent, whether it can ever remove a strategy played with positive probability in some Nash equilibrium, why weak dominance is where the classmate's worries actually bite, and why the later elimination of the dominating mixture doesn't retroactively undermine the original elimination.

Please write: the question itself, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme (total 20 marks) with maybe five must-hit points, marks for each, and a one-line note per point on how students typically blow it. Please double check the arithmetic in the matrix and in any worked reasoning in the model answer — if there's any step in your model answer, any payoff computation, or any claim about order-independence where you're less than fully confident, flag it explicitly at the end and say what a careful grader would want verified, rather than just presenting everything as settled.
