I teach a mid-level ethics course, about 22 students, one 75-minute session, and we're about to hit supererogation -- acts beyond duty. I don't want to lecture the definition at them; I want them to build the concept's structure themselves and then watch it misbehave. Design the session for me.

Here's the conceptual load I want them to run into, in this order if you can manage it. First, the standard three-part structure: a supererogatory act is one it's permissible not to do, good to do, and worthy of praise for doing. Second, the credit paradox -- if omitting it leaves you blameless, what exactly is the praise tracking? And does praising the one who acted leak a silent criticism onto the one who didn't? Third, the competence wrinkle: if one participant is just better at the relevant skill (think of the difference between a random bystander and a trained lifelier at the beach), does the duty floor rise for them -- and if so, doesn't that threaten the "beyond duty" part of the whole idea? Fourth, the remainder problem: everyone meets duty, nobody's done anything wrong, and the outcome is still bad.

So I'm imagining something like a staged scenario or role-play with a rulebook that defines duties exactly, plus costly windows of action the rulebook doesn't cover, and outcomes that depend on the uncovered acts -- but you design the actual thing. The students should have to draw the duty line themselves, under some pressure, before they've heard the term supererogation or the definitions. Maybe they play a committee that has to write or amend the rulebook, and every proposed amendment either swallows the optional acts into duty or leaves the remainder sitting there. You could secretly assign one student extra capability in the scenario so the group has to decide whether skill changes obligation -- but make that work mechanically, don't just narrate it.

Give me: rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (tokens, grade things, whatever), a rough minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the four ideas above. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it, and which part of the problem (I suspect the credit paradox) no activity can really reproduce and I'll have to just supply in the debrief.

Also, don't put the word supererogation anywhere until the last ten minutes -- I want the reveal to land as them realizing they've been living inside a definition they wrote themselves.
