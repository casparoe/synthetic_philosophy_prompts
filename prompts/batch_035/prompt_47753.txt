I teach a second-year philosophy of science course — eighteen students, one 80-minute seminar — and I'm preparing a session on induction for the week after we finish Hume. Rather than lecturing on Goodman, I want an activity in which the students construct the new riddle themselves before anyone names it. Please design it for me, with the whole response in British English.

The effect I'm after: students should first make confident inductive bets, then discover that their own evidence equally supports a rival hypothesis that makes the opposite prediction about the very next case, and then — this is the part I care most about — discover that they can neutralise one rival predicate only by cooking up another, so that the regressive structure of the problem is something they generate rather than hear about. I've sketched a three-round prediction game with coloured counters and a sealed envelope containing the dealer's hidden rule, but I'm open to a better mechanism if you have one; whatever you propose, it needs a payoff scheme (points or tokens, not money), a role for at least one student as the dealer or "nature," and a moment where a reveal forces the bettors to confront a hypothesis of the form "colour so far, then the other colour after some undisclosed changeover point."

Specific constraints:

1. The words "grue," "bleen," "Goodman," and "new riddle" must not appear — not in the handouts, not in my patter — until the debrief. The students should have to coin their own name for whatever predicate does the damage, because I want them to feel that it isn't a philosopher's contrivance but something their own betting practice licenses.
2. Round three should require the students themselves to formulate a second-order predicate of the same general shape — one built from the first-order one the way the first-order one is built from green and blue — and to see that whatever complaint they made against the first-order predicate applies equally against their reason for trusting the second. Design the round so they can't just refuse to play along.
3. Include a full debrief script mapping specific moments in the game onto the parts of the problem: what "confirmed equally" means here, why simplicity or familiarity can't be invoked without circularity, and what entrenchment would amount to if the class were a linguistic community rather than eighty minutes long.
4. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong on the first run — e.g., students concluding that the moral is just "beware weird predicates," or the dealer accidentally leaking the rule — and how to handle each.
5. Finally, be frank about which feature of the actual philosophical problem no one-shot classroom activity can reproduce, and say what I must supply in the debrief to make up the gap.
