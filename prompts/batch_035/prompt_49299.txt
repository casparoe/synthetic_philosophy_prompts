I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm preparing a piece on whether great scientific discoveries are, in a loose sense, overdue — whether they are pulled into existence by converging pressures rather than conjured by singular individuals. My anchor case is special relativity. The standard narrative treats the Michelson–Morley null result of 1887 as the decisive crack in the edifice that Einstein then demolished in 1905. But the historical record suggests Einstein himself later downplayed the experiment's role in his thinking, and figures like Lorentz and Poincaré had already assembled much of the mathematical machinery — the transformations, the relativity principle, even glimpses of the consequences for simultaneity — largely from Maxwell's electrodynamics and its internal puzzles, without needing new data to push them. My thesis is that even if Michelson and Morley had never built their interferometer, special relativity would have been published by 1920 anyway, and probably close to 1910.

Before I commit to that claim in print, I want to know how much of it survives scrutiny. Give me your credence in the counterfactual itself — a number — and then decompose it rather than handing me a gesture at "it depends." At minimum I'd want the following terms separated out: the probability that the conceptual prerequisites for special relativity (the relativity principle, the abandonment of absolute simultaneity, the mathematics of the transformations) were available to several working physicists by 1905 regardless of the experimental situation; the probability that the internal tensions in electrodynamics alone, without the ether-drift anomaly, would have sufficed to force the question within your fifteen-year window; the probability that the sociology of the discipline — journals, prizes, the network of theoretical physics as it actually existed — would have permitted a young patent clerk, or someone structurally like him, to publish the synthesis; and whatever further terms you think belong, such as the risk that the subject instead calcifies into an ever-more-baroque ether programme, as Ptolemaic astronomy did for centuries. Assign each term a rough probability with one or two lines of justification, then show me how they combine into your final number.

Two further things. First, tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and what historical evidence — a letter, a priority dispute, a neglected manuscript — would move that term most, so I know where to point readers who want to check my work. Second, my desk editor's credence in my thesis is around 0.75; her predecessor, who reviewed a book on scientific genius some years ago, puts it nearer 0.3. Given your decomposition, tell me which specific sub-question those two most likely disagree about — whether they disagree about the history of physics, or about the general theory of discovery that the case is being recruited to support.

One constraint on your answer: I don't want the piece to lean on the fact that special relativity is a multiple-discovery-adjacent case and therefore flattering to my thesis. So close by flagging honestly which features of this case do not generalize — where the "overdue discoveries" frame would break if I applied it to, say, natural selection or plate tectonics — so I know how much my one example can be asked to carry.
