I watched the 2009 movie Moon a while back and I've been chewing on it ever since, so bear with me. The whole setup is basically Sam Rockwell alone on a lunar base, his contract's almost up, he has an accident in a rover, and then he wakes up back at the base and eventually finds another version of himself -- turns out the company has been cycling out clones (or copies, or whatever you want to call them) the whole time, and each new Sam wakes up believing he's near the end of his three year stint. The old Sam is dying and the new one is basically him, memories and everything.

I never studied philosophy, but a friend mentioned this thing by a guy named Derek Parfit about a "teletransporter" -- a machine that scans you, destroys you, and rebuilds an exact copy on Mars. Parfit asks whether that's you surviving or you dying and being replaced, and his answer (as it was explained to me anyway) was kinda radical: the question itself is empty, there's no further fact of the matter, and what we should care about is stuff like psychological connectedness, not whether it's "the same person" in some deep sense. Also something about how that should make us less afraid of death.

So here's what I want: read Moon as a take on exactly this question. The clones aren't even a teleporter -- the original Sam Bell is (I think?) back on Earth somewhere with his family, and these are just copies that keep waking up in his place, which seems even more brutal than Parfit's scenario. When the dying Sam and the new Sam meet and start working together, what is the movie actually saying about what matters in survival? Is it taking Parfit's side, or is it showing something his thought experiment misses -- like, the two Sams clearly don't treat each other as "the same person," they argue, they hide things from each other, there's something like jealousy and tenderness between them, and the movie gives each one a separate arc. That seems to cut against "there's no fact of the matter."

Also the ending, with the Sam clone escaping to Earth while the old one stays behind to die -- is that supposed to feel like survival, or like one person saving another? I honestly can't tell, and I keep going back and forth.

A few things I'd love you to hit: (1) whether the movie's version of copying is actually a harder case than the classic teletransporter, (2) which specific scenes do the most philosophical work (you can reference scenes loosely, no need for exact lines), and (3) an honest assessment of where my Parfit framing distorts the film, if it does -- maybe the movie is really about labor exploitation or loneliness or the daughter storyline, and I'm forcing the philosophy on it. I'd rather be told that than have you just agree with my reading. I'm writing a long post about this for a film forum and want it to be actually good, not just fan theory vibes.
