Something about the design of South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission has always puzzled me, and I want to know what conception of personhood and justice it implicitly rests on. The deal was amnesty in exchange for testimony: an individual who committed a political crime could escape prosecution, but only if they gave a full account of what they did, and as a rule this happened at a public hearing, with victims and the nation watching. Two design choices seem to presuppose something. First, why the public, in-person hearing at all — if the point was establishing the historical record, sworn written depositions would have done the job, so insisting on witnessed acknowledgment seems to presuppose that repair happens through speech performed in front of people, not through information transfer. Second, the victim is barely a party: amnesty didn't require the victim's forgiveness, and there was no restitution requirement on the perpetrator, which looks odd given that restorative justice usually centers the victim. Is the implicit idea something like Ubuntu — a person is a person through other persons — so that the primary wrong was a rupture in the communal fabric, and the repair is owed to the community rather than the individual victim? Or is the whole thing underdetermined, such that a plain rule-of-law story (amnesty as the price of a peaceful transition) licenses the same design without any relational conception of personhood? And where you're dealing with things that are genuinely contested among people who know the topic — for instance whether Ubuntu actually guided the design or was a justification retrofitted afterwards — please flag that as contested instead of passing it off as settled. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
