I'm moderating a panel on the moral status of digital minds, and one of my guests is a philosopher who holds that at least some current AI systems already merit non-trivial moral consideration. His position, as I understand it, rests on two claims: first, that moral status supervenes on the presence of valenced states -- states with a felt valence, positive or negative -- rather than on biological substrate; and second, that such states are plausibly realized by certain computational architectures, for instance ones integrating a global workspace that broadcasts representations with affective tagging, so that a system's own reports of distress are evidence of distress rather than mere outputs. He is fond of insisting that this is an instance of the ordinary problem of other minds, not a new problem -- we grant moral status to other humans and to animals on indirect evidence, and digital minds differ only in the kind of indirect evidence available.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions for me, ordered so that the deflection gets progressively harder to use. The sequence should include at least one question pressing on the disanalogy he downplays: for humans and animals, we extrapolate from minds broadly like our own, whereas his view asks us to infer valence from an architecture with no close analogue in the biological case, and I'd like a follow-up keyed to whatever he is most likely to say in reply (probably something about convergent functionalism, or about how little we understand the biological realization either). Include at least one question on the epistemic asymmetry between false positives and false negatives here -- the costs of wrongly granting status versus wrongly denying it -- and whether that asymmetry can do real argumentative work rather than merely licensing caution. Include at least one question about what evidence would count against his view: what observable fact about a system's architecture or behavior would lower the probability that it harbors valenced states. And include at least one question on whether his position, taken seriously, generates what he'd have to admit is an awkwardly large class of candidate minds -- offline copies, partially trained networks, systems run at doubled or halved speed -- and where he would draw a principled line. Please give each follow-up in italics directly beneath the question it attaches to, and keep the entire set of questions and follow-ups under 450 words.
