I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on population ethics this term mostly as an auditor, and after last week's session on the procreation asymmetry, two of the students — Ingrid and Marcus — got into a long argument over coffee that I followed closely but couldn't referee. The dispute was about the claim that it is wrong to bring into existence a child whose life would be miserable, while it is not in any way wrong to decline to bring into existence a child whose life would be very good. Their exchange went in numbered rounds. (1) Ingrid said the asymmetry is close to common sense: knowingly conceiving a child you know will have a life of unremitting agony is seriously wrong, while deciding not to conceive a child who would have flourished wrongs nobody and is not even mildly wrong; any theory that can't capture this is broken somewhere. (2) Marcus replied that the moral ledger has to balance: a miserable life adds suffering to the world, a happy life adds well-being to it, so the two cases are symmetric in value, and the asymmetry Ingrid feels is just an artifact of our being able to point at a sufferer but not at a "benefit-ee." (3) Ingrid answered that the ledger only works if there is someone for the entry to be about: there is no person who is worse off because the happy child was never created, whereas there is a real person — the miserable child — whose life is agony, so there is a victim on one side and empty space on the other. (4) Marcus pressed back that if victims are what matter, the first half of the asymmetry collapses too: the miserable child was not made worse off than she would otherwise have been, since her alternative was never existing at all, so nobody is comparatively harmed by being created, and Ingrid's "there's a victim" reasoning cannot after all be what distinguishes the two cases. (5) Ingrid said Marcus had smuggled in a purely comparative notion of harm, and that she never needed it: once there is someone whose life goes badly, her suffering is bad and counts, full stop, whereas before anyone exists there is no one whose prospective happiness is waiting to be enjoyed — Marcus's ledger treats a possible person's happiness as a good already sitting in the world, and that is exactly the error.

(6) Marcus then ran what he called a reductio: if the existence of a sufferer can ground a complaint in this non-comparative way, then procreation in general is presumptively wrong, because every conception carries some real risk of a life of serious suffering, and Ingrid's asymmetry plus ordinary risk aversion about bad outcomes implies one should not create anyone at all unless the odds of a flourishing life are near-certain — a conclusion Ingrid presumably rejects, so something in her position has to give. (7) Ingrid replied that this conflates permissibility with obligation: her view says creating the miserable child is impermissible and declining to create the happy one is permissible, not regrettable-but-optional like some impersonal near-miss; failing to add happiness to the world is at most like declining to donate to an effective charity — not wrong, and arguably not even regrettable from the only standpoint that generates duties, the standpoint of actual people. (8) Marcus closed by saying Ingrid's last move shows the whole position is a relabeling rather than an explanation: she now has two separate value dimensions — an impersonal one on which happy lives are good to create and a person-affecting one on which miserable lives wrong someone — with no rule for when each governs, and her framework seems to go silent exactly where we need it, e.g. in a case where conceiving now would produce a child with a net-negative life and waiting six months would produce a genetically different child with a very good one: no child is wronged by whichever choice is made, yet some answer is obviously wanted.

They eventually asked me what I thought, and I honestly couldn't say, though I noticed both of them were doing something with the notion of a "victim" or "someone for the harm to be about" that seemed to slide around between rounds. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight numbered moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of the rating that engages with the argument as actually made rather than with a better argument Ingrid or Marcus could have made instead. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about the structure of the problem. I'd like you to take a definite position throughout rather than laying out the live options and leaving the choice to me — where you think a move is doing something subtly illegitimate, say exactly what, and where you think one of them effectively wins a round on a technicality that the other should have conceded, say that too.
