I have to defend the very possibility of historical explanation against an argument from chaos, and I keep losing ground each time I try. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Many historical processes are sensitive in the way chaotic systems are: at various points, differences far too small for any record to preserve — a nail working loose from a horseshoe, a courier taking one road rather than another, a decision-maker's fever — would, had they gone otherwise, have led to large differences in the subsequent course of events.

(2) Take any large historical outcome E that we standardly explain: the loss of a battle, the collapse of a state, the timing of a war. If the process leading to E was sensitive at any point, then each of the small events on the actual path was, in the circumstances, necessary for E — had any of them gone otherwise, E would not have occurred, or would have occurred differently enough that it would no longer be E.

(3) An explanation of E that omits factors necessary for E is incomplete: it leaves unexplained something without which E would not have happened.

(4) Every historical explanation of a large outcome omits almost all such small events; no narrative could do otherwise, since they are unrecorded and unrecordable.

(5) A radically incomplete explanation of E is not an explanation of E at all; at best it explains a coarsened or probabilified stand-in for E.

(6) Therefore no large historical outcome has ever been explained, and none is explainable in principle. What passes for historical explanation is really something else — narrative, hindsight, rough generalization about structural tendencies.

The colleague pressing this on me is happy with (6) as applied to history — he takes it to vindicate a Tolstoyan suspicion of great-man narratives — but he concedes the argument does not stop there. Since every explanatory enterprise abstracts from most of the causally relevant detail (weather models idealize, selection explanations ignore developmental noise, even physics omits), the same chain would show that nothing anywhere has ever been explained. Neither of us can accept that. So something in the chain is wrong; I just cannot see which link.

For what it is worth, here are my loyalties: I want to keep (1), which seems like plain truth about the world, and (4), which seems like plain truth about explanation-giving. That leaves me suspecting the trouble lies in (2) or (3), but each denial lands me somewhere uncomfortable. If I deny (3), I find myself saying explanations may omit necessary conditions — and then it becomes unclear why an explanation that mentions the archduke's route but not the weather, the horseshoes, or the state of everyone's digestion is not as arbitrary as any other truncation. If I deny (2), I seem to be denying that the small events matter, which concedes history to the structuralists on the very grounds the old rhyme about the nail, the shoe, the horse, and the rider was supposed to resist.

Tell me which step to reject and why it looked safe. Then price the alternatives. If the flaw is an equivocation, say precisely between which readings of which term; if the argument is valid under some disambiguation of its premises, say which premise is cheapest to give up and what its denial commits us to elsewhere, both in historiography and in the sciences that abstract. I am particularly interested in whether the grain of an outcome — what counts as the same E happening, rather than a different outcome — can be made to do real work here, and whether appealing to it avoids collapsing into the view that an explanation is simply whatever truncation happens to interest us.
