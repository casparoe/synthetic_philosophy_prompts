My best friend and I have been going in circles for about a year now on whether self-deception is even possible, and I'd rather have a diagnosis of why we're stuck than a ruling on who's right.

The setup: he thinks self-deception is a contradiction in terms. To deceive someone you need to know the truth and keep it from them. If you're both the deceiver and the target, you'd have to know the thing and not know it at the same time — one mind can't be the con artist and the mark. I'm a software engineer and I find his version clean: it's like asking the compiler to catch the bug in its own source, where the code doing the catching would have to already contain the thing it's supposed to flag. That state just can't exist. So on his view, everything people call self-deception decomposes into ordinary biased processing — you search harder for evidence you like, you demand less of it, you change the subject a beat early, you never run the test that could fail. Nothing paradoxical, no divided mind, and once you see that, "he's deceiving himself" is just a dramatic label for motivated reasoning. Call his side the deflationists.

My problem is that I know cases that feel like more then that. The guy whose marriage is obviously failing who "just hasn't really thought about it," who flinches when the topic comes up, who gets weirdly angry at neutral questions. Something is being managed there, and "he just never ran the test" doesn't seem to capture it. But whenever I try to say what the extra thing is, I end up saying stuff like "part of him knows," and my friend immediately asks: which part? Is he conscious of it or not? If he's conscious of it, he's not deceived; if he's not, then nothing is being hidden from anyone. And I get stuck, because both answers seem to lose the phenomenon.

We've been at exactly this point for a year. Neither of us has moved an inch and honestly neither of us can say why we hold our sides at this point — the arguments feel like post-hoc justifications for positions we arrived at some other way.

My current hypothesis (treat it as just a hypothesis): the disagreement is verbal, and it's really about whether "belief" is binary or graded, or maybe about whether "knows" is doing personal-level or subpersonal work — he's answering an architecture question about what one mind can represent, and I'm answering a descriptive question about whether "he's deceiving himself" earns its keep as a way of talking about people, and we've been treating those as the same question. But there's a nagging alternative: maybe there's a real empirical disagreement hiding in there about whether the biased-processing story actually explains the flinching and the anger, and we just don't know the psychology well enough to tell.

So what I want is: (1) a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions about how many levels a mind has that neither of us can argue for — based on what each of us actually says, not a survey of the literature; and (2) given that diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction we're missing, a kind of psychological finding that could move one of us, a change of question, or an argument that we should expect to stay stuck forever and why. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the point you're least sure of — I'd much rather get an honest hedge than a clean verdict dressed up as certainty.
