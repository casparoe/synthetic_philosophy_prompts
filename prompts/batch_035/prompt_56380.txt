I recently watched a recorded public lecture in which a bioethicist summarized what she called "the standard case against repugnant markets" — kidney sales, vote selling, price gouging during disasters. Her summary, as I noted it down, went like this:

"The philosopher Michael Sandel and others argue that markets in certain goods — kidneys, votes, emergency supplies after a hurricane — should be prohibited because they exploit the desperate. When a destitute mother sells a kidney to feed her children, her consent counts for nothing, so the sale isn't genuinely voluntary, and a market that runs on such transactions is morally akin to coercion. Economists have confirmed the second half of the case: Alvin Roth, who coined the term for these markets, showed empirically that introducing money crowds out moral motivation — paying for blood, for example, reduced the supply of blood, and fining parents for late pickup at daycare made them pick up their children later. So markets don't just allocate goods; they change who we are, and some goods — votes, body parts, basic necessities in a crisis — are too precious to be left to supply and demand. The upshot is a general rule: the more precious a good, the less it belongs in a market, and the burden of proof lies with anyone who wants to create a new market in one."

I found this persuasive, but two things nag at me. First, I vaguely recall that Roth is a market designer who built kidney-exchange programs — which sounds like the opposite of a witness for the prohibition side. Second, the lecture ran together organ sales, vote selling, and price gouging as if a single argument covered all three, and I suspect the strongest objections actually differ across these cases. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful defender of the anti-commodification position would accept as a fair statement, what is a simplification such a defender would want to qualify, and what is simply wrong. In particular, I would like to know whether the lecturer's exploitation framing matches how philosophers in this tradition actually structure their case or whether it collapses distinct objections they keep separate; whether Roth and the empirical crowding-out results are being presented accurately, or as more settled and more one-sided than they are; and whether her closing burden-of-proof rule follows from the parts of the summary that do survive scrutiny. I came away half-convinced that kidney sales should remain illegal, and I would like to know how much of that conviction the summary's errors are responsible for, as opposed to its sound parts. Finally, please be explicit about where your audit is most likely to go wrong — which attribution or empirical claim you are least confident about — and what argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict on whether the summary, taken as a whole, is fair.
