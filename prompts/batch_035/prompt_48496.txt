In most liberal democracies, bribery law criminalizes only the quid pro quo: an official must trade a specific exercise of discretion for a specific benefit. Around that narrow core, the same legal systems tolerate a wide penumbra: ethics rules that permit gifts to officials up to a modest ceiling, campaign contributions that are legal precisely because they are open and registered rather than concealed, and lobbying that is regulated through disclosure rather than banned. Political scientists have long sorted such cases by who condemns them: practices that both the public and elites condemn count as black corruption, practices that elites condemn but citizens tolerate as gray, and practices that no one condemns as white; the rules I have described look as if they were drafted from somewhere inside that scheme, criminalizing the black cases and merely registering the rest. What view of public office does this arrangement presuppose — that office is a kind of market in which access and influence may be priced, so long as the transaction is transparent and stays below certain ceilings, or that office is a trust in which the exchange logic itself, rather than the concealment or the size of the payment, is the wrong-making feature? Reconstruct the implicit theory that could license all three elements at once (the bribery ban, the gift ceiling, and the contribution-plus-disclosure regime), say whether the elements are mutually consistent or whether they in fact presuppose rival views, and tell me what would have to change in the rules if the trust conception were enforced strictly: would the ceilings and disclosure registers be replaced by outright prohibition, or would they drop out as no longer needed?
