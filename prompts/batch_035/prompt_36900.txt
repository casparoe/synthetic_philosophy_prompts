A novelist friend of mine who does a philosophy podcast tried to explain Kripke's book on Wittgenstein and rule-following to me, and I want to use the idea for a character — a translator who slowly becomes convinced she's never meant anything by her words — but I don't want to butcher it. His summary: 'Kripke proved that there is no fact of the matter about what anyone means by a word. You think you mean addition by "plus", but for all you know you might have meant "quus" — a function that matches addition on every calculation you've ever done and then diverges — and nothing in your past behavior or your past mental life could rule that out. So Wittgenstein concluded that meaning is a kind of useful fiction: there's nothing there, but language still works because we all agree to go on treating each other as if we meant things.' Is this fair? Some of what bugs me: did Kripke think he *proved* this, or is it a skeptical argument the book sets up and then answers somehow (I've heard the phrase "skeptical solution" but didn't follow it)? And is it really Wittgenstein's conclusion, or Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein? The "useful fiction" bit also sounds more deflationary then I'd expect. Go through his summary and tell me which parts a philosopher would accept, which are subtly off, and what my translator character should actually be worried about.
