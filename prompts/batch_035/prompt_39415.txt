As a novelist, I find myself returning to Groundhog Day whenever I try to think about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility: the claim that no one can be truly responsible for what they do, because responsibility for an act would require responsibility for the self that performs it, and responsibility for that self would require having made oneself the way one is — an impossibility, since one did not choose the self that began the choosing. Read the film through that argument. The crux is Phil's transformation: everything that makes it possible comes from outside him — the loop itself, which he neither caused nor chose; the endless time to practice and fail; the day's fixed script of small sufferings he is forced to witness, down to the old man who dies no matter what Phil does. If his redemption is wholly a product of circumstances he did not create, does the film dramatize Strawson's point — that even the most admirable among us is, in the deep sense, off the hook? Or does it resist the argument, since within the loop Phil still has to do the work of changing, and the film plainly wants us to admire him for it? I would like a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously, and that closes by saying whether the film ends where Strawson himself ends — he holds the argument sound but nearly inert, changing almost nothing about how we should live — or whether it manages to say something the argument, as an argument, cannot.
