I'm a structural engineer who audited an evening course in metaphysics last spring, mostly out of curiosity, and one topic left me with a puzzle I can't find a name for. It's about change. I bent a bracket in the shop last week: before, it was straight; now, the very same bracket is bent, and nobody thinks the bracket went out of existence in between. I moved a desk from my garage to my study: same desk, now in a place it wasn't. I used to be shorter than my sister; now I'm taller. What bothers me is that these changes don't look relational. Becoming an uncle is easy — it's a relation to my sister's child, and I can change my relational properties without changing at all, just as I become farther from Paris when Paris doesn't move. But being bent isn't a relation to anything; the bentness is just how the bracket is, full stop. Yet "the bracket is bent" was false last Tuesday and true today, of one and the same bracket. So either the bracket has a property simpliciter that it both has and lacks, or the property is somehow had relative to something — but relative to what? If the answer is "a time," I don't see where the time got into the sentence, and "bent at Tuesday" starts to look like a relation after all, which seems to give up on the bracket simply being bent.

My friend, who is finishing a physics PhD, says I'm overcomplicating it: objects are extended in time the way a pole is extended in space, so the bracket is one long item whose earlier stretch is straight and whose later stretch is bent, and "the bracket is bent" is true of a stretch of it, the way "this pole is under the overhang" is true of a portion of the pole. Two things about this bother me. First, when I point at a pole and say "this pole is ten feet long," the whole pole is right there in front of me; if the bracket is a long thing extended in time, then when I say "the bracket is bent" I seem to be talking about only a slice of it — but my sentence never felt partitive, and I never meant "part of the bracket is bent." Second, I'm not sure the picture explains anything rather than renaming it: saying the bent stretch and the straight stretch are portions of one long bracket just relocates my question, since now I want to know what makes them portions of one bracket rather than two neighbors.

So, my questions. Does this cluster of puzzles have an established name, and is there a literature on it? If so, tell me what the name is, what the main responses are — including whether my friend's picture is one of the standard ones — and what the strongest complaint against each response is. If what I've described is really several distinct puzzles wearing one coat, I'd like them separated cleanly. And if nothing established fits exactly, say so and coin a term: define it precisely and mark the boundary cases. One thing to head off: I'm not asking about Ship-of-Theseus-type puzzles about which of several candidates the rebuilt ship is, or about how much material can be swapped before a thing counts as new. I'm prepared to grant that identity over time is settled. My puzzle is about what the bearers of changeable, apparently non-relational properties must be like in cases where there is no dispute at all about which thing we're talking about. The label matters less to me than the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges run.

Please structure your answer as follows: (1) "What you've noticed, and its name" — roughly 200 words identifying the puzzle or puzzles; (2) "Nearest neighbors and what separates them" — roughly 250 words on the nearby concepts and the differences; (3) "The responses on offer" — roughly 450 words laying out the main positions and the worst objection to each, including whether my friend's picture is among them; (4) "Boundary cases and coinage" — roughly 200 words on the edges of the phenomenon and, if the label doesn't exist, your own term with a definition.
