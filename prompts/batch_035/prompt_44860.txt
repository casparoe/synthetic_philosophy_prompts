I teach ethics, not political philosophy, but a colleague of mine is deep into Nozick, and after years of hallway arguments I've noticed my own gut verdicts don't add up to anything I can state. Let me just list them.

If a million people each voluntarily pay twenty-five cents to watch a once-in-a-generation basketball player, and he ends up with far more than anyone else, nothing unjust happened — even if the resulting pattern wrecks the distribution we'd approved of beforehand. But in actual societies, where a lot of wealth traces back centuries to conquest and land seizure, I don't think the current holders have clean title just because every transaction since the seizure was voluntary. Except I also find the claims of the original owners' great-great-grandchildren much weaker than "tainted title" would predict — the taint seems to fade somehow, though I can't say where or why. Inheriting a legitimately earned fortune seems perfectly fine to me. And taxing it for redistribution feels like robbery in the voluntary world but like partial restitution, maybe even something owed, in the conquest world — same tax, opposite verdicts.

Is there a principle that captures all this, or close? My colleague says Nozick himself admitted that in the real world the entitlement conditions never actualy held, and offered something about rectification — but nobody seems to know how far back rectification goes or where it stops. If no principle saves all my verdicts, tell me which one has to go and why that one rather than another. And then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for a case I haven't given: reparations where descendants of the harmed and of the beneficiaries long ago intermarried, so the tax and the check would partly go to the same households.
