I'm taking a module this term on philosophical method, and we've just finished a segment on thought experiments and intuition pumps — how they can do real argumentative work, and how their vividness and stipulations can smuggle in conclusions. For my essay I want to read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' as a case study, and I'd like your help thinking it through. The story is a thought experiment in narrative form: a utopian city whose happiness depends, by strict stipulation, on the perpetual misery of one child. But it's also unusually self-conscious about its own machinery. The narrator keeps adjusting the description to the reader's taste — asking us to imagine the city as our own fancy bids, adding technology or orgies if we find the place too bland — and the famous walk away at the end is deliberately left blank. Read the story as an intuition pump in Dennett's sense, but also against that idea: does the narrative form answer the standard objections to intuition pumps (stipulation without argument, framing effects, no way to check the premises), or does it amplify them — for instance, does the stipulated necessity of the child's suffering do the argumentative work while vividness makes it feel discovered rather than assumed? And what does the thin, undescribed ending do: is the refusal to characterise the ones who walk away an honest limit or a way of flattering the reader's escape fantasy? I'd like a committed reading: say plainly whether Omelas is a fair pump or a rigged one, and what it manages to do that the standard trolley-style examples never can. Please answer in British English, and don't rely on quotations from the text beyond what I've summarised.
