I'm a philosophy PhD student running the discussion sections for a large intro ethics lecture — three sections of roughly 22 students each, 80 minutes, low-tech classroom, no real stakes permitted. The lecture has already covered Mill and Kant, and my students have emerged with what I find a discouragingly tidy picture: utilitarianism says push the fat man, Kantianism says don't, pick your team. What they have no feel for is the specific internal pressure on the deontological side — not the banal fact that constraints cost you good outcomes, but the genuinely odd feature that a constraint against, say, killing forbids exactly the act that would minimize the number of killings. The constraint appears to demand that you allow more of the very thing it exists to prevent, and it does so precisely because the constraint binds *you* rather than binding the world. I want an activity where they run into that structure with their own choices before anyone names it. The phrase "paradox of deontology" should not appear until the debrief at the earliest, and I'd rather they never hear "agent-relative" until the mapping at the end.

Constraints on the design. First, no transplant cases, no bridges, no trolleys, no drownings — this cohort has seen all of them in high school debate and produces canned answers on contact. Whatever the forbidden act is, it should be stylized enough that they can't pattern-match it to a stock case, but described vividly enough that they feel like authors of it rather than movers of tokens. Second, the aggregate must be visible and quantified — a running public tally that makes the impersonal arithmetic unmistakable by the end — while the rule against committing the act yourself must be felt as applying to each participant personally, in the first person, at the moment of choice. If the game ends with students merely regretting a coordination failure, it has failed; they should end individually confident they did the right thing while staring at a board showing that their doing the right thing, multiplied across the room, produced more of the forbidden thing than a single violation by one of them would have. Third, they should have to commit or refrain *live*, in front of peers, not on a written sheet collected and tabulated.

I have a sketch of my own and I suspect it's broken, so tell me whether to repair or replace it. My mechanics so far are basically a threshold public-goods game: each round one student is designated the actor for that round; the actor can either commit one violation (pay a token into a "fault" pool) and thereby cancel a scheduled batch of five violations against the group's common score, or decline and let the batch stand. Five rounds, escalating batches, a scoreboard throughout. Two problems I can already see: (a) the utilitarian answer is transparent from round one, so nobody is tempted by the constraint and nothing interesting happens; and (b) more fundamentally, paying the token isn't a violation of anything these students care about — it's just a fee — so the deontological pull never gets a grip at all. The forbidden act has to *be* the thing the rule is about, and the rule has to be one they've internalized during the activity itself, not one I announce. I don't know how to get both the internalized personal rule and the clean arithmetic, and I suspect that tension is the actual design problem.

So, deliverables: full rules and materials (cards, tokens, whatever is needed for 22 students in 80 minutes, with the last 20 reserved for debrief), the payoff scheme, a round-by-round timeline, and the script or structure for the debrief — specifically the moment where the board shows the aggregate and each student is asked whether they'd change their choice now, and how to handle it when some of them say yes and others dig in. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect social pressure, a student who treats the whole thing as an exercise in looking virtuous, and confusion about whether the rule is moral or arbitrary — and how to preempt each. And be honest about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief: my worry is that whatever I invent, committing a stylized violation in a classroom carries no remainder, whereas the actual view I'm trying to make them feel has its grip partly in the fact that the victim has a claim against *you specifically*, not against the world. If you think the answer is that the game should be iterated with rotating roles so that every student ends up on both sides of the rule, say so, but show me how that works with my section sizes and time budget.
