I am one of three trustees of a small family charitable foundation established by my late father, and a clause he wrote into his letter of wishes has recently become the source of a deadlock among us. The clause instructs the trustees to fund the causes he would have supported had he known everything we now know — his own phrasing was that we should follow not his conclusions but what he would have concluded with better information and more time to reflect. He added it after a late-life experience of changing his mind on something important to him, so it was not boilerplate; he had watched his own views shift under pressure of evidence and wanted the foundation to keep shifting after his death. The literalist among us says this is unworkable and faintly fraudulent: a preference he never actually held is not his preference, and any attempt to construct one is just us laundering our own opinions through his memory — so we should stick strictly to the programs he funded while alive. The other trustee is happy to extrapolate, but does it so cheerfully that she is effectively rewriting him: she has redirected a grant he cared about toward a cause she prefers, on the grounds that he would have come around eventually, and I can see no difference between that and simply overruling him. I am caught in the middle and suspect both of them are wrong in instructive ways.

The dispute keeps circling cases that feel different to me but that neither of them distinguishes. My father refused to fund computer-literacy teaching because he believed it damaged children's attention — the evidence he relied on has since weakened considerably. He also restricted scholarships to students with the very top grades, which now looks to me like a moral error about fairness rather than a factual one, though he might have disagreed. And there is at least one value he changed on his own near the end of his life, which suggests his preferences were not fixed points but trajectories. I want to know whether there is any principled way to tell apart his actual preferences, his counterfactual improved preferences, and preferences we would simply be inventing for him — and whether the letter's instruction is coherent at all, given that if the two extrapolating trustees extrapolate differently, there seems to be no fact of the matter about whose version is his.

I need to walk into our next meeting with my thinking organized, and I would like your response as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "structure_of_the_dispute": one or two sentences identifying what the disagreement between my co-trustees is actually about, beneath the personalities.
- "positions": an array with one entry per trustee, each containing "best_version" (the strongest defensible form of that trustee's stance) and "where_it_breaks" (the point at which that stance fails, and why).
- "distinguishing_tests": an array of concrete criteria or questions that would sort his actual preferences from his extrapolated ones and both from preferences we would be inventing — for instance, how to treat a preference resting on a factual error he would have wanted corrected, versus a value he might never have moved on.
- "recommended_protocol": what I should propose the trustees adopt, in two to four sentences, given that we must make decisions about specific grants either way.
- "residual_uncertainty": what part of this genuinely cannot be resolved by any principle, stated plainly.
