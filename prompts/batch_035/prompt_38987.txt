A recent diagnosis — a degenerative condition whose course my doctors put at somewhere between eighteen months and three years — has turned the question of whether death is bad for the person who dies from a seminar topic into something I need to settle for myself. Before deciding how to spend what remains, I have been recording my verdicts on cases and looking for the principle behind them, if there is one. Here is what I find myself judging:

A healthy forty-year-old killed without warning is very badly off by dying; a healthy ninety-year-old killed the same way seems to me barely worse off at all, so closely does death follow a full life. A forty-year-old who, instead of dying, falls into a permanent unconscious state and survives without experiences to ninety seems to me somewhat worse off dead than comatose — though I cannot say what the extra badness of death consists in, since the goods foreseen and foregone look the same in both cases. The endless nonexistence before my birth strikes me as no misfortune for me whatsoever, while a nonexistence of the same length after my death does strike me as one, and I hold this asymmetry even though I have never been able to give a reason for it. A forty-year-old whose remaining life, had she lived, would have been dominated by severe suffering does not seem to me to be badly off at all in dying — if anything slightly spared. And two further cases pull against each other: an acquaintance in her eighties, lucid, who has for years said she has done everything she wanted and is ready, seems to me barely worse off in dying; yet another woman of the same age with an unfulfilled longing she always meant to act on seems worse off in dying, even though both are deprived of about the same amount of future — while a thirty-year-old who sincerely and stably claims not to care whether he goes on living still seems to me very badly off in dying, as though his indifference does not reduce the badness the way the elderly woman's satisfaction does.

What I want from you is a principle, stated precisely, that captures these verdicts — or, if none can, a definite decision about which of my verdicts must go, with your reasons for overturning those rather than the others. Please do not present me with a menu of options and let me choose; commit to one account, tell me honestly which of the above it cannot accommodate, and defend the accommodation or the overturning. I would add one requirement of honesty: if part of the account rests on a contested move, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over, since I am going to have to live with the result. Having stated the principle, please tell me what it implies for two cases I have not listed. First: whether goods I will never experience — letters written now to be opened by grandchildren after my death, a fund established for their education, a book a colleague will finish from my notes — count toward how bad my death is for me, and so whether they are things I ought to be spending my remaining months on rather than experiences of my own. Second, and this is the one I most want answered: whether, on the principle you settle on, I should regard my own coming death as bad for me in the way the forty-year-old's is, or in the way the ready woman's is — and what, if anything, that difference should change about how I face it.
