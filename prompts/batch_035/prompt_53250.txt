I write alternate-history novels, and after twenty years I've developed an ear for which counterfactuals feel live and which feel like fantasy. I'd like to know whether there's a principle behind that ear — and whether it tracks how history actually works, or only what readers of the genre will accept.

My verdicts, such as they are: a novel in which the Sarajevo assassination fails but a general European war erupts in 1916 anyway strikes me as fully plausible — the alliances were set, the general staffs had their timetables, and a crisis was going to come. A novel in which the Reformation fizzles because Luther dies young strikes me as cheating: the press, the corrupt curia, and rising lay literacy produce some rupture with or without him. A novel in which Lee wins at Gettysburg and the North lets the South go I can barely make work — every chapter after the divergence has to be nudged toward the outcome, and readers smell the thumb on the scale. And yet a novel in which Alexander falls early in Asia and the eastern Mediterranean develops into something unrecognizable seems to me not just possible but natural — though I suspect that's because I know antiquity so thinly that everything in it feels up for grabs. The two battle cases are what trouble me most: one battle lost in Pennsylvania and I protest that history doesn't bend that cheaply; one battle lost in Asia and I accept that everything downstream gets rewritten.

My tentative principle is this: a counterfactual is live when the actual outcome was overdetermined — when enough independent causes were already converging on it that adding or removing one doesn't turn it. That explains the 1916 war and the Reformation, and it explains Gettysburg if I say Union victory in the long war was overdetermined, so Confederate independence requires fighting the overdetermination. But I can't tell whether the fit is genuine or whether I've reverse-engineered a rationale out of my genre's habits — alternate history is written and read by people who already believe great men moved antiquity and forces move modernity. So: is there a principled account that saves my verdicts, or must some of them go, and which? What does the resulting view say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a novel in which the Mongol withdrawal from central Europe in 1242, usually credited to the great khan's death, never happens and the invasion rolls on — is the later shape of Europe live to be transformed by that, or was the retreat overdetermined by distance and pasture regardless? And does the principle draw a line I could actually use in my craft — a point in history before which a single life is a usable divergence point and after which it is not?
