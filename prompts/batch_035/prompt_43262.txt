Background: I'm a retired physicist (statistical mechanics, mostly, last twenty years in materials science) who fell hard for philosophy after retiring, and my current reading group is doing Leibniz. I went in expecting to find the best-of-all-possible-worlds stuff ridiculous, and the ridiculous parts are still ridiculous, but the argument underneath it got its hooks into me. Anyway, I wrote up my own position after the discussion, and now I want someone to tell me whether it's a stable package or whether I've assembled a machine that can't run. You're a professional philosopher with a specialty in Leibniz and early modern rationalism, but please don't lecture me on what Leibniz actually said unless it matters for my question — the object here is my own set of views.

Here's what I believe. (1) The principle of sufficient reason: no fact obtains without there being a reason why it is so and not otherwise. I know Hume and later Russell dumped on this, but as a working scientist I never once got to say "it just is," and I can't accept it now either — especially not about things like the values of the fundamental constants, where "brute fact" strikes me as giving up. (2) The principle of least action is not mere bookkeeping. The way I was taught it, it's just an elegant repackaging of the differential equations, but the more I sit with it the more it strikes me as metaphysically real: the trajectory of a thrown ball genuinely is the one that extremizes the action over the whole path, and the fact that dynamics can be written as an optimization problem at all feels like it's telling us something about the world, not just about our formalism. (3) I'm an atheist, and comfortably so — there's no chooser, no mind surveying options and picking. (4) The history of life on this planet is saturated with waste. Five mass extinctions, the overwhelming majority of species dead, smallpox grinding on for centuries. Evolution is a tinkerer that routinely gets trapped on local optima, and I see no way to describe the biological record as any kind of optimum, local or global. (5) I think symmetries and conservation laws are the closest thing physics has to necessity — Noether showed that they come together, and I'm genuinely tempted to say the deep structure of the laws could not have been otherwise. (6) I think quantum chance is real: Bell-type experiments did in the local hidden variable programs, and the particular moment at which a given atom decays has no sufficient reason, period, and I'm at peace with that. (7) Possible worlds talk leaves me cold — I take it as useful formalism for logicians, not as a claim that there's a space of alternatives out there that something could be choosing among.

So: does this hang together? Some of these feel like they rub against each other — for instance I can see that if everything needs a reason why it's so and not otherwise, someone's going to ask me for the reason the laws are these, and if I say the laws are necessary, that seems to drag the wasteful actual history in with it, which I refuse. And the least action thing and the extinction thing both live in my head as claims about what kind of world this is. But I genuinely don't know which pairs are the real problems and which ones dissolve if you're careful — I suspect at least one of them is a pseudo-conflict, since we were always taught the action formulation and the local formulation are equivalent. Please think carefully before answering, maybe start with a quick brainstorm of which commitments actually interact and which are independent, and then go through the real tensions ranked by severity, and for each one tell me what would have to give and roughly what giving it up costs me. I'd rather hear that I have to abandon something I care about than get a diplomatic "they're all fine together."
