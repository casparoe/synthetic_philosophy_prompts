As part of my coursework in an upper-level bioethics seminar, I have to give a twenty-minute presentation defending a moderate version of the hedonistic imperative — the program, associated with David Pearce, of using biotechnology to phase out suffering throughout sentient life. My version is deliberately moderate: I will argue not for wireheading or uniform bliss, but for engineering what Pearce calls hedonic set-points upward while preserving motivational gradients, so that experience remains informative about the world — we still prefer success to failure, we just stop paying for the difference in subjective agony. I want to claim that this is a worthwhile long-term research program and, eventually, a legitimate use of reproductive genetic choice, while conceding that it is not obligatory in the way strong utilitarians claim.

The audience is the problem. The seminar is taught by a professor whose own work is on the expressivist objection — the disability-studies critique that selecting against a trait sends a social message that devalues people who live with that trait, whatever the parents' intentions. Most of my classmates share that orientation; several have argued in previous sessions that prenatal selection even for serious conditions is continuous with eugenics. There are also two MD/PhD students, one in psychiatry and one in neuroscience, who will hear my proposal as a claim about brain plasticity and will think in terms of opponent-process regulation and homeostatic compensation — the phenomenon where manipulating a reward system triggers counter-regulation that drags affect back toward a set range. And there is one classmate who is a straightforward Singer-style utilitarian, who will push me to go further than I want to go: if suffering is bad, why settle for gentle gradients, and why restrict the program to humans rather than factory-farm animals, where the numbers are astronomical?

Please prepare a Q&A sheet for me. List the eight or so objections I will actually face from this room, in their vocabulary rather than mine — the expressivist worry about the message sent by selecting against low-mood capacities, the Deaf-community analogy to cochlear implants and the erasure of a culture, the eugenics-continuity charge, the neuroscience objection that hedonic adaptation makes the whole project self-defeating at the level of mechanism, and the utilitarian's complaint that my moderation is arbitrary — ordered roughly by how much damage each could do to my grade and my credibility. For each, give a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and note honestly where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front in the presentation itself so that I am not conceding it under fire. Identify the one reply that has to land for my position to survive the seminar at all. Finally, tell me the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — the one I most need a prepared answer for even though I would rather it never came up.
