I have drifted, over the last few years, towards a view about what matters in a life which I want to put under proper scrutiny before acting on it: that the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and of suffering, and that what we call pleasure contributes nothing in itself — it is merely the felt ending of a want, and once no want is outstanding, there is nothing further that a life could use. Call this the negativist view. Rather than testing it piecemeal against intuitions as they happen to strike me, I should first construct the standard any theory of this shape must meet. Please write me that specification: the desiderata an adequate theory of what matters in a life has to satisfy, where by adequate I mean fit to guide a person's own choices about how to live — not to rank lives for public purposes.

Number the desiderata so that they can be referred to singly. Between them they should settle, at minimum: the cases the theory must get right — including whether placid wantlessness (the contented retiree, or the contemplative whose desires have slowly burnt out) really does no better than a life of intense striving in which every want is satisfied; whether unmotivated positive affect (a bliss that arrives without any prior want) improves a life at all; and what to say about a friend who, after a minor stroke, reports no suffering but also no pull towards anything, and insists that on balance his life is about the same as before; the distinctions it must respect — between the absence of suffering and the presence of pleasure, between relief and reward, between a want not being felt and a want being satisfied, and between a life not going badly and a life going well; and the empirical findings it cannot contradict — hedonic adaptation, and the dissociability of wanting and liking in the affective sciences.

Then do three things with the list. First, mark which desiderata are genuine and which are prejudices of one camp — in particular, whether the demand that some states be positively good, and not merely not bad, is a real requirement or a hedonist's parochialism. Second, tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: I suspect that the requirement to discriminate between lives that differ only in positive feel sits badly with the requirement to explain why we prefer rich equanimity to dreamless sleep, and I want to know whether that tension is fatal or merely apparent. Third, identify which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and then score standard hedonism, desire-satisfaction theories, and objective-list theories against the list — briefly, as the scoring is the least important part; the criteria and their motivating cases are where I want the weight.

End with a final line of the form \bottomline{...}, enclosing "no conflict" if the desiderata are jointly satisfiable; otherwise the numbers of a jointly unsatisfiable subset, for example \bottomline{3, 7}; or, if you judge that no adequate list can be met by the negativist view at all, \bottomline{negativist view untenable}.
