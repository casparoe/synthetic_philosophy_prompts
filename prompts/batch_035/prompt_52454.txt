Longtime LessWrong/EA Forum reader here, been in rationalist group houses for like a decade. Last week our house had a whole blowup: Alice posted to the mailing list calling out Bob for never doing his dishes, complete with a photo of the counter. Then someone dug up that Alice herself had a two-week chore strike back in February, and the thread became entirely about whether Alice was allowed to say anything at all. Bob never disputed the dishes — he just kept replying that she was "the last person who gets to talk." It half resolved itself when people got tired of it, but I've been chewing on it since and keep flip-floping.

Please write a dialogue between two characters hashing this out: one who holds that hypocrisy genuinely forfeits your standing to blame (the forfeiture person), and one who holds that hypocritical blame is still correct in its content — at worst awkward or unfair in it's delivery, not something the target gets to dismiss. Make them both competent and make them actually engage with each other's points. I want the forfeiture person to have a real story about WHY hypocrisy matters and not just vibes — e.g. something like blame being a demand the blamer implicitly claims to be meeting herself, or the target's entitlement to resent being governed by someone who exempts herself from the same rule. And I want the other one to press the classic counterexamples: the reformed ex-smoker who seems to have MORE standing than a never-smoker despite a perfect hypocrisy score, and the awkward fact that "you did it too" is a textbook fallacy everywhere else. Ideally they'd also touch on the difference between lacking standing to blame and the blame simply being unkind or unfair, and on whether blaming a third party in front of the culprit ("didactic blame," like sighing about the dishes to your other housemate within earshot) dodges the hypocrisy problem entirely.

Have them use Alice and Bob as their running example if that helps ground it. At the end, please add a short postscript where you say which side you'd actually bet on and — more importantly — what argument or consideration would move you to the other side, or where the dialogue as written is most likely to have talked past a stronger version of the opposing view. I'd rather know the weakest joint in the reasoning than get a confident verdict handed to me.
