I'm a professor of philosophy at a university with a large divinity school, and after thirty-eight years in the department my closest colleague is retiring. I've been asked to give the principal toast at his retirement dinner. He is a Molinist, and has been since his dissertation: he holds that God governs the world through middle knowledge — that God selected this world knowing, from all eternity, what every possible creature would freely do in every possible circumstance — and that this secures exhaustive providence without causal determinism. The room will be roughly half philosophers, including at least one Calvinist who regards middle knowledge as a sixteenth-century intrusion, and half family, church friends, and former students who will hear "God has a plan" as unproblematic common ground. Both halves constrain what I can say. If I say only that God has a plan, I will have said nothing that required his forty years of work, and the toast will betray him by omission. If I explain counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, the other half is lost, and the toast becomes the seminar he spent a career trying not to give. And some things I must not say at all: he has never accepted that God determines each event, so any line about God having "ordained" his career, or about his marriage being "meant" in a sense that bypasses their free choosing, would state what he regards as false.

Please draft the toast — about six minutes, warm, and funny if it can be. I want at least one moment where the Molinist claim does real work: a line or joke that could not be delivered unchanged at the retirement of a Calvinist colleague. I also want it to be generous to the compatibilists and the Calvinist in the room rather than a victory lap; he has always argued with them as friends, and the toast should sound like that. Finally, it should end facing his actual retirement rather than in abstraction: the divinity school has offered him a part-time post, which I am confident he will decline, and I'd like the closing to make sense of that choice in his own terms — what a man might do with the remaining years once God, who always knew what he would freely choose, has stopped scheduling them for him.
