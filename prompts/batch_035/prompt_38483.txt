For a seminar paper on the doctrine of double effect, I've written down my settled verdicts on the trolley cases, and my TA has asked me to confirm the package is coherent before I build the paper around it. Here is what I hold:

(1) In the standard switch case, it is permissible to divert the runaway trolley onto the side track, killing one to save five; in the standard footbridge case, it is wrong to push the man into the trolley's path.

(2) The doctrine of double effect gives the correct explanation of this difference: in the switch case the death of the one is a merely foreseen side-effect of turning the threat away from the five, whereas at the footbridge the death is intended as the very means of stopping the trolley.

(3) In the loop variant — the side track curves back toward the five, so that if the one man were not lying on it, the trolley would travel around the loop and kill all five; his body is what physically stops it — I still judge that diverting is permissible.

(4) A friend who has read further into this literature than I have offered me a way to hold (2) and (3) together. Her claim is that the diversion in the loop case is the very same act it would be if the side track dead-ended and no one lay on it; the additional danger created by the man's presence is a further, extrinsic fact about the situation, not part of the act itself, and that is why the diversion remains permissible even though the agent's plan now relies on his body stopping the trolley. I am inclined to accept her story.

(5) I also hold that the distinction between killing and letting die carries independent moral weight, over and above the intended/foreseen distinction.

(6) Finally, I believe that whether an act is permissible cannot turn on remote counterfactuals about situations that did not obtain — the permissibility of what I actually do is fixed by the actual situation, not by how things would have gone under hypothetical variations of it.

Please audit these six commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and at what cost? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and do not assume I have a favorite commitment to give up. Where you come down on a verdict, please say what would change your mind — which case, argument, or consideration would move you to the opposite conclusion — and indicate where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
