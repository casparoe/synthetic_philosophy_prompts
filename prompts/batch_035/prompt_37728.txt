Compare maxmin expected utility and imprecise probabilities as rival ways of taking the Ellsberg paradox seriously rather than explaining it away. As I understand the two positions: each replaces the single prior over an ambiguous urn with a set of priors, but they part ways at the last step. Maxmin expected utility, in the formulation of Gilboa and Schmeidler, evaluates each act by its worst-case expected utility over the set and chooses the act with the best such evaluation. The imprecise-probability view, associated with Levi and Walley, instead lets the comparison between acts remain indeterminate when the priors in the set disagree about which act is better. I would like a careful comparison that takes both on their own terms, covering at least: (i) which observed Ellsberg-type choice patterns each can represent — in particular, what the imprecise view can say at all once a choice is forced, and whether plain maxmin expected utility can represent an agent whose attitude toward ambiguity varies with stakes; (ii) the role of hedging, since both stories are usually said to attribute Ellsberg preferences to the fact that ambiguous and risky acts hedge differently — say whether this is equally true on both pictures and what rides on it; and (iii) what rationality amounts to on each picture, given that maxmin expected utility builds a decision rule into the representation while the imprecise view refuses to, and it matters whether that refusal is a virtue or an evasion.

Then test both on a case. Take Ellsberg's three-color urn: 30 red balls, and 60 more that are black and yellow in unknown proportion. The standard finding is that people bet on red rather than black, and on black-or-yellow rather than red-or-yellow. Now make it sequential. Before choosing between (a) a bet on black paying $100 and (b) a sure $50, I offer you, for $10, the option of first seeing one ball drawn at random with replacement — the color is revealed, the ball goes back, and then you choose. Many people who would take the sure $50 in the one-shot version plausibly pay the $10 in the sequential version: seeing a black or a yellow ball seems like real news. (Note that a red ball is not: every prior in the set agrees that red comes up one time in three, so a red observation confirms nothing and rules nothing out.) Say what each framework predicts about the value of this peek. For maxmin expected utility: under prior-by-prior updating of the set, can any observation move the worst-case ranking of the two options, and what does that imply about what the agent should be willing to pay — is the peek worth nothing, less than nothing, or something? For the imprecise view: does the option to learn have value precisely because it can shrink the set of priors, and can that value even be priced while comparisons remain indeterminate? Be explicit about any assumption you need concerning how a set of priors is updated, and flag it if you are reaching for a principle, such as dynamic consistency, that the framework itself does not supply. Finally, tell me whether the sequential case genuinely discriminates between the two frameworks, or whether it shows only that both inherit the same unresolved problem about ambiguity and learning.
