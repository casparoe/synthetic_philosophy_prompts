I've been keeping a notebook of my own gut verdicts about how to update on evidence that comes in uncertain — not "the coin landed heads," but "I'm 70% sure that's a jellyfish rather than a cloth" — and I'd like to know whether some one principle of rational update captures the pattern, or whether I have to give some verdicts up.

For clarity, the rule I take to be on the table works like this: when your new evidence leaves you with weights on a partition (say, .6 jellyfish, .4 cloth), you set your new probabilities for the cells to those weights and hold fixed your conditional probabilities given each cell, letting everything else shift only as those fixed conditionals drag it. My verdicts:

1. A colleague tells me a coin is biased 2:1 toward heads; I'd put .7 on his being right. I end up between my prior for heads and 2:3, and this rule gives what feels like the right landing point. Treating his report as flat-out evidence and then discounting feels like double-counting my doubt.
2. Glimpsing a cloth-like shape on the beach and becoming 60% sure it's a jellyfish: I should raise my credence in a sting in proportion to that .6 — not a full conditional-style jump, not nothing. The rule feels right for graded looks.
3. Two independent glimpses of the same object, each leaving me 70% on jellyfish: my final credence should exceed .7. But the rule, applied twice to the same partition, just resets my jellyfish credence to .7 again — the second input overrides the first instead of accumulating with it. Something about treating the new weights as brute inputs is wrong here.
4. The jellyfish glimpse, plus an independent rumor that jellyfish are infesting the coast: order of processing shouldn't matter, and I believe the rule gets that right — provided neither piece changes what the other indicates.
5. A day of malaise leaves me 70% sure I'm coming down with something, without telling me what. Then I hear a flu outbreak has started. Now the malaise means something different: I should re-evaluate it in light of the flu news, and I can't get that by holding my malaise-conditionals fixed while updating on flu. Order matters here, and should.
6. A strong premonition that some news broke overnight, with no proposition I can point to: I keep concluding no update is licensed at all, though I can't see why a caused-by-news state should be evidentially inert.

My tentative gloss is that every genuine case of uncertain evidence is ordinary conditioning in disguise, on some suitably enriched partition — the two-glimpse case is really conditioning on "received signal 1 and signal 2," the malaise case on getting-sick-and-flu-going-around — and that apparent order-sensitivity marks a failure to have found the partition. Test that gloss against the cases and repair or bury it. If it survives, say what stops it from being vacuous: a rule that can always retroactively find the partition that fits smells like curve-fitting with unlimited parameters. Then tell me which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and what it says about the one I flip on: a glimpse I suspect might have been a reflection rather than a view of the object itself.

One request: if you think some of my verdicts rest on conflating distinct things — say, the strength of a signal with the reliability of its source, or my confidence in a proposition with the weight of the evidence for it — say so plainly and tell me which verdicts are artifacts, rather than constructing a principle that humors all of them. I'd rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that saves them by fiat.
