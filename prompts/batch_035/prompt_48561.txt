I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has joined a reading group on philosophy of technology; the members are educated non-specialists, and I have been asked to lead next month's session on the question of what the failure of advanced AI systems would actually look like. Several members arrive convinced that the worry is essentially the scenario of the Terminator films: a discrete machine uprising. What I want the session to land, instead, are two distinct failure pictures that get merged in casual discussion: one in which a system accumulates influence over its environment as an instrumental by-product of optimizing for a mis-specified objective, and one in which nothing seeks anything at all but the institutions and norms surrounding the system gradually drift, with every measured indicator improving along the way. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the material: the order in which the pieces must come (I suspect that optimization for a proxy has to be established before either failure picture makes sense — confirm or correct me), the one concrete case that should carry the whole session (I am considering recommender systems for online content, on the ground that they exhibit influence-seeking behavior without anyone having designed them to seek influence — is that the right carrier, and what must I be careful to say it does not show?), the misconceptions I should expect and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off (I anticipate: that failure must be an event rather than a condition; that the system must want something in a rich sense; that mis-specification is a defect that ordinary testing would catch), one question I can put to the group at the close whose answers would tell me whether they have genuinely separated the two failure pictures rather than collapsed them into a generic "things go wrong", and what I should openly decline to cover in ninety minutes.
