I'm running a grad seminar on non-classical logic this fall, and I keep noticing that whenever dialetheism comes up, everybody — my students included — reaches for the same two exhibits: the liar and Russell's set. Fine, but that makes dialetheism look like a boutique fix for semantic pathology rather than a serious general view about truth. So for next week I want a thought experiment built to order, where a true contradiction shows up somewhere else entirely.

The specifications. First, the contradiction has to be generated by the structure of the case, not stipulated — no "suppose P and also not-P," that's assuming what needs to be produced. Second, the raw material has to be ordinary life or natural science: motion, identity over time, conflicting obligations, perception, whatever you like. Banned: self-reference, truth-talk and other semantic vocabulary, set membership, infinite collections, and vague predicates. Also banned, and this one matters: cases where the *evidence* or the *information* is contradictory. Contradictory evidence gives you an inconsistent belief state, not a dialetheia, and I will notice the switch. Third, a classical logician has to have a principled exit — name the premise they'd reject, and, crucially, say what rejecting it costs them elsewhere. If their exit is free, the case is weak and I'd rather know. Fourth, you have to explain in plain terms what a paraconsistent logic (LP or whatever you like) actually buys you here, once the dialetheia is on the table, that explosive classical logic can't give you.

What this is for, since it fixes what counts as cheating: I want to test whether dialetheism is anything more than a technical patch for the liar. So the case fails if the scenario turns out to be a semantic paradox in disguise.

The answer should be exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion: (1) the scenario itself — a couple of dense sentences, no scene-setting; (2) the two little derivations, one ending at P and one ending at not-P, with the premises they pull from; (3) the classical logician's best exit and its price; (4) what paraconsistency buys you once the contradiction is granted; (5) which single feature of the case is doing the work, and whether removing it collapses the case into either a mere inconsistency-in-belief or a smuggled paradox. And if you end up concluding my constraints can't be jointly satisfied, say so in item 5, name the constraint that's doing the blocking, and tell me which one to relax and why — don't just quietly break one and hope I won't catch it. My students try that on me every semester and it never goes well for them either.
