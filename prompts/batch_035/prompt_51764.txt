I run a film club at my high school — about fifteen members aged 16 to 18, one ninety-minute session, with a projector and a whiteboard. For our next meeting I want to tackle why we voluntarily watch films that make us cry. Design an activity in which the members themselves make real, recorded choices about consuming sad versus neutral material — points or small stakes rather than money — so that they run into the apparent contradiction before I name it: we claim to avoid sadness, yet we repeatedly choose it in fiction, and often prefer a film that leaves us in tears to one that leaves us flat. The activity should turn on sadness specifically, not fear or disgust, since we already devoted a session to horror. Include the rules, the payoff scheme, a prediction step in which members commit in advance to how they expect the others to choose, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto at least two competing resolutions — for example, whether we enjoy the sadness itself or enjoy something else that we can only get by way of the sadness — and that shows which recorded choices could distinguish between those resolutions. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the paradox no classroom version of the experience can reproduce.
