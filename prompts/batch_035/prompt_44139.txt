I am preparing for a seminar discussion on Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), and there is a passage early in the book that I cannot fully parse. Please help me understand it. The passage follows Burke's explanation, addressed to his young French correspondent, of why he is not a member of either of the two London clubs that had praised the French Revolution — the Constitutional Society and the Revolution Society. He then describes the first of the two:

The first, calling itself the Constitutional Society, or Society for Constitutional Information, or by some such title, is, I believe, of seven or eight years standing. The institution of this society appears to be of a charitable and so far of a laudable nature; it was intended for the circulation, at the expense of the members, of many books which few others would be at the expense of buying, and which might lie on the hands of the booksellers, to the great loss of an useful body of men. Whether the books, so charitably circulated, were ever as charitably read is more than I know. Possibly several of them have been exported to France and, like goods not in request here, may with you have found a market. I have heard much talk of the lights to be drawn from books that are sent from hence. What improvements they have had in their passage (as it is said some liquors are meliorated by crossing the sea) I cannot tell; but I never heard a man of common judgment or the least degree of information speak a word in praise of the greater part of the publications circulated by that society, nor have their proceedings been accounted, except by some of themselves, as of any serious consequence.

Please structure your response with the following four sections, in this order:

1. "The passage in plain English" (roughly 100–150 words): a straightforward, non-ironic paraphrase of what Burke is actually saying about the society and its books.
2. "The irony, unpacked" (roughly 200–250 words): an analysis of the layers of sarcasm at work. In particular, address: (a) what the comparison of the exported books to liquors supposedly improved by crossing the sea implies about how French readers received these English radical pamphlets; (b) what the remark about books that "might lie on the hands of the booksellers" tells us about the demand for the society's publications; and (c) how the description of the society's purpose as "charitable" functions here.
3. "References and context" (roughly 150–200 words): explain what a "Society for Constitutional Information" would have been doing in 1780s London, what "lights" means in this period vocabulary, and why Burke would treat this club so dismissively when the French National Assembly had honored the English sympathizers of its revolution.
4. "Why it matters" (roughly 150–200 words): how this small, sneering aside connects to the larger argument of the Reflections — specifically, to Burke's preference for political knowledge embodied in inherited institutions and practice over political knowledge derived from abstract doctrine propagated by pamphlet. Does this passage count as an early anticipation of the reasoning now associated with Chesterton's fence (the idea that one should understand what an inherited institution is doing before tearing it down), or is Burke making a different kind of point here? Please take a position and defend it briefly.
