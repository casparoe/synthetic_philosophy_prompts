"In the Second Meditation, Descartes reaches a first truth that the evil demon cannot touch. What exactly does this truth establish, and what does it fail to establish? Explain why it survives the demon doubt, and be careful not to read into it more than Descartes is entitled to at that stage of the argument."

And here is the student's answer, typed out as written:

"Descartes' famous phrase cogito ergo sum appears in Meditation Two and is the first indubitable truth of the Meditations. It works like this: it is a syllogism. The major premise is that whatever thinks exists, the minor premise is that I think, and the conclusion is that I exist. The major premise is self-evident and therefore immune to the demon's doubt, and this is what guarantees the certainty of the conclusion. That I think is indubitable because doubting is itself a form of thinking, so the demon's attempt to make me doubt that I think only confirms that I think. The same goes for deception: the demon can make all my beliefs about the external world false, but deception is itself thinking, so even a maximally powerful deceiver cannot stop there being a thinker.

What the cogito establishes is that I am a thinking thing. And since the demon could supply all of my sensory experiences without any body or physical world existing, thinking cannot be a mode of anything physical. So the cogito establishes that I am an immaterial substance and that the physical world either does not exist or is not known to exist at this stage. It also establishes incorrigibility about my own mind: the demon may falsify my beliefs about external objects, but whatever seems to me to be occuring in my mind is occuring exactly as it seems, since the demon cannot alter my thoughts without making me think something else, which still leaves me thinking. So although the cogito tells us nothing about the world, it secures an indubitable inner realm, and that is the foundation on which Descartes rebuilds everything else."
