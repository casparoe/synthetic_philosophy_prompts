My two roommates have been going at it for like two weeks about whether self-deception is even possible, ever since a third roommate got dumped and within days had fully convinced herself the guy was never that into her anyway — which, okay, maybe, but she'd been telling us for months he was about to propose, and everyone else saw the crash coming. Anyway. Dana holds the classic line: self-deception is impossible on its face, because deceiving someone requires knowing the truth and wanting the target to believe the false thing instead, and if you're the target, you'd have to know and not-know the same fact at the same time. Her image is that it's like standing in a bucket and trying to lift yourself off the ground. Marcus thinks that's way too quick and points to the obvious stuff: the uncle who's clearly closeted at 60, the dad who 'doesn't have a drinking problem' while hiding bottles in the garage, founders who are certain their startup is fine right up to the bankruptcy filing. People do this, he says, so the philosophy needs to catch up to reality, not the other way around.

Dana replies that those are just cases of being wrong with style. On her account what's really happening is motivated reasoning: your wishes bias where you look for evidence, what you notice, what you count as good enough proof — but at no point does anyone believe p while knowing not-p. It's self-persuasion, not self-deception, and there's no paradox because you only ever hold one belief, just one you got to dishonestly. Marcus comes back with the time-lag idea: maybe I can't fool my current self, but I can arrange things today — avoid the doctor, only reread the encouraging test results, steer conversation away from the topic — so that next-month's me is genuinely ignorant and believes the false thing. Deceiver and dupe are different selves at different times, so nobody occupies the impossible state, and self-deception is real after all. Dana's comeback: by that logic con artists don't deceive their marks either — they also just arrange the evidence in advance and let the victim's own mind do the dirty work, and we have zero problem calling that deception, so either Marcus's move proves way too much or it does nothing. And Marcus says fine, exactly — that just shows the 'paradoxical' cases and the con-artist cases are the same kind of thing, and since we call the second deception without blinking, we should stop treating the first as a paradox and admit people deceive themselves all the time.
