I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who came to philosophy of science rather late in life, and next month I present to a university reading group my case for ontic structural realism: the view that the relational, symmetry, and invariance structure of our best physics is ontologically primary, whilst objects — particles, for instance — are at best derivative nodes in that structure. My argument rests on two planks: the permutation invariance of identical quantum particles, which strikes me as making individuality physically meaningless; and the observation that through theory change we typically retain mathematical structure rather than our commitments about things. The audience is a philosophy of science reading group: a handful of graduate students, a devoted entity realist in the Hacking and Cartwright mould who holds that intervening on entities in the laboratory is what warrants belief in them, a constructive empiricist of the van Fraassen school, and a senior figure notorious for pressing the Newman objection against anyone who so much as utters the word structure. Please draft my question-and-answer preparation sheet: the six or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do and phrased in the vocabulary its members would use, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft, together with the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks.
