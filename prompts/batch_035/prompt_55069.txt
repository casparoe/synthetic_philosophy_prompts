The idea concerns the standard story that Solomonoff induction justifies Occam's razor: shorter programs get more prior weight, and this preference for simplicity isn't an arbitrary add-on but a theorem. When people raise the objection that Kolmogorov complexity depends on the choice of universal machine, the usual reply is the invariance theorem — machine choice only changes complexities by an additive constant. My worry, coming from engineering: an additive constant is a fixed cost that never amortizes. If the constant is, say, a few thousand bits, then for any dataset or prediction horizon shorter than that, the machine choice dominates everything, and no actual agent — human, scientific community, or ML system — ever operates at data scales where the constant becomes negligible. So it looks like the razor's justification holds only in an asymptotic limit nothing finite ever reaches, and the practical warrant for preferring simple hypotheses must be coming from somewhere else: maybe the empirical track record of simplicity priors in particular domains, maybe something about which machines a physically realizable agent can even implement. My hazy thought is that the honest conclusion is that Solomonoff induction justifies a weaker claim than "simplicity is the right prior," something more like "no computable prior beats it in the long run, and the long run is longer than you can afford" — and that this distinction matters for people building actual approximate-Bayesian systems.
