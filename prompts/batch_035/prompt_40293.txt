My thesis supervisor and I have reached an impasse over Parfit's nonidentity problem, and rather than keep trading the same intuitions at each other, we want an outside credence to triangulate against. The case as we set it up: a society chooses a depleting policy, which changes the timing of conceptions so that different people, with lives worth living, exist a few centuries later than would have under a conservative policy. The person-affecting thought is that the depleting policy wrongs those later people; the nonidentity problem says it cannot have wronged them, because under the conservative policy they would never have existed at all, and a life worth living is not worse for the person living it than nonexistence. My supervisor's credence that the problem, once properly formulated, forces us to give up something we wanted to keep — either the person-affecting restriction itself, or the claim that the depleting policy wrongs anyone at all — is about 0.75. Mine is 0.25, because I suspect the whole thing rests on an optional essentialism about origins: the premise that the later people would not have existed under the alternative policy arguably only holds if a person is essentially the product of that particular gamete pair, and I'm not sure that's true.

Please give me your own number, but far more importantly, the decomposition. I'd want at least these terms, plus any others you think belong: the probability that origins essentialism (or something near enough) is true, so that the later people really would not have existed under the alternative policy; the probability that a life worth living cannot be worse for its subject than nonexistence, even if some philosophers think bad things within such a life can still count as harms to that very subject; the probability that, if both of those hold, the only options left are impersonal principles about the quality of lives or an admission that the wrongness must be explained in some other way entirely (agent-relative duties, expectations, the character of the choice); and the probability that we are entitled simply to explain away the intuition that something bad happened here, rather than finding something it is true of. Give each a rough probability with a line of justification, show how they combine into your final credence, then say which term the final number is most sensitive to, and whether my supervisor's 0.75 and my 0.25 most likely turn on the origins-essentialism term or on the one about the badness of a life worth living. Please answer in British English, and keep the whole response under 450 words.
