I write novels, which means I spend a frankly embarrassing amount of my life thinking about blurbs — those little quotes where other writers call my book luminous or unputdownable. And here's the thing I keep chewing on: a lot of those blurbers didn't really form their view from the text alone. They read the reviews, they know the ambient consensus, some of them skimmed me on a plane. There's a whole aesthetic economy where almost nobody's judgment is actually first-hand, and everybody sort of knows it and the blurbs keep working anyway. So that's the territory: satirize it for me.

Specifically, write a customer-facing FAQ (with a tagline up top and at least one glowing user testimonial at the bottom) for a subscription app — name it whatever you like — that outsources your entire aesthetic life. It tells you what's beautiful, which novels are masterpieces, whether the sunset over the lake was any good this morning, all crowdsourced from users who actually had the experiences, so you never have to see, hear, taste, or read anything yourself. Around 600-700 words, glossy marketing-department voice, the kind of cheerful FAQ that's technically answering your questions.

The jokes have to come from the real philosophy of aesthetic testimony, so that an aesthetician nods instead of winces. Work in at least these, without ever naming them: the acquaintance principle (the app will happily tell you the concert was sublime, but the fine print has to concede something's missing — and then, this is important, sell the missing part back to you as a premium tier); the gap between merely knowing-that something is beautiful and actually acquiring the aesthetic state or response yourself (offer both, at different price points, and be candid that only one is refundable); and the worry that testimony transmits the verdict but not the justification (the numbers team promises to "reproduce the intuition from scratch" — and definately doesn't). Bonus points for a testimonial from a user with impeccable taste they have never once used, and for a clause about what happens to your aesthetic profile if you accidentally experience something directly — treat it as a contamination event, with a workflow for it.

Don't state the objections — let the FAQ absorb them and monetize them. Keep it deadpan throughout; the horror should leak out entirely through the pricing structure and the tier names. And as a novelist, one personal request: somewhere in there, acknowledge the writers. We're apparently one of your most reliable data sources. I'd like to know what we're being paid.
