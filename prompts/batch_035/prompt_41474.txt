I teach a philosophy of history seminar, and two of my students have spent all term arguing past each other about periodization. I've about given up on getting a debate out of it — I want an adversarial-collaboration writeup instead. One student (the realist) holds that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are real historical episodes: actual transformations in European life, such that if historians had never named them, something importantly like them would still have needed naming. The other (the constructivist) holds that all three are retrospective impositions: later carvings of a continuous stream of events that reflect our categories and our needs, not breaks in the events themselves.

Please act as a professional philosopher of history with mediation experience, think carefully, and do some brainstorming before drafting. Then draft the joint statement both students could sign: a numbered agreed-claims section (including claims both can sign about what the period labels have done to the discipline, for good or ill, whichever they think), followed by a disagreements table where each residual disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, then a cruxes section — for each crux, an argument or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

The crux I want front and center is the one they fight about most: what would even count as evidence that a period boundary does explanatory work rather than mere bookkeeping. The constructivist proposed a test: survey a hundred specialists on when the Middle Ages end. She predicts a spread of answers so wide that the boundary can't be tracking anything in the evidence; the realist predicts clustering that shifts with regional specialty (a historian of Italy vs one of England) but still clusters. Both agree the survey bears on the crux. I want the document to assess whether it would actually settle anything or just measure conventions of speech among historians — and I suspect a chunk of their dispute is verbal, turning on what "real" means for a period. If any part of the disagreement is verbal, say which part and why.

Last thing, since I'll be grading this: please seperate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. Also don't lean on specific book titles or attributions you're not sure of — describe positions rather than name-dropping.
