Last night two of my friends — Mira, who works in statistics, and Joel, who's finishing a philosophy PhD — spent the better part of two hours arguing about the Allais paradox at my dinner table, and I've been asked to referee. I'd like you to adjudicate it. You are a professional philosopher specialising in rational choice theory, so please don't hold back on the technicalities. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the possible readings of each move; several of them are easy to misread as weaker than they are. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall.

The disagreement unfolded in five numbered moves:

1. Mira began with the standard line: in the Allais problem almost everyone prefers a sure million over a gamble with a small chance of nothing, but then reverses that preference once the common consequence (a 1% chance of zero in both options) is introduced — and that pair of preferences violates the independence axiom. A rational agent maximising expected utility couldn't hold both, so the majority of people are simply irrational, and the paradox is a measurement of our error.

2. Joel answered that this is too quick, because there's a well-known reconstruction of the Allais preferences as perfectly rational, provided one declines to reduce compound lotteries: treat the second choice as a two-stage affair in which a first draw determines whether you get to play the common part, and the "violating" preferences come out consistent. On that reconstruction, he said, what Allais subjects violate is reduction of compound lotteries, not independence — and reduction is a bookkeeping convention, not a requirement of rationality.

3. Mira replied that this concedes everything: when the problem is actually presented in the decoupled two-stage form, most people give the expected-utility-consistent answer and abandon their Allais preferences — they hold one set of preferences under one description of the very same choice and the opposite set under another. Whichever description you prefer, switching on presentation alone is framing dependence, the textbook mark of irrationality, so the "rational reconstruction" only relocates the error.

4. Joel said that shows people are unreliable detectors of structural equivalence, not that their underlying concern is worthless. The Allais agent's real objection to the second gamble, he said, isn't a computational slip: it's that the bad outcome would involve walking away with nothing from a situation in which a sure million was on the table, and that kind of outcome — ending with nothing while the alternative was certainty — is a genuinely worse state of the world to live in, regret and all. If utility attaches to final outcomes, then describe the outcomes fully and the preferences may be consistent after all.

5. Mira's closing move: that rescue is too cheap, because it proves too much. If you may redescribe outcomes to include regret, comparison with forgone alternatives, and whatever else the preference responds to, then any preference whatsoever can be made expected-utility consistent, and the theory stops constraining anything — an axiom that cannot be violated by any possible pattern of choice has no normative content. Joel shot back that a theory with no violations is exactly what an ideal looks like, and that Mira's demand for falsifiability confuses a normative standard with a psychological model, at which point they both looked at me.

Please explain who is right about which specific points, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — I suspect there's something neither of them put on the table.
