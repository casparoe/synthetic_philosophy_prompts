I'm a science and ideas writer, and over the years my magazine's fact-checking desk has built up a quiet body of rulings on when copy may call something "alive" — a question that comes up more often than you would think, and never with a rulebook. The rulings to date, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Viruses, in a feature on influenza: the desk required "not technically alive," and last year, in a piece on giant viruses, it vetoed "living particle" outright.
- Bacteria revived after millions of years in amber and in deep-sea sediment: "living bacteria" and "still alive" passed without objection, though they had done nothing metabolically the whole time.
- HeLa cells: allowed as "living cells," even "a living piece of Henrietta Lacks," though a cell line in a flask is nobody's organism and cannot survive outside the medium we supply.
- A mule, in an essay on domestication: "a living animal," sterility notwithstanding.
- Prions: strictly "infectious proteins," never "alive."
- Digital organisms, in an artificial-life story: we could say they are "alive, after a fashion," with the signal phrase, but the bare adjective "living" was ruled out.
- A brain-dead man on a ventilator, in a feature on organ donation: the desk kept "his body is alive" and "life support" in the same paragraph as the flat statement that he was dead.

I now have a synthetic-biology story due Friday. Researchers have built protocells that copy themselves, pass on heritable variation, and respond to selection — but they cannot make their own parts: every lipid and nucleotide is supplied by the experimenters, and chemically these "cells" are elaborate consumers. The desk will ask me by Thursday whether the lede may say "living cells," or must say "cell-like" or "not technically alive," and I would like to walk in with something better than my gut.

So tell me: which rules would actually reproduce the desk's track record? I can see a self-maintaining-chemistry rule and an evolution-of-a-lineage rule in the running, and there may be others that fit as well. What does each rule predict about the protocells? What is your best guess about what the desk will do, and why? Then name the further case that would best split the rules that agree on the protocells — some borderline thing a science desk is likely to face (a frozen embryo? a seed bank? a genome-free cell? something stranger) on which those rules come out differently. And if the track record genuinely underdetermines the protocell call, say so plainly, and tell me what principle we would be adopting with each choice — because whatever the desk rules this week will, in practice, become our definition of life for the next decade of copy.
