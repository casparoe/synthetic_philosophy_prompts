Bit of background: I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training. I got into this rabbit hole through work, actually — we use formal methods stuff (model checkers, that kind of thing) and somewhere along the way I stumbled on epistemic logic, where you have a "knows that" operator and apparently the standard systems have a rule that if an agent knows A, and knows A implies B, then the agent automatically knows B. And this cascades, so a properly rational agent in these systems ends up "knowing" every logical consequence of everything it knows. People call this the problem of logical omniscience, and from what I can tell it's treated as a genuine open problem that philosophers and even some economists care about.

Here's what I don't get though. Isn't this basically an engineering problem that we've already mostly solved? Computers verify proofs — Lean checks a full formal proof in seconds, SAT solvers chew through formulas with millions of variables, there are model checkers that explore state spaces no human could hold in their head. So if the original issue was that humans can't run the deductions in their heads, we've built machines that can do the derivations for them. An agent with a big enough theorem prover attached can, in principle, derive every consequence of its knowledge base worth deriving. Yet I keep running into papers and blog posts that treat logical omniscience as a live theoretical problem rather than a limitation we outgrew with better hardware. Someone even claimed it matters for cryptography, which honestly sounds off to me — crypto is math, if anything machines are *better* at it than us.

So, setting me straight: where exactly is my confusion? Is the problem about who can do the derivation (humans vs. machines), or is it something about the modeling itself — like, is the issue that treating an agent as closed under logical consequence produces predictions that are wrong no matter how much compute you give the agent? And if so, what would an example of that look like — what does an "omniscient" agent get wrong that a bounded one gets right? Also, if you can, explain the cryptography connection, because I'd have guessed provers make crypto *more* airtight, not that there's some philosophical problem lurking there. Feel free to be concrete, I'm fine with technical detail, I just don't have the philosophy vocabulary.
