I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I need a referee for an argument that ran through two departmental coffees without resolution. My fellow students Maya and Theo got into it after Maya mentioned that she dreads flying, even though she fully believes, and can cite the statistics for, the claim that the drive to the airport is more dangerous than the flight; the dread shows up on every trip without fail. (1) Maya opened by claiming her case refutes the view that emotions are evaluative judgments: the judgment that flying is safe is squarely in place, yet the fear persists. (2) Theo answered that the fear must be anchored in some residual belief—perhaps an implicit one about not being in control—that coexists with her considered judgment, so the case shows at most that our belief-forming systems are fragmented, not that fear isn't judgment-like. (3) Maya called this ad hoc and unfalsifiable, since any persistent emotion could be explained away by positing hidden beliefs that nobody can independently point to. (4) Theo then shifted ground: if fear were a mere bodily feeling, as Maya seemed to be implying, it couldn't have an object at all—one fears the crash, the turbulence, the loss of control, not one's own racing pulse—so whatever fear is, it represents the world as dangerous, which he said is already halfway to his preferred picture, on which emotions are perceptions of value and Maya's dread is something like a perceptual illusion. (5) Maya replied that perception is the wrong model too: genuine perception involves detectors keyed to observable properties, and danger isn't observable, so the analogy buys intentionality at the price of explaining how fear could ever track anything. Neither would budge. Please adjudicate each of the five moves, saying who is right about what and where each overreaches, and then take a definite position on which of the three pictures of emotion—judgment, perception, or feeling—best survives their exchange, with your reasons. Write continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in an email.
