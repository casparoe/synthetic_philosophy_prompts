I sit, as the resident novelist, on the advisory board of a national literary prize. Our administrator has proposed replacing the judges' free deliberation with a weighted points system: each submitted novel scored by a paid panel against fixed criteria — prose quality, plot resolution, emotional payoff, broad appeal — with the highest total taking the award automatically. His rationale is that recent controversies over unpredictable judging have cost us sponsors and goodwill; readers and publishers, he says, do not really want the burden of defending a surprising winner, they want an outcome they can foresee and feel good about, and a formula delivers that calm. Half the board finds this sensible. My objection is, I suspect, at bottom Dostoevsky's — the Grand Inquisitor's claim that most people will gladly trade freedom for happiness and security, and whatever it is in Ivan's parable that is meant to answer him — but I cannot simply cite a nineteenth-century novel in a board paper. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the next meeting: recommend accepting, rejecting, or modifying the proposal, in terms the board could act on, and give the administrator's rationale its strongest form rather than a caricature. Make the Dostoevskian structure of the disagreement explicit where it genuinely does work, and say honestly where it does not — I do not want a literary flourish dressed up as an argument.
