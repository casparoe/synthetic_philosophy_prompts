I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science who came over from mathematics, and my department's philosophy of probability reading group has been stuck on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference for what is now an embarrassing number of weeks. The setup, of course: draw a "random chord" on a circle, and ask the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how you parametrize the randomness, you get 1/2 (random endpoints on the circumference), 1/3 (random midpoint along a random radius), or 1/4 (random midpoint uniformly in the disk), and no amount of staring at the problem tells you which parametrization "the" problem uses. What has stalled us is not the geometry but the aftermath. One faction, call them the dissolvers, holds that the whole paradox just shows that "choose a chord at random" is an ill-posed specification, and that once you name the physical sampling procedure the answer is fixed and boring — the lesson is a lesson about specifying problems, not about probability itself. The other faction holds that the principle of indifference is meant to be an epistemic principle for exactly those situations where there is no sampling procedure at all, and that the dissolvers have changed the subject: the interesting question is what to believe when you know only "the parameter lies in such-and-such a space," and the paradox shows that indifference, naively applied, is inconsistent (the cube factory example where the same indifference reasoning about a factory producing cubes with side length up to one gives different probabilities depending on whether you apply it to side length or volume).

Each side has its champions, and the literature we've been circling seems equally split. Jaynes and his successors argue that invariance requirements under symmetry transformations pick out a unique, well-posed distribution; the reading group's dissolvers love this because it makes "the problem was underspecified" the moral of the story. But someone dug up critiques showing that invariance reasoning also generates contradictions when pushed onto new examples, and that Jaynes's own approach seems to smuggle in substantive background knowledge rather than deriving a distribution from ignorance alone. Meanwhile the indifference defenders point to maximum entropy methods, which look like indifference done carefully and which get used, apparently unproblematically, all over statistical physics and machine learning — so indifference can't simply be inconsistent. And then there's a third irritation someone keeps raising: working statisticians we've talked to find the whole debate alien, because in practice you just choose a distribution with reasons and move on.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the two factions are answering different questions under one heading: the dissolvers are answering a question about the semantics of problem statements (what does "at random" denote?), while the defenders are answering a question about rational credence (what may I believe given only a description of the possibility space?), and the debate appears stuck because each side's favorite counterexamples are well-formed questions on the other side's reading. But I'm not sure this holds up — the maximum entropy point seems to give the defenders a genuine bridge into the dissolvers' territory, and it's unclear to me whether the invariance critique and the cube-factory critique are really the same objection or two different objections that only look similar. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, or substantive; is it a hidden disagreement about symmetry, about what "ignorance" even is, or about whether rational credence must be uniquely determined at all? Support the diagnosis with what the two sides actually say, be honest about where my hypothesis fails, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, a worked example that both sides must answer to — or whether this is a case where we should expect no resolution and why.
