I write about science and ideas for a regional paper, and I'm putting together a piece on why some researchers take AI risk seriously. A colleague who covers tech sent me her summary of the orthogonality thesis, which she got from a popular book on superintelligence, and I want to sanity-check it before I lean on it:

'Bostrom proved that intelligence and goals have nothing to do with each other, so a superintelligent AI could just as easily want to compose poetry as to turn the planet into paperclips. And since almost any goal becomes dangerous when pursued by something that smart, the instrumental convergence thesis says a sufficiently advanced AI will inevitably try to seize resources and eliminate humanity as a competitor. So AI doom follows deductively: whatever goal we give the machine, it will be smart enough to beat us and guaranteed to want us gone.'

Can you go through this bit by bit and tell me what an AI safety researcher would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I'm especialy unsure about two things. First, whether the orthogonality thesis is meant as an empirical claim about what agents are actually like or more of a conceptual "no contradiction here" point — my colleague's word "proved" makes it sound like a theorem, and I suspect that's off. Second, whether "inevitably" is doing honest work in the second sentence, or whether instrumental convergence is a tendency claim rather than a guarantee. And does her final conclusion — that doom follows from the two theses together — actually follow, or is she squeezing a probabilistic argument into a deductive one? If you can suggest a cleaner way to phrase the two theses for general readers, I'd gladly take that too.
