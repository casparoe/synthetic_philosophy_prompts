Since the early 1990s, U.S. federal courts have made trial judges gatekeepers of expert testimony. Under the Daubert decision of 1993 and Kumho Tire in 1999, a judge admitting a proposed expert must assess whether the reasoning or methodology underlying the testimony is reliable, and the Court offered a now-standard, explicitly non-exhaustive list of considerations: whether the theory or technique can be and has been tested, whether it has been subjected to peer review and publication, its known or potential error rate, the existence of standards controlling its operation, and its general acceptance in the relevant community. The announced purpose is to keep junk science — phrenology, bite-mark analysis, facilitated communication — away from juries while admitting legitimate expertise. I am a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I have been trying to work out what philosophy of science, if any, this gatekeeping practice presupposes, because its components look like they were assembled from incompatible shelves.

The falsifiability factor is recognizably Popperian; the error-rate factor seems to presuppose something like frequentist statistics and sits awkwardly with expertise that is not naturally quantitative; and general acceptance is the very criterion Popper spent his career deriding — truth by community vote, the consensus of the tribe. Yet all of these appear on one list as interchangeable considerations for the same judgment, with no stated priority among them. A court could in principle admit testimony under the acceptance factor that it would exclude under the testability factor. My suspicion is that the practice does not presuppose any single demarcation criterion at all, but I want to know whether the resulting mixture is genuinely incoherent or whether it is a defensible implicit position — perhaps something like a reliabilism about testimony, or a division of epistemic labor in which the community, not the individual method, is the primary unit of reliability — that the case law happens to track without articulating.

Here is a test case I have been chewing on. Suppose a historical chronology revisionist of the Fomenko school — someone who argues by statistical correlations of dynastic narratives and astronomical datings that the accepted timeline of the ancient world is inflated by roughly a millennium — were to offer expertise in a case where the chronology of some historical document mattered. Fomenko-style work claims to be testable, and it does issue quantified claims; it has been published, if mostly in books and in-house venues rather than mainstream journals; it could in principle state an error rate for its dating methods; and it has a community that accepts it, though a small and self-referential one. Does the Daubert framework, taken as a body of practice rather than as an aspiration, have principled grounds to exclude this testimony, or does it depend on a judge's background sense of who counts as the relevant scientific community — that is, on a demarcation judgment smuggled in through the general-acceptance factor? If the latter, the framework may presuppose no demarcation criterion at all, but rather deference to the institutional boundaries of academic science.

So, three questions. First, reconstruct the implicit demarcation view the practice presupposes, if any, and say which rival views of what separates science from pseudoscience the practice quietly rules out. Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: is there a single coherent account under which all five factors are evidence of reliability, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined such that several incompatible demarcation views would each license it? Third, tell me what would have to change in the practice — in the formulation of the factors, in what judges are trained on, in how the relevance of the community-acceptance factor is described — if the underlying view were made explicitly Popperian rather than consensus-reliabilist, or vice versa.

Please be candid about the fragility of the reconstruction. I would like you to say where in your argument the reasoning is most likely to fail — which inference from the practice to the implicit view is weakest — and what would change your verdict: for instance, whether the existence of some body of cases in which courts weighted the factors in a consistent order, or evidence that the factor list is treated as a genuine checklist rather than as a rhetorical gesture, would overturn your conclusion about whether the practice presupposes any demarcation commitment at all.
