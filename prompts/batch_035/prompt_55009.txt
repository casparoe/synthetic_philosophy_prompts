At a memorial gathering, my two philosophy-trained friends got into a running argument about whether my late uncle 'survives' in any literal sense. Dana appealed to Hofstadter's strange-loop picture: the 'I' is a pattern — a self-referential loop in a brain — and patterns, unlike souls, can be copied. Everyone who knew my uncle well carries a partial model of him in their own brain: his phrases, his tastes, his characteristic reactions. So fragments of his 'I' are literally running on other hardware, and death destroyed only the central copy. Marcus said this is poetry masquerading as metaphysics: a caricature of a person isn't the person, or else a detailed biography would be a partial resurrection too. Dana replied that the 'I' in your own head is also a caricature — a compressed model of far more complex underlying biology — and that any stage of you is only a partial copy of the previous stage anyway, so if caricature suffices for persistence within a life, it should suffice across lives. Marcus answered that within a life the copies are causally chained, each stage spun off by the last, whereas the fragments scattered through friends' brains are causally inert with respect to one another and never jointly constitute a single viewpoint — none of them is having my uncle's experiences or can speak for him. Dana's last word was that demanding one viewpoint betrays exactly the Cartesianism the strange-loop view was supposed to cure, since on that view even a single brain's 'I' is many competing partial models that merely feel unified. They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who's right at each stage, identify what if anything both are missing, and say who is more right overall.
