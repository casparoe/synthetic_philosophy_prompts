I'm taking a philosophy of science course this semester and we just did a couple weeks on simultaneous invention -- the Newton/Leibniz calculus priority fight, Darwin getting Wallace's letter from Ternate and panicking, all that. It got me thinking about patent law, which we covered last spring in an IP survey course, and now the two things won't line up in my head.

Here's what's bugging me. Patents are built entirely around the idea of invention as a singular individual achievement. The system gives one person (or company) a ~20 year monopoly, the term doesn't shrink based on how close anyone else was to the same idea, there's basically no defense of "I came up with this independently" -- if someone else filed first, you're out of luck, even under first-to-file rules where being literally first across the line is what counts. And the whole incentive story people tell for patents -- "no exclusive rights, no invention" -- only makes sense if inventions are these contingent acts that wouldn't happen without the prize.

But my whole point in the seminar paper was that the history of science suggests a lot of discoveries are sort of "in the air" and multiple people make them once the groundwork is there. And patent law actually seems to know this! There's the non-obviousness / inventive step requirement, which is the law more or less admitting that some ideas are just what any competent person in the field would eventually hit on, and those don't get protected. So the system carves out a zone of "this would have been discovered anyway" and refuses patents there, but then for everything outside that zone it treats the invention as if it would definately never have happened otherwise.

So my question: what view of discovery and invention does the patent system as it actually operates presuppose? Is it something like "inventions come in two kinds, the inevitable ones and the genuine creative leaps, and we can tell them apart at the margin"? Can it really presuppose that, given how fuzzy the obviousness line is in practice? And I guess I'm asking too whether the different parts of the system presuppose consistent things -- the incentive story, the term length, the no-independent-inventor-defense rule, the obviousness doctrine -- or whether some of these actually pull in opposite directions and the courts just never notice.

Then, if you replaced the implicit "inventions are rare individual acts" picture with the "discoveries are often inevitable" picture, what would have to change? Shorter terms? Prizes instead of monopolies? Something else? I'd also like to know where the current system is actually underdetermined, like maybe it's compatible with a bunch of different theories of invention and the incentive story is just the one people like to tell. Basically reconstruct the implicit theory for me the way we've been doing in class for other institutions, and be honest about the parts that don't fit neatly.
