I write literary fiction, and over fifteen years of practice I've settled into a deflationary view of my own creativity, borrowed loosely from Douglas Hofstadter: everything I write is an instance of what he called knob-twiddling, or variations on a theme. I take some template I've absorbed — a marriage-in-decay plot, an unreliable narrator, a braided chronology — and turn its dials (register, distance, stakes, ending) until something that feels new to me emerges. There is no creation from nothing, only parameter search over inherited structures, and the sense of breakthrough is just what it feels like when the search lands somewhere distant from the template. I find the view liberating rather than bleak, but I want to stress-test it before I build my next book on it. What would count against it? Would a genuinely unprecedented formal invention — a structurally new way of telling a story that resists description as a variation on prior templates — refute it, or would it be absorbed by positing deeper knobs or a larger library of templates? Are there facts about my own process or about literary history that the view predicts and its rivals don't? And I'd like an honest assessment of the view's habit of self-defense: when any apparent counterexample gets redescribed as "just more twiddling," has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a fatal flaw for a view of this kind or merely a sign that it is a framework rather than a hypothesis? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed knob-twiddler should concede as real exposure rather than talk away, and close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, how much risk the view actually carries.
