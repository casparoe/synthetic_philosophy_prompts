I've been working through a philosophy-of-science textbook chapter on the scientific realism debate, and its treatment of structural realism struck me as suspiciously tidy. Here is the relevant paragraph, quoted from memory but I believe faithfully:

"Scientific realism claims that our best theories are approximately true, including their claims about unobservables. History is unkind to this: phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether each underwrote serious empirical success in their day and each is now regarded as false, so by induction our current theories are probably false as well. Structural realism, proposed by John Worrall in the late 1980s, concedes this but rescues the realist: what survives theory change is not what theories say about unobservable entities but their mathematical structure. Fresnel's ether theory was false, yet its equations were carried over essentially intact into Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, and it is continuity of structure, not of ontology, that realism needs. Accordingly, the structural realist holds that all we can know of the world is its structure — there are no objects, only relations. Since this is a single, unified position, it escapes the pessimistic induction entirely, for the ether was never really part of what made Fresnel's theory successful."

I'm inclined to be sympathetic to structural realism, and parts of this sound right to me, but I've also seen references to a split between epistemic and ontic versions of the view that this textbook presents as one thing, and I don't have the background to tell whether the other claims are subtly distorted too. Please go through the paragraph piece by piece and tell me which claims a working structural realist would accept as stated, which ones conflate distinct positions or overstate a modest thesis, and whether the final sentence — that the view escapes the pessimistic induction entirely because the ether played no role in the theory's success — actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary. Where the paragraph runs together things that philosophers of science keep apart, I'd like to know exactly where the seams are and why they matter.
