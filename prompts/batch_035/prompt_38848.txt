At the AI lab where I work, one faction argues that no model should be deployed without worst-case guarantees, and their workhorse analogy is computer security: shipping a model with a known failure mode is like shipping software with a known vulnerability; pre-deployment evals are penetration testing; post-deployment correction is patching; and just as nobody accepts "this vulnerability will probably never be exploited," nobody should accept "the model will probably never cause serious harm" — so we should certify the absence of worst-case behavior rather than settle for low average-case risk. The opposing faction answers with its own analogy, drawn from infrastructure, aviation, and drug approval: we fly on planes with small-but-nonzero failure probabilities, we approve drugs on expected benefit, and a blanket demand for worst-case guarantees would have forbidden essentially every technology we have; deployment plus monitoring and iteration is how engineering has always worked.

I'd like an audit of both analogies, since together they carry most of our internal debate.

For the security analogy, give the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the adversary, the exploit, the zero-day, the red team, the patch, and the CVE database — and then say where it breaks. The obvious fracture is that a misaligned model is not a motivated attacker planning against your specific deployment; on many threat models it's closer to a random draw from a bad tail of the training distribution, and worst-case reasoning over distributions without adversaries risks being dominated by adversarial hypotheticals of vanishing probability. But also say where the adversary framing is more apt than the average-case faction admits: deceptive alignment, specification gaming under deployment pressure, and other threat models where the model's behavior genuinely is optimized against your oversight. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the licensing conclusion from the ones that don't.

Do the same for the safety-engineering analogy: planes, drugs, and ALARP-style tolerable-risk reasoning all assume repeated independent trials, a surviving operator, and feedback from incidents. The standard objection is patchability — software and aviation safety are iterative regimes because failures are localized and recoverable — so ask whether a failure mode that is irreversible or self-amplifying removes the option that analogy licenses. Say what each analogy smuggles in: the security analogy imports a strategic opponent, which inflates the case for worst-case guarantees if none exists; the safety analogy imports the ability to learn from failures, which inflates the case for iterative average-case deployment if a first major failure is terminal.

Finally, tell me whether either faction's conclusion survives once its disanalogies are on the table, and if neither analogy can carry the weight, sketch what a better one would look like — what source domain, if any, has the right structure of one-shot stakes, uncertain failure probabilities, and no adversary.
