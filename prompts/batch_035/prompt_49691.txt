My book club just finished Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day and we got into a proper fight about Stevens that I want to settle. Half of us think he's a basically good man who served a bad master, the other half think his dutifulness is itself the moral failure — he hides inside duty so he never has to exercise judgment at all. I've been reading some Kant on the side and it's making it worse: the famous shopkeeper who treats customers honestly only because it's good for business acts merely in accordance with duty rather than from duty, and Kant thinks only acting from duty has genuine moral worth. Stevens looks like the mirror image — he acts from duty all right, maybe too much from duty, so much that it eats everything else in him (his father, Miss Kenton, the whole gooseberry-bush realization at the end). So does Kant's criterion, applied honestly, make Stevens a paradigm of moral worth or a cautionary case? My Kant-sympathetic friend says the whole point is that Stevens' maxims were corrupt — you can formally act from duty while having organized your life around never having to judge — but I'm not sure Kant gives us the resources to say that without smuggling in exactly the kind of substantive judgment he wanted to ground.

There's also this worry I keep bumping into, I think it goes back to Michael Stocker: if you center moral worth on acting for the right reasons, you seem to require a kind of estrangement — you help your friend because it's right, not just because you love him, and that seems to get the psychology of actually good people wrong. Stevens is almost a reductio of this: he's the man who does everything for the formal reason and nothing for the human one, and Ishiguro shows us precisely what that costs. Though maybe that's unfair to Kant, who never said you shouldn't act from affection, only that affection-driven right action isn't the thing with incomparable worth. And there's a further wrinkle: it's not obvious Stevens is acting from duty rather than hiding behind duty, which might be self-interest or cowardice dressed up — think of his speech to Dr. Carlisle about dignity, where he gestures at his father's career because he can't actually say what dignity consists in.

So give me a reading that's fair to both the Kantian framework and the novel. Where does the moral-worth lens genuinely illuminate scenes — I'm thinking of the evening he keeps serving downstairs while his father dies upstairs, and the moments where duty functions as a weapon in his conversations with Miss Kenton — and where does the novel resist the Kantian reading? And here's the thing I actually want answered: at the end, on the bench at Weymouth, crying in front of a stranger and resolving to practice bantering — is Stevens a man who spent his life acting for the right reasons with simply the wrong content in them, or a man who never really acted for reasons at all, just from avoidance? If it's the second, the novel is an indictment not just of Stevens but of a whole picture of what moral worth looks like, and I'd like to know whether you think Ishiguro knows he's doing that.
