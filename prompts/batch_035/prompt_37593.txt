I'm a software engineer who has spent the last few years writing reinforcement-learning code for warehouse robots (simulated, so far), and I've gotten absorbed in a question that sits uncomfortably between engineering and philosophy: whether the phrase "avoid unnecessary side effects" can be turned into a precise objective without first writing down everything humans care about.

Concretely: in my simulator, a robot is rewarded for moving crates, and I can add a penalty term for "impact on the environment." The obvious approaches all seem to smuggle in value judgments. I can penalize divergence from a default state where the robot never acted — but then the penalty depends entirely on which counterfactual world I pick, and that choice looks like it encodes what I think matters, not any physical fact. I can penalize deviation from what would have happened had the robot paused at each step — but then I've bought the problem that the counterfactual and actual worlds drift apart over time, so any long-lived agent racks up "impact" no matter how carefully it behaves, while also acquiring incentives to restore the world to some baseline (repairing the vase it broke, or preventing anyone from noticing it broke) rather than to not cause the harm in the first place. I can penalize the agent for changing how easily various auxiliary goals could be achieved — but which goals count as auxiliary, and whether making some goals harder while making others easier nets out as "impact," looks like a value judgment again. Meanwhile, at the physical level, breaking a vase and nudging it one centimeter are both just state transitions; nothing in the physics labels one a "side effect" and the other not. That labeling seems to come from us.

So here is the proposition: there exists a notion of "impact on the world" that is (a) definable without a complete specification of human values, and (b) rich enough that an agent minimizing it, while otherwise pursuing its task, is substantially safer than an unconstrained one. What is your credence in it? Please don't just say it depends on how you define "impact" — give me a number and the decomposition that produces it: the probability that impact is a value-independent natural kind rather than a projection of our concerns onto physics; the probability that, given that, a computable measure can approximate it from the agent's own world-model without the choice of counterfactual baseline doing hidden normative work; the probability that such a measure survives an agent that optimizes hard against it, given that penalty terms in reward functions are notoriously gamed; and any other terms you think belong. Then combine them and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or experimental result would move that term most.

For reference, my own credence is around 0.35. A colleague of mine puts it at 0.65, on the grounds that the physical world supplies enough structure — causal graphs, reachable states, reversibility — that a sufficiently clever measure could carve "impact" at something close to a real joint. Where do you think the disagreement between the two of us most likely lives: in whether the concept is value-laden at all, or in whether the value-ladenness can be routed around by cleverness at the measure-design level?
