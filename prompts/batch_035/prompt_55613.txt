Been reading the rationalist blogosphere since like 2012 and now I'm somehow the designated person at our local meetup who has to teach AI alignment to newcomers. We've been getting a wave of people showing up because of the news cycle — ChatGPT, doomers on podcasts, whatever — and they're smart but their whole mental model comes from Terminator and op-eds. Next month I'm running the intro session: 90 minutes, a whiteboard, no math, maybe 20 people. Last time someone gave this talk it was an hour and a half of ranting about decision theory and half the room never came back, so I want to actually plan it. Don't explain alignment to me, I've read the canon — I need the teaching plan.

The thing I keep going back and forth on is what order the pieces go in. There's a dependency question I can't solve: do you need something like the orthogonality thesis (intelligence and goals being separable) in place before the problem even sounds like a problem, or does leading with it make newcomers think you're assuming your conclusion? And there's a bigger structural question I want the plan to take a stance on: do I present alignment as ONE problem or as several problems that got bundled under one label? Like — is it a specification problem (getting what we actually want into the system), a robustness problem (the system keeping that as it scales and learns), and an interpretability problem (being able to check whether any of it worked)? Or is that split wrong, and the honest first pass is that it's one problem about the gap between what we can specify and what we value? I genuinly don't know whether one-problem or many-problems is the better opening frame, and it changes the whole arc of the session, so I want the plan to argue for one of them, not just list both.

Also need the misconception map. I can predict some of the incoming ones — "it's Terminator robots with feelings," "just program it not to hurt people / Asimov laws," "a sufficiently smart AI would figure out morality on its own," "this is a policy problem, not a technical one," and "we can just turn it off" — but tell me where in the sequence each one should get defused, because if you handle them too early you sound defensive and if you wait too long they've already curdled into the person's whole model of the topic. The carrying example is the other big thing: I am so tired of paperclip maximizers, and half the room has already seen it and treats it as a joke. What one concrete scenario should carry the whole session instead, what does it need to have and to lack, and why that over the alternatives you're rejecting. Then a closing question I can actually ask out loud that would show me whether someone got the real thing versus whether they just enjoyed the doom. And be honest about what 90 minutes can't do — what I should openly punt on and say so to the room. Answer in American English please, most of the meetup is American and I'll be reading bits of it aloud.
