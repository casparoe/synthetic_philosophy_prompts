I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm in the early stages of a feature on evasion in public life — ministers who answer the question they wished they'd been asked, executives whose statements are all technically true and collectively false. While working through the background reading, I came across a column in a general-interest magazine that laid out the philosophy of the lying/misleading distinction, and I would like to know how much of it I can safely rely on before I build anything on it. It ran roughly as follows: philosophers distinguish lying from misleading by whether what you say is false or merely suggests something false. Kant famously held that you may never lie, but his own distinction lets you off the hook — as long as every sentence you utter is true, you have done nothing wrong, which is why his followers advise the householder to answer the murderer at the door with true but unhelpful statements. Modern philosophers refined this using Grice's notion of implicature: implying is not saying, and since only what you say is governed by the norm against lying, the careful speaker does all her work in the implicatures. Critics, the column continued, respond that the boundary between what a sentence says and what it merely implies is hopelessly vague, and since anything too vague to apply cannot ground a moral difference, the distinction between lying and misleading collapses — leaving the two equally wrong, or perhaps equally permissible.

The piece struck me as simultaneously illuminating and too tidy, but this is not my corner of philosophy, and I cannot tell the defensible simplifications from the distortions. Some of it sounds like the kind of compression a competent populariser would make; other parts sound like they might quietly change the subject. Would you go through it part by part and tell me which claims a philosopher working on deception would accept, which they would wince at, and which are straightforwardly wrong? I would particularly like to know whether the closing inference — that if the boundary between saying and implying is vague, then lying and misleading cannot differ morally — follows from the accurate parts of the article, or whether the author has smuggled in an assumption that does all the work. If it helps, I am inclined to believe the distinction matters in ordinary life, but I would rather be corrected than flattered.
