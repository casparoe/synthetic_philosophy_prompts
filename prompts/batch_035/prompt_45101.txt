hey, so I write a column on science + ideas for a mid-sized outlet, and for the last year I've kept a folder of what I privately call "Gödel abuse": incompleteness theorems invoked to explain why the tax code can never be finished, why postmodernism is right, why your marriage can't be fully understood from inside, why this one weird diet works. it's an inexhaustible genre. my usual move is a grumpy explainer about what the theorems actually say, but honestly nobody reads those. so this time I want satire.

Write me an Onion-style news article, headline plus roughly 600 words, reporting that a prestigious-sounding think tank (make one up, maybe "the Institute for Applied Incompleteness") has announced a bold new extension of Gödel's work to everyday life — something like "no self-improvement system can be both complete and consistent." Include quotes from the center's director, who is very pleased; from the life coach who is already selling a workbook based on the finding before peer review; and from an actual logician who keeps trying to point out, correctly and in complete sentences, that incompleteness is about consistent formal systems strong enough to express arithmetic, that it concerns provability-in-a-system rather than truth in general, and that none of this means "everything is relative" — and who is thanked for his "interesting perspective" and ignored.

The humor has to come from getting the real content right: the jokes should turn on the gap between what the theorems say and what everyone wants them to say, not just be generic "math is hard" jokes. so e.g. the director should be wrong in ways a logician could correct in a seminar, the workbook should have exercises that misuse the notion of a "system," and maybe someone at the press conference asks whether the new theorem applies to itself. keep it deadpan, no winking at the reader, and by the end the reader should feel exactly the pain of every mathematician who has ever been cornered at a party about this. also fine to slip in that Gödel himself proved completeness for first-order logic, just to twist the knife.
