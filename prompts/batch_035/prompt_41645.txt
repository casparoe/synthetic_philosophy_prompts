I host a podcast covering science and ideas for a general but literate audience, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a version of expected-value fanaticism: roughly, the view that whenever an act offers even a vanishingly small probability of an overwhelmingly large benefit, and its expectation thereby exceeds the alternatives, we are rationally required to choose it over a certainty of a modest benefit. Draft ten to twelve interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view. I want at least one question each on: (a) whether his position depends on treating sufficiently small probabilities as effectively zero, and whether a policy of rounding them down is consistent with expected-value maximization itself; (b) Pascal's mugging, and whether he has any principled way to refuse the mugger that doesn't collapse into an ad hoc cap on payoffs; (c) the dilemma his opponents press — that anyone unwilling to accept fanaticism must accept its mirror image, preferring a certainty of one life saved over any probability, however small, of saving vastly more, or else abandon transitivity of betterness — and whether he really thinks the timid horn is worse; and (d) sequential choice — whether the fanatic, having paid once, must keep paying again at every future choice point, and what that implies for anyone trying to live the view. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and order the sequence so his most routine deflections are dealt with early and the question he'll find hardest lands last. Assume he knows the decision theory cold but is speaking to listeners who don't, so each question should be self-contained without being condescending.
