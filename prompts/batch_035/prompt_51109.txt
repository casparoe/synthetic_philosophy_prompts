I'm a novelist about two-thirds through a near-future book where a corporate lab announces they've made the first genuinely new life form, and the plot hinges on whether people treat it as a discovery or just an artifact. The thing itself is a membrane-bound droplet that runs a replicating RNA-like genome and harvests energy from a chemical gradient, and it mutates and all that, but it can't synthesize its own membrane building blocks, so it only "reproduces" as long as the lab keeps feeding it the right medium. Halfway between a virus and the simplest cell, and I've built several scenes of people screaming at each other about whether it counts. So the definitions of life need to do actual narrative work in this book, not just hand-waving.

Here's what I want: take the main candidate definitions of life — the NASA-style working definition (a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution), autopoiesis, the Schrödinger-flavored thermodynamic ones where life is a local pocket that maintains order against entropy by exporting disorder, metabolism-first and replication-first style definitions, and Gánti's chemoton if you want to include it — and rank them by how cleanly they deliver verdicts on my borderline cases, with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. The cases: (1) my droplet, (2) a plain virus, (3) a sterile mule, (4) a bacterial spore that's been dormant for 40,000 years and can still revive, (5) a self-replicating factory system humans left on Mars that mines, repairs itself, and iterates on its own designs through automated trial and error, and (6) something I invented — a cave full of mineral crystals that grow copies of themselves from dissolved silica with heritable flaws, zero metabolism, a million years per generation.

Then a second pass, because this is the part my book actually needs: for whichever definition does best on my invented cases, where does it give my fictional lab's PR department trouble? Like, what would rival scientists or lawyers in the story grab onto to argue the droplet isn't alive, and does a defender of that definition have a decent answer? My editor thinks the whole debate is boring technics and wants me to just declare the thing alive and move on, and honestly I'm half tempted, so also tell me whether one of these definitions is so much more practically useful for world-building that I can just pick it and stop worrying. I don't need citations or a reading list — I need the shape of the fight and where my droplet falls in it.
