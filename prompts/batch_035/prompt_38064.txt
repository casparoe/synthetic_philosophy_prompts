I've been on the rationalist side of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've decided to finally write the population ethics post I've been threatening for years. I want to sanity-check my central argument before I publish, since I expect the comments to be unforgiving. Here is the argument. Parfit's nonidentity problem shows that a couple who conceive now, knowing their child will have a seriously compromised but still worth-living life, do not make that child worse off: had they waited, a different child would have existed instead, and you can't be worse off than nonexistence. It follows that no one whose life is worth living can complain about the terms on which their existence was chosen. Now, since essentially every policy with a long horizon — climate policy above all — shifts the timing of conceptions downstream and thereby changes who comes to exist, the problem generalizes: our grandchildren's lives are worth living, so our present emissions choices cannot have made them worse off than they would otherwise have been, and every "we are wronging future generations" argument fails in exactly the way the conception case does. So the person-affecting rhetoric around climate change is strictly confused, and the only honest remaining case for aggressive emissions reductions is the impersonal one about aggregate welfare — which, stated plainly, most people reject, since it commits them to the repugnant conclusion. My question, then: is this generalization from the conception case to the climate case sound, or is there a disanalogy in the standard treatments that I'm missing? And second, is the non-comparative or threshold account of harm really the standard repair, or does it just relocate the damage — and if so, where exactly does it break? I'd especially like to know which single premise of mine a careful person would attack first, so I can shore it up or concede gracefully.
