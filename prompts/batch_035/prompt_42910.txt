For some years now I have taught the unit on interpersonal utility comparisons, and each year I find myself grading students' arguments for positions I cannot claim to hold consistently myself. Let me set out my untutored verdicts on a range of cases and ask what, if anything, they add up to.

When an anesthetist with a single dose of morphine faces two patients with comparable injuries, one visibly in agony and one stoic, I regard the comparison between their suffering as entirely in order: imprecise, corrigible, but the sort of judgment a responsible clinician makes and should make. When a redistributive policy is defended on the ground that a dollar confers more well-being on a poor person than on a rich one, I am comfortable with that too, at least in broad strokes. When the comparison crosses species — whether a sow's lifetime of confinement is worse than a human's week of severe pain — I grow hesitant, but I still think there is a fact of the matter, just an epistemically difficult one. When the comparison concerns an alleged utility monster whose claim to gigantic pleasure from consuming everyone else's resources must be weighed against their modest losses, I find myself doubting the meaningfulness of the very comparison my utilitarianism instructs me to perform — the conclusion is so repugnant that I reach for the thought that the monster's self-report settles nothing, though I cannot say why it settles less than a human's.

Here are the verdicts I suspect cannot all be saved. Comparisons of suffering strike me as relatively solid — continuous with perception, as it were. Comparisons of happiness strike me as mushy: I cannot take seriously the question whether the serene person of settled routine is happier than the intense person of wild oscillation who would find serenity deadening. Yet an hour of the intense person's despair seems exactly as comparable to an hour of the serene person's contentment as two headaches are to each other, which suggests the asymmetry is an artifact of my sympathy rather than a feature of the comparisons. Further, the constructions philosophers offer to ground aggregation — asking what a fully informed, impartial observer would prefer among fully specified lives, with the parties' own preferences somehow extended to cover others' circumstances — strike me as elegant and useless, since I cannot see how to evaluate the extended preferences without already performing the comparisons they were meant to authorize. And although comparing my own welfare across periods of my life seems effortless, I do not believe that ease transfers to the interpersonal case merely by adding persons.

So my question: is there one account of what interpersonal utility comparisons are — about their semantics, their epistemology, or their metaphysics — that vindicates the verdicts I am confident in (the anesthetist's, the redistributor's, arguably the one about the sow), explains away or reinstates my wavering on happiness and the monster, and says something nontrivial about the informed-observer construction? Or must some confident verdict be surrendered to gain a unified picture? I would like candidate principles stated precisely, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, together with their verdicts on a case I have not considered — say, whether the welfare of the dead can be compared with that of the living, or whether my ease with intrapersonal comparisons across time has any bearing at all on the interpersonal question — and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the best principle leaves behind.
