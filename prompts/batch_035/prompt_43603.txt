I'm a novelist, two-thirds of the way through a book set in Ottoman Syria, and I've written myself into a corner that I suspect is a genuine one rather than a failure of nerve — which is why I'm writing to you rather than to my editor.

The premise: my protagonist, a cloth merchant in Damascus, learns that Death keeps a ledger, and that his line in it has already been written — a single word naming a place, with an appointment for dawn tomorrow. She appears, I've established over four hundred pages, only where the ledger says she will be, and she has never once miswritten. The merchant glimpses her in the souk, looking faintly impatient, and understands that his entry was fixed before he ever knew of it. His options: bolt that night for Aleppo — a hard ride, but once there he could vanish among the caravanserais — or stay in Damascus and go to ground.

Here is the corner. If the ledger says Aleppo, then fleeing delivers him into her hands; if it says Damascus, then staying does. So it seems he should go wherever the ledger does not say — but the ledger says wherever he goes, because it has never been wrong, and it was set down before his choice and untouched by it. My editor says the scene is elegant but pointless: if Death is never wrong, the man is doomed whichever way he rides, so let him ride to Aleppo for the pacing. My protagonist, or rather what I can hear him arguing in the margins, insists that fleeing is still the sensible act, because the ledger makes nothing happen; it merely records what he will do. And a logician friend who read the draft muttered that the choice and the ledger entry are as good as the same fact written twice, and then declined to elaborate.

What I want from you is not pacing advice but the logic of the thing, because I'd like the ending to honour it. Please tell me:

1. What the most defensible account of rational choice says he should do, given the setup exactly as I've specified: prediction without causation, a perfect track record, the entry fixed before his decision.
2. Whether his position is genuinely different from my editor's reading — doomed either way — and, if so, what exactly the difference consists in.
3. Whether anything changes if, in the final draft, he lets a coin decide — a coin flipped in a manner no ledger, however infallible about his character, could have anticipated. I'm aware this may amount to asking whether she predicts his acts or only his dispositions; tell me if so.
4. Whether the ending I confess I want — he does the rational thing and dies of it, or he survives because the rational thing happened to be the unpredicted one — can be made internally coherent, or whether the book must simply choose between depicting him as rational and depicting him as alive.

Where a defensible answer exists but you would be guessing which one applies to my setup, say so rather than smoothing it over. And please tell me how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point in your reasoning where you feel least sure. Since I'm being published in London, kindly write your reply in British English.
