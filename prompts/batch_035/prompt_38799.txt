my reading group (half econ grad students, half philosophers, we've been going for a year on ethics + welfare econ) is wrapping up a term on discounting and the far future. We read Ramsey's argument that caring less about future well-being just because it's future is ethically indefensible, then a pile of climate economics where the discount rate quietly decides everything (Stern vs Nordhaus style fights), then Parfit on bias toward the future. For our last session I want a decision tree that sorts each of us from raw intuitions about cases to a named position on discounting. The branch nodes have to be questions someone can answer without knowing what position it leads to — built from concrete cases like: a mild headache today vs a slightly worse one next month for you personally; an operation you had last year and have completely forgotten vs an equally painful one coming next year; a big benefit to a specific person alive now vs an equally big benefit to an equally real person born in 2230; whether the relative badness of two future events should be allowed to flip depending on what calendar date you ask the question from; whether your current self's impatience gets to boss around your later selves or only your own cases.

Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the list of isms: pick the axes you think generate the space (I'd guess things like pure vs epistemic discounting, agent-neutral vs agent-relative, constant vs declining rates, whether death's badness depends on how it relates to the time of your life) and then locate the actual positions in that space. Leaves should include at least: classical exponential discounted utilitarianism, quasi-hyperbolic present-bias views (both the "descriptively true but irrational" and the "defensible" version), the declining-discount-rate family in the Chichilnisky / Weitzman vein, pure uncertainty discounting with no pure time preference, person-affecting or existence-conditioned restrictions, and full temporal neutrality in the Sidgwick-Nagel tradition. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and mark the unoccupied cells of the space with a quick diagnosis of why nobody lives there (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). Also flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate with cases alone — my hunch is epistemic discounting with a heavy uncertainty tail and a small pure rate are basically indistinguishable until you pin down the metaphysics, but tell me if im wrong.

About 8-10 leaves total, audience is smart people with zero decision theory background so keep cases concrete and don't use math beyond "1% per year". The whole point is that we end up sorted by what we actually say about cases rather than which camp we'd been flirting with in the discussion, so please no branch question of the form "do you believe in pure time preference?" — that's the label, not the thing.
