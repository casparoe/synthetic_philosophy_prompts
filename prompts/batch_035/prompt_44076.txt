The exam question was:

"Alice stands next to a switch at a fork in a runaway trolley track. The trolley is heading toward five people; if Alice pulls the switch, it will be diverted onto a side track where it will kill one person. (a) Explain the doctrine of double effect. (b) Apply it to Alice's case and say whether she may pull the switch. (c) Now consider a variant where the side track loops around and rejoins the main track just before the five, so that if the one person weren't on it, the diverted trolley would continue around the loop and kill the five anyway — the one person's body is what stops it. Does the doctrine of double effect give the same verdict here as in the ordinary case? Defend your answer."

Here's the student's answer:

"The doctrine of double effect traces back to Aquinas's discussion of self-defense. He held that killing an attacker can be permissible when the attacker's death is not intended but merely foreseen as a side effect of an act of self-preservation. Later writers generalized this into a four-part test: an act with a good and a bad effect is permissible if (1) the act itself is not wrong, (2) the bad effect is not intended, only foreseen, (3) the good effect does not come about by way of the bad effect, and (4) the good is proportionate to the bad. The core idea is a moral asymmetry between what an agent intends and what an agent merely foresees.

In Alice's case, the doctrine clearly permits pulling the switch. Alice intends to save the five; the one person's death is foreseen but plays no role in her plan. This is exactly the point Foot was making when she invented the bystander case — that even a non-consequentialist can allow diverting the trolley. Compare the surgeon who kills one patient to harvest organs for five: there the doctrine forbids it, since the death is intended as a means to the rescue. The doctrine of double effect is basically the principle that killing is worse than letting die, and that's precisely what the consequentialist denies.

In the loop case nothing changes. The one person's death is still a side effect of diverting the trolley, not part of what Alice is trying to accomplish — what saves the five is the trolley going onto the side track, and the death just happens on the way. Since Alice doesn't intend the death, the doctrine gives the same verdict: she may pull the switch."
