I serve on the constitutional review committee of our graduate student association, and we must decide by next month whether to recommend an amendment barring organized electoral slates. The trigger: last spring's election was swept by a self-declared "party" of about a dozen members who ran on a shared platform, held a whip system on senate votes, and publicly censured one of their own for voting against the bloc on a budget motion. A petition signed by a quarter of the association now demands that we amend our constitution to prohibit parties and slates in student elections — the petition's sponsors explicitly invoke Simone Weil's wartime argument against political parties, which, as they read it, holds that a party is not merely an imperfect association but a machine structurally built to manufacture power out of money, slogans, and number-counting; that it offers members the warm feeling of collective thought while actually substituting "we think" for thinking; and that a party's first loyalty is to its own growth and survival rather than to truth or justice — so that even a party whose platform is entirely correct corrupts the judgment of anyone inside it. Our committee is split three ways. One faction takes the Weil reading seriously but objects that her argument proves too much: if parties corrupt thinking by pre-committing members to positions, then caucuses, unions, religious groups, and even our own petition's signatories would have to go too, and Weil herself admitted that any institution — a church, a nation, a trade union — can degenerate into a party without being one. A second faction doubts the whole framing: student government has no army, no police, and no real power, so the mechanism by which parties supposedly corrode thought and concentrate power may simply not operate at our scale; coordinating votes in a student senate is more like roommates agreeing on dinner than like a machine for domination. A third faction wants a middle path — not banning slates outright but banning the whip system and bloc-discipline of members once elected, on the theory that what's objectionable in Weil's diagnosis is the transfer of judgment, not the association as such. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should state a clear recommendation — adopt the amendment, reject it, or adopt a specified modification — with reasons the committee could actually act on, and it should give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Address the three factions' objections explicitly rather than assuming they will go away. Keep the memo under 700 words; lead with the recommendation.
