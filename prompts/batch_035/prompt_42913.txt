I've been a regular on rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the LessWrong days, and I've noticed that one of our community's recurring fights — the progress-studies insistence that knowledge and capability compound against a background of doom-cyclicalist talk about institutional decay and civilizational senescence — is a rerun of an argument that has been running, without resolution, for over two millennia. On the cyclical side: Polybius, who in Book 6 of his Histories lays out the anacyclosis, the procession of constitutions from kingship through tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and mob rule and back again — though, tellingly, he also believed Rome's mixed constitution could substantially brake the cycle, which is a strange escape hatch for a theorist of inevitable recurrence to leave open. Ibn Khaldun, in the fourteenth century, ran dynasties on group cohesion, with each dynasty decaying over about four generations as solidarity hardened into luxury, while separately insisting that the sciences accumulate within a civilization and can be transmitted onward to a successor. Spengler treated whole cultures as organisms with limited lifespans, each running through a fixed sequence of artistic and political forms — and even claimed that Western mathematics, of all things, had essentially exhausted its creative possibilities, which is about as strong a claim that a culture's line can come to an end as anyone has made. Toynbee, whose A Study of History began as a taxonomy of some twenty-odd civilizations responding to challenges, drifted across the later volumes toward treating the whole story as pointing to a religious terminus — effectively conceding the plot a direction. The linearists, meanwhile — Whig history, Enlightenment progress narratives, our own compounding-knowledge people — hardly ever deny that particular polities, religions, and institutions rise, saturate, and decay; they say that's local noise on a trend. What strikes me is that every great cyclist smuggles in something linear, and every linearist concedes the local cycles, and after two thousand years nobody has budged.

My working hypothesis, offered as just a hypothesis, is that this is not one substantive disagreement at all but two groups describing different variables at different granularities under a single name. Pick knowledge, energy capture, or population as your variable and the series looks cumulative; pick any particular polity or institution and it rises and falls; impose an identity condition that groups polities into a single "civilization" with a birth and a death and you get Spengler; deny that such identity conditions carve anything real and the cycles dissolve back into trend plus wiggle. On this reading the stalemate persists because no theorist on either side ever fixed, before looking at the data, the rules for individuating the thing that is supposed to be cycling — so each generation re-slices the same past to fit its preferred curve.

But it can't be that simple, because the cyclists did make what look like commitments with teeth: Spengler attached dates to the West's slide into its terminal phase, and Polybius told us exactly what the mixed constitution was supposed to buy and for how long. So please diagnose the disagreement rather than survey the theorists. Is it verbal, substantive, or a compositional error — the shape of a whole inferred from the shapes of its parts? Is there a real empirical question buried in it, e.g., whether macrosocial variables are stationary or trending, and if so, what would count as progress: a civilizational identity condition fixed in advance, a statistical test that distinguishes a genuine cycle from a random walk with drift, an agreed-upon variable? Or is the shape-of-history question ill-posed, and if so, what question should we be asking instead?
