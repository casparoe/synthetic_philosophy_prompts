I've been reading a popular history of analytic philosophy, and its chapter on Russell closes with a summary of the theory of descriptions that I'd like to have audited. The author is a professional philosopher, so I'm inclined to accept it, but parts felt off to me. Here is the gist, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct:

Russell's theory of descriptions, introduced in "On Denoting" (1905), holds that a phrase like "the present King of France" only looks like a name. It is really a disguised conjunction: to say "The King of France is bald" is to say that there is exactly one King of France and that whatever is King of France is bald. Russell's guiding idea came from his epistemology: we are acquainted only with our own sense data and the particulars directly before the mind, so any name purporting to pick out something beyond that — "Julius Caesar," for instance — must really abbreviate a description. Because the phrase isn't a name, the famous sentence isn't about anything at all, and so comes out neither true nor false. This let Russell dissolve Meinong's menagerie of nonexistent entities — the golden mountain, the round square — without having to posit them, and it explained why "George IV wished to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley" is informative in a way that "George IV wished to know whether Scott was Scott" is not. Strawson's 1950 critique showed that Russell had mistaken the logic of ordinary speech: in uttering "The King of France is bald," one asserts no existence claim; one presupposes it, and the utterance has a truth value only if the presupposition is met. The theory of descriptions is now chiefly of historical interest as an early exercise in logical analysis.

Please go through this claim by claim. Which parts would a philosopher of language accept as stated, which are common simplifications, and which are outright wrong? In particular, is there a misattribution anywhere — a view that belongs to a critic of Russell being presented as Russell's own, or a strand of Russell's thought from elsewhere in his work being presented as the core of the 1905 paper? Note that some of these errors may interact, and I'd like to know whether the summary is even internally consistent. Finally, the last sentence delivers a verdict: does that verdict follow from anything true in the summary, and does it survive the fact (if it is a fact) that the debate over descriptions continued long after Strawson, over things like quantifier scope, incomplete descriptions, and so-called referential uses? I want to know which corrections, if any, actually matter to the verdict, and which don't.
