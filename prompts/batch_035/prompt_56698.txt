i keep getting into arguments with my brother about whether "collapse" even means anything as a concept and i've decided the way to settle it is to figure out what an actual theory of societal collapse has to get right before i'll take any of these books seriously. so write me the spec: the requirements list for an adequate theory or explanation of civilizational collapse -- the cases it has to get right (western roman empire, the late bronze age eastern mediterranean, the classic maya, easter island -- pick whatever you need from these), the distinctions it can't just paper over, the results from archaeology and paleoclimatology it can't contradict. for each requirement say what's motivating it, i.e. what goes wrong with a theory that lacks it.

some things i specifically want you to deal with in the requirements themselves:

- my brother's line is that collapse is never a real thing, it's just change, and the people who talk about collapse are either nostalgic for aristocracies or selling doom books. so one requirement has to be about whether a theory can distinguish "collapse" from "transformation" in a non-question-begging way, or whether that distinction is itself impossible to draw (in which case say so, that would actually vindicate him and i want to know). does it count as collapse if the political superstructure goes but daily life for a farmer is basically unchanged? does population have to drop? complexity? living standards? who is the collapse a collapse *for*?
- easter island specifically, because the popular ecocide story and the revisionist "it was fine until europeans showed up" story are both out there, and a decent theory should have something to say about how contested empirical cases constrain it. it can't just pick whichever version is convenient.
- the anthropic-ish worry: we only get detailed evidence from places where writing or durable records existed, which is not a random sample of collapses. is handling that a real requirement or is it just a caveat people mumble?
- whether a theory needs to explain non-collapse too. empires that weathered the same shocks (egypt through the bronze age crisis, roughly) -- if your theory of collapse can't tell you why the same drought or trade disruption kills one polity and leaves the neighbor intact, is it a theory of anything?

mark which requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps (tainter-style complexity/energy people, the resilience people, the "there are no collapses only transformations" people). and if you think some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied -- like maybe "observer-neutral definition" and "the concept does real explanatory work" -- flag that, because that's the part i most care about. no need to score the existing books against the list, i'll do that myself and probably badly.
