I've been following the argument between illusionists and their critics for a while now, and it seems completely stuck. On one side you've got people like Dennett and Frankish saying phenomenal consciousness as normally conceived -- the raw feel of red, the what-it's-like -- is a kind of user interface the brain presents to itself, and that once neuroscience explains why the brain generates these judgments and reports, there's nothing left to explain: the "feel" was never a real ingredient, just a way the system describes its own discriminative states. On the other side you've got people like Chalmers and Goff saying that this leaves out the very thing we started with, and that an account which predicts all reports and behavior can still fail to explain experience -- some of them even argue illusionism is self-defeating, since an illusion is presumably something that's experienced, so positing an illusion of consciousness seems to presuppose consciousness.

My honest observation is that in ~30 years of this nobody has switched sides. Illusionists keep saying the critics are confusing the properties represented with the act of representing; critics keep saying the illusionists are explaining access, not phenomenology, and that "you only think there's a difference" is itself a thought there's something it's like to have. So my question is about the structure of the thing, not a verdict on who's right. Is this a substantive disagreement or a verbal one -- are the two camps just using "consciousness" for different things (a functional/representational kind vs. a primitive phenomenal kind), so that the illusionist wins by default on their definition and the anti-illusionist wins on theirs, and the "disagreement" is really a fight about which concept deserves the word? Or is it a genuine disagreement about standards of explanation: the illusionist demands that any explanandum be functionally individuated, and the anti-illusionist thinks that's a methodological choice, not a discovery, and that some seemings should count as bedrock data rather than as theory-laden reports to be explained away? Or maybe the two sides are actually answering different questions under one heading -- "how does the brain produce these reports" versus "why should there be something it's like" -- and the stalemate persists because each side treats the other's question as illegitimate?

I'd also like your view on what would even count as progress here. Would it take a conceptual distinction nobody has drawn yet, a better science of introspection that shows the reports are less trustworthy than they seem, some clever thought experiment, or is the real answer that this particular question is ill-posed and the field should expect permanent stalemate? And if you think it's ill-posed, explain why the participants don't see that -- smart people on both sides have had decades to notice it.

(small confession: I started out on the illusionist side and haven't moved either, which is part of why I'm suspicious of my own read on where the impasse lies. please factor that in.)
