I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of physics course, and we've just finished the substantivalism-versus-relationism unit, ending with Newton's bucket. Over the break I read a popular book on the history of ideas about space, and its chapter on Newton gave an account of the bucket argument that I want to check against what we covered. The gist of it was this:

Newton hung a bucket of water from a twisted rope and let the rope unwind. As the bucket began to spin, the water stayed flat; later, when friction made the water share the bucket's rotation, the water climbed the sides and formed a concave surface. Newton's point was that the water's behavior cannot be explained by its motion relative to the bucket, since the water is momentarily at rest relative to the bucket precisely when its surface is most curved. Rotation, he concluded, must be motion with respect to absolute space — a real, invisible container in which the bucket turns. The argument settled the question for two hundred years, until Mach observed that the water might simply be rotating relative to the fixed stars and the rest of the matter of the universe, with no need for absolute space at all. Einstein then proved Mach right: in general relativity, space is nothing over and above the web of relations between events, so Newton's container was simply a mistake, and the bucket experiment is now regarded as a curiosity from the history of science.
