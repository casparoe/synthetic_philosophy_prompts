I teach a third-year philosophy-of-mind seminar for eighteen students, and the topic has just become awkwardly personal: last week, after a session on Hofstadter's careenium — his thought experiment in which a brain is simulated by myriads of tiny marbles, his sims, careening around a frictionless pool table, with higher-level symbols just being recurrent clumps of marbles — a student asked me plainly whether the symbols push the marbles around or only the other way about, and I caught myself hand-waving. I would rather they collide with that question themselves than hear me finesse it. Design a ninety-minute in-class activity that stages the careenium: give me roles and rules (perhaps some students as sims obeying strictly local collision rules, and others required to give causal explanations of what happened at different levels of description), a structure in which the puzzle of downward causation, multiple realisability, and the apparent causal idleness of symbol-talk emerges from what the students do rather than from anything the facilitator says, and a debrief that maps the specific moments of the activity onto those parts of the problem. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, whatever the facilitator must supply about the fact that in the real careenium nobody designs the marble trajectories and nobody stands outside the system. Please write the answer in British English.
