Two members of my ethics reading group have argued about the demandingness objection to consequentialism for years and have finally agreed to run an adversarial collaboration. The first holds that the objection simply fails: if it is wrong to let someone die when you could save them at little cost, there is no principled point at which the growing size of the sacrifice makes it permissible, so the extremity of consequentialism's demands is a discovery about morality, not a defect in the theory. The second holds that the objection succeeds because agents are permitted to devote a disproportionate, but not unlimited, share of their resources to their own projects and commitments — an agent-centered prerogative of roughly the sort Scheffler proposed — and that consequentialism's real error is treating the impersonal standpoint as the only legitimate one. Draft the joint statement they should be able to sign: a numbered section of claims both accept (e.g., about rescue cases, about how much more we ought to do than we do, about what the disagreement is *not* about); a disagreements table stating their residual disagreements in wording each would accept for their own side; and the cruxes — I suspect one is whether any principled limit can be placed on the prerogative without collapsing it into either the consequentialist view or an unbounded license, and another is whether the intuitions driving the objection survive a debunking explanation of why we would have them regardless of their truth — with, for each crux, an argument or case both agree would bear on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. As you draft, keep clearly separate what both sides genuinely accept from formulations that merely paper over a dispute, flag any point where you are ascribing to one side a concession it would not actually make, and do not present either side's position on contested cruxes as the settled view among philosophers who work on demandingness.
