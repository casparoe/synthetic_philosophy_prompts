I'm an undergraduate taking a course called Well-Being and Public Policy, and I'm starting my term paper on the World Happiness Report. The professor keeps saying that any measurement practice smuggles in a theory, and that we should learn to read the theory off the practice, so that's what I want to do here. I'll describe the practice as I understand it, and then I have questions about what it presupposes. Please answer them in the sections I list at the end.

The practice, concretely: each year the Report ranks countries by the national average of one question, the Cantril ladder, on which respondents place their own life on a scale from the worst possible life they could imagine for themselves to the best possible life they could imagine, zero to ten. Alongside the ranking, the Report produces an explanation of each country's average: national results are regressed on six variables — GDP per capita, having someone to count on in times of trouble, healthy life expectancy, perceived freedom to make one's own life choices, charitable giving, and perceived absence of corruption — and the unexplained remainder is expressed as a distance from a hypothetical worst-case country the Report constructs and calls dystopia, so that a country can be said to have more happiness than its measurable circumstances predict. The rankings have also become political objects: Finland markets itself on its long streak at the top, the UAE at one point appointed a minister whose portfolio was happiness, and Bhutan has long promoted a rival index, Gross National Happiness, built around pillars of a distinctive national vision of the good life.

Here is what I want reconstructed. First, the ladder question itself: it does not ask how often people feel joy or how much pain they are in, nor whether their desires are satisfied, but how satisfied they judge their lives to be against a standard of the best life they can imagine. Does that presuppose that well-being is a global reflective judgment rather than a felt experience, and if so, does it also presuppose something about whose standard counts — since two people with identical lives can imagine very different bests, and a person whose expectations have been ground down may rate a grim life highly? Second, the aggregation: the Report averages, so a country of uniformly moderately satisfied citizens ties with one that is half delighted and half despairing. What does that presuppose about the distribution of well-being, and does anything in the practice besides the average — say, the dystopia baseline — suggest the Report's authors are uneasy about it? Third, the dystopia construction itself: it seems to presuppose that a country's surplus over its circumstances is some real national stock of contentment that causes the survey answers rather than being inferred from them. Is that coherent, or is the explanation circular? Fourth, the political uses: what does a government that sets out to top the rankings presuppose — that well-being is the kind of thing a state can produce on demand, and that citizens' self-reports are what should be optimized, such that raising reported life satisfaction by managing expectations would count as success? And is Bhutan's rival index genuinely a rival theory of well-being, or just a different weighting of the same theory?

I'd like the paper-adjacent answer organized as follows: (1) The implicit theory of well-being, about 350 words, covering what the ladder question and the choice of a life-evaluation measure over an affect measure jointly presuppose, and which standard views of welfare they rule out; (2) Aggregation and the dystopia baseline, about 300 words, on what averaging presupposes about distribution and whether the dystopia residual presupposes an underlying quantity of well-being, including whether that is coherent; (3) What the political uses presuppose, about 300 words, on the state's role and on whether governing for the top rank differs from governing for well-being; (4) Where the practice is underdetermined, about 200 words, on which of these commitments the practice strictly needs versus which it merely fits well with, i.e., what rival theories would license the same rankings; and (5) What would have to change, about 200 words, on what a version of the practice built on a hedonic measure, or a prioritarian one sensitive to the worst-off, would have to change in the survey, the aggregation, and the political target. Please be explicit about where you think the practice presupposes something philosophically fragile, as opposed to something it could comfortably survive.
