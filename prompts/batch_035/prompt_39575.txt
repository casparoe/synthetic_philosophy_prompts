My roommate and her brother argued for basically an hour at dinner last week over restaurant ratings and I got stuck listening to the whole thing. It started because we were picking a place to eat and there was this taqueria with a suspiciously high rating and only a handful of reviews. Her brother said online ratings become close to worthless once a place gets popular, because reviewers see the current average before they write theirs, so the reviews aren't independent evidence — they're partly people just echoing the crowd. My roommate called that way too cynical: each reviewer actually ate the food, and their own experience is real evidence that doesn't vanish just because they also happened to see the average. He conceded that, but said the problem is subtler: imagine everyone is perfectly rational and each person weights their own experience only a little against the accumulated rating. Then a couple of unlucky early reviews drag the average down, everyone after that rationally discounts their own good meal, the rating never recovers, and the final score reflects two people's bad nights instead of a thousand people's dinners. She said that cuts both ways — lucky early reviews can inflate a mediocre place the same way — and that real rating systems have fixes anyway, like recency weighting or hiding the average from reviewers, so whatever pathology exists is an engineering problem, not a deep point about rationality. He replied that the deep point survives any fix: no matter how you tune the system, each rater is still choosing between trusting the aggregate and trusting themselves, and once the aggregate is big enough the rational choice is almost always to defer, which means fresh information basically stops entering the system — rational people herding, and the herd locking in whatever the first few movers happened to feel. She answered that he's describing the system working correctly, not breaking: if your one dinner really is weaker evidence than a thousand accumulated dinners, then deferring is what a rational agent should do, so the "pathology" is just updating properly. He came back with: fine, each individual deferral is correct each time, but the aggregate outcome is still worse than if people had just pooled their raw experiences, so it's individually rational and collectively dumb — his analogy was everyone standing at a concert to see better, which each person is right to do and which makes everyone worse off. She said maybe, but ratings do move over time, new restaurants do climb, and dissenters who genuinely trust their own taste do drag averages around, so the system isn't frozen the way his story needs it to be. At that point they asked me to referee and I honestly could not.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and adjudicate each move — who's right about what, and which moves are weak? And is there something both of them are missing? Please write your answer in American English.
