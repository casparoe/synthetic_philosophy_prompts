i've been reading about how the east german state basically folded in on itself in '89 with barely a shot fired, and i keep coming back to performance legitimacy as the thing to test against it — the idea that a non-democratic regime can be legitimate in its citizens' eyes if it delivers: jobs, growth, order, a welfare state. on paper the GDR should be the showcase case for that theory: by most accounts the most prosperous state in the eastern bloc this side of the soviet union, full employment, cheap childcare, nobody sleeping rough. and yet it disintegrated in about two months once hungary started letting people through in the late summer of '89 and the leipzig monday demonstrations got going. honecker got pushed out by his own politburo, the wall came down after a famously botched press conference, and when the state was formally dissolved a year later basically noone rose to defend it.

here's what i want: analyze 1989 in the GDR strictly through the lens of performance legitimacy. treat it as a mapping exercise — who is the "audience" whose approval constitutes the legitimacy (workers? the party elite? the stasi officers who declined to shoot?), what exactly counts as the performance being judged (absolute living standards, or something relative to a comparison group?), and what the theory predicts should happen when performance dips versus what actually happened.

one wrinkle i find fascinating and want you to take seriously. almost everywhere in the GDR you could watch west german TV — crime dramas, game shows, ads, all of it. except two pockets, one around dresden and one up in the northeast, where the terrain blocked the signal; people sarcastically called those the "valley of the clueless." if legitimacy was being benchmarked against the west germans every evening, the theory seems to predict what actually happened once enough citizens could compare. but then i trip over this: most GDR citizens could watch western TV for three decades and the state still lasted forty years. so which is it — was the comparison slowly eroding legitimacy the whole time, or does comparison only convert into a withdrawal of legitimacy under some extra condition? what condition, and does the theory have anything to say about it?

last thing: be honest about where performance legitimacy is just silent and something else — fear, exit options, gorbachev, elite self-preservation — has to carry the explanatory weight. especially the elite: why did the politburo fold rather than do what beijing did months earlier? if the answer is that the leaders themselves stopped believing in their own performance story, that seems like a different mechanism than citizens withdrawing legitimacy, and i'd like you to keep the two apart. dont lean anything on contested details of what happened; take the broad strokes above as given.
