I run developer experience at a company of around 700 engineers, and one of my jobs is deciding when a de facto practice gets promoted to an official standard — we write it down, it goes in the handbook, teams get audited against it — versus when we leave it alone, or even actively kill it. This comes up a couple times a month: code formatting, how internal services get named, where the error message goes on a dialog, you name it.

A while back I read David Lewis's book Convention, the 1969 one, where he treats conventions as solutions to recurring coordination problems — everyone conforms mainly because they expect everyone else to conform, and some other arrangement would have worked about as well. It struck me that this is basically my job in theory form, but I can't turn it into anything I can actually run. When a proposal lands in my inbox I have maybe twenty minutes before I have to say "codify it, leave it alone, or burn it down," and the people making these calls (sometimes me, sometimes managers with no theory at all) need something they can follow mechanically.

So: write me a decision procedure grounded in whatever from Lewis is actually usable here. I'm imagining a series of questions asked in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure outputs "leave it alone" and why, and the points where it has to hand the decision back to a human because the question is a values call, not a fact. Two parts of the theory I especially want baked in, plus one worry of my own:

- The arbitrariness point: a convention needs a workable alternative. If only one arrangement could ever have worked, it's not a convention, and treating it like one is a category error — so the procedure has to have some test for "is there really a second way this could have gone."
- The mutual expectation stuff, which I only half remember — common knowledge, everyone expecting everyone to conform, whatever the right version of that is. I suspect this matters for deciding whether writing something down helps (creates the common knowledge) or hurts.
- My worry: a handbook rule nobody follows is worse than a practice everyone follows without a rule, because now the rule is dead letter and the handbook loses authority. The procedure should have something to say about when codifying is how a convention dies.

Then run it on three real-ish cases:

1. Error dialogs: about 80% of teams put the human-readable explanation before the error code, for no reason anyone can articulate other than "that's what everyone does." There's a proposal to make the code-first style official becuase a big customer asked for consistency across products.
2. Code review: nobody actually reads the whole diff before approving, everyone knows nobody does, and there's a proposal to write a rule that reviewers must read 100% of every diff. I suspect this one isn't a coordination problem at all — more like everyone individually benefits from slacking — but I'd like the procedure itself to detect that, and I want to know what it outputs when it doesn't apply. Does it punt, or does it still say something useful about what to do instead?
3. Service naming: every internal service gets prefixed with the owning team's name, and honestly I hate it — it's ugly and it locks in team boundaries. I'd like the procedure to tell me whether and how you can break a working convention on purpose, and what it says about the transition. There's clearly a version of breaking it that's fine and a version that's a disaster, and I can't tell which is which.

For each case, say what the procedure outputs and where it had to punt, including any output you think I won't like.

Last thing: at the end, tell me how confident you are overall, and specifically separate the steps of the procedure that are genuinely Lewis from the ones where you (or I) have just made something up that sounds Lewisian. Single out the step you're least sure actually tracks the theory. I'd rather know the procedure is half-invented than think it's more orthodox than it is.
