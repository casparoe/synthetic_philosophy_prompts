For a seminar paper in graduate epistemology I have to write up my overall position on perceptual knowledge, and before I submit it I would like an outside audit of whether the package is actually coherent. Here is the set of commitments I have arrived at over the semester. (1) I am an internalist about justification: the factors that make a belief justified must in principle be accessible to the subject, at least in the sense that the subject could recognize them on reflection; facts about the reliability of a belief-forming process that the subject has no inkling of cannot by themselves justify anything for that subject. (2) I am a dogmatist about perception: a visual experience as of a red wall can give immediate, prima facie justification to believe that the wall is red, without any antecedent justification for believing that vision is reliable; the justification does not need to be earned by first establishing the credentials of the source. (3) I think the bootstrapping pattern of reasoning is epistemically worthless: a driver with no independent information about her fuel gauge who, each time the gauge reads near empty, subsequently notes that the tank is indeed near empty, and on that basis eventually reasons her way to the conclusion that the gauge is reliable, does not thereby come to know that the gauge is reliable. Acquiring that kind of conclusion that cheaply would be a case of easy knowledge, and I am confident it cannot be had that way. (4) I endorse epistemic closure: if someone knows the premises of a valid argument and competently deduces the conclusion from them, the conclusion is thereby known, provided the subject had no defeating evidence. (5) I think track-record reasoning is fine when the records are supplied independently of the source being checked: the same driver could come to know her gauge is reliable if a mechanic, using a dipstick, told her the tank level after each of her readings and she compiled a concordant record — but I do not allow the analogous reasoning when her own subsequent glances at the tank supply the confirming entries.

My worry is that these do not fit together, but I honestly cannot see where the problem is, and I do not want to telegraph which one I would give up. Please structure your reply as follows. First, a section titled "Bottom line" of at most four sentences stating whether the package is tenable and, in one line, where the main strain sits. Second, a section titled "Tensions, ranked by severity" of roughly 300 words: identify which pairs or subsets of my commitments genuinely pull against each other, order them from most to least severe, and for each say concretely what would have to give — which commitment must be revised or reinterpreted, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere. Third, a section titled "Apparent tensions that dissolve" of roughly 150 words: pick out the conflicts that only look like conflicts and show, briefly, how they dissolve on inspection; please be discriminating here rather than flagging everything, since I have been told my set mixes real tensions with merely apparent ones and I do not know which is which. Fourth, a section titled "The cheapest fix and its costs" of roughly 150 words: say which single revision would clean up the most, and be explicit about what I would be losing — in particular, whether the fix preserves my ability to say both that the driver in my own-observations version of the case fails to know, and that the driver in the mechanic version succeeds, since I would like to keep that contrast if I honestly can.
