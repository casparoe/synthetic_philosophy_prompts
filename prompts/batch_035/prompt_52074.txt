I teach graduate metaphysics, and at a recent departmental colloquium a visiting speaker — a committed Humean about laws of nature — leaned repeatedly on the slogan that the laws don't govern what happens; they merely describe what happens. The sentence did real dialectical work for him at least once: a student asked what distinguishes a genuine law from a merely accidental regularity, and he brushed the question off by saying that once you stop thinking of laws as governing, there is nothing further to ask. But the more interesting exchange came afterwards. My colleague in the physics department, who is no Humean and thinks of the fundamental laws as constraints rather than summaries, invoked the very same sentence to argue that laws cannot genuinely explain anything: a description of the pattern, he said, cannot tell you why the pattern is there. I came away unsure whether either of them was actually entitled to what he wanted from that one line, and I want to work out the possibilities properly before I raise it at our next seminar.

You should act as an expert in metaphysics and philosophy of science, with particular command of the dispute between Humeans (simple regularity views, the Mill–Ramsey–Lewis best-system view) and anti-Humeans (nomic necessitation among universals, dispositional powers, primitivism about lawhood), and with special attention to the role the word "governing" plays in that dispute. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with some quiet brainstorming: what distinct theses could the describe/govern contrast actually be expressing? Then organize them from weakest to strongest. Which readings are platitudes that no anti-Humean would contest — e.g., that law-statements have the grammar of descriptions rather than commands, that there is no cosmic legislator, that laws do not issue permissions and prohibitions to anything? Which are substantive Humean theses — e.g., that there is no necessitation relation over and above the pattern of events, that the laws supervene on the total distribution of local matters of particular fact, or that "governance" is a metaphor a respectable ontology must paraphrase away? Which are strong enough to be false — e.g., that laws cannot figure in genuine explanations because they merely compress the facts, or that the question of why a regularity is lawful rather than accidental is a pseudo-question? And are any of the readings orthogonal to one another? In particular, could one hold that laws are descriptive all the way down and still insist they explain, or conversely grant that laws necessitate while denying that they "govern" in any sense that matters?

The deliverable I need is a numbered menu rather than a survey of Humeanism: each entry should state one precise thesis behind the slogan, attach a verdict — platitude, defensible but contentious, or false — and then say two further things about it: whether it would support the speaker's dismissal of the law/accident question, and whether it would support my colleague's dismissal of nomic explanation. Please note explicitly where the two uses pull apart, since the speaker, as far as I could tell, wanted the law/accident distinction relocated rather than abolished (into whatever systematizes the facts best, presumably), while my colleague wanted something eliminated outright. Close by telling me what single question I should have put to each of them at the colloquium — the question that would have forced the slogan to show which reading it was standing on.
