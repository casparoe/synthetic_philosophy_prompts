Last month my roommate Dev and our friend Marta were over at my place, and we ended up watching a compilation of perceptual illusions. One clip has stayed with me, mostly because of the argument it started. The clip shows a dark screen on which a red disk flashes on the left; a fraction of a second later, a green disk flashes on the right, and the two flashes alternate a few times. Nothing on the screen moves. Yet all three of us, independently, saw a single dot gliding from left to right that switched color, abruptly, roughly halfway across. The switch was the eerie part — it seemed to occur at a definite spot on the path, as if the dot were keeping a schedule.

Marta went first. "Think about when that experience could have been built," she said. "The color switch sits halfway across the path, but your brain couldn't have known the second color until the green flash actually arrived — before that it would have had to know the future. So the experience can't have been assembled as it unfolded. The brain must have gathered everything, waited for the green, constructed the whole glide with the switch in place, and only then handed it to you — stamped as live. It was a delayed broadcast dressed up as live coverage. So the experience happened later than it seemed to."

Dev disagreed. "If experience were running behind the world like that, you couldn't react to anything quickly — you couldn't catch a ball or swat a fly. But my real objection is different: I think the experience happened on time, and what got altered afterward was the record, not the show. You saw the flashes as they came in, and within moments your memory was doctored into the smooth glide-with-a-color-change story. The event happened when it seemed to happen; only your memory of it is false."

Marta said the reaction argument proves little — a fifth of a second is nothing, and all perception is already late, so the "now" you live in is always slightly manufactured. Then she pressed on his story: "It says the switch at the midpoint never happened to me at all, that I merely remember it. But I remember it as vividly as anything I've ever seen, and a memory is a memory of an experience. If the switch never entered my awareness, what exactly am I remembering? And if you say the constructed glide did occur, just 'outside awareness,' then you've conceded my delayed broadcast."

Dev answered that her backdating was empty. "A delayed broadcast can be detected because the game exists independently of the broadcast. Here the only access anyone has to the experience is the experience itself, so 'it happened later than it seemed' compares the broadcast not to the game but to itself. Honestly, I suspect the question 'when exactly did the switch enter your experience?' may not have an answer — there may be nothing there to be dated."

Marta called that a cop-out: "Everything that happens happens at some time. If you can't say when, that's a fact about your ignorance, not about the world."

They turned to me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what — not just who wins overall — and tell me whether there is something both of them are missing. I'd rather you commit to definite verdicts on each point, including their final exchange about whether the timing question has an answer, than lay out the possibilities and leave the choosing to me. Please write in American English.
