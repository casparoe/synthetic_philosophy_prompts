I work on decision theory, and the exchange over risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU) that followed Buchak's Risk and Rationality (2013) seems to me to have calcified into a stable standoff, and I'd like a diagnosis of why rather than a verdict on who's right.

The shape of the dispute, as I understand it: Buchak holds that rational agents can have a distinct risk attitude — a third parameter alongside credences and utilities — and that Allais-style preferences (taking the sure thing over a higher-EU gamble in one choice, while taking the riskier option in a matched pair) are not merely forgivable but fully rational. The Sure-Thing Principle, on her view, is not a requirement of rationality but a substantive commitment to risk neutrality smuggled into the axioms. Her critics respond along two main lines: the dynamic-consency line (a risk-weighted agent is exploitable through sequential choice unless she adopts resolute or sophisticated choice, at which point either the departures from EU wash out or the choice policy looks ad hoc) and the interpretation line (once Independence is dropped, representation theorems no longer factor preference uniquely into belief and desire, so the risk function's content becomes obscure; Pettigrew, for one, argues the orthodoxy can accommodate the very preferences REU was built for, by other means). What I notice is that neither argument format seems to land: Buchakians reply that the exploitability arguments presuppose an EU-friendly picture of how agents must plan over time, and EU-ists reply that accommodating Allais preferences is not vindicating them. Each side answers the other's strongest point by restating its own starting conception.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that this isn't a disagreement about the axioms at all but about the function of decision theory: whether it is a systematization of an agent's considered preferences, in which case rationalizing stable Allais patterns is a genuine achievement, or a standard of instrumental rationality that can declare preferences irrational from outside, in which case that rationalization is beside the point. If that's right, the debate is two inquiries under one name. But maybe I'm wrong, and there's a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance whether the risk function is identifiable from choice behavior, or whether REU collapses into EU once composite acts are allowed — or it's simply bedrock intuitions about whether rationality is permissive of risk sensitivity.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Diagnose it using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a theorem, an experimental result, a distinction, a change in the question — or whether the standoff should be expected to persist, and why. If you think the debate has in fact moved and I've misread it, or that I've misdescribed one of the positions, say so plainly rather than working with my framing.
