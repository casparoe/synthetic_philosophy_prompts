I'm taking a seminar that's loosely about longtermism and my friend there has an argument he claims shows consequentialism refutes itself through the butterfly effect, and I honestly can't find the broken step. Here it is. (1) The history of the world is chaotically sensitive: for want of a nail the horseshoe was lost, for want of the horseshoe the rider, the message, the battle, and eventually the kingdom -- trivial causes like a delayed rider or a rainy day really have decided wars, dynasties, and migrations, which means they decided everything downstream of those too. (2) Nothing marks off my trivial actions from history's trivial causes: whether I answer this email now or in an hour could, through some chain I can't trace, shift who meets whom, who's standing where, and centuries from now produce a wildly better or worse world. (3) The future contains vastly more value and disvalue than the present does -- most people, most joy, most suffering, is ahead of us -- so whatever the calculus of consequences weighs, the far-future terms dominate the near-term ones. (4) An act is right iff it maximizes expected value, and expected value includes all those far-future terms. (5) For any act I might do, the far-future effects are unpredictable in both sign and size; as far as I can tell the noise is symmetric, so nothing I can estimate about the near future tells me which act comes out ahead once the far future is included. (6) Therefore no act I can choose is, as far as I can tell, better than any other -- the deliberation-relevant differences are all swamped. (7) But consequentialism was supposed to be the action-guiding view, the one that tells you what to do by looking at outcomes; a consequentialism that says "flip a coin, it's all noise" guides nothing. (8) So either consequentialism is false or it's useless, and he thinks it's the same thing either way.

The seminar has mostly been about cluelessness so I know there's literature on this, but I want to work out for myself which step is the one to hate. For what it's worth, I'd really like to keep (4) in some form, and honestly (1) and (2) seem basically undeniable to me -- small causes do decide big things and I'm not exempt from causation.
