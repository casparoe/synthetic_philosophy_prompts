I'm putting together a seminar piece on whether Lakatos survives contact with messy real cases, and I want to use cold fusion as the test. So we're on the same page, here are the salient facts: Fleischmann and Pons announced at a press confrence at the University of Utah in March 1989 that electrolyzing heavy water with a palladium cathode produced more heat than chemistry could account for — they said nuclear-scale, and hinted the ash was helium. Within months, major labs like Caltech and MIT tried to replicate and got nothing, a Department of Energy panel said no, and the mainstream walked away. But the field never actually died: it renamed itself LENR, kept running its own conferences and journals for thirty-odd years, and its defenders still point to claimed novel results — a correlation between excess heat and helium-4, tritium blips, thresholds on how fully the palladium loads with deuterium — plus the 2011 E-Cat demos that never survived independent testing.

The obvious mainstream story is "textbook degenerating research programme." I want the actual Lakatos analysis, done carefully and running in both directions.

First, the mapping. What is the hard core here — just "excess heat in condensed matter is real," or is the field committed to anything more than that? And notice the weird part: cold fusion's defenders have always been kind of proud of not having a theory — the calorimetry was suposed to speak for itself. Does Lakatos even have a category for a programme whose hard core is nearly empty and whose positive heuristic is basically absent? Where is the protective belt — which auxiliaries absorbed the failed replications (loading thresholds, claims that the effect is spotty and hard to trigger, artifact stories), and were any of them independently testable, or were they all post-hoc?

Then the degeneration question, honestly. The community claims genuine novel predictions, the helium-ash correlation especially. Lakatos's criterion is predictions that are novel relative to the existing belt and then get confirmed, so by his lights does anything from the last thirty years count? And here's the part I actually care about: what does Lakatos say about when to quit? He warns against dumping a degenerating programme too fast — his Copernicus example stagnated for decades before Kepler and Galileo bailed it out — so is there any principled point, on his own terms, at which the mainstream's 1989 walkaway was rational rather than just fashion? If the answer is that there isn't one, that the verdict is always retrospective, then say that plainly — that's the gap Feyerabend walked through, and I want to know whether cold fusion is the case that shows it.

One more thing. The community's own rhetoric is almost a parody of the sociology Lakatos was reacting against — the establishment buried us, the Galileo gambit, and so on. But Lakatos is supposed to be the repair kit against exactly that move: the hard core is unfalsifiable by methodological agreement, which is precisely why degeneration is the only rational response to it. Does this episode vindicate the repair kit, or show it doesn't work when a community just... stays, forever?

Take the physics as given — the mainstream verdict on the excess heat claims is right for our purposes, and I'm not asking whether cold fusion is real, I'm asking what follows for the framework. Keep it Lakatos-only, no scoring against Popper or Kuhn, and no pretending the framework is silent where it actually speaks.
