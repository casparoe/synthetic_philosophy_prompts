I'm a pricing actuary — fifteen years in health and life insurance, first in group pricing, now in individual underwriting — so my working vocabulary is risk classification, pooling, experience rating, underwriting loadings, adverse selection, moral hazard, and expected loss. I've recently started reading political philosophy in the evenings, and I keep running into luck egalitarianism, usually glossed as "the just distribution is the one that tracks choice, not luck." That gloss sounds like something I could model. So what I want from you is a translation, not an explainer.

Restate the luck egalitarian position in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. I take it the core claim is that inequalities are unjust when they trace to circumstances rather than choices — which, in my terms, I'd guess is something like: exposure to risk you could not decline, versus risk you were priced for and accepted. Include the way the position handles its hard cases: Dworkin's device of hypothetical insurance against being born with poor talents or a costly medical condition (which I'd be tempted to model as ex ante pricing against an unobservable risk class), his distinction between brute luck and option luck, and at least one dispute internal to the family — say, the expensive-tastes disagreement between Dworkin and Cohen, or the worry that a choice isn't really voluntary when the alternative is dire. Map each element onto the nearest thing my vocabulary has. Is brute luck roughly "risk you couldn't insure at any premium anyone would charge, or didn't know to charge for"? Is option luck just a policy someone was rated for and signed?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophical position translate cleanly into underwriting concepts, which had to be distorted to fit, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? Here's my working suspicion, from arguing about this with a friend who works in welfare policy: in insurance, classification is predictive and never normative. We charge smokers more not because we judge them, but because expected loss differs; a smoker's loading carries no more condemnation than a steelworker's occupational rating. So the whole luck-egalitarian question of whether a person is responsible for a choice looks to me like it has no counterpart in what I do — or at most it maps onto the distinction between a rating factor you're allowed to underwrite on and one you're not. Is that a genuine residue, something my framework cannot say and the philosophers can? Or is it a sign that the philosophers' choice/circumstance distinction is doing less work than it appears, and could in principle be replaced by a purely statistical one? Tell me which you think it is, and, if the philosophers are saying something worth saying, what an actuary would have to add to her vocabulary to be able to say it.
