In my philosophy of mind reading group we keep a running tally of our adviser's verdicts on when an agent counts as believing a proposition that follows from things she already believes — she likes to say that what matters is what we can attribute to real agents, not to idealized ones. Her verdicts so far: (1) someone who believes that all bachelors are unmarried and that Nigel is a bachelor, but has never put the two together, does believe that Nigel is unmarried; (2) someone who accepts the Peano axioms and insists she stands behind all of their consequences does not thereby believe Fermat's Last Theorem; (3) the same person does not believe that 617 is prime, even though checking it is tedious rather than difficult; (4) a student who has verified every individual line of a long proof of a theorem, but has never surveyed the proof as a whole and cannot reconstruct it, does believe the theorem. Next week's case: someone who accepts the axioms of Euclidean geometry and has never thought about the question — does she believe that the interior angles of a triangle sum to two right angles? The proof is a two-minute construction once one thinks to draw the auxiliary line, but she has not thought to draw it. Please predict the adviser's verdict; set out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well, noting which of them come apart on this case and what each predicts; give your best guess, with reasons; and propose the further case that would best discriminate among the candidate rules — I would particularly like to know whether the rules diverge on an agent who could verify an argument if it were shown to her but would never find it herself, or on an agent who once worked something out and has since forgotten it.
