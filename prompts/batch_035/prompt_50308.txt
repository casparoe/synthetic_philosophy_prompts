So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, but a while ago a friend who's doing a philosophy degree told me about this thought experiment and it's been living in my head rent-free since. The setup (I think it's due to a guy called Max Black, some paper from the 50s): imagine a universe that contains exactly two spheres and nothing else. The spheres are perfect copies of each other, and the universe is completely symmetric — everything looks the same if you reflect it through the midpoint between them. So there is literally no property that distinguishes them: same size, same mass, same everything, and since there's nothing else in the universe, they don't even have distinct absolute locations — each one is just "the other one, at distance d", in both directions. Black's point was that this seems like a coherently imaginable world where Leibniz's principle (the idea that no two distinct things can share all their properties — apparently called the identity of indiscernibles) just fails: there are two spheres there, not one, and yet nothing at all distinguishes them.

As a programmer this messes with me, because we spend our whole careers dealing with exactly this distinction. In Python there's `is` vs `==` — two separate list objects can be equal in every observable way and still be two things, each with it's own identity. In databases we hand out surrogate keys precisely because two rows can otherwise be identical, and everyone who's written Java knows the equals/hashCode saga. So my question: what are the most important implications of Black's spheres thing for how I should think about identity vs equality in code? Does it suggest that identity is something imposed on a system from the outside (an observer, a naming scheme, a memory address) rather than something the system itself carries? Is our habit of giving everything an ID closer to a view where identity comes from relations to other things, or does it actually show the opposite? And I'd also like an honest take on whether the experiment is even supposed to tell us anything about worlds like ours, where nothing is ever perfectly symmetric anyway — or whether it's just a philosophers' toy and the real lesson for someone like me is something else entirely. Assume zero jargon on my end, so if you need a term like "haecceity" please explain it in plain words lol.
