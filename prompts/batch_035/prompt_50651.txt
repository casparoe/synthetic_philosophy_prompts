My two closest friends from grad school have been fighting about the dogmatism paradox for literally a year now, and I've been drafted to moderate something. Quick setup in case the name isn't ringing a bell: the argument goes (roughly) that if I know I have hands, and I know that having hands rules out being a handless brain in a vat being fed the very experiences I'm having, then by a super modest-looking closure principle I should be able to know I'm not a brain in a vat; but it seems I can't know that; so maybe I don't know I have hands. One friend is basically a Moorean about this -- of course you know you have hands, you can literally see them, wave them around, so the skeptic has to be wrong somewhere and the "somewhere" is the claim that you can't know you're not deceived. The other friend takes the opposite exit: she's fine granting that you don't know you're not a brain in a vat (that just strikes her as obviously right), and instead rejects closure -- knowledge doesn't always transmit across known entailments, which she illustrates with the old zoo case where you know the animal is a zebra without knowing it's not a cleverly disguised mule. The annoying thing is they agree on almost all the conclusions they care about (skepticism is false, we know tons of ordinary stuff), which makes the fight feel extra unproductive, and recently it's gotten sidetracked into whether they even mean the same thing by "knows," since she leans contextualist-adjacent and he doesn't.

We've decided to do the whole adversarial-collaboration thing: a single document both of them could actually put their names to. Here's what I want it to contain. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign -- about the argument itself (that it's valid, what exactly its premises are), about the zoo case and what it does and doesn't show, about the difference between closure failing and the justification simply not transmitting to the conclusion, and about what would count as a genuine cost for either side. Then a disagreements table: the precise points where they part ways, with each disagreement phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording -- I've watched them each reject the other's paraphrase of their view about four times, so this matters. Then the cruxes, as best you can identify them. My guesses: whether some closure principle survives once you make it precise enough to be non-trivial; whether giving up closure has everyday consequences (my closure-denier friend swears it never costs you anything outside skeptical scenarios, her husband the Moorean thinks that's way too convenient); and how each side handles lottery cases, where you seem to know you'll lose even though every ticket could win. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, and write down what each of them would predict about it -- we want the predictions on record so nobody can weasel later. And if you think some chunk of their dispute is just verbal (I have my suspicions about the "knows" thing), say which chunk and why, since if that's right we could maybe shrink the fight to the part that's real.

One more thing: we invented a case at our last meeting and I'd like it worked in somewhere if it fits. Call it the auditor case: a hospital auditor knows a batch of records is accurate because she carefully verified each page, and she knows that if the records are accurate, then no page was swapped out after she left the room. But when a lawyer later asks her "do you KNOW no page was swapped after you left?", she feels pulled to say no -- yet she'll happily testify she knows the records are accurate. The Moorean says her first-order knowledge is what's solid and the reluctance about the lawyer's question is a pragmatic effect; my other friend says the auditor genuinely doesn't know the no-swapping thing and this is exactly closure failing in a completely ordinary, non-BIV setting. Neither will let the other characterize the case their way, so getting an agreed description of what happens in it would be a small miracle. Keep the tone neutral throughout -- this document is supposed to be the thing we finally all sign, not another round of the fight.
