I keep going back and forth on when I'm willing to say a machine literally believes something vs when I'm just talking loosely, and I want to know if there's a principle behind my gut verdicts. My old thermostat: I'd never say it believes the room is cold, even though it detects temperature. My chess app: I happily say it thinks my queen is undefended, even that it's trying to lure my bishop into a fork. My spam filter: no, it doesn't believe the email is phishing, it just classifies. But my Roomba, which scurries back to its dock when the battery's low and retries the chair-leg tangle from a different angle, I do say things like it knows it's stuck, and I feel barely any embarassment about it. Then there's the case that bugs me most: when a chatbot is shown a photo and confidently insists it can't see images, I'm genuinely tempted to say it has a false belief about itself, and that feels like a different category from the chess app thinking about my queen. Last one: I say my phone keyboard learned that I always mistype my friend's name, but I'd never say it believes that's how her name is spelled. My rough guess at a principle is that I use belief-talk when the system can be wrong in a way that shows up to me as a mistake rather than a malfunction, though the chatbot case seems to break that since it's kind of introspecting. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion: (1) the principle you'd actually offer for these verdicts, (2) which of my verdicts it accommodates worst, (3) its verdict on a new case — a warehouse robot whose usual pick spot is empty, which then searches nearby spots and only radios a human when those come up empty too, do I believe it believes the item is missing, (4) whether my mistake-vs-malfunction guess can be repaired or should just be dropped, and (5) what consideration would most change your mind about the principle you picked, i.e. where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
