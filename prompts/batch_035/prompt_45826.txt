I'm a novelist, three books in, all fairly conventional family dramas, and for my next project I keep circling this old chestnut from a philosophy elective I barely passed in college — Buridan's ass, the donkey that starves to death standing exactly between two identical haystacks because it has no reason whatsoever to go left instead of right. Everyone treats it as a joke, like, obviously the donkey just picks one, but the part that haunts me is that "just picks one" is doing all the work and nobody explains it. What IS a choice that comes from nowhere, when preference, reason, and desire all cancel out? I want a story that stays with that unsettling remainder instead of dissolving it.

Here's the setup I've been chewing on: my protagonist is a woman in her late fifties who, after a divorce where she spent two years agonizing over whether to leave (and her husband never even noticed she was deciding), makes a rule for herself: whenever two options fall inside what she privately calls the dead zone — close enough that her reasons evenly cancel — she refuses to deliberate. She flips a coin. She rolls dice. Once she let which parking meter expired first decide between two job offers. For restaurants and couches it's a great system, almost comic. But decades on, the accumulated coin flips have hardened into something that looks exactly like a life, and her adult daughter — who is the opposite, a chronic pros-and-cons list-maker who once took nine months to choose a mattress — accuses her of never having wanted anything. Every city, every friendship, every dog: picked by chance. And the accusation lands, because the mother genuinely can't tell wether she came to love the city the dice chose or whether she'd have loved the other one just as much, and whether there's even a difference between loving something and having had it long enough.

Write me this short story, maybe 2500 words. I'd rather you not name-drop Buridan or any philosopher — the daughter can find an old philosophy paperback with a donkey on the cover at a yard sale at most, no more explicit than that. What I want the story to actually dramatize is the two questions underneath the puzzle: can an arational choice bootstrap itself into real preference over time (is her attachment to the dice-picked city anything more than sunk cost wearing a costume?), and is the deliberating daughter actually any freer, or is she just the donkey with extra steps, starving between two identical mattresses? For the ending, resist the tidy therapeutic resolution where someone learns to trust herself — leave it genuinely open, maybe on a moment where one of the two women finally faces a choice where she does have a preference, a real one, and can't quite recognize it as one, or recognizes it and flips the coin anyway. That last possibility should feel like it could be either defeat or liberation, and the story shouldn't tell us which.
