The thing I keep chewing on lately is two-dimensional semantics. My understanding of the pitch: you evaluate a term twice. On the first pass you don't yet know which world is actual, so you interpret the description against whatever world turns out to be actual — that's supposed to capture the epistemic/a priori direction, and it's how the usual examples get classified as necessary but a posteriori (Hesperus being Phosphorus, water being H2O). On the second pass you hold the actual world fixed and ask what's true in other worlds considered counterfactually. Kaplan does something in this neighborhood with the character/content split for indexicals, and Chalmers and Jackson build a whole epistemology out of the two-dimensional grid.

Here's the task: restate this machinery in the vocabulary I actually use day to day — lexical vs. dynamic scoping, environments and evaluation contexts, staging and partial evaluation, code specialization, compile-time vs. run-time binding, hygienic macros if that helps. Translate it piece by piece, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about most: what maps cleanly onto my vocabulary, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in it at all. My working theory is that the primary intension is just evaluation before the world-variable gets bound, and the secondary intension is the result of that first evaluation cached and frozen into a constant — i.e., staged computation, nothing mysterious — and I'd definately like pushback on that. The nagging worry is the part about what counts as a priori in the first place, or which description language is privileged when you do the first pass; that feels like it has no counterpart in programs at all, and I want your honest read on whether that's a real gap in my vocabulary or a sign the 2D debate was less deep than advertised.
