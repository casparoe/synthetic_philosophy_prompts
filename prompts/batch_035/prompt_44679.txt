Next month I give a talk defending a particular reading of the Dutch book argument to a department that is mostly mainstream epistemologists, plus one person working in accuracy-first epistemology and two decision theorists who regard the whole betting apparatus as a quaint relic. My thesis is that the classic result was never a decision-theoretic justification of the probability axioms, and that running it that way -- you'd be irrational because a clever bookie could extract your money -- concedes too much to the easy objections: that rational agents can simply decline bets, that the argument needs linear utility for money and forced betting at one's own prices, and that an agent whose preferences among acts are otherwise coherent would be irrational only in a money-losing, not an epistemically defective, sense. On my reading, the argument's real engine is interpretive rather than pragmatic: susceptibility to a Dutch book is the credal analogue of inconsistency among full beliefs, a structural defect of one's system of degrees of belief which the betting scenario merely makes visible. The sure-loss theorem is meant to show that any degrees of belief fit to figure in rational action must be probabilities, not that being non-probabilistic loses you money. I plan to defend three steps: an interpretive premise tying degrees of belief to dispositions to accept bets at certain prices; the theorem that incoherent prices jointly permit a sure loss; and a bridge claim that sure-loss liability is a formal incoherence rather than a financial harm. My claim is that the bridge does the normative work, and that the money-loss framing is inessential decoration.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. Structure it with these sections, in this order. First, a section titled What they will hear my thesis as: one short paragraph on how this specific audience will frame my position before I have said much -- especially the two decision theorists and the accuracy-first epistemologist, since each will hear a different and differently threatening version. Second, a section titled The objections: roughly eight, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that member of the audience would actually put it, and each followed by a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and then a candid note on where that reply is soft. Include at least the obvious live targets: what my interpretive premise says about agents who refuse to bet at all or whose utility for money is nonlinear; what happens to my reading once diachronic Dutch books and conditionalization enter, since restricting the bookie's strategies to keep the argument going can look like it quietly changes the subject from incoherence to something strategic; and why my structural-defect story is not just superseded by accuracy-based arguments, which the accuracy-first person will surely claim deliver the same verdicts with a properly epistemic currency. But do not limit yourself to those. Third, a section titled The question I hope nobody asks: the single most dangerous one, with why it is dangerous and my best available answer if it comes anyway. Fourth, a section titled What to concede on the day: the points where I should visibly give ground rather than fight, and what conceding them costs my overall thesis. Keep the whole thing to something I could actually read on a train the morning of the talk.
