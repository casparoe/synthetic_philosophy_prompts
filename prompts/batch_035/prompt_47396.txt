For context: I am a retired analytical chemist who has spent the last two years reading ethics, and I have a question about an analogy that seems to do a great deal of work in the literature on aggregation.

The analogy, which I take to be a descendant of Bentham's hedonic calculus, is the hedonic ledger: pleasures and pains are treated as credits and debits in a single account, and the rational aim for a life, or a population, is to maximize the balance. In the utilitarian writings I have been reading, this picture is wielded against lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that some pains are so bad that no quantity of pleasure, however large, compensates for them. The argument runs: a ledger has a single numeraire, every entry is convertible into every other at some finite rate, and so thresholds beyond which no exchange is possible are a conceptual confusion, comparable to a bookkeeper who refuses to accept that a thousand modest credits can balance one large debit. As someone who spent a career establishing when two quantities may legitimately be placed on a common scale — which requires either a shared dimension or an empirically calibrated exchange relation — I suspect the analogy is smuggling in precisely the premise at issue. But I would like a careful audit rather than my hunch.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the currency, the exchange rate between pleasure-units and pain-units, the account holder, and the solvency condition at which the balance goes negative. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in two candidate disanalogies: first, that monetary debt is fungible because money is redeemable outside the ledger, whereas there is no external redemption in which pleasures cancel pains, so the convertibility is stipulated rather than discovered; second, that the ledger presupposes that hedonic states are representable on a single real-valued scale, whereas in economics lexicographic orderings are well-defined and coherent preferences that no continuous utility function can represent — which suggests that a threshold view may be a coherent ordering rather than an arithmetical error, and that the analogy assumes the conclusion it is used to reach.

Finally, tell me whether the dismissive verdict on lexical thresholds survives once these disanalogies are on the table; and if the ledger cannot carry it, either propose a better analogy for how pleasures and pains relate, or say plainly that their commensurability is an open question that no analogy should be permitted to settle.

I would be grateful if you kept the whole answer to no more than 600 words. A compact correspondence table is welcome; terse, declarative prose is preferable to hedging.
