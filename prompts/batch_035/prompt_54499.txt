I'm taking a graduate seminar on wellbeing and prudential value, and a friend has put an argument to me that I haven't been able to shake off. It turns on pain asymbolia — the condition, typically reported after certain kinds of brain injury, in which patients say they feel pain (they can locate it, grade its intensity, describe its quality) but don't mind it: they don't withdraw, don't grimace, sometimes seem faintly curious about the sensation. His argument runs as follows. (1) If pain is bad for the person feeling it, then either it is intrinsically bad — bad in virtue of its felt quality alone — or it is bad only in virtue of something else that normally accompanies it, most plausibly the aversion, distress, or dislike. (2) The asymbolic feels the pain but has none of the accompanying aversion. (3) If pain were intrinsically bad, something bad would be happening to the asymbolic when the needle goes in. (4) Nothing bad is happening to her: she does not suffer, and she is the best-placed judge of how things are going for her at that moment. (5) So pain is not intrinsically bad; whatever badness pain has, it borrows from the aversion. (6) The same stripping test applies to every unpleasant experience — nausea, dread, itch, exhaustion — so no experience is intrinsically bad, and experiential badness is always conferred by conative attitudes. From this my friend draws a large conclusion: that hedonism about welfare is refuted, and that theories of wellbeing should be built on caring rather than on felt quality.

My trouble is that the argument appears to prove too much. Turn the crank of (6) one more time: the aversion itself — the disliking, the suffering — is an experience too, so if no experience is intrinsically bad, the suffering is not intrinsically bad either; it is bad only if some further attitude dislikings the disliking, and that attitude only if a still further one dislikings it, and so on. Either the chain terminates in an attitude that is intrinsically bad after all, contradicting (6), or it never grounds anything, and we arrive at the result that nothing is bad for anyone — not even agony, provided the victim is supplied with enough layers of meta-indifference. My friend waves this off, but I have noticed that he will not actually say that the suffering of a person in ordinary agony fails to be bad.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I have a suspicion the trouble sits at (4), which seems to treat the asymbolic's indifference as a transparent gauge of her own good — as though her not minding it settles that it is not bad for her — and that inference looks like it presupposes the very attitude-dependence the argument is meant to establish. But I can't quite make the charge stick, since deferring to subjects about their own conditions doesn't look question-begging in other contexts. For my part, I would rather keep (1) and (2) as genuine data about asymbolia than dismiss the condition as somehow unreal or misdescribed, and I would like to keep (5) if possible, since something in its neighborhood strikes me as right; it is (6)'s generalization that I would most happily lose. Please give me the diagnosis: name the step to reject, explain why it looked fine in the setting where my friend deployed it, and price the alternatives — including what my friend must pay to keep his conclusion by biting the bullet on the regress.
