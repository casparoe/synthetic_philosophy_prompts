Below is the draft. Draft:

**Whose track record am I?**

Suppose an AI assistant is asked whether the Eifel Tower was completed in 1889, and it wants not just to answer but to say how confident it is. One natural strategy: look at how often you got similar questions right before. On questions of historical fact, you were right 96% of the time. So say "96% confident". But why stop there? On questions about 19th-century French architecture you were right 100% of the time (you got both of them right), and on all questions you were right 91% of the time. Each of these is a track record of "similar cases", and they give different answers: 100%, 96%, 91%. Which one are you?

This is the reference class problem, usually traced back to Reichenbach: whenever you estimate a probability for a single case by counting frequencies in a population of "similar" cases, the facts about the case itself don't tell you which population to count. The case belongs to many nested classes — all questions, history questions, architecture questions, questions about this very tower — and the classes disagree.

Here is the move I want to make. Statisticians face something like this all the time, and they don't regard it as a paradox: they call it building a hierarchical model, and the answer is not to pick one class but to weight many, with the weights learned from how much data each class contains. A class with two members gets little weight; a class with thousands gets more. On this view the reference class problem isn't a special philosophical puzzle at all — it just dissolves into the ordinary question of how much structure your statistical model can afford given the data it has.

The residue: for genuinely unique cases, ones where every candidate class is small (estimates of existential risk, say), there is no large class to lean on, and the dissolution fails. I think this is where the real problem lives, but I'm not sure how to say more without hand-waving. Comments welcome.
