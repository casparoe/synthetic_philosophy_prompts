Write a satirical help-center FAQ page for MoralMatch™, a fictional peer-to-peer app that lets users trade their moral commitments. The piece should run roughly 600–700 words, formatted as a genuine FAQ would be — section headers such as "Getting Started," "Making Your First Trade," "Troubleshooting," and "Account Limits," each containing a customer question in bold followed by a deadpan customer-service answer.

For readers who need the concept: in moral trade, two people who care about different things can both do better by each shifting their efforts toward what the other values — you donate to my favorite cause and I stop consuming the product you find abhorrent, so each gets more of what they care about at less cost to themselves. The satire should draw its humor from real features and real difficulties of this idea, so that someone who knows the moral-trade literature finds the piece fair rather than a strawman:

- Trade only creates a surplus where the parties value things differently; the FAQ should treat this as an exciting growth opportunity (e.g., helpfully suggesting that users befriend people with maximally different values).
- Moral commitments are treated as fungible inventory, with exchange rates between goods that are not obviously commensurable (how many units of opposing X does one unit of caring about Y trade at?).
- The FAQ must quietly acknowledge, in customer-service voice, that some things cannot be traded because they are not the user's to trade — third parties' interests are not tradable stock. At least one Q&A should show the rep declining a user's attempt to bargain away someone else's welfare, with no editorializing; the flat refusal is the joke.
- Counterparty risk should apply to the user's own future self: values change after reflection, and the FAQ should treat this as a known issue with a workaround, perhaps an escrow service or a cooling-off period on conscience.
- The app's fee structure or "premium tiers" should gently expose the pathologies of marketization: places where pricing a commitment changes it, or where the user discovers mid-transaction that the item they listed is one they no longer want to sell.

The overall target is not the idea of moral trade itself but the posture that treats all values as commensurable, tradable, and stable — the piece should exaggerate that posture, not invent absurd features the real view lacks. The voice must stay in character throughout: chipper, corporate, never winking at the reader. Close with a brief italicized note or final Q&A that lands the underlying problem in one stroke — something on the order of a user asking how to trade away a scruple they didn't know they still had, and the rep answering, in perfect deadpan, that this is the app's most common support request.
