Write a sestina in the voice of a lifelong eliminative materialist on the last page of her final book — a philosopher of mind retiring after forty years spent arguing that belief, desire, and the rest of folk psychology are the misplaced theoretical posits of a false theory, destined to go the way phlogiston and caloric went, rather than to be smoothly reduced or otherwise vindicated. The six end-words should be believe, want, hope, fear, intend, and regret. The permutation should do philosophical work: as the six folk-psychological terms rotate through their positions, each keeps acquiring new neighbors and new grammatical roles, enacting the eliminativist's own point that these words take their content from their place in a folk theory rather than from any real kind in the brain, so that no occurrence ever means quite the same thing twice. The envoi, which by convention gathers all six words back into its closing lines, is where the tension should land: she must bid farewell using the only vocabulary she has — she believes the theory, wants it vindicated, fears the successor neuroscience is further off than she once said, intends to stop pretending this is modest, and regrets something she should name. The poem should leave genuinely open whether this reflexive tangle is the fatal self-refutation critics allege — you cannot, without embarrassment, believe that there are no beliefs — or just what any scientific revolution sounds like from inside the dying paradigm, the way a phlogiston chemist must have reasoned in phlogiston's own terms on the way out. A philosopher of mind should recognize the Churchlands' actual position, including that it is put forward as an empirical prediction rather than an analytic thesis, and not a caricature of it; a poet should find that the form, with its words circling and never quite dying, is itself an argument.
