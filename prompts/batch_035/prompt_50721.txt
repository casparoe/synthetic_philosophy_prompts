I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I spend a lot of time thinking about decision-making — for myself, and for the systems I build — and over the years I've settled on a set of views about information and choice. Before I write these up for an internal talk on how we should treat data collection in our products, I'd like a consistency check. Here is the package:

(1) I'm a thoroughgoing Bayesian: credences should obey the probability calculus, choices under uncertainty should maximise expected utility, and I take any other decision procedure to be either Bayesianism in disguise or straightforwardly incoherent.

(2) I accept what I understand as Good's theorem on the value of information: if an observation is costless and relevant to the decision at hand, then an expected-utility maximiser can never do worse by consulting it before acting. So peeking is always at least weakly good, and a rational agent should never decline free, decision-relevant information.

(3) At the same time, I believe there are ordinary situations in which you're better off not being able to peek. In a salary negotiation, if the employer knows I will costlessly learn their true maximum before responding, their behaviour changes — they build in less flexibility — and I may come out worse than if I had been structurally unable to learn anything. Likewise, for a trading bot we operate, there are signals we deliberately filter out precisely because counterparties model what the bot can see. So I'd sometimes pay real money to be unable to receive free information — by delegating, escrowing, or instructing an intermediary never to tell me.

(4) I think commitment devices are rational and often enormously valuable, and that their value depends on being hard to unwind. I also think a commitment typically becomes worthless if, before the critical moment, you can still costlessly observe decision-relevant information: knowing dissolves the binding.

(5) I think it is sometimes perfectly rational to decline information that could not change any decision you face — a diagnostic result when the treatment plan would be identical either way, or a brutally frank assessment of a manuscript you have already resolved to finish regardless.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each one, say what would have to give, and what the cheapest fix costs elsewhere in the package. I'd particularly like a verdict on whether (3) and (4) force me to abandon (2), or whether (2) survives if it is read with enough care — and I genuinely don't know which answer I'm hoping for, so please don't pull your punches in either direction. Please respond in British English.
