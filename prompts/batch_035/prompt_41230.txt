I sit on the Data Access Committee of a large research biobank, and in three weeks we must rule on a data-access request that has split the committee roughly down the middle. I'd like a draft written opinion I can circulate to members before the vote.

Some background. Two decades ago the biobank enrolled around eighty thousand participants using a one-page consent form authorizing use of their samples and records for "health-related research, including genetic research, by qualified investigators," with nothing further specified. Recruitment was run largely through clinics in lower-income neighborhoods, where the advertised draw was a free comprehensive health screening; a later internal review described this as an inducement that some participants could not reasonably have refused. For roughly five percent of enrollees, the form was signed not by the participant but by a family member, under a then-permitted policy for people with impaired decision-making capacity. The pending request comes from a team that wants whole-cohort genomic and records data to build polygenic scores for behavioral and social traits — educational attainment, impulsivity, risk of long-term unemployment — and to share individual-level scores with a government ministry that intends to use them in early-intervention programming. Nothing of this kind was on the horizon when the consents were signed; a stratified survey we ran in the interim found that a large majority of participants accept "health research" broadly, but a solid majority object to non-medical research that predicts behavior, and nearly half object to any government access whatsoever.

Please draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation we can actually vote on and an implementation sketch (e.g., what re-contact or opt-out procedures would look like if you recommend conditions). The reasoning should engage the strongest points on each side rather than caricature either: the proposers' argument that a signed, ethics-approved form authorizing "genetic research" by "qualified investigators" does not become invalid merely because some participants would now prefer otherwise, and the objectors' argument that consent to uses that were unforeseeable at the time is not consent at all. Wherever you land, the memo should take a position on which of the original consent's features did the validity work — the breadth of the description, the health-screening inducement, the proxy signatures, or the foreseeability of this particular use — rather than resting only on how sensitive behavioral genetics happens to be.
