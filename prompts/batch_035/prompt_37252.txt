My friend Rhian is the most consistent person I know when we argue about who is worse off than whom, and who is owed what. She has never read a word of political philosophy, but over the years she has issued verdicts on a long string of cases, and she sticks to them. Her rulings so far, all on the question of whether someone counts as disadvantaged and owed public support:

1. A woman blinded in an accident fifteen years ago: fully adapted, in work she loves, income equal to her peers, reports that her life is as good as anyone's, and needs nothing that money could buy. Rhian: she is still worse off than a sighted counterpart on the same income, and if a support scheme has places, she is entitled to one. Contentment, Rhian insists, is beside the point.

2. A colleague with cultivated tastes for fine wine and solo travel, on the same income as everyone else, permanently dissatisfied because he cannot afford the life he has come to want. Rhian: not disadvantaged; his problem, not ours.

3. A grandmother raised in deep poverty: little schooling, arthritis, a tiny pension, wants nothing from life now except her radio and her garden, and says so. Rhian: one of the worst-off people we know; top priority for support.

4. A man who inherited comfortably and earned well, then gambled both away; now destitute. Rhian: his destitution is a misfortune but not an injustice — charity may help him, but he has no fairness-based claim on support.

5. Two colleagues on identical salaries, one with a chronic condition whose medication absorbs a third of her pay. Rhian: the matching payslips settle nothing; she is worse off, and the health service should absorb the cost.

The new case is Dev, a friend of ours. A head injury three years ago took his sense of smell. Food is mostly texture to him now; he has given up cooking, which was his one hobby; otherwise he is healthy, in full-time work, income unchanged, and a recent assessment found his overall life satisfaction back at its pre-injury level. Our council runs a small discretionary scheme: personal development grants of a few hundred pounds a year, for courses, clubs and equipment, for residents counted as disabled. Does Dev qualify?

Please answer in British English. I'd like you to lay out the rules that could fit her whole record, saying where each one strains; then give her likely verdict on Dev under each of those rules, with your best guess and your reasons, identifying the closest analogue among her earlier rulings. Since her record may not settle it, I'd also like an honest statement of what she would be committing herself to either way, and a description of the further case that would most cleanly decide which rule she is actually running.
