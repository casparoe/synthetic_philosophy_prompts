I'm a philosophy professor planning a sabbatical project on Whitehead's philosophy of education, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a workable proposal. Take the role of an expert on Whitehead and on the philosophy of education generally, and please think carefully before drafting — brainstorm a few possible framings first and pick the strongest.

The idea: everyone knows Whitehead's warning against "inert ideas" — mental reception of ideas without using them or testing them against experience — and his three-stage rhythm of romance, precision, and generalisation through which understanding supposedly develops. My suspicion is that the standard reading treats the rhythm as a developmental-psychological claim about chronological ages: wonder first, discipline later, synthesis in maturity. On that reading the view looks empirically naive and easy to refute, since children happily acquire precision and adults can be freshly swept up in romance. But I think the rhythm is better read as a normative claim about the structure of understanding itself: at any stage of learning, any subject matter admits these phases, and teaching is mistimed not in the sense of being aimed at the wrong age but in the sense of imposing the wrong phase — precision before the learner has felt the pull of the problem, generalisation before precision has given it content. If that reading is right, Whitehead's model isn't a (bad) empirical theory that developmental science could disprove, but something closer to a thesis about a precondition for knowledge being lived knowledge rather than inert knowledge — which connects to his broader insistence that process and novelty are fundamental. The question my sabbatical would answer: does the normative reading of the rhythm stand up, and does it change what Whitehead's philosophy of education is actually good for?

Write a two- to three-page proposal with: a precise statement of the interpretive question; why the standard chronological reading, and the quick dismissals that trade on it, leave the question open; the method — close conceptual work on Whitehead's scattered remarks across the essays on education, plus test cases drawn from concrete teaching situations (say, a first proof-based mathematics course, or introducing a second language) where the two readings yield different verdicts about what would count as good teaching; what a decisive result would look like; what would count as the project failing (e.g., if the developmental and normative readings turn out to be textual cart blanche, separable only by stipulation); and an honest assessment of what a one-year sabbatical can and cannot settle. Don't include a bibliography or cite secondary literature — I'll fill in references myself. I'd rather the proposal refer to positions and arguments than to names and page numbers.
