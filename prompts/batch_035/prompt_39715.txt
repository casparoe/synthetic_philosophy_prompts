My oldest friend and I have argued for years about what money should be allowed to buy. Neither of us has any academic background in this; we simply keep returning to it over dinner, and over time I have assembled a fairly complete record of her verdicts. I would like to predict how she will rule on some new cases before our next argument, and I find I cannot do it with any confidence.

Her record so far: she thinks a debt-ridden man selling a kidney to a broker should be illegal; she sees nothing wrong with selling one's hair to a wig maker; she is fine with paid plasma donation, even though she knows most donors are poor students doing it twice a week; she holds that selling one's vote is never permissible; she would prohibit a hardware store from tripling the price of generators the night before a hurricane; she has no objection to services that pay people to hold places in line; she finds scalpers distasteful but would keep them legal; and she has defended a woman carrying a child for a childless couple in exchange for a fee.

Her two recurring complaints are that "some things simply aren't for sale" and that "it isn't a fair deal when one side has no real choice." The trouble is that these pull in different directions, and her record seems consistent with more than one underlying rule: perhaps a rule about desperation and unfair advantage, perhaps one about goods that are corrupted by being sold, perhaps one about harm to bystanders. I cannot tell which she is really running, or whether it is some unstable mixture.

The new cases are: a financially comfortable graduate student selling her eggs for eight thousand dollars; two friends in different cities agreeing to trade votes, each voting for the other's preferred candidate in her own local race, with no money changing hands; and a hotel quadrupling its room rates during a wildfire evacuation. Please predict her verdict on each case, lay out the candidate rules that fit her record and where they diverge on these cases, give your best guess with reasons, and suggest one further case that would most cleanly reveal which rule she is actually applying.
