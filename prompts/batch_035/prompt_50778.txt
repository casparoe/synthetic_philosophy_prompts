I teach a seminar on confirmation theory, mostly grad students, 12 of them, one 90-minute session, and I want a game that makes the problem of old evidence actually land instead of being a slide. Everyone in the room can recite the issue — if you're a good Bayesian, your credence in evidence you already know is 1, so conditioning on it can never move your credence in a theory, and yet Mercury's perihelion shifting the field's credence in general relativity feels like the paradigm of confirmation. They nod along and then have no feel for why it's a problem at all. I want them to hit the wall themselves before I say the word "condition."

Here's the shape I have in mind, but you'll need to make it concrete and runnable: run a prediction/betting market over a small family of rival "theories" — simple generative rules for a dataset, like deterministic sequences or colored-card generators, none of them real science, so nobody has priors imported from physics class. Phase one: participants observe a chunk of data and trade credences with a proper scoring rule or token betting so they actually feel their probabilities tighten. Phase two: introduce a NEW theory, invented mid-game, that retrodicts everything they've already seen (say it predicts the same data as the best incumbent, or makes it all come out exact). Now they have to trade again. The question the game should force on them, without me asking it: does credence in the new theory go up, given that the data it got right was already priced in? Let them discover that the scoring/betting structure gives them no mechanism for the update they all feel they should make — the feels-right answer and the formal answer come apart in their own wallets, or token wallets.

Then the twist I want you to build in: at some point hand out sealed envelopes. The envelope contains the derivation showing that the new theory entails the observed data — the participants didn't KNOW the entailment before, they only learn it when they open the envelope. Time the envelope drop so some students want to open it and others want to keep it sealed, and see whether "learning the entailment" lets them do the update that conditioning on the data forbids. I want the debrief to map this onto the idea that maybe what got learned wasn't the old evidence but something new about it, and to be honest about whether that fix is real or a dodge — does it work when the theory was reverse-engineered from the data, which in the game it literally was? In real science the person who builds the theory already knows the data, so the envelope trick may not be honest, and the debrief should expose that gap rather than paper over it.

What I need from you: full rules, materials I can prep in an evening, the payoff scheme (tokens or points, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the debrief mapping game-moments onto the formal issue, a frank note on which features of the real problem the game can't reproduce no matter how well I run it, and what will predictably go wrong the first time (I assume students will just refuse to update or update wildly for social reasons — tell me if that's actually a bug or secretly part of the lesson). Also tell me how big to make the data set: too small and everything confirms everything, too big and nobody can evaluate the theories fast enough to trade.

Start your answer with the overall design in a sentence or two, then everything else — no preamble, no restating what I asked for.
