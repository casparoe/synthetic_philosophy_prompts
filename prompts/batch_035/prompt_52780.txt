Small confession: I'm a bioethics PhD student and I've gotten myself stuck on something for my dissertation on the dead donor rule, and I think the framing I need is the "what does this practice presuppose" move, because the argument I want to make is basically that transplant practice doesn't have ONE implicit definition of death, it has two incompatible ones running in parallel, and nobody in the clinical world seems bothered.

Here's the thing I keep staring at. In the same hospital, on the same week, two things happen. Under brain death protocols, a patient is declared dead by neurologic criteria while their heart is still beating, they're warm and perfused, and organs are recovered from a body that by circulatory standards looks alive. Meanwhile, down the hall, in a donation after circulatory death (DCD) case, life support is withdrawn, the heart stops, and the team waits a fixed interval -- which varies by institution, something like 2 to 5 minutes of asystole -- before declaring death and going in for organ recovery. And in some DCD protocols (normothermic regional perfusion, where it's used) they then clamp off the vessels to the head and restart circulation to the organs, which means a body just declared dead by loss of circulation gets its circulation back, at least below the neck.

So the question I want to pose to people: what definition of death does this combined practice actually presuppose? It can't be whole-brain death, because DCD declarations happen when the brain may still have residual activity for minutes after asystole, and nobody waits for the brain to die. It can't be circulatory death in any strict sense, because (a) brain-dead donors have beating hearts, and (b) the 2-to-5-minute interval only gets you permanence, not irreversibility -- the patient is dead when nobody will TRY to restart the heart, which is a fact about intentions, not physiology. And the NRP thing seems like it should embarrass any circulatory criterion but it apparently doesn't. What I want is a careful reconstruction of the implicit theory (or theories) of death that makes this whole edifice coherent -- if it does. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it's merely compatible with. Say whether the parts presuppose consistent things or whether the practice is only holding together because the two tracks never have to justify themselves to each other. And then tell me what would actually have to change, in the protocols, if we made one definition explicit -- like, if a hospital committed to a higher-brain definition, what happens to the DCD waiting period, and if it committed to a strict circulatory definition, does it lose every brain-dead donor? I suspect the answer is that the practice is philosophically underdetermined in a way that's load-bearing, and that "irreversible" quietly shifted to "permanent" over the years without anyone conceding that the definition of death shifted with it, but I want someone to actually work through whether that's right or whether there's a principled disjunctive account (death = EITHER whole-brain OR permanent circulatory arrest) that isn't just a legal fig leaf.

I don't need case law cites or committee reports quoted back at me, I have a folder full of those. What I need is the philosophical reconstruction done properly. GO.
