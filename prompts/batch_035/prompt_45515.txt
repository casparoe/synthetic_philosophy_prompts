I write novels, and for the past two years I've been working on one narrated by a Jesuit missionary who's a convinced Molinist -- I wanted the theology to actually carry the plot, not just be set dressing. The embarrassing part is that somewhere along the way my own character converted me. I now genuinely buy middle knowledge: God knows, for every possible creature in every possible circumstance, what that creature would freely do, and picks which world to actualize on the basis of that knowledge. It appeals to me because it's basically what I do when I plot a book. I know my characters completely before chapter one, including what they'd do in scenes I never write, and I choose which world of scenes to commit to based on that. Their freedom feels real from the inside of drafting even though everything's settled for me before a word goes down.

So here's my question, playing it straight: what would count against Molinism? I know there's a famous complaint that the counterfactuals of freedom -- what a free agent WOULD do -- don't seem to be made true by anything: not by the agent's actual choices (those haven't happened yet in the unactualized circumstances), not by God's beliefs (that looks circular), not by the agent's nature (that seems to collapse free choices into the kind of determined output Molinism was supposed to avoid). I want the full map of defeaters, not just that one -- which are potentially fatal, which merely awkward, and which ones Molinists themselves concede as costing the view something (versus what only Thomists or open theists press). And here's the thing I'd find most persuasive personally: tell me whether my author analogy helps Molinism or quietly wrecks it. Because when I invent a character, I'm inventing the dispositions too -- there's nothing my character 'would do' prior to my writing her, she's a bundle of my choices. If God's relation to possible creatures is like that, doesn't middle knowledge just collapse into God determining everything, which is exactly what Molinism was invented to avoid? Also, honest question: is this view even risking anything, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if so, is that a defect for a metaphysical claim, or just what metaphysics is like? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable for me, and note which (if any) have analogues in how I actually relate to my characters, since I suspect the novelist case is the one place where I can feel the view working.
