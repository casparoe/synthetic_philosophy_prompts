I teach an upper-level social choice seminar, and I'm writing the take-home midterm. Please draft one essay question on Arrow's impossibility theorem that a student cannot answer by reciting the proof sketch or the standard "no voting system is perfect" gloss. The question should turn on two proposed escape routes: (a) restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences, and (b) a rule invented by a hypothetical classmate, call it the triad rule: if a Condorcet winner exists, rank it first and rank the remaining alternatives by majority preference; if no Condorcet winner exists, fall back on Borda scores. For each route, students must identify precisely which of Arrow's conditions is being relaxed or transformed, whether the relaxation is a mere technical dodge or a substantive concession, and — for the triad rule specifically — construct a small profile in which the Borda fallback makes the social ranking sensitive to the addition or removal of an irrelevant alternative. Students should also have to say whether the theorem, properly interpreted, was ever a threat to democratic legitimacy in the first place, or whether the conditions it imposes (especially independence of irrelevant alternatives) smuggle in a controversial view of what a social welfare function is for. Please provide the question as it would appear on the exam, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks total that names the specific moves separating an A answer from a B answer, with a one-line note on how students typically go wrong at each point.
