so I'm a novelist, been at it for about fifteen years, and I've been asked to sit on one of those festival panels about "the work versus the man." They want me to open with a short statement of where I stand. I sat down to write it and realized the things I actually believe might not fit together, so before I embarrass myself in front of a paying audience, can you tell me if this hangs together?

Here's what I believe, as best I can state it:

(1) The text is the text. A novel is an object made of sentences, and its merit or lack of it lives entirely in the sentences. What the author did at home is as irrelevant to that as his tax returns.

(2) At the same time, I think the novel's real and distinctive value is moral — that the form earns its keep by getting you to inhabit lives that aren't yours, that a reader who has lived inside a great novel is genuinely better equipped for other people, and that this isn't a happy byproduct but the point of the whole enterprise.

(3) I still love and teach one particular mid-century novelist whose private conduct, now that the biographies are out, was monstrous — to his family, his friends, everyone he ever got close to. It has never once occurred to me to stop teaching him, and I reccomend him to students constantly.

(4) Last year I refused to blurb a novel by a guy in my orbit after a friend told me, in detail, how he had treated her. I told his editor it wasn't about the book. I don't regret it.

(5) I do think there's a real difference between a novel that's about cruelty and a novel whose texture is cruel — the second kind is damaged as art. The meanness is soaked into the form itself, and no amount of technical brilliance repairs it.

A writer friend of mine says I'm just running whatever morality is convenient on the day — teach the dead monster, snub the living one, praise cruelty-in-the-sentence whenever it flatters my taste. I don't think that's what's going on, but I honestly can't say why, which bothers me more than the accusation does.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher who works on art and ethics, and I want you to actually think before you answer — brainstorm the possible clashes first, then sort them. Which of the five are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and what's the cheapest revision that cleans up the most? Rank the tensions by severity instead of telling me they're all equally bad, and be specific about which commitment has to bend for each one. One more thing: please keep straight what's actually settled among people who study this stuff versus what's genuinely contested — I know there are live fights about whether moral flaws can even be aesthetic flaws, and I don't want to walk onto that panel repeating one side of a fight as if it were the consensus. If any part of your verdict depends on a contested view, say so rather than presenting it as settled.
