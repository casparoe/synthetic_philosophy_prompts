I teach a 200-level philosophy of science course — 16 students, one 80-minute session — and for the first time this term I have to make underdetermination of theory by evidence land as something felt rather than recited. The topic has also stopped being merely academic for me: last year my own lab kept its incumbent model of the system we study through three rounds of increasingly awkward data, and when I pressed for the reason, the honest answer was that the evidence left several rivals standing and replacing the incumbent would have meant redoing two grant-funded experiments. That is exactly the experience I want my students to have from the inside, so please design the activity around discovery, not exposition: no lecture up front, and the terms of art should not be spoken until the debrief.

To keep them from reciting the history of astronomy, set the game in a fictional system. My scenario: a deep-sea fish collected on a fictional expedition, the "lampfish," which is sometimes observed to glow, with a field log that records, for each night, whether it glowed and a few ambient conditions. I'll want you to write the full twenty-card observation deck and specify the hidden rule that generates it, but build the deck so that at least three structurally different theory families — say an internal rhythm, a response to some environmental trigger, and a response to the behavior of another organism — each remain fully compatible with the complete run. That means engineering the log so the candidate correlates deliberately co-occur: if the fish glowed on every calm, moonless night, then a tide-driven clock, a direct response to water conditions, and a prey-cue story all fit, and that collinearity is the point of the exercise rather than a flaw in it.

The structure I have in mind: teams act as rival research groups, see the first batch of cards, write a theory plus a prediction for the next card, and score points as cards are revealed — with each team allowed at most one auxiliary patch to save a failing theory, purchased at a fixed point cost, so they feel the trade-off between saving a theory and cheapening it. Somewhere in the middle, I want a funding round in which I, as the expedition's sponsor, will back only one group's follow-up study, presumably the current leader — and I'd like you to decide whether students are allowed to defect to the leading theory, since I suspect the defection dynamics are where the sharpest debrief material lies. At the end, the sponsor's chosen theory should be read aloud as "what the expedition concluded," before I reveal that the full deck fits the abandoned rivals just as well.

Please give me the rules, materials, scoring, and a timeline that fits 80 minutes; then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinction I'm after — the accumulating cards standing for finite evidence, the patches standing for the holistic escape route, the funding round standing for the fact that when evidence underdetermines, something else decides — and that ends by distinguishing the underdetermination that is merely not-yet-resolved from the kind that no further cards would remove. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about what the game cannot reproduce: real auxiliary hypotheses can be independently tested later, real science has decades and careers in it, and my students will know the game is rigged to have no winner — say plainly which parts of the idea only I can supply in the debrief.
