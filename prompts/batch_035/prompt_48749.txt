I've been arguing with a coworker about Goodhart's law and I want to nail down what I actually believe about it, case by case, because my gut verdicts don't obviously add up to one principle.

The cases, with my verdicts. The Soviet nail factory given a quota in tons that produces a few gigantic nails: clearly Goodhart, about as bad as it gets. A company paying programmers per bug fixed until someone starts planting bugs: also classic, but somehow feels different from the nails — there's an actual schemer involved. A hospital under a four-hour ER wait target that starts parking patients in hallways to stop the clock: bad, but less bad than if they'd ignored the target entirely, since some of the wait time genuinely got fixed. A school where teachers teach to a standardized test that's actually well-designed: I keep wanting to call this a win rather than a failure, even though it's textbook proxy-optimization. Evolution optimizing fitness: fine, no Goodhart anywhere in sight, though I can't say why, since evolution is about the most ruthless optimizer there is. And the case that bugs me most: a company that selects hires purely by top score on a skills test that only correlates maybe 0.5 with actual performance. Nobody games anything, there's no adversary, the test never changes — but the top scorer is probably partly just lucky that day, and I still think it counts as the proxy going wrong.

My tentative principle: the damage from targeting a measure is proportional to how much of the proxy-goal link the optimization pressure itself destroys. The nails and the bugs fit it (the pressure severs the link). The teaching case fits if I say a good test barely gets damaged by pressure. But the hiring case is awkward — nothing gets destroyed, the imperfection was there all along, yet it still feels like Goodhart. And evolution doesn't fit at all unless I claim fitness isn't a proxy to begin with, which feels like cheating. Also, a friend keeps telling me people split this whole thing into like four flavors — one that's basically regression to the mean with no bad actor involved, one that's adversarial gaming, etc. — but honestly the labels seem to be doing work that the actual explanation should be doing.

So what principle captures my verdicts, and which verdicts does it force me to drop?
