I teach political philosophy, and I have been asked to contribute a position statement to an interdisciplinary working group on nuclear policy — mostly engineers, political scientists, and one retired strategist. I have drafted the substantive part, but before circulating it I want a rigorous consistency audit. My sense is that some subset of what I believe is jointly unstable, but I genuinely cannot tell where the trouble is, or whether there is any. Here is the position:

(1) Nuclear deterrence is a fact rather than a folly: secure second-strike forces have kept a great-power peace of unprecedented length, and this stability is compatible with — indeed presupposes — that the leadership on both sides is rational.

(2) A threat is credible to a rational adversary only if that adversary assigns a high probability to its being carried out. But a rational leader who has already absorbed a first strike, and whose retaliatory strike guarantees his own country's annihilation, has no reason to carry out retaliation as a voluntary choice at that point. So the central threat of deterrence, understood as a considered decision made at the moment of truth, is incredible between ideally rational agents — the classic deterrence paradox.

(3) The way out is structural. Credibility should be manufactured by removing the individual decision from the last moment: pre-delegation to lower echelons, launch-on-warning postures, procedural or automated response arrangements — anything that makes the probability of retaliation high independently of what any one person would choose once the missiles are inbound. I endorse this direction in principle. A rational deterrent cannot rest on a final decision that rationality itself cannot support.

(4) At the same time, I hold that command and control must satisfy what I call the always/never requirement: the system must always work when duly authorized, and never work otherwise — and the "never" side, meaning protection against false alarms, accidents, miscalculation, and unauthorized use, is close to non-negotiable, however small the residual probability can be engineered to be.

(5) I publicly support a declared policy of no first use, and I believe such a declaration would make deterrence more stable, not less: it removes the incentive to preempt, and it signals that nuclear weapons exist solely to deter nuclear weapons.

(6) I also believe the nuclear umbrella over allies remains credible under no first use. An adversary contemplating a large-scale conventional invasion of an ally would still have to reckon with nuclear escalation, so the guarantee to allies survives the declaration.

(7) Finally, I hold that it is categorically wrong to intend the massacre of civilian populations — even conditionally, even under a policy that saves lives — and I say exactly this to my students, while also teaching them that deterrence is justified and that it works precisely because the adversary understands the threat to include such massacre.

Please go through the set pair by pair. Tell me which tensions are real and which merely look like tensions, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it up costs — including what it costs to keep everything and simply accept the tension. I am particularly interested in whether the structural move in (3) is doing the work I take it to be doing, but do not spare that commitment on my account. If the cheapest repair is a distinction rather than a revision — some distinction I have failed to draw that dissolves one of the apparent conflicts — say so, but only if it genuinely holds under pressure.
