I'm in a third-year cognitive science course on the unity of consciousness, and this week we did split-brain patients — the callosotomy surgery for epilepsy, Sperry and Gazzaniga's experiments, the chimeric faces task, the famous one where the left hemisphere sees a chicken claw and the right sees a snow scene, and when the left hand points to a shovel the speaking hemisphere confabulates a story about cleaning out the chicken shed. After going through all that my professor offered an analogy that's been bugging me since. He said basically: think of the intact brain as two computers on a network, with the corpus callosum as the cable connecting them. In a normal person the two machines sync up so seamlessly and at such high bandwidth that you get what feels like a single unified user experience — one desktop, one user, even though there are two processors underneath. The split-brain operation is just pulling the plug on the cable: nothing inside either machine changes, no software is damaged, you simply separate what the network had fused. And then the conclusion he wanted us to take away: the unity of consciousness in healthy people isn't intrinsic or fundamental at all, it's an achievement of networking — we are all, in some sense, two agents whose cable is fast enough that we never notice the seam.

Something about the move from the analogy to that conclusion felt too quick to me, but I can't fully articulate why, and a couple of my classmates thought it was obviously fine. So I'd love an audit of the analogy. Like, first give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of each computer, what plays the role of the operating system or the "user" whose unified desktop is supposed to be consciousness, what's the cable, what's the bandwidth, what are the protocols the two sides use to sync. Then tell me where the mapping breaks down. A few candidate disanalogies I keep half-noticing but can't evaluate: the two hemispheres aren't two general-purpose machines with duplicated software, they're heavily specialized (one speaks and interprets, one is better at spatial stuff and faces), which seems different from two identical networked computers; there are subcortical structures and shared resources below the callosum (attention, the brainstem, one shared body), so maybe "pulling the plug" leaves more connection intact than the analogy suggests — split-brain patients don't feel like two people in daily life and can even do cross-cuing tricks; and the "cable" might not just transmit information between two finished agents but partly constitute what each side is, in which case the pre-surgery patient wasn't literally two networked minds waiting to be unplugged.

So: which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion and which are just noise? Specifically, does the "unity of consciousness is a networking achievement, not an intrinsic feature" conclusion survive once the differences are on the table, or was the cable picture quietly doing the work — e.g., by smuggling in the idea that the two sides were already complete independent agents before the cut, when maybe the division into "two" is partly an artifact of the surgery and the tests? And if the computer-network analogy is misleading in the ways you think, is there a better analogy that keeps what's true about the split-brain data without the baggage — or is the right move to drop analogies entirely here?
