I'm teaching a third-year metaphysics course, and we do persistence of objects through time in the last two weeks. This year I want to run it backwards from my usual sequence: quiz first, lectures second, so the students get sorted by what they actually believe about the cases before they learn which camp gets called what. Can you write that quiz for me?

Ten-ish questions, each one a concrete scenario with two to four answer options. The cases I want in there (add more if you need to fill out the logical space):

- The poker that was straight yesterday and is bent today — one and the same thing with incompatible properties? Does that bother them, and if it does, which fix do they reach for: saying "straight at Monday" relates the thing to a time rather than giving it a second property, or saying there's no single thing that was both straight and bent?
- Fission: an amoeba splits, or something more dramatic like a duplicating machine. Who comes out, and how many?
- Counting: Tibbles the cat, and the cat-shaped thing sharing all of Tibbles' parts except a single hair — how many cats are lounging on the mat?
- Gradual replacement, ship-of-Theseun style, but pushed until the answers come apart — at what point, if any, do they stop saying it's the same thing, and do they give a sharp cutoff or a sorites-style "no fact of the matter"?
- The time-travel duplicate: I shake hands with my younger self. Are there two of me in the room, and if so what's the standing of the thing I'm shaking hands with — a second full-fledged me, an earlier part of me, what?
- The rotating homogeneous disc, if you can phrase it so a student who's never read the literature can still have the intuition.

Rules: no question may just ask whether objects have temporal parts, or whether they're "wholly present" whenever they exist — that polls whether they did the reading, not what they believe. Every question must be answerable by someone who's never heard any of the vocabulary, and the answer options should be plain descriptions of what's going on in the case, never labels.

Then a scoring key: patterns of answers map onto endurantism, the worm view, and stage theory. If some answer pattern doesn't correspond to any of these, name it "unnamed" and add one sentence on whether you think nobody holds it because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And please be honest about which pairs of views the quiz can't actually seperate — my guess is the worm and stage answers coincide on almost everything except the counting and time-travel questions, so flag that rather than pretending the instrument is cleaner than it is. If some question only discriminates because of how you worded it rather than a real disagreement, say so.

Audience: second- and third-years who've done intro metaphysics (basic modality, they've met the Ship of Theseus in first year) but nothing on persistence. That's the whole point of running the quiz first.
