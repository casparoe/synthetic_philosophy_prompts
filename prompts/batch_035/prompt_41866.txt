A friend from my birdwatching group, who's read a lot more philosophy than I have, explained Dennett's idea of free-floating rationales to me the other day. I only got into philosophy after retiring (biochemistry was my line of work for 40 years), so I cant always tell sense from smooth talk. Anyway, he put it roughly like this: 'Dennett showed that reasons exist objectively in nature, with no mind needed to recognize them. A tree's leaves are broad because broad leaves catch more sunlight — that's a real reason, and it was a good reason long before any scientist or anyone else ever thought of it. The reason is even recorded right there in the tree's genes. So rationality isn't something minds create; it's something minds find — the reasons were out there all along, waiting to be discovered. And since natural selection weighs these reasons and picks accordingly, selection is itself a rational process, and in a sense the biosphere has been doing our thinking for us all along.'

I'll admit I like the sound of grounding reasons in nature instead of in somebody's head, and I'm definately inclined to believe him — it appeals to the old materialist in me. But the last step felt like a leap. He seemed to go from 'reasons can exist without a thinker' straight to 'evolution is a thinker,' and I cant quite pin down where, or whether, that went wrong. Could you go through his summary and tell me what a Dennett-style Darwinian would actually endorse, where he's overstating or conflating things, and whether that final claim about selection being rational follows from the parts that are accurate? Please keep it under 300 words — my attention span for philosophy is still developing.
