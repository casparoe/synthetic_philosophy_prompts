I'm putting together a position statement on the knowledge argument for my philosophy of mind reading group, which has spent most of the term on Mary, and before I circulate it I want a consistency check from someone who will poke at it rather than nod along. Here is roughly where I've landed.

(1) I'm a physicalist: the physical facts exhaust all the facts there are. (2) When Mary leaves the black-and-white room and sees red for the first time, she genuinely learns something — I'm unmoved by the ability hypothesis; she comes to know a truth she did not know before, and doesn't merely gain skills of recognition, imagination, and memory. (3) I nonetheless think the knowledge argument fails against physicalism, via the phenomenal concept strategy: the truth Mary learns is a fact she already knew, now tokened under a new, recognitional mode of presentation — nothing new in the world, something new in her conceptual repertoire. (4) I lean on the same strategy to explain why zombies seem conceivable: the zombie scenario is ideally conceivable but not metaphysically possible. (5) I take this pattern to be routine and already familiar from the water/H2O case and from Perry-style indexical cases like Heimson's: there too a subject can know all the objective facts and still be missing a way of thinking about one of them, with no new fact involved. (6) I hold a fairly standard inferential-role view of concept individuation: what a concept is is fixed by its a priori inferential role — this is how I explain why discovering that water is H2O was empirical (the watery-stuff role is available a priori) and, more generally, how a priori entailment relations get fixed. (7) Phenomenal concepts, however, I take to be special: they are cognitively isolated, with no a priori inferential connections to any physical concepts, and they cannot be acquired from any description however complete — possessing one requires having, or being appropriately wired for, the experience itself. (8) Separately, I hold that there are no brute necessities: every necessary truth is grounded in something. Water being H2O is grounded in microstructure, and I find an ungrounded, stopping-point identity between pain and some neural state no less mysterious than the dualism it was meant to replace. (9) And yet I affirm psychophysical identities as necessary a posteriori truths at which explanation simply runs out. (10) Finally, I think Mary, while still inside the room, could in principle come to know the entire phenomenal concept strategy — it is itself just a body of physical and a priori knowledge — so she could know that she lacks only a concept, and know exactly which one she will gain at the door.

Does this package hang together? Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only look to be? I'd like you to rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally, and for each real one tell me what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere on the list. If you think two of my commitments only seem to conflict because I've conflated a distinction — say, between facts and modes of presentation, or between knowing a truth and having it available under a given concept — I want that identified explicitly rather than scored as a tie to be broken. And if there is a single revision that would clean up more of the list than any other, tell me which one and why. Please don't soften the audit on the theory that these are all familiar positions; the people reading it next week won't.
