I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and my reading group is doing a unit on animal cognition where Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of creatures keeps coming up — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian, from his mid-90s writing (Darwin's Dangerous Idea and the little book Kinds of Minds). Quick recap so we're working from the same thing, since I want the definitions doing the work, not your memory of Dennett: Darwinian creatures have fixed routines, shaped only by blind trial and error at the level of the species; Skinnerian creatures can learn within their own lifetime, but their trials are still blind in a specific sense — the creature has to actually perform the action to find out how it goes; Popperian creatures run those trials off-line, internally, rehearsing or pre-playing an act and selecting before committing the body; and Gregorian creatures are ones that import cultural mind-tools — labels, methods, ways of carving up a problem — that make their internal trials smarter. I keep comming back to this taxonomy because I genuinely can't tell if it carves nature at a joint or is just a fancy way of saying "dumb, less dumb, clever, clever with culture."

So here's what I want you to do: construct a thought experiment about a single animal that pulls the taxonomy apart in an unusual direction. Specifically, the same animal should come out Gregorian with respect to one part of its behavior and merely Skinnerian — or even Darwinian — with respect to a closely coupled part of the very same activity. Not the boring defaults: not "human vs. wild animal," not a clean trained-vs-untrained contrast, and not Gregorian across the board (thats the trivial case for us). Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph, realistic enough that an animal-cognition lab could film it — no ape narrating its inner life, and crucially no stipulated internal states; the classifications have to be derivable from the definitions I gave above plus what an external observer could see. And you can't smuggle in the verdicts by stipulation — the whole point is deriving them, so if you find yourself writing "the animal, being Gregorian here, does X," you've cheated.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough: for each sub-behavior, say which kind of creature the animal is and why, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the classification if you remove or weaken that feature.
