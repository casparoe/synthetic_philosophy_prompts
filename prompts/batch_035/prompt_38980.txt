I teach philosophy of physics, and every spring I walk my graduate seminar through the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic debate, and every spring I come away with the same uncomfortable feeling: that the discussion has been stationary since roughly 2012, and that nobody on either side expects anything to move it.

To make the stalemate concrete: after Harrigan and Spekkens gave the field its terms in 2010, the question became whether the quantum state corresponds to an observer-independent fact about a system's underlying state or only encodes an agent's partial information about that state. The Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem then appeared to deal the epistemic view a near-fatal blow, showing that psi-epistemic models conflict with quantum predictions — but only given an auxiliary assumption, preparation independence, roughly the claim that independently prepared systems possess their underlying states independently. Since then the psi-epistemists have forked: some simply accept that quantum systems prepared independently can be correlated in the relevant way, a move critics compare to superdeterminism as a response to Bell; others, especially the QBists, seem to decline the ontological models framework altogether, treating the quantum state as an agent's personal degrees of belief and denying that the framework's picture of an observer-independent underlying state describes anything the theory is about. The psi-ontists reply that declining the framework is not a response to the theorem but a refusal to engage it, and that a theory whose central quantity is somebody's belief owes us an account of the world those beliefs are about.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: both sides have long since absorbed the formal results, and the live disagreement is not about quantum mechanics at all but about what a physical theory must supply — an inventory of what exists versus a normative calculus for situated agents — so that the dispute over whether the wavefunction is "real" is partly verbal, papering over a deeper dispute about scientific realism itself. But I may be wrong; perhaps it is a genuinely substantive disagreement about which auxiliary assumptions are epistemically expensive, and the stall simply reflects the difficulty of pricing assumptions like preparation independence.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Which parts are verbal, which substantive, and which are two camps answering different questions under one heading? Where does the QBist refusal of the shared framework sit on that map — is it a substantive metaphysical claim, a methodological one, or an evasion? And given your diagnosis, say honestly what would count as progress: a framework-neutral reformulation, ψ-ontology theorems with weaker assumptions, a principled way of comparing the cost of preparation independence to the cost of superdeterminism — or an argument that the question is ill-posed and progress should not be expected. Please answer in American English.
