i'm a philosophy professor serving on my university's academic integrity committee, and we're about to vote on something I have real qualms about, so I want a draft memo I can circulate beforehand.

The situation: we've been piloting an automated system that flags suspected contract cheating and unauthorized AI use in coursework. It operates at a scale no human panel could — it screens thousands of submissions — and its accuracy numbers in the pilot looked genuinely decent. But it's a black box in an important way: even the vendor can't give a reason for an individual flag that's more specific than the system assigning that submission a high probability. The proposal on the table is a hybrid model: the system flags, and then a designated faculty reviewer confirms each flag before charges go forward. The selling point is that this keeps a human in the loop and therefore keeps responsibility where it belongs.

Here's my worry, and I want the memo to take it seriously rather than treat it as philosophy-professor hand-wringing. When a human grader accuses a student, there's someone who made a judgment and can answer for it — can explain why, defend it, be shown to be wrong, and feel the weight of wrecking someone's semester if they were careless. If our reviewer is just ratifying flags they can't independently verify — they see a confidence score and a few highlighted passages, and they're expected to clear a hundred flags a week — then the human in the loop looks less like a responsible agent and more like a designated blame-absorber. And when we eventually expel a student who was innocent, or let a cheater through because the system missed them, it's not clear anyone answers for it: the vendor points to the reviewer, the reviewer points to the system, the comittee points to the policy. Philosophers call this kind of thing a responsibility gap, and I want the memo to actually reckon with whether the hybrid proposal closes the gap or just launders it.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend a concrete decision — adopt as proposed, adopt with conditions (say which ones), or reject and propose an alternative — and give reasons the rest of the comittee could actually respond to. Several of them think the accuracy numbers speak for themselves, and one has pointed out, fairly, that human detection of cheating is itself biased and inconsistent, which the system might improve on; she deserves a real answer, not a pat on the head. Also please address the question I keep getting asked and keep fumbling: if we reject the system, are we really saying imperfect human judgment is more acceptable than better-performing machine judgment, and if so, why? Something I can circulate, maybe 900 words, and no gesturing at needing more study — we vote in three weeks.
