I'm a structural engineer at a mid-sized firm, and over the past eighteen months my work has quietly reorganized itself around an AI assistant. I use it for preliminary load calculations, for checking designs against the building code, and for drafting the justification memos that accompany our submissions. The pattern is always the same: I describe the structure and the loading case, it returns the numbers and the relevant clauses, and I review the output — which in practice means confirming that it looks reasonable, since re-deriving the calculation by hand would take a day I don't have. Two things happened recently that unsettled me. First, a senior colleague asked, half-jokingly, what I would do if the tool were withdrawn tomorrow, and I realized the honest answer is that I could still handle the routine cases but not the difficult ones — and, more alarmingly, that I no longer know how I would find out which was which. Second, I caught myself signing off on a set of calculations that I could not reproduce, could not fully explain, and had verified only by asking the same tool, in a fresh session, whether its earlier answer was correct.

When I raised this with a friend over dinner, he said it was just the latest chapter in an old story: engineers trusted slide rules, then hand calculators, then finite-element software, and nobody claims the calculator generation understood less because they no longer did long division by hand. I think something is genuinely different here, but I can't pin down what, and I'd rather not build my unease out of pure nostalgia. The differences I can point to — that I could at least state the method the calculator was applying, whereas I cannot state the method this thing applies; that a calculator is transparent, while my only way of checking this tool is the tool itself; that my own competence is decaying while the quality of the output stays constant — each of these seems as if it might cut the other way too, so I don't trust my own list.

So my questions, really, are these. If I keep working this way, is something of value being lost, and if so, what exactly? Is the relevant understanding now located somewhere — in the firm, in the tool, in the combination of the two — such that it's enough for someone to understand even if no individual person does? And practically: is it responsible of me to keep signing off on calculations I could not personally defend if challenged, or is the demand that I be able to defend them personally one that only made sense before tools like this existed? I'd appreciate a careful answer, not reassurance.
