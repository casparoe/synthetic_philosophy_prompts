I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice and decision theory, twelve students, one 90-minute session on diachronic rationality. Design an in-class game, run with tokens and a raffle rather than real money, that makes money pumps something participants feel rather than diagram. I want a three-round arc:

(1) An elicitation round, run under cover of a "market survey," where each participant privately records (a) a straight choice between two bets and (b) a minimum selling price for each bet separately. The bets should be a high-probability small-prize bet and a low-probability large-prize bet with matched expected values — give exact numbers. The point is that a predictable fraction of the class will choose one bet while pricing the other higher, without noticing any tension.

(2) A trading round where one student plays an arbitrageur and the rest can accept or refuse each pairwise trade. Design the exact sequence of offers the arbitrageur should make so that a participant with the choice/price reversal from round 1 can be relieved of tokens through a sequence of trades each of which she accepts at the time, ending up back where she started minus a fee. Specify when the arbitrageur must be allowed to make offers sequentially and privately, and why simultaneous bundle offers would kill the pump.

(3) A commitment round that brings in the diachronic side: a smaller prize now versus a larger prize at the end of the session, with an option to pay tokens up front to bind one's future choice. Some participants should be "sophisticates" who foresee their later reversal and pay to bind, some "naives" who don't, and the round should make visible that consistency across time is a different condition from consistency at a moment.

Then a debrief that maps each round onto the formal distinctions: acyclicity versus transitivity, the choice-versst-price reversal as a violation of the economic preference model rather than of logic, and the synchronic-versus-diachronic contrast. The debrief should also press the escape hatches honestly: the claim that being pumpable shows exploitability but not irrationality, that refusing to trade at all is a rational response to the institution of sequential offers rather than evidence of inconsistent preferences, and the defender's move of charging that pairwise acceptance under separate offers fails to reveal a coherent preference ordering in the first place. Say for each escape hatch whether the arbitrageur's behavior in the actual round we just ran rebuts it or leaves it standing.

Finally, tell me two things candidly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — e.g., too few students exhibiting the reversal, participants catching on once they see the arbitrageur profit, the arbitrageur botching the offer sequence — with concrete fixes; and which feature of the money-pump argument the game cannot reproduce no matter how I stage it, which I will have to supply by lecture. My guess is the stakes are the problem — losing tokens to a classmate is too small to make the intuition that pumps track exploitation rather than inconsistency scale up — but tell me whether you agree or whether the unreproducible part is something else, like the fact that real pump arguments need indifference at each step rather than strict preference.
