Context: we were at a craps table and there was this guy betting on 12 "because it hadn't come up all night." Mara said that's the exact thinking the house edge feeds on — dice don't remember. But her claim was bigger than that. She said once you know the chance of something, your confidence just is that number: if a die is fair, your confidence in each face is 1/6, and anyone whose confidence drifts off that without new information is irrational, full stop.

Dev said sure, whatever, about the "it's due" guy. But the table had been going wierd all night — one shooter rolled a suprising number of hard sixes, and at some point the pit boss swapped the dice out. Given all that, why shouldn't his confidence in a given face sit a little off exactly 1/6? The 1/6 comes from a manufacturer's spec, and specs can be wrong, or dice can drift.

Mara said if the dice are actually off, then 1/6 was never the chance to begin with — the real chance is whatever the physics actually delivers, so her rule survives in the form: match your confidence to the real chance, whatever it is. Dev's "little off" is only rational to the extent it tracks evidence that the real chance is off.

Dev said at that point the rule says nothing. "Match your confidence to the real chance, whatever it is" is just "believe what's true" in a costume. If the rule only binds people who already know the exact chance, and nobody ever does (dice getting swapped mid-session is precisely the kind of thing that leaves you unsure), then the rule never actually fires. And her move-3 concession — "rational to the extent it's tracking evidence" — is just "update on evidence," which nobody disputes. The special claim was supposed to be that the chance itself, as a number, has authority over you.

Mara's last word was that approximate knowledge is the normal condition for everything — you're never exactly right about the half-life of uranium either, and that doesn't make every knowledge claim empty. But she looked less sure by the end.
