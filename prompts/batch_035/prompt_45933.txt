I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a module called Theories of Distributive Justice. Our tutor runs a peer-marking exercise: before each tutorial, each of us is given one anonymised essay written by a member of the seminar group, which we mark against the departmental criteria and return with written comments; the marks are then discussed and moderated in the tutorial. I have been assigned the essay below and have to return it marked by the end of the week.

The essay question set for this fortnight was: "Does the levelling-down objection give us reason to abandon egalitarianism in favour of Parfit's priority view?"

The essay as I received it reads in full:

"The levelling-down objection was first pressed by Derek Parfit in Reasons and Persons (1984). It asks us to compare two possible outcomes. In the first, some people are much worse off than others, though everyone's life goes reasonably well. In the second, a disaster reduces the better-off to the level of the worst-off, so that everyone is equally badly off, and no one gains anything from the change. On the telic version of egalitarianism, which holds that inequality is in itself bad, the second outcome is in one respect better than the first: it is not unequal. But in the second outcome no one is better off and some people are worse off. Parfit concludes that egalitarianism is, in his sense of the term, self-defeating: the theory's own account of what is bad counts, in some cases, in favour of changes that leave everyone worse off and benefit no one.

The priority view escapes this result. On this view, benefits to the worse-off matter more, and the value of an outcome is a weighted sum of individual well-being, with the weights increasing the worse off people are. Since the view assigns no value to equality itself — equality matters to it only instrumentally — it never counts a pure levelling-down as any kind of improvement. In the case above, the second outcome is simply worse.

This seems to me decisive. Any theory that implies, in some case, that there is a reason in favour of a change which makes some people worse off and benefits no one has been struck down by that case. Nor can egalitarians reply that equality is merely one value among others, since the objection concerns what their theory counts as bad, not what it all-things-considered recommends. And they cannot retreat to the relational claim that the wrong of inequality lies in domination or hierarchy, because that is simply a different theory. The levelling-down objection therefore gives us conclusive reason to abandon egalitarianism in favour of the priority view."

The departmental scale is the standard UK undergraduate one, with 70 and above a First, 60–69 an Upper Second, and 50–59 a Lower Second. The criteria are: accuracy in the use of texts; quality of argument; organisation and clarity.

Please mark the essay for me. Please set out your response under the following headings, in this order, and observe the rough lengths:

1. "Provisional mark" (about 60 words): a numerical mark and the band it falls in, with a one-sentence justification.
2. "Comments on the argument" (about 300 words): take the essay point by point from start to finish, saying for each step whether it works, and wherever you comment, quote the actual words of the essay so the writer can locate the point; be even-handed between what succeeds and what fails.
3. "Comments on accuracy and use of texts" (about 120 words): anything misattributed, misremembered, or internally in tension, again with quotations.
4. "What the writer does well" (about 60 words): the two strongest things about the essay, stated plainly.
5. "Note for the tutorial" (about 50 words): the single issue I should be ready to raise in the moderated discussion, whether or not it appears in the written comments.

A few requirements for the comments: they must be specific enough that the writer could revise the essay from them alone, so no general impressions without a quoted passage attached; they should be phrased as if returned to the writer directly; and the mark should reflect the criteria rather than the confidence of the prose, since fluent writing can disguise shaky steps. Please do not adjust the mark out of sympathy for a fellow student: the whole point of the exercise is that I give the mark the essay deserves.
