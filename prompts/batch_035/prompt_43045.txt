I am grading closed-book final exams for a second-year course in metaethics, and I would like help with one answer. The paper gave students forty-five minutes per question. The question on which this answer was written read as follows:

Explain, keeping them clearly separate, what Hume noticed about arguments and what Moore noticed about definitions. Are the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy the same fallacy under two names, or do they come apart? If they come apart, illustrate each separation with a philosopher who commits one but not the other.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

Hume noticed that moralists in his day began by describing how things are and ended up telling us what we ought to do, without ever explaining the transition. In the Treatise he argued that no ought-statement can be derived from any number of is-statements, and this is the fallacy he called the naturalistic fallacy. Moore, in Principia Ethica in 1903, gave the fallacy its modern defense with the open question argument: for any natural property N that we propose as the definition of good — pleasure, evolutionary fitness, whatever society approves — it remains an open, substantive question whether things that have N are good. If a question is genuinely open, the proposed definition must be wrong. So good cannot stand for any natural property at all, which is why Moore concluded that good is a simple non-natural property, known by intuition. The two fallacies are really the same one: defining good as pleasure is just a disguised way of deriving an ought from an is, since once the definition is in place you get normative conclusions out of purely descriptive facts about what gives pleasure. Because they are the same, a utilitarian who defines good as pleasure is committing Hume's fallacy, and anyone who infers an ought directly from an is has plainly not learned Moore's lesson either.
