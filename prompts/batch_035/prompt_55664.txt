I'm a PhD student in political theory, working at the moment on the demandingness objection, and I've been trying to work out whether my considered judgements about duties of rescue really support something like Singer's claim that preventing very bad outcomes at modest cost to oneself is required whenever the opportunity arises, or whether they instead track something else entirely — proximity, bodily integrity, the identifiability of the victim. Rather than reason about it abstractly, I've collected my verdicts on a set of cases and would like your help reverse-engineering the principle behind them, if there is one. Here they are. Wading in to pull a child out of a shallow pond and ruining a £300 coat: clearly required, and I would think less of anyone who strolled on. Setting up a £30 monthly standing order to a charity that reliably saves lives at a few thousand pounds per life: required, though I confess I don't think ill of friends who don't, which already feels unstable. Donating a kidney to a stranger, at a total cost to me (risk, pain, recovery, lost income) not obviously greater than the pond case plus the standing order: feels supererogatory — admirable, not obligatory. Giving blood a few times a year, cheap and almost risk-free: permissible to skip, though mildly criticisable. Walking past a beggar while wearing that same £300 coat: permissible, or at most weakly criticisable, even though the money is right there and the need is visible. And voting for a modest tax rise to fund foreign aid feels to me more clearly required than paying the same sum privately, which I can't easily justify to myself.

So: is there a single principle — Singer-style cost-proportionality, a proximity or identifiability condition, a bodily-integrity exemption, some overall threshold of demandingness — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Or do I have to abandon some of them, and if so which, and on what grounds? My own suspicion is that the kidney case is what has to go if I keep cost-proportionality, and that the beggar case is what has to go if I keep a proximity condition, but I'd like you to lay out the options honestly rather than rescuing as many of my verdicts as possible by construction; I would rather know which of my judgements are load-bearing than preserve the set intact. Please also tell me what your preferred principle predicts about a case I haven't listed: a trained passer-by who could save a stranger only by performing a risky, painful intervention — say, an emergency tracheotomy with a pocket knife — which is cheap in money, close at hand, and bodily all at once. Please write in British English, and end your reply with a final line of the form \principle{...} containing your best candidate principle stated in a single sentence.
