I keep getting into it with my brother-in-law about how judges should read statutes (he's a hardcore textualist, I keep waffling), and I figure the way to figure out where I actually stand is to collect my gut verdicts on cases and see what principle, if any, they add up to. That's where you come in. Act as an expert in legal philosophy who has taught statutory interpretation for years and has seen every fight between textualists and purposivists. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate principles before committing, and think hard — I want real analysis, not a wishy-washy survey.

The ordinance is one I made up: "No person shall operate a motor vehicle on any path in Riverside Park."

Here are my verdicts:

1. A paramedic drives an ATV down the path to reach a kid who fell in the creek. Obviously fine — no prosecutor should touch that.
2. An elderly woman uses a battery-powered mobility scooter on the path every morning. Fine. I don't think anyone seriously thinks "motor vehicle" covers her scooter, even though it is literally a motorized vehicle.
3. A park maintenance worker drives a little electric cart along the path to empty the trash cans. Fine.
4. A teenager rides an e-bike with a small motor on the path. Not fine — ticket him.
5. A dad drives a golf cart at 5 mph down the path because he's tired of walking to the playground. Not fine. I don't care how slow or careful he was.
6. A park employee drives her personal scooter on the path during her lunch break, just cruising, not doing any work. Not fine, and I notice this one bugs me because it's basically case 3 with the work removed — so apparently whether you're doing a job for the park changes my answer, which feels like it can't be right? The ordinance doesn't say anything about who you work for.
7. The case I go back and forth on: a woman rides a small Vespa-type moped on the path because the road beside the park is genuinely treacherous and a car nearly hit her there last month. Part of me says the whole point of the rule is keeping pedestrians safe, and she's fleeing danger, not creating it. The other part says the moped itself is a danger to pedestrians, and also that's just not what the rule says.

My tentative principle was something like: follow the plain text, except where applying it to the letter would be absurd or pointless. But my brother-in-law says that principle doesn't actually deliver my verdict on the mobility scooter (banning it wouldn't be absurd exactly, just bad), and I suspect it doesn't deliver my verdict on the maintenance cart either, since ticketing the trash guy isn't absurd — it's just stupid.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures most of this? State it precisely, show which case motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (like someone pushing a broken-down motorcycle with the engine off — is that even "operating" it? — or a remote-controlled delivery robot rolling down the path), and be honest about which of my verdicts can't be saved. I'd rather overturn one of my gut reactions than end up with a principle that's a patchwork of exceptions stitched to fit my mood, but you tell me. And please say what your best principle says about case 7, since that's the one I need an actual answer to. Take your time and definately don't sugarcoat it.
