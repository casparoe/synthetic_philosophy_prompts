The axiom of choice strikes me as misdescribed as a single controversial principle. For any finite collection of nonempty sets, the ability to pick one element from each is a theorem of ordinary set theory — you just make the selections one at a time — and it seems the same reasoning should extend to any collection that can be enumerated, so that the axiom only does real work for uncountable families. Moreover, since each member of the family is nonempty by hypothesis, there is always an element available to pick, so the selection never involves genuine arbitrariness. Given this, shouldn't we regard countable choice as a harmless consequence of the other axioms, reserve whatever controversy remains for the full uncountable version, and stop presenting the axiom of choice as one indivisible assumption?
