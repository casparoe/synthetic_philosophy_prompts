My seminar on historical explanation has produced two camps who have spent the term arguing past each other, and I'd like to make the final session constructive. On one side are the students I'll call the person-centered camp: they hold that individual leaders are a genuine independent variable in history, and that some outcomes are not just improbable but impossible without particular people. On the other side are the structural camp: they hold that outcomes at the scale of whole societies are generated by macrosocial conditions — economic crisis, institutional breakdown, mass party organization, ideological currents — and that swapping individuals changes timing and texture, not type. The flashpoint, every year, is the same counterfactual: would there have been something like Nazi Germany without Hitler?

Draft the joint statement these two camps should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the following components.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. This should include what both sides accept about the historical record itself (Weimar's crises, the dependence of the Nazi party's cohesion on Hitler personally, the role of elite miscalculation in 1933); about the rules of counterfactual reasoning both will follow, including some discipline they currently lack — I want them to explicitly agree on the difference between "close" worlds (minimal edits to actual history) and "far" ones (large edits), and to agree on which kind of world each claim is about. And it should include their shared recognition that "would there have been Nazi Germany without Hitler" bundles at least three distinct questions that must be answered separately: whether some authoritarian regime emerges in Germany by the mid-1930s; whether that regime pursues a genocidal program of roughly the historical scale; and whether it launches a war of roughly the historical kind. I expect them to agree that the questions can have different answers without either camp conceding anything, and the statement should say which.

Second, a disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept — the person-centered students will not sign "the leader was merely an accelerant," and the structural students will not sign "the structures were merely background conditions," so those formulations are out unless reworded.

Third, the cruxes. I have my suspicions about where the real ones lie, and I'd like the document to confirm or correct me. One candidate: the depth of the substitute pool in interwar Germany — was the Weimar environment rich in plausible substitutes (demagogues who could have played Hitler's role), or poor, such that Hitler's specific profile of charisma, ideological fixation, and risk tolerance was effectively irreplaceable? The statement should treat this as an empirical question about leader-rich versus leader-poor environments, not as a matter of taste, and should note which of the three bundled questions it bears on. A second candidate: the distinction between what I'd call structural overdetermination — many sufficient paths converge on the same outcome, so the outcome is robust to individual substitutions — and path dependence, where small substitutions cascade. A third, which I suspect is partly verbal and should be labeled as such if so: what "inevitable" means, since the camps have been using it respectively to mean overdetermined, highly probable, and close to deterministic, and the document should separate the verbal residue from the substantive disagreement that survives the disambiguation.

Fourth, for each substantive crux, a test that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. These need not be new data — they can be comparisons both camps already accept as relevant, such as cases where movements lost their central figure and trajectory changed sharply versus cases where a successor of different character changed little, or cases where macrosocial conditions that looked sufficient produced no comparable outcome at all. The requirement is only that both sides would acknowledge the comparison as evidence, and that their predictions genuinely diverge; a test on which the two camps predict the same outcome does not belong in this section.

Close the statement with a short paragraph, jointly signed, on what the two camps now take the Great Man question to actually turn on — phrased, if possible, so that a third historian who thinks both camps are asking a badly posed question could still sign it. Format: numbered agreed-claims section, disagreements table, cruxes with tests and predictions, closing paragraph.
