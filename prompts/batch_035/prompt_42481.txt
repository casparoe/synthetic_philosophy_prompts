I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a large introductory ethics course, and I'd like to anticipate how the professor I work under — call him Professor R — will rule on a case scheduled for next week's discussion sections, so I can steer my students toward the most instructive disagreements instead of being caught flat-footed. His verdicts on hypotheticals are quick and firm, and over the semester I've assembled something like a track record. So far he has ruled that:

- a bystander may divert a runaway trolley away from five workers and onto a side track, where it will kill one worker;
- one may not push a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path in order to stop it from killing five;
- a surgeon may not harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five dying patients;
- a pilot may not bomb civilians in order to break the enemy's will to fight, even if doing so would end a war sooner and save many more lives;
- a civil engineer may divert a mudslide away from a village of five houses and into the path of a single ranger's cabin, killing the ranger, where the diversion is the only way to save the village.

Next week's case is new to the syllabus: an unmanned locomotive is rolling toward five track workers, and the only available move is to divert it onto a curved siding that rejoins the main line just short of the five. A maintenance supervisor is on the siding, and the locomotive will be stopped only by striking him — his body is the one thing that keeps it from reaching the five.

Acting as a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of harming and allowing harm, please think carefully before committing to an answer, perhaps by first listing the candidate rules that could underlie Professor R's verdicts. What rule best explains his five rulings, and which rival rules fit them just as well? What does each candidate rule predict about the locomotive case, and where do the predictions come apart? Give me your best guess as to his verdict, along with your reasons for preferring whatever reading of his pattern supports it. Then name the single further case that would most cleanly divide the surviving rules, and say what each predicts on it. Throughout, please say how confident you are in your claims, and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
