I'd like you to take on the role of a careful referee in political philosophy — someone equally at home in consequentialism, deontology, and the literature on political ethics — and adjudicate a dispute between two colleagues of mine, move by move. Here is the background. During a flood emergency, a city emergency-management director believed, on solid but incomplete evidence, that a neighborhood's tap water had been contaminated and that boiling wouldn't remove the contaminant. She had two hours to decide what to say at a press conference. She knew that if she announced the suspicion of contamination, panic buying of bottled water and an exodus across bridges would very likely cause more immediate deaths than the contaminant would (which she estimated as low-probability but severe). She also knew that if she said the water was safe and she was wrong, seriously ill people would have drunk it on her say-so. She announced that the water had been tested and was safe — an outright lie, not an evasion.

At a seminar afterparty, two of my colleagues argued about it. Marco's opening move: she did wrong, full stop. Intentionally deceiving the public you serve is a violation of the trust that defines her office, and the fact that it produced good consequences is exactly why rules against lying exist — rules matter most precisely when breaking them would be expedient. Lena's reply: a rule-worship that lets citizens die so an official can keep her hands clean is not morality but self-indulgence; if lying was the only way to prevent the greater harm, then lying was what she ought to have done, and Marco is confusing the sadness of the choice with its wrongness. Marco's second move: this proves too much. If outcomes license deception whenever officials sincerely judge the balance to favor it, then every government in history has had a standing excuse, and the public's ability to rely on official statements — the very thing that made her lie effective — depends on officials not reasoning this way. Lena's second move: she conceded the risk but introduced what she called the real lesson of "dirty hands" — that the act was justified and yet remains a wrong done to the deceived citizens, which is why a good official in that position should feel lasting guilt, and why the director was right to lie and would still owe those citizens, if she was wrong about the water, something more than a utilitarian shrug: an apology for a wrong, not merely an apology for a mishap. Marco's final move: he said this is double-talk. If guilt is genuinely fitting, then the act was wrong, so she shouldn't have done it; if she should have done it, then the guilt is theater, and Lena has smuggled in a contradiction by calling the same act both what she ought to have done and a wrong. Lena just smiled and said that's exactly the paradox of political office, and Marco said a paradox you're proud of is just an inconsistency you've stopped minding. They eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Please go through their exchange move by move: assess each of the four moves (Marco's opening, Lena's first reply, Marco's second, Lena's dirty-hands move, and Marco's final charge) as ranging from clearly right to clearly mistaken, with brief justification for each. Then address the central question directly: is Lena's position — that the lie was justified, was a wrong, and calls for guilt that is more than mere theater — a coherent one, or does Marco's final charge land? In adjudicating, take seriously the idea of a "moral remainder" (the thought that the right act can leave a residue of wrongdoing owed to the person deceived) but don't assume it works; part of your job is to say where it does genuine explanatory work and where it may be relabeling. Please be specific about which premise each of them would have to give up to stop talking past each other, and say who comes out more right overall. Reason carefully before you rate the moves; don't just declare a winner and work backward.
