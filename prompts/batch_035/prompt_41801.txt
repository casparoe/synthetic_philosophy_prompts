I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of mathematics, and a friend of mine has become enamored of a skeptical argument built out of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem — Skolem's paradox, extended with a twist I hadn't seen before. Her argument runs as follows. (1) If ZFC is consistent, then since it is a countable first-order theory with an infinite model, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives it a countable model M. (2) ZFC proves that there is no bijection between the natural numbers and the reals, so M, being a model of ZFC, satisfies "the reals are uncountable." (3) Yet M's reals are, from the outside, countable — that is simply what it means for M to be a countable model. (4) So whether a set is uncountable is relative to a perspective: uncountable from inside M, countable from outside; there is no model-independent fact of the matter about cardinality. (5) And by compactness, the relativization goes deeper: there are models of arithmetic, and indeed of ZFC, whose "natural numbers" contain nonstandard elements, objects the model itself certifies as finite but which are not finite in any ordinary sense. By parity with (4), finitude is model-relative too. (6) If every cardinality notion — finite, countable, uncountable — is relative to a model, then set-theoretic discourse does not describe a single independent mathematical reality; it describes whatever the model of the moment makes of it. This is basically Skolem's own relativism, she says, with the compactness twist making it airtight.

Here is what bothers me. The statement of the paradox in (1) through (3) is itself made from somewhere: to say that M is "countable" is to say that there is a bijection, in the ambient universe, between M's domain and the natural numbers. If (4) and (5) are right, that ambient perspective is just one more model's perspective — the external notion of countability used to generate the paradox is exactly the kind of notion (6) declares to have no determinate content. The argument seems to prove too much: it saws off the branch it sits on, since without a usable notion of countability-from-outside, premise (1) never says anything determinate and the paradox never gets off the ground. When I put this to her, she shrugged and said that the ambient perspective's being just another model is precisely the point — a Putnamian moral about there being no privileged interpretation — and that she is happy to live with it. I am not sure living with it is coherent, but I also cannot find a clean flaw in her chain.

So: tell me which step you would reject and what rejecting it costs. Is there an equivocation somewhere between the internal reading of "there is no bijection" — quantifying over M's own sets — and the external reading deployed in (3)? Does the compactness step (5) genuinely behave in parallel with the Löwenheim–Skolem step, or is she exploiting a disanalogy between nonstandardness about the naturals and externality about bijections? And is her "no privileged perspective" shrug a stable position, or does it undermine the very theorem-stating work she needs the outside perspective to do, leaving her with no way even to formulate her conclusion? For my part, I would most like to keep the internal/external distinction intact — I would rather explain why Skolem's paradox is benign than concede her relativism — but I want to know exactly what, if anything, I have to pay for that, and whether her shrug can be cashed out as something more than a gesture.
