The theme I keep returning to is the separateness of persons objection — the thought that utilitarianism improperly aggregates wellbeing across distinct lives in something like the way each of us legitimately trades off gains and losses within a single life, and that in doing so it fails to respect the moral significance of the boundaries between people. My sense is that the literature has moved well past the slogan, but I can't tell which of the live questions is actually worth a term paper's worth of effort rather than a paragraph.

So, putting yourself in the position of my supervisor for a moment: which open questions in this debate most deserve a student's attention, and in what order would you prioritise them? I have at least three candidates, and I'd welcome others. First, there is the question of what the objection really amounts to — is it a complaint about aggregation as such, or about distribution, and if the latter, does prioritarianism escape it while retaining the summing? Second, there is the intrapersonal analogy itself: when opponents press that there is no unified subject across a life any more than across persons, and that a person is just a series of experiences, is that a serious riposte or a metaphysical red herring? Third, there is the question of whether formal results — aggregation theorems purporting to show that rational individuals behind an veil-of-ignorance-style device would endorse sum-ranking — dissolve the objection or merely relocate it. Which of these (or something else) should I work on?

I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning behind the ordering. For each question you prioritise, I'd like to know what makes it worth the words: whether it is upstream of the others in the sense that progress on it would change how the rest are framed, whether it is tractable for someone with a solid background in logic but only basic decision theory, and whether it is neglected in a way that leaves room for a genuinely original contribution rather than a rehash. I'd also like you to be explicit about any question you would deprioritise and why — whether because it is effectively settled, or because its answer would not change much downstream. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, as if composing a careful letter, and in British English throughout.
