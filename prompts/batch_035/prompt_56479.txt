Among philosophers and sinologists writing in English, the interpretation of Gongsun Long's white horse dialogue has been stalled for decades, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another adjudication of it. On its face the thesis is that a white horse is not a horse, defended in a few surviving moves: that the term for horse names shape while the term for white horse names shape together with color, so the two expressions do different naming jobs; that a request for a horse can be satisfied by a yellow or black one while a request for a white horse cannot, so the two differ in what they exclude; and a stretch of argument responding to the objection that since horses do in fact have colors, a colored horse can hardly fail to be a horse. Three broad readings of this material persist. On the first, the argument is sound given the way classical Chinese nouns behave: on this view they function more like mass nouns naming stuffs or stuff-kinds than count nouns naming discrete objects, so the negated copula is an identity claim about what the two expressions pick out, and since they pick out different portions of stuff, the claim comes out true. On the second, the dialogue is a knowing equivocation between identity and predication — a sophism crafted for display in disputation contests, to be admired as technique rather than believed. On the third, it is a serious claim in a logic of kinds: that a compound term never denotes exactly what its head term denotes, a point about the extensions of complex expressions which modern logic would concede and then declare trivial. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because the text is ambiguous but because the parties disagree about what would even count as evidence: the entire surviving corpus of the School of Names is a handful of short, partly corrupt chapters, so each reading is compatible with everything we possess, and what presents itself as a disagreement about Gongsun Long is really a proxy fight over whether early Chinese thought contained logic in anything like the Greek sense. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden substantive question underneath — say, a question about the grammar of classical Chinese nominalization that historical linguistics could in principle settle — with the interpretive disagreement merely its shadow? Tell me whether this is a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two camps answering different questions (what the man actually believed versus what the text can be made to do) under a single heading, and support the diagnosis with what each reading must say about the other in order to coexist so peacefully for so long. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: an archaeological find adding substantially to the corpus, a grammatical consensus about how the relevant nouns and copulas worked, or an honest admission that the question is permanently underdetermined — and if the last, say what that implies about the practice of reconstructing a whole philosophical tradition from fragments this thin.
