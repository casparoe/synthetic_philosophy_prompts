I'm on the academic integrity council at a mid-size state university, and we're revisiting our policy on how we handle confirmed cases of cheating. Quick background: right now everything is confidential. Student gets found responsible, gets a grade penalty, maybe a suspension, does an online ethics module, and that's it. No one else ever knows. Some of our council members think this is a disaster -- they say we've built a guilt-based system where the only thing that matters is whether you personally feel bad and jump through the repair hoops, and that cheating here is rampant precisely because there's no social cost at all. Their proposal is to publish names (or at least anonymized case descriptions that identifying details would make obvious) in a monthly community bulletin, on the theory that shame is a social emotion, it's what a community is for, and the fear of being seen is the only deterrent that actually works. The other faction says shame is a terrible moral emotion -- it attaches to the whole person rather than the act, it produces hiding and denial instead of repair, and we'd just be teaching students to get better at not getting caught.

One wrinkle I want the memo to actually deal with: someone in our last meeting brought up that old anthropology distinction between "guilt cultures" and "shame cultures" -- the idea that some societies run on internalized conscience and others on external reputation, and there was a whole mid-century thing about guilt cultures being supposedly more "developed." She wants us to say our policy choice is really a choice about what kind of campus culture we're deliberately building, i.e., that cultures can choose. I half buy it but I also suspect the dichotomy itself is kind of cooked (plenty of shame in supposedly guilt-based institutions, plenty of internalized conscience in supposedly shame-based ones, and a lot of the fieldwork behind it got picked apart later). The memo shouldn't just take either the dichotomy or her voluntarism about culture for granted.

So: draft the council's decision memo. Recommend a concrete policy (keep confidentiality, adopt publication, or something in between with actual specifics -- what gets published, to whom, when, and what the removal path looks like), and give the reasoning. It should treat the pro-publication side's deterrence and community-accountability argument at full strength, not as a strawman, and it should say honestly what's lost on whichever side loses. It should also engage whether shame in a bounded community like a campus can be the "reintegrative" kind (where you're marked as having done wrong but the door back is clearly open) rather than the stigmatizing kind, and what institutional design details actually make the difference between the two -- because if it comes down to details like timing, wording, and whether there's a re-entry ritual, the memo should name them. Keep it to something I can circulate before the meeting, so roughly a page and a half, with a clear bottom line the council can vote on.
