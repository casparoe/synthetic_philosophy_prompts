Last night my friend and I got into a genuinely long argument about Arrow's theorem, which feels like a ridiculous thing to do but here we are. Context: I sit on the council of a small scientific society (about 40 voting members) and we're redesigning how we elect our president. We currently use first past the post, and after last year's three-way race where someone won with 31% and an absolutely furious runner-up, there's real pressure to switch to something like alternative vote or Borda. My friend's line is that the whole exercise is pointless: Arrow showed every ranked voting system violates some fairness condition, so we're just choosing which unfairness we get, and we may as well keep first past the post and stop pretending there's a principled answer. I think this is lazy -- it treats the axioms as equally weighted when it seems to me like independence of irrelevant alternatives is doing all the work in the theorem and is also the one we have least reason to care about in a 40-person club, plus no dictator and unanimity seem non-negotiable. But he pushed back that if I get to pick and choose which axioms matter, I'm not refuting him, just confessing to preferences.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to council? It should recommend one concrete voting rule for the presidential election (and, if you think it should differ, say what to use when we need to produce a full ranked ordering, like for council shortlisting), with the reasoning spelled out so the other councillors -- mostly scientists, no philosophers -- can actually engage with it. Please give my friend's position a fair shake rather than just waving it away; if there's a version of his point that survives, I want it named and answered in the memo. Also feel free to note where our tiny scale genuinely changes what we should value versus a national election, since that seemed to be the crux of half our argument. Prefer if you keep it to something I could plausibly circulate, not a treatise.
