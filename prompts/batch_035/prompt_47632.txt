My dissertation committee meets in three weeks to settle the final outline of my thesis, and the two people whose opinions matter most on it cannot agree about whether I should include a chapter defending the Everettian treatment of probability. I have to live with whatever outline they settle on, and I am honestly not equipped to referee the dispute myself, so I will recount their exchange as faithfully as I can and ask for a verdict.

Some background, briefly, since both of them assume it. In the many-worlds interpretation, every outcome of a measurement occurs in some branch. My supervisor thinks the chapter should present the decision-theoretic programme, associated with Deutsch and Wallace, on which an agent's rational preferences over branching futures can be shown to weight branches by their squared amplitude — the Born rule — rather than by counting them. The second reader thinks the whole programme is circular and the chapter should instead present probability in many-worlds as an open problem.

The exchange, as it went. First, the second reader: in Everett, nothing is at risk in the ordinary sense. If the theory says a detector will click with probability one percent, there is a branch where it clicks and a branch where it does not, and both contain a copy of the experimenter. Saying the click has probability one percent appears to assert nothing, since no outcome fails to occur. So the Born rule is not a statement of probability at all, and the celebrated derivations do not help, because their axioms about rational preference simply build in the answer they pretend to derive. Had the axioms told agents to care about branches in proportion to amplitude, the theorem would have gone the other way just as smoothly.

My supervisor replied that no axiom anywhere instructs anyone to care in proportion to amplitude. The axioms are structural constraints on preference — roughly, that small changes in the physical situation produce only small changes in preference, and that care adds across disjoint alternatives. The squared-amplitude weighting is the output of the proof, not an input: it is a substantive theorem that any agent whose preferences satisfy those constraints must weight branches by the Born measure. And the complaint that nothing is at risk ignores the agent's position before the measurement: she does not know which branch she will find herself in afterwards, that uncertainty is genuine, and the weights are exactly what quantify it.

The second reader pressed two points. The uncertainty is phony, he said: the agent knows in advance that both successors exist, and there is no further fact about which one is her — identity either splits or is indeterminate, and in neither case is there anything she fails to know. As for the axioms, the continuity constraint says that small differences in amplitude make small differences in preference; but whether amplitude is the right currency for caring about futures is the entire question at issue. An agent who cares about each future copy equally, regardless of its amplitude, is not irrational in any ordinary sense, and the axioms exclude him by decree — which is precisely the circularity: the theorem assumes a weighted-caring structure and then derives weighted caring.

My supervisor answered that equal care per copy is not a neutral default and is not even coherent. Branches are not well-defined individuals: decoherence splits gradually and at different resolutions depending on how finely you look, so there is no fact about how many copies there are, and branch counting has no determinate content. Worse, he said, branch counting is self-undermining in practice: an agent who weights branches equally will accept wagers that drain almost all of his amplitude into bad branches, so across many experiments nearly all of what there is of him resides in branches where he has been ruined. The only stable policy is weighting by amplitude, and that is what the theorem establishes and the ruin argument illustrates.

The second reader's last word was that the ruin argument smuggles in the measure a third time: nearly all of what there is of him is itself defined by amplitude, which is the very thing under dispute — by the branch-counter's lights, he is fine in the majority of branches, and what his opponent calls ruin is, on his accounting, rare. One cannot prove the Born rule from a premise already stated in Born measure. And the indeterminacy of branching cuts both ways: if there is no fact about how many branches there are, perhaps there is no fact about their weights either, and neither counting nor weighting is more objective than the other.

That is where they left it, and where I am. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why, so that I can see who wins on which specific point rather than only overall. Please also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — if there is a consideration that would change the picture neither has raised, I want to know it before the meeting. Write your reply as continuous prose only, please, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter.
