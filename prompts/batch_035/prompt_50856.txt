I handle citizen engagement for a city of about 300k, and every couple of months a department shows up at my desk wanting "a citizen panel" for something — budget priorties, a street redesign, lately advice on what AI tools the city should adopt. Right now the choice happens by vibes, and the outcome is always the same: open call, whoever shows up shows up, which turns out to be the same thirty retirees who answer every consultation we run. A council member has started asking who exactly these "citizens" are, so I need something better. Write me a decision procedure my small, non-specialist team can run on each request in an afternoon: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with "no panel for this one," and where it punts to me because the question is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure should actually engage the arguments for sortition, not just gesture at "more participation": that open calls and elections select for the confident and well-off while a lottery gives you the average person; that a fifteen-person panel can only mirror the population so much, so there's a real difference between a panel being descriptively representative and being legitimate; and that even good volunteers turn into a professional participant class with interests of their own if you never rotate them — so the procedure needs something like term limits for panelists, and a rule for when reusing the usual crowd is acceptable. Then run it on the three requests I named, including at least one where the answer is one I won't like (I suspect the AI one is it).

Sections please, in this order with rough lengths: (1) The procedure, ~400 words; (2) Handoffs, ~80 words; (3) The three cases, ~350 words; (4) What it can't do, ~80 words. And be decisive — where the procedure lands somewhere, land it and say why, don't lay out options and leave the picking to me.
