I've been reading a popular book on the physics and metaphysics of time that leans hard on the oldest image available: time is a river, running one way from source to mouth, with a fixed bed and banks that were laid down long before we arrived. The book invokes Heraclitus's river — the one no one steps into twice, because the water is never the same — and uses the image to draw two conclusions: first, that the flow of time is an objective feature of reality and not merely how things look from inside; and second, that we are in the position of swimmers carried along by the current toward the mouth whether we like it or not, which the author takes to license a fairly strong fatalism about the direction of a life.

Meanwhile I've been reading Borges — "A New Refutation of Time," "The Doctrine of the Cycles" in A History of Eternity, "The Garden of Forking Paths," and the late lecture on time collected in This Craft of Verse — and it has occurred to me that the river is very nearly the picture he spends his whole career taking apart, though almost never by attacking it directly. The passage that struck me most comes at the close of "A New Refutation of Time," where, having run his idealist argument that each moment and each self is defined only by its contents, he draws a moral in which time is a river that sweeps him along — but the twist is that he declares himself to be the river, and then repeats the same move with a tiger that destroys him and a fire that consumes him, ending on the note that he, unfortunately, is Borges. That is not the swimmer-in-the-current picture at all.

Please audit the analogy as my book uses it. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the source, the mouth, the banks, the bed, the current, the water, the bather or swimmer, the flood — and say explicitly where the book leaves cells blank. Then lay out the disanalogies and sort them into those that actually matter for the book's two conclusions and those that are mere decoration. I can see at least three candidate breakdowns but don't know how to weigh them: (1) the spatialization worry — nearly everything that makes the river image vivid is borrowed from space (bed, banks, direction, a flow one can stand beside and watch), so the image may smuggle in an external standpoint that no one occupies; (2) the bather problem — Heraclitus's own point seems to cut against the book rather than for it, since the one who steps in is also never the same, and Borges appears to radicalize this into the claim that the river image only works if the river and the swimmer are made of the same stuff, which dissolves the passenger picture entirely; and (3) the channel problem — real rivers fork into deltas, meander, form oxbow loops that nearly close on themselves, and periodically change course entirely, while the book needs a single fixed channel, and Borges of all people wrote the forking one, and treated the circular variant as something the Stoics and Nietzsche took seriously rather than as an obvious confusion. Tell me which of these (or which others) genuinely damage the book's argument and which only add texture. Then give your verdict: does the book's conclusion about objective flow and fatalism survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And is there a better image on Borges's own terms — he offers the tiger and the fire as parallel images in the same breath as the river, and elsewhere the forking garden and the circle — or is the deeper lesson that every image of time is thing-like or space-like in a way that already assumes what it was supposed to explain, so that the choice is between images that fail differently rather than one that succeeds?

Two requests about the honesty of the audit. First, tell me what would most likely change your verdict — e.g., a reading of Borges on which he is cataloguing metaphors rather than theorizing about time, or a way for the book to disclaim the external standpoint while keeping the flow. Second, flag where your own reasoning is most likely to fail — my suspicion is the subtlest trap is treating Borges as arguing for a theory of time at all, when he may be doing something closer to demonstrating, from the inside, what it is like to be the river.
