Been listening to a philosophy podcast about vagueness (the heap stuff) and they gave this summary of the two main views near the end, and I want to check how much of it I should trust. Here's roughly what they said:

Vagueness is when a word has blurry edges — "heap", "bald", "tall" — and philosophers basically agree that there's no exact number of grains of sand where a heap begins. Two camps try to explain this. The first, epistemicism, says the blurriness is entirely in our heads: there really is a sharp cutoff point, we just can't ever know where it is, kind of like how there's a fact of the matter about a particle's position that quantum mechanics can never reveal to us. The second, supervaluationism, says there genuinely is no fact of the matter: as you remove grains one at a time, every single statement of the form "this many grains makes a heap" comes out neither true nor false, and this means even a sentence like "there is a heap on the table" gets no truth value at all. So on the supervaluationist picture classical logic has to be given up — the law of excluded middle fails for vague sentences, and that's the price you pay for saying there's no cutoff.

Then their takeaway was: so whichever view you pick, if you're a borderline case of "tall", it's literally neither true nor false that you're tall — for the epistemicist because you can't know which side of the line you're on, for the supervaluationist because there's no line.
