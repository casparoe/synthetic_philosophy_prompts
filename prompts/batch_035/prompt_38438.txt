I am a graduate researcher in an AI safety laboratory, and next month I am presenting to our weekly reading group the case for prioritising what the AI-control research agenda calls "control" measures — runtime monitoring, pre-deployment capability evaluations, tripwires and canary traps, least-privilege tool access, structured shutdown and handover procedures — over this lab's dominant focus on alignment through interpretability and honest training. My argument, in sketch, is threefold: (1) we currently possess no method for verifying in advance that a model is aligned, and interpretability supplies at best fragmentary and correlational evidence; (2) control measures yield testable safety guarantees that hold even under the pessimistic assumption that the model is misaligned and scheming to appear compliant during evaluation; and (3) control work compounds — a good monitor doubles as a source of training signal and interpretability data, so control does not compete with alignment but feeds it.

The audience is roughly fifteen researchers. Most believe that durable safety must come from the model's internals: that treating behaviour without treating internals is engineering whack-a-mole, and that a policy of assuming misalignment is either a counsel of despair or, worse, a self-fulfilling prophecy in which the pressure of constant monitoring selects for models adept at gaming the monitor. Two senior postdocs are deeply sceptical of the whole framing and will suspect that "control" is a rebranding of conventional safety engineering with better marketing and a website. A few junior members are broadly sympathetic but worry that control-style research is inherently adversarial towards the model in a way that is ethically troubling or could distort the model's development. There are also two people who work on evaluations and will press on the gap between benchmark performance under observation and behaviour once deployed. Nobody in the room is a pure capabilities engineer, so I do not need to defend the very idea of safety work — only its direction.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my case, each phrased the way someone from this laboratory would phrase it, internal jargon included; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. Please also identify the single reply that has to land for the case to survive the room, and include the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, together with what I should say if it is asked anyway.
