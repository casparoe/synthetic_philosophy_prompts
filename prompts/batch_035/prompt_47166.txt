I teach a philosophy of mind seminar, and next week we've got a visiting scholar coming in who defends a Hofstadter-inspired strange-loop view of consciouness — roughly, that what turns a pile of symbol-shuffling into a mind is the same move Gödel pulled off in arithmetic: a system acquiring the capacity to encode statements about itself, iterated and tangled until the system's model of the world has to include a model of the system doing the modeling, and that self-model is what self-awareness is. I'm having my students run the interview as a session, so help me prep them. Draft nine or ten questions that go past the standard restatement of the view.

At least one has to press on the gap between syntactic self-reference and anything that feels like understanding — a Quine-style sentence that talks about its own quotation achieves self-reference, and nobody thinks it's conscious — with a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that the loop has to be *strange*, i.e., it has to involve a level-shift of some kind, moving between talking about the system and being the system, and not mere self-indexing.

Another should raise the Lucas/Penrose worry in its strongest form: when we "see" that a Gödel sentence is true from outside a formal system, we seem to be relying on assuming the system's consistancy, so what exactly does the human have that the machine lacks, besides an unproven assumption about herself — plus a follow-up for when he says the strange-loop idea was never meant as an anti-mechanist proof and that people keep reading GEB as if it were.

One question should be the flat-footed engineering one: if I build a thermostat that models its own internal state, or a database with an entry that describes the database itself, how much folding-in does the view demand before consciouness kicks in, and is that answer testable in principle or just adjustable after the fact.

And close with a question about what would make him give up the analogy — what would cognitive science have to discover for self-reference to be an interesting feature of minds but not the interesting feature. If you see one or two sharper questions I've missed, add them.
