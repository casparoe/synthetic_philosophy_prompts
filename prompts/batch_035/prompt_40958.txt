My roommate is a philosophy major and has been on an anthropics kick, and last week she handed me an argument she calls the "no-news principle," which she thinks dissolves the whole self-sampling vs. self-indication mess, and honestly I can't find the broken step. It goes like this. (1) You update on evidence E in proportion to how likely each theory said E was to occur — no likelihood difference, no update. (2) Your total evidence about which theory is true is exhausted by the non-indexic facts you know plus one indexical fact: that this particular stream of experiences, the one you're having, is yours. (3) For any two theories that both guarantee that at least one observer has exactly this stream of experiences, the likelihood of that indexical fact is 1 on both — given that the stream occurs at all, it trivially couldnt have belonged to anyone else. (4) So once two theories agree on the non-indexical facts and both put at least one "you-shaped" observer somewhere in the world, nothing you could ever observe can shift your credences between them, and all the anthropic machinery is idle.

Then she applies it. Presumptuous philosopher setup: 50/50 priors between T1 (a trillion observers) and T2 (a trillion trillion observers), experiences distributed the same way in each, and she says I have to stay exactly at 50/50 no matter how many zeros she adds — that's her way of saying the self-indication people are just wrong. Fine, I was almost ready to buy that. But then she proved too much for my taste. Take her own favorite variant: a coin flip, and on heads one person is created in a blue room, on tails a thousand people are created, 999 of them in red rooms and one in blue. You wake up, look around: blue. By her own step (1), that's evidence — the likelihood of "my room is blue" is 1 on heads and 1/1000 on tails, so you should end up near-certain of heads. But step (3) applies here just as hard: given that somebody wakes up in blue, that experience couldn't have been anyone else's, so why isn't its likelihood 1 on both sides of the coin? Either blue-room-me is entitled to a massive update or nobody ever gets one, and it looks like the same principle has to decide both cases. She says the cases are different becuase in the second one there's a fact about which room "you" ended up in that was out of your control, but that seems to just restate the question rather than answer it.

For what it's worth, I'd much rather keep the blue-room update than the 50/50 in the presumptuous philosopher case, but I have a nagging feeling that keeping the first drags the second along with it. So: which step do I actually need to reject here, why did that step look fine to both of us at first, and what does rejecting it cost? I gather both the self-sampling people and the self-indication people think they have an answer to this exact kind of case, and I'd like to know who's paying what.
