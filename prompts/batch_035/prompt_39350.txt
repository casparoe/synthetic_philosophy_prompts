For over a decade I've watched the sanction and deplatforming arguments recycle through the blogs and forums I read, and I've concluded the only way out of pattern-matching to a team is to write down my gut verdicts on concrete cases and see what principle, if any, survives them. Here they are:

1. A conference withdraws an invited talk from someone who spent the previous year directing harassment campaigns at individual junior scholars: clearly fine.
2. A platform permanently bans a user for a joke in poor taste posted a decade earlier, with no appeal and no path back: clearly not fine.
3. The same platform permanently bans an account that has spent five years pumping out fabricated medical claims, likewise with no appeal and no path back: fine — and I notice that my "no endpoint" objection in case 2 does not move me here at all.
4. A university department quietly declines to host a speaker whose published views the faculty consider vile, citing scheduling reasons: fine, and even the quietness seems fine.
5. Thousands of strangers coordinate to get a mid-ranking employee fired for a political opinion expressed off-hours on a personal account: clearly not fine.
6. A public intellectual is roundly mocked for an op-ed he wrote in bad faith, the mockery snowballs, and two years later the episode is still raised whenever he publishes: fine when it happened, though it troubles me more in retrospect.
7. Our friend group quietly stops inviting someone who said something genuinely cruel to one of us, never telling him why: fine, and I would do it again.
8. A publisher pulps a dead author's book after racist private letters surface: not fine.

My tentative principle is that social sanction counts as legitimate punishment only when it is proportionate, legible (the target can in principle find out what he did and who imposed it), and bounded (there is a defined or at least predictable endpoint) — the properties formal punishment at least aspires to, and the ones I've been trained by fifteen years of legibility discourse to look for. But my own verdicts embarrass the principle: case 3 has no endpoint and I hold to it; case 7 is illegible and unbounded and I hold to it; case 4 is illegible by construction. So either the principle needs clauses that don't read as special pleading, or some verdicts have to go. Please give me one principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied by someone who disagrees with me about the substance; say which case motivates each clause; tell me what the principle yields on a case I haven't listed — a coordinated campaign to strip someone of a professional licence rather than a job; and be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and which of those I should actually give up.
