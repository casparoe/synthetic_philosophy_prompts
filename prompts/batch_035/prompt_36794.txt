I teach philosophy of mind, and for the past two years I've been sitting in on a reading group run by colleagues in computer science who work on mechanistic interpretability — the research program that tries to reverse-engineer what large neural networks are doing by decomposing them into "features," "circuits," and "attention heads" that purportedly detect things like "French," "code comments," or "deception." The working assumption seems to be that somewhere inside the network there are discrete, compositional, causally efficacious representations — that if you find the right units and the right vocabulary, the system's behavior will read as a kind of computation over structured states. As a longtime skeptic of Fodor's language of thought hypothesis, I keep having the uneasy feeling that this entire empirical research program quietly presupposes something very close to LOT: mentalese realized in silicon.

I'd like your help reconstructing the implicit theory. What exactly does the practice of searching for features and circuits presuppose about representation — discreteness, compositionality, causality, stability across contexts? Which rival views of mental content does it rule out: could a thoroughgoing connectionist, or someone who thinks representation in these systems is a matter of inferential role across the whole network rather than localizable vehicles, still license the methodology, or does the practice genuinely require the stronger view? And I'm especially interested in whether the field's own findings pull against its presuppositions: the recent work on "superposition" — where networks seem to store more features than they have neurons, cramming concepts into overlapping, non-interpretable directions — looks to me like empirical pressure on the discreteness assumption, yet the research program absorbs it without revision, the way an old paradigm absorbs anomalies. That smells like philosophy of science, not just engineering.

So tell me: is mechanistic interpretability a covert revival of the language of thought, and if so, which version — the strong Fodorian one, or a weaker structural realism about representations that aLOT skeptic could accept? Distinguish what the methodology strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say where it is underdetermined, such that several theories of representation would license the same experiments; and tell me concretely what would have to change in the day-to-day practice — the probing, the ablation studies, the search for "the" circuit underlying some behavior — if the presupposed view were replaced by a fully distributed, holistic alternative. I'm not asking for a survey of the LOT literature; I'm asking what this particular practice commits its practitioners to, whether they know it or not.
