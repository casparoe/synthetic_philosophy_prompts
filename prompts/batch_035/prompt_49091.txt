I'm a philosophy PhD who somehow ended up writing a paper on statutory interpretation, and in two weeks I'm presenting it at a law school faculty workshop. The thesis, roughly: the textualism vs. purposivism fight isn't a substantive disagreement about what words mean or what legislatures are like, it's a disagreement about which *default convention* courts should attribute to the enacting legislature when the statute underspecifies. So on my view the question "is textualism correct?" is malformed -- the correct answer depends on which set of background conventions makes the overall practice of legislation work best, the same way choosing whether to drive on the left or the right isn't about which side is metaphysically correct. Textualists are choosing a low-variance convention that lowers interpretive transaction costs, purposivists a convention that buys flexibility, and the fight over "what Congress meant" is a proxy fight over which coordination equilibrium to entrench. My sketch of the argument: (1) statutory meaning in hard cases is underdetermined because legislatures are massively plural agents with no unified intention, (2) so courts must be doing something *constructive*, not merely descriptive, when they pick a reading, (3) the honest way to pick is on institutional-design grounds, (4) this reframes the debate but also ends up slightly favoring textualism, since purposivism's gains are real but get capitalized away by sophisticated legislatures drafting around them, whereas textualism's predictability compounds. My department loved it; I suspect the law school will not.

The audience: a general faculty workshop at a law school that skews textualist -- two former Easterbrook clerks, a person who did empirical work on how congressional staffers actually draft (so my "legislatures are plural agents" premise will get pushback with receipts), a legal historian who works on the legal process school and will be protective of Hart and Sacks, and one critical legal studies person who will say my whole coordination framing smuggles in a legitimacy the practice doesn't have. The paper's respondent is a serious administrative law person. It's a 30 min Q&A and I've never presented to lawyers before, only philosophers, who fight dirty but about different things.
