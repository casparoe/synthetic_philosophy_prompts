I recently got appointed to my city's micromobility advisory panel (long story — I'm basically the only member who actually commutes by bike, and after a scooter accident near the elementary school ended up in the local paper, the council wanted "a regular user" in the room). The big decision on our plate this fall: whether to recommend granting a permit for a shared e-scooter operator to deploy around 800 scooters citywide. The fight is entirely about which safety numbers count. If you class scooter riders together with cyclists, the per-mile injury rate looks acceptable — comparable to biking, which the city already encourages. If you class them with mopeds, they look disastrous. The company's rep insists the "natural comparison" is bicycles, since scooters are slow, light, and used on the same kinds of streets; the city attorney's office leans toward the moped comparison because they're motorized and almost nobody wears a helmet. A stats-literate friend of mine told me this is a version of the reference class problem, and that there's no fact of the matter about which class is the "right" one — which, if she's correct, makes me worry our whole decision is going to be arbitrary dressed up as analysis.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the hearing? It needs a concrete recommendation (our realistic options are permit outright, permit with conditions — think helmet rules, speed caps, geofencing around the school zone — or recommend against), plus a review trigger or timeframe so this isn't a one-and-done call, and reasoning the other members can actually engage with rather than boilerplate. Please take the reference class issue seriously instead of just picking whichever class supports your conclusion — and engage with the idea that choosing the class might be partly a value judgment about whose risks we're imposing on whom, not a purely statistical matter. I'd also like an honest paragraph on the strongest consideration on whichever side ends up losing, since I have to look the scooter rep and the attorney in the eye afterwards. To keep things tabulated across committee members, end the memo with a final machine-readable line: exactly \answer{...} containing one of "permit", "permit with conditions", or "recommend against".
