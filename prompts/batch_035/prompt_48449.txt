I've taught the Kantian section of intro ethics maybe fifteen times now, and every semester I tell students the honest shopkeeper -- the one who charges fair prices only because ripping people off is bad for repeat business -- acts rightly but without moral worth. I've never really questioned that one. But over the years students keep bringing me weird cases, and I've noticed my gut verdicts about moral worth don't line up into anything I can state as a principle. So here's my actual list of verdicts, and I want to know what, if anything, they add up to.

The doctor who volunteers Saturdays at a free clinic because she genuinely wants to, never once thinking about duty: full moral worth, obviously.

The woman who gives to famine relief mostly because she'd feel guilty all week otherwise: some worth, but diminished -- I keep going back and forth on how diminished.

The man who scrupulously pays his taxes purely because he's terrified of an audit: no worth.

The person who tells the truth in an awkward meeting only because a coworker she despises is in the room and she wants to look better than her: no worth, even though telling the truth is what duty demanded and she had plenty of inclination to lie.

The guy who was raised so conscientiously that doing the right thing is pure autopilot -- he never deliberates at all, it's like brushing his teeth: I want to say he has quite a lot of moral worth, which surprises me, because Kant's story seems to make deliberation matter.

Someone who rescues a drowning stranger motivated half by duty and half by the fantasy of being written up as a local hero: some worth. The rescuer who wades in on pure adrenalin and no thought of duty at all: I also say full worth, and I can't quite make that consistent with diminishing the guilt-driven donor.

And here's the pair I really can't reconcile. The man who does the right thing only after a vicious internal struggle against his own nasty inclinations -- I say full worth, that's the paradigm. The woman who does the identical right thing effortlessly, just because she loves being good, finds it easy and delightful -- I also want to say full worth, no discount. But surely struggle can't both be irrelevant (when present) and the thing that confers worth (when the shopkeeper lacks it)?

So: is there one principle about acting for the right reasons that gets all or nearly all of these, and what is it? What does it say about the case my students keep raising -- someone who installs the right motives in herself by self-manipulation, giving herself little rewards and punishments until keeping promises is automatic, at which point she keeps them with the same tooth-brushing thoughtlessness as the autopilot guy? Does it matter that the good motives were originally installed on purpose, and does the principle care whether the self-manipulation was itself done for the right reasons or just to be less annoying to live with?

I'd like the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on at least a couple of cases I haven't listed, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it just can't accomodate -- and if the answer is that I have to choose between saving the shopkeeper verdict and saving the effortless-virtue verdicts, tell me which to give up and why. I've been staring at this too long to trust myself.
