I'm teaching a philosophy of mind seminar this term and for the last unit I want to pair Dennett's late-career stuff on counterfeit people with Blade Runner (the 1982 one, if the cut matters). Dennett's line, roughly, is that once you can manufacture something that passes as a person, you've created a counterfeit — a kind of fraud — and that being taken in by it doesn't obligate you to anything: the counterfeit isn't owed rights just because the impersonation is convincing, and the people who build it should be liable for the deception. He was writing about chatbots, but the film is almost a thought experiment for his position, and I can't tell if it supports him or demolishes him, which is why I want a careful reading.

On Dennett's own definition, Roy Batty and the other replicants look like counterfeit people in exactly his sense: manufactured entities engineered to pass as human, whose claim to moral standing is, on his view, a con. Except the whole moral arc of the film pulls the other way — Roy comes back for Deckard on the rooftop, delivers his monologue about the things he's seen that will be lost, and the film treats extending him concern as the obvious right response, not as getting suckered. So my question: is Blade Runner just making the mistake Dennett warns about, or does it expose a gap in his argument? Some angles I'd want covered: the replicants aren't impersonating anyone to defraud them — they're trying to survive, and mostly they're trying not to be identified, which seems to change what the counterfeiting amounts to; and the Voight-Kampff test presupposes an empathy-based criterion of personhood that the film then shows failing in both directions (humans in that world are cold, Roy shows more compassion than anyone). Does that break Dennett's intentional-stance story, or was he never relying on an empathy test in the first place?

There's also the biology question I keep going back and forth on with a colleague: the replicants aren't digital fakes, they're engineered organisms — grown, embodied, with real memories (implanted, in Rachael's case, but experientially real to her). Dennett seems to draw the genuine/counterfeit line partly in terms of origins and design intent rather than substrate, which I actually find the most interesting and least discussed part of his view. Does the film pressure-test that line or quietly endorse it? And a last one for my students, who keep wanting to jump straight to "so is ChatGPT a person": what does Blade Runner add to the AI-rights debate that the arguments alone don't — is there something the dramatization does that the essays can't? I'd like a reading where Dennett could plausibly win and one where the film does, and then your honest verdict. No need for scene-by-scene citations from the script, I know the film well, I just want the philosophy taken as seriously as the movie.
