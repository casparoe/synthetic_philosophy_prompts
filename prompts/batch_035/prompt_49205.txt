Every fall I run a session of our department's open philosophy-of-time reading group (undergrads, a few grad students, assorted townies who like arguing) and this year it's future contingents -- the sea battle argument. The participants always show up with two feelings they think are just one feeling: "it's not determined yet whether there'll be a sea battle tomorrow" and "it's not true yet that there'll be a sea battle tomorrow". So I want a decision-tree handout, one page, that sorts people into positions by cases rather than by labels.

Everyone enters the tree by conceding that past- and present-tensed claims are either true or false (they'll all grant that, I'll make sure of it). After that, every node should be a question about a concrete situation someone with no background can answer: a fair coin I'll flip tomorrow, a storm system that has already formed out over the ocean and may or may not hit us Friday, a general who has secretly made up her mind about the attack but hasn't told her staff, whether it still makes sense for the generals on the other side to deliberate tonight, and, if you can fit it in without the whole thing collapsing into a theology seminar, God knowing on Monday how the battle goes. The question wording has to stay plain -- no "bivalence", no "soft fact", no "branching" in the node questions themselves, those words can only appear at the leaves.

Leaves should include: the straight denial of bivalence for future contingents (the standard reading of Aristotle, and the Lukasiewicz-style version), supervaluationism about the open future, Ockhamism (the battle sentence is already true or false, but that's a soft fact about now that settles nothing), the thin red line view, the growing block if it earns a spot, and honest-to-god fatalism as the leaf nobody should want but the tree needs for completeness. Keep it to around ten nodes or fewer so it fits on the page.

Two extra asks, and I'll admit these are the real point. First, mark any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't seperate with honest questions -- I suspect Ockhamism vs. the thin red line is basically untestable by cases, and I'd rather you flag that than invent a question that pretends to distinguish them. Second, point out any consistent path through the tree that leads nowhere: no named position occupies that combination of answers, and say whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it. I want the handout to teach the logical space, not just hand out name tags.
