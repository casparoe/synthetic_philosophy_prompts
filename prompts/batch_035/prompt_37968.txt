I'm contributing a short piece to a volume on the ethics of digital minds, and my angle is what Derek Parfit would have made of s-risks. Parfit died in January 2017, just as these debates were taking shape: s-risks are risks of outcomes containing suffering on an astronomical scale — a future in which vast numbers of digital minds, far cheaper to create and run than biological ones, are generated in conditions of severe suffering, or in which competition between powerful AI systems treats their sentience as an externality. He knew of and cared about existential risk, but the systematic treatment of suffering-risk came after his death. Act as a professional philosopher with expert command of Parfit's corpus, from Reasons and Persons through On What Matters to his late, unfinished work on population ethics. Think carefully and sketch the relevant parts of his views before committing to anything; then give exactly five numbered claims, each one or two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion, about the positions he would likely take on s-risks. Anchor each claim in something he actually argued — the Asymmetry, the Non-Identity Problem, his lifelong struggles with the Repugnant Conclusion, his views on the relative moral weight of suffering — and make at least one claim a point where you believe his existing tools would have left him genuinely uncertain.
