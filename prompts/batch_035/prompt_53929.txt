For the past several years I have moderated an argument between two camps over the moral status of what the sociological literature calls honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures. Camp One defends dignity-culture norms: every person has a worth that cannot be taken away by insult, grievances are best handled with forbearance or private correction rather than public campaigns, and offenders are to be corrected, not ruined. Camp Two defends what its critics call victimhood-culture norms: because dignity is often honored only on paper, moral seriousness about harm requires cultivating sensitivity to slight, encouraging the injured to appeal to third parties, and treating victimhood as a claim on institutional remedy rather than something to absorb. Each camp considers the other's regime a moral degradation — one a culture of fragility and manufactured grievance, the other a culture of callousness dressed up as resilience.

I want the joint statement they could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration — the document they should have written instead of the mutual caricatures. Please produce it with the following structure:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These must be claims both camps could actually put their names to, covering at minimum: the descriptive facts about how the three regimes assign moral status and handle grievances; what each regime gets right, as conceded by the opposing camp; and any shared value commitments (e.g., about cruelty, proportionality, or the treatment of the vulnerable). Do not pad this section with platitudes — every claim should be one that a partisan of either camp might initially have resisted.

2. A disagreements table. Each row states one issue on which the camps remain apart, with two columns: the formulation of Camp One's position that Camp One would accept as fair to its own view, and the corresponding formulation for Camp Two. Wording that either camp would reject as slanted is a failure of this section.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one camp to revise. I suspect — but leave this to your analysis — that at least one crux concerns the two failure cases the camps keep trading: the person for whom private forbearance means years of silent suffering with no remedy, and the fabricated or frivolous accusation that mobilizes third parties into disproportionate reputational destruction. Both camps must agree that a given case or argument bears on a crux before it can be listed, and for each such case you must record, in advance, what each camp predicts the case would show — including the prediction each camp makes about how often its own regime's failure case occurs, relative to the other's.

4. A section on the comparison to honor culture, which both camps usually treat as a defeated relic. Include the harder question neither camp likes: whether there are settings — communities where formal enforcement is absent and reputation is the only protection, or subcultures where deterrence through willingness to contest insult genuinely prevents predation — in which either camp would concede that honor norms are the least-bad available regime, and if so what that concession does to the claim that one's own preferred culture is morally superior simpliciter rather than superior-for-us.

5. A closing section separating verbal from substantive disagreements. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words — for example, over whether "morally superior" ranges over a whole culture's typical operation or its performance under favorable conditions, or over whether the standard typology conflates two independent dimensions (the source of moral status: contested reputation, fixed worth, or ascribed injury; and the mode of remedy: self-help, forbearance, or third-party appeal) such that real communities mix them — say which parts are verbal and why, and state what remains once the verbal residue is removed.

Throughout, the standard is exactness over eloquence: formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, cruxes that are genuinely load-bearing, and predictions specific enough to have come true or failed. The document should read as something two honest partisans could sign while still disagreeing — which is precisely what they have so far failed to produce.
