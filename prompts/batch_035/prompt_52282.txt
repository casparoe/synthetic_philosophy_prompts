A philosophy youtuber I watch did a video on Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and I took notes, but I have a feeling he garbled some of it. Here's my notes of his summary, can you tell me how much to trust?

He said the paper attacks two pillars of logical empiricism. The first dogma is the analytic/synthetic distinction: the idea that truths of math and logic are true purely by virtue of meaning, independent of fact, while synthetic truths depend on how the world is. The second dogma is radical reductionism, the Carnap-style project where every meaningful statement gets translated into statements about immediate sense experience. Quine's argument against the first dogma is that every attempt to explain "analytic" bottoms out in notions like synonmy, meaning, and necessity, which are just as obscure, so the whole cluster is defined circularly. For the second, he argues our statements face the tribunal of experience as a corporate body rather than one by one (he credits Duhem for this), so no individual statement has its own empirical content, and reductionism collapses. His conclusion, per the youtuber, is that the analytic/synthetic distinction is straight-up false and that there simply are no analytic truths. He also drew the picture of knowledge as a web of belief, with experience only touching the edges (math and logic being deep in the middle), and said Quine's upshot is that since any statement CAN be held true come what may, Quine is basically recommending we revise the laws of logic whenever physics gets awkward — like dropping excluded middle for quantum mechanics, which he said Quine was sympathetic to.

Some of this sounds right to me (the circularity point and the Duhem stuff especially), but a few things nag me. Did Quine really claim to prove there are no analytic truths, or was his conclusion more modest? And "any statement can be held true come what may" — is that Quine telling us to do that, or just describing a feature of confirmation? Also something felt off about how confidently he tied the web metaphor and the quantum logic thing to this specific paper. Could you go through the summary and separate what a Quine scholar would accept, what's a simplification, and what's actually a misrepresentation — and tell me whether the "therefore revise logic whenever physics gets awkward" conclusion actually follows from the parts he got right?
