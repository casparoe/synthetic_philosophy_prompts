My roommate got deep into online poker and has become the guy at dinner defending sunk costs. His move: decision theory 101 says money already in the pot is irrelevant, only future costs and payoffs matter -- but every real player talks about being pot committed. You've put in so many chips that folding feels insane, and he insists the pros are right to feel that way, it's not a leak in their thinking. Then he slides from poker to everything else: staying in a PhD program, a failing startup, a long marriage, the US in Vietnam. His line is basically "you're pot committed in your career too -- the pot is everything you've built, your skills, your network, your track record. Walking away means forfeiting the pot you paid into for years." He uses this to argue that the sunk cost "fallacy" is mostly a fallacy committed by economists, that honoring sunk costs is often rational, and the textbook rule is naive. Someone else at the table usually brings up the Concorde and dung flies at this point, so I know that whole backstory, don't need the museum tour.

I half buy it and half think it's a trick. Help me audit the analogy. Map out what corresponds to what: the pot, the chips you put in versus the chips the opponents put in, the bet you still have to call, the odds you're being offered, the opponents, the showdown. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. It seems relevant that in poker the pot is still winnable -- it's part of your future payoff, not actually sunk in the way your PhD years are -- and that an opponent literally built half the pot for you, whereas in life nobody is matching your years of effort with years of their own that you get to keep if you see it through. If anything the poker case seems to support the textbook view: the correct rule there is still pure forward-looking pot odds, and feeling "committed" is just a fast way of computing them. So is my roommate's own analogy quietly against him?

But then again: are there places where the picture genuinely transfers -- commitment effects, the fact that having persisted this long is evidence about the project's fit or your own information, signalling to future partners or employers, the way option value compounds in some projects -- so that honoring sunk costs does real rational work? Sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the conclusion he wants (staying in is rational) versus the pedantic ones. And at the end: does his conclusion survive once the analogy is gone, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If there's a better analogy for when honoring sunk costs is actually rational, I want that too -- something that keeps what's true in his poker case and drops what isn't.
