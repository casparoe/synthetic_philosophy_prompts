I'm a high school senior on our student council, and a thought experiment got out of hand. Our council decides things like whether to extend library hours or change the parking lottery by arguing for an hour and then raising hands, and I began to wonder what would happen if instead we let students wager play-money on which proposals would actually raise average attendance and exam scores, rewarded the bettors who turned out to be right, and adopted whatever the market favored. When I described this to a teacher, she told me that an economist named Robin Hanson had proposed something along these lines for real governments, under the name futarchy: the voters would choose the measure of national welfare that society commits to maximizing, and prediction markets would decide which policies will maximize it, with the government bound to enact whatever the market prices as best. I want to think carefully about whether a scheme like this could actually work, and before I read any further I want the specification first, because I notice the arguments I keep encountering are one-case-at-a-time — someone points to manipulation, someone else points to a prediction market that called an election correctly, and I can't tell which considerations are load-bearing.

So here is my request. Write the requirements any workable futarchic system would have to meet: the cases it must get right, the failure modes it must survive, and the distinctions it has to respect. Among the cases I most want covered are ones I have been chewing on. There is the problem of conditional bets: a contract that pays out only in the world where the policy is actually adopted, so that whenever a policy is unlikely to be chosen the potential payouts are diluted and hardly anyone bothers to trade, leaving the market thin exactly when a decision is pending. There is the saboteur, who bets against a policy and then works quietly to make it fail, so that the market was right about the outcome and the outcome was wrong about the policy. There is the majority choosing a welfare metric that quietly ignores a minority, and the related pressure where the thing being measured degrades under the effort to maximize it. There is the horizon problem, that the effects of serious policies arrive decades out while traders want their stakes settled this year. And there is the thought I keep circling, that manipulation is harmless noise to be profitably absorbed by informed traders only when the manipulator is betting on the outcome, and not when the manipulator is betting while also redefining the scoreboard or shaping the event itself. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it, and say which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of people who already like or dislike markets. Please commit: if you believe some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say which subset and why, since that is the part I most want to see; and tell me which single requirement, in your judgment, does the most to decide whether futarchy is viable at all. Don't spend much space scoring existing proposals against the list; the criteria are what I want, since the scoring will depend on knowing the proposals and the criteria are where the thinking is. And I would rather you took definite positions and defended them than laid out the options and left me to choose. One note on form: please write continuous prose, the way one writes an essay or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — I know this subject tempts everyone into lists, and I want to see whether the requirements still stand up when they have to be argued out in sentences.
