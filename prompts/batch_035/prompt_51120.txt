For a reading group on rational agency I've been asked to write up my considered positions on intention, so that the others can poke holes in them. Here they are. First, deciding to do something is sufficient for intending it: intention is directly under voluntary control, so that once you have decided, nothing further is needed for the decision to count as an intention. Second, intentions have a rational inertia — once formed, you should not reconsider them absent new information, even if the reasons for which they were formed have lapsed. Third, rational action is always supported by the balance of reasons at the time of the action: nothing about the past can make it rational to do now what there is now decisive reason against doing. Fourth, in Kavka's toxin puzzle (a billionaire will pay you a million at midnight if a brain scan shows that at that moment you intend to drink a mildly unpleasant toxin at noon the next day; the money is yours to keep either way, and you decide at noon whether to drink) I hold that a perfectly rational, fully self-aware agent cannot get the money, and that this is no defect in the agent or the theory — some payoffs are simply unreachable for such agents. Fifth, Ulysses having himself bound to the mast was rational, and this seems to me the same phenomenon the toxin puzzle isolates: rationally paying a cost in advance to constrain a later choice. Sixth, you can rationally intend to do something you believe you probably will not do, as when someone who has abandoned every prior diet still genuinely intends to stick to this one. Please audit this set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what would the least costly revision be? Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything, and be explicit about which single commitment, if revised, would clean up the most.
