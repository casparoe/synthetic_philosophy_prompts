I teach metaphysics and the philosophy of physics, and at last week's graduate seminar two of my doctoral students — Marta, who is writing a dissertation on Zeno, and Dev, whose background is in the philosophy of mathematics — spent the better part of an hour arguing about Thomson's lamp, the thought experiment from Thomson's 1954 paper in which a lamp is switched on at time zero, off at the one-minute mark, on again after a further half minute, off after a further quarter minute, and so on, each interval half the length of the one before, so that infinitely many switchings are completed exactly two minutes after the start. The question, famously, is whether the lamp is on or off at the two-minute mark. I chaired the session and deliberately stayed out of it; now that each of them has emailed me a summary of what they take their strongest point to have been, I find I am not comfortable simply declaring a winner, and I would value a careful outside adjudication.

Their exchange ran as follows. (1) Marta opened by claiming that the puzzle amounts to a proof of impossibility: at two minutes the lamp must, by excluded middle, be either on or off, and yet the sequence of its states (on, off, on, off, ...) has no limit, so nothing in the infinitely many switchings settles which it is; a scenario in which a perfectly well-defined physical question lacks a well-defined answer cannot describe a possible state of affairs, so no lamp can be toggled infinitely many times in a finite interval. (2) Dev, drawing on Benacerraf's 1962 reply to Thomson, accused her of confusing an incomplete description with an impossibility: everything the setup specifies concerns times strictly before two minutes, and it says nothing whatsoever about the state at two minutes. The description is silent rather than contradictory — it is consistent with the lamp's being on at two minutes, off at two minutes, or, for that matter, reduced to a puff of smoke — so the correct conclusion is that the question is underdetermined, not that the supertask cannot run. (3) Marta conceded the formal point but pressed further: descriptions can be incomplete, but the world cannot be; if such a lamp existed it would be determinately on or off at two minutes, so all Benacerraf shows is that the thought experiment fails to describe any one possible world completely, which leaves entirely open whether such a world exists — and that, she insisted, is a question for physics (whether a switch can toggle ever faster without limit) rather than for logic. (4) Dev replied that this mislocates the lesson: Thomson's point was precisely that we have no settled method for determining what holds once a supertask is complete, and the lamp exhibits this — continuity reasoning succeeds for some quantities, since the switch's position, with its ever-shrinking excursions, does converge to a limit, while it fails for others, since on/off has no limit — so which limits exist is not something the bare mathematics of the situation dictates. (5) Marta's closing rejoinder was that if one takes the shrinking excursions seriously, the physical description converges after all: the switch comes to rest somewhere, and a real switch at rest in a definite position either closes the circuit or breaks it, so on a physically realistic lamp the underdetermination dissolves — it is an artifact of treating on/off as an abstract alternating series rather than as a mechanism.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right only under some qualification, and justify each verdict briefly. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether there is anything of substance that both of them missed. I would rather you not split the difference for the sake of diplomacy; if one of them is winning on points, I should like to be told so plainly.
