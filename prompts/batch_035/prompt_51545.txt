so i'm a novelist, and my third book just came back from my editor with a big problem. it's narrated by a genuinely bad man -- charming, funny, and over the course of the book he does something unforgivable to someone who trusts him, and he never fully owns it. i wrote him that way on purpose. my editor liked the book but says i'm asking readers for something she thinks doesn't come apart the way i think it does: she's very fond of this idea that reading a novel is like forming a friendship. you spend hours with this voice, it becomes intimate and one-directional, you come to know its way of seeing -- and friendships, she says, change you, and you're responsible for the company you keep. so a book that makes you enjoy the company of a bad man, that lets you feel what it's like to be him from the inside, is corrupting the reader in a way a book about a good man isn't. she's half-suggesting i revise so the narrative itself punishes him more clearly.

i want to actually think this through instead of just defending my draft. can you audit the friendship analogy for me? what maps onto what -- the friend is the narrator? the author? the book itself? is reading really the friendship or more like eavesdropping on someone's diary? where does the mapping break -- a friend can betray you, reciprocate, notice you; whatever it is in the book can't do any of that. and which of the breaks matter for her conclusion and which don't? i suspect the analogy sneaks in things about agency and reciprocity that a voice on a page just doesn't have, but maybe i'm the one being sneaky. also tell me whether her conclusion -- enjoyably bad narrators are a real moral problem for readers -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if friendship is the wrong picture, what would you put in its place? a mirror, a mask, a long train ride next to a stranger who never shuts up? sorry for ramling, the book is due in six weeks.
