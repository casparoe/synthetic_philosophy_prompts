I'm taking a political philosophy course this term, and the professor teaching it has a maddening habit: she refuses to state her theory of freedom outright and instead just issues verdicts on whatever cases we bring to seminar, daring us to reconstruct the view underneath. A few of us have started keeping a log. So far it reads: the slave whose master is indulgent and has never once blocked anything he wanted to do — not free; the subjects of a dictator who in thirty years has never restricted a single person's choices — not free; the wife whose husband holds the legal power to control her every move but grants her every wish — not free; the man who can't afford a train ticket to see his dying mother — free, and she was emphatic that he is unfortunate rather than unfree; the shopkeeper who pays protection money monthly after the mob broke one window — not free; and the citizen who is taxed and regulated under laws she despises but can vote, campaign, and litigate against — free. Her running gloss on the first three is that it's not about what he does but about what he could do, and on the last that what matters is that the law is yours to contest.

Next week two of us get to bring new cases, and I'd like to walk in with predictions. Case A: Mira drives for a delivery platform. The algorithm assigns her routes and can deactivate her account at any time, without stated reasons and without appeal; in the platform's eight years in her city it has never once deactivated a driver without cause, and her pay has gone up steadily. She can quit tomorrow, but her ratings and route knowledge are worthless elsewhere, so quitting means a large pay cut. She has also, I notice, stopped contesting unfair ratings and accepts every surge assignment. Case B: Devin drives a licensed taxi. A municipal board can revoke his license, but only for enumerated violations, after a hearing, with judicial review — and it did revoke his license once, two years ago, over a paperwork error that took him four months to fix.

Will she call Mira free or unfree? What about Devin? Honestly, I care less about the answers than about the rule she's implicitly running — whether it's republican non-domination in something like Pettit's sense, a broadened negative liberty on which standing threats and living at another's mercy count even without actual interference, or some third view that fits her record just as well. Note that her verdicts on the first three cases seem to rule out any view requiring actual interference, and her verdict on the poor man rules out counting mere inability as unfreedom, so I want to know what actually survives. Please organize your reply in four sections, in this order: Candidate rules — the views consistent with her six verdicts so far and what each predicts for Mira and Devin (about 250 words); Predictions — your best guesses for both, with the reasoning (about 150 words); Best fit — which rule you'd bet on and why (about 100 words); and The case that would settle it — the single further case we should bring to seminar to pull the surviving rules apart, and what each rule would say about it (about 100 words).
