After a career in endocrinology I have lately been catching up on philosophy of mind, and a popular science book I just finished argues roughly this: emotions are, at bottom, perceptions of bodily change, so we do not tremble because we are afraid but are afraid because we tremble; because these perceptions are just interoceptive sensations, emotions are feelings all the way down, which leaves no room for cooler states like pride or relief to count as genuine emotions; the theory establishes that emotion and cognition are separate systems, so an entity without a body — or with its bodily feedback loop severed — could not fear anything at all; and appraisal theories that analyze emotions as judgments about one's situation have been decisively refuted by experiments in which manipulating facial expression shifts reported feeling. The physiological part appeals to my old instincts, but the sweeping parts bother me. Which of these claims would a careful philosopher of emotion accept, which conflate distinct positions, and do the sweeping conclusions survive once the conflations are untangled? In particular, does a bodily-perception theory really entail that emotions are nothing but feelings, and can facial-feedback experiments refute judgment theories outright, or only complicate them?
