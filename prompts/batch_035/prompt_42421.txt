I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the deflationary theories of truth, and I also help construct prompts for an evaluation dataset on philosophical topics. Most existing material on deflationism is either textbook regurgitation or vague gestures at "post-truth" politics, so I want something that rewards a model for actually understanding the position — and that can be completed entirely from internal knowledge, with no citations or literature lookup.

Write a satirical press release, roughly 500-650 words, issued by a university's Office of Communications, announcing that after a comprehensive strategic review the Department of Philosophy is "streamlining" its Truth portfolio: the Division of Substantive Truth Research is being eliminated, its investigators reassigned, and its remaining duties consolidated under a part-time disquotation clerk. The register should be deadpan institutional-corporate throughout — buzzwords about efficiency, redundancy, and right-sizing — and the irony must stay legible without any winking.

The satirical target is deflationism itself, and the humor must come from getting the view right rather than caricaturing it. At minimum, the piece should include:

- A joke turning on the idea that truth has no underlying nature or essence to investigate, so the research program had nothing left to study — the joke being that the deflationists consider this a discovery, not a failure.
- A joke about the equivalence schema: each instance (e.g., that "snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white) says everything there is to say, so the department's new clerk's job consists of instances and nothing else.
- A joke about the expressive or generalizing function of the truth predicate — the reason we need it at all is to say things like "everything in the committee's report is true" without reciting the report — perhaps framed as a workflow efficiency the review discovered.
- A quote from a distraught correspondence theorist (invented, never a named real philosopher) insisting that something must make true sentences true, and the deflationist chair's reply that this complaint appears to be an instance of the schema restated in an anxious tone of voice.
- An "unresolved workplace incident" involving the Liar sentence, which the restructuring was never designed to address and which HR is handling badly. Here the satire should be fair to the deflationist: the paradox is a genuine problem for the view, and the press release should not pretend the review solved it.

Constraints: the piece must remain internally consistent from the deflationist side — the chair may not slip into conceding that truth is, after all, a deep substantive property when the paradox shows up, unless that slip is itself clearly marked as the joke. A deflationist reading the piece should nod along and find the exaggerations ones they'd happily own; never attribute to them a position they would disown. Puns on "inflationary" theories of truth are welcome but should not carry the whole piece. The result should be readable to a bright undergraduate who has taken one course in philosophy of language, while rewarding a specialist who knows exactly which claims are doing the work. Use no real philosophers' names and no references to specific publications.
