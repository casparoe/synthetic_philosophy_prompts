I've been reading advocates of what's sometimes called the hedonistic imperative — the project of abolishing suffering altogether through germline engineering, designer drugs, and related biotechnology — and I keep running into a public-health analogy that seems to be doing a lot of argumentative work. Suffering, on this framing, is a disease: it has a pathophysiology, it spreads through lineages by inheritance, and the abolition project is an eradication campaign — the moral equivalent of the smallpox program, which drove a human pathogen to zero and was certified complete in 1980. The analogy is deployed against two objections at once. Against "it's utopian": we took smallpox from millions of annual cases to extinction with 1960s technology, so nobody should call the phase-out of suffering infeasible. Against "it's presumptuous": we don't regard smallpox eradication as an assault on the bodily autonomy of the infected, so we shouldn't regard reprogramming the reward architecture of future generations as an assault on their identity.

One writer I came across pushes the analogy into territory I hadn't seen before. Since the smallpox endgame succeeded by surveillance and containment — chasing the last endemic cases village by village rather than mass-vaccinating the whole planet — he argues the endgame of abolition should likewise be targeted: seek out the remaining "reservoirs" of suffering (chronic pain syndromes, the worst affective states in factory-farmed animals, the most suffering-dense wild populations) and contain them there, rather than re-engineering every sentient organism on earth. He even suggests a certification criterion modeled on the smallpox one: some year in which no uncontained episode of severe suffering occurs anywhere in the biosphere.

Please audit the analogy thoroughly. I'd like an explicit correspondence table first — what plays the role of the pathogen, the reservoir, the vector, the vaccine, the last endemic case, and the certification of eradication — and then a separation of the disanalogies that matter for the analogy's intended uses from the ones that don't. Some candidates worth weighing: smallpox was an external replicant with a discrete genetic identity and universal human agreement that it was bad, whereas suffering is a functional capacity of nervous systems that natural selection built precisely because it does things; eradicating a virus leaves the human organism as it was, whereas eradicating suffering rewrites the motivational and affective phenotype of every future sentient being; and whether there is any well-defined sense in which suffering has "cases" that can be counted and "reservoirs" that can be ring-fenced, as the surveillance-and-containment extension requires.

Then give me your verdict, and commit to it rather than laying out the options: does each of the two licensed conclusions — feasibility and moral cleanliness — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does the targeted endgame the writer derives from the analogy follow from it at all, or is it the one part of the program the analogy cannot carry? If you think a different public-health comparison (endemic rather than epidemic disease, or something from outside public health entirely) would track the abolition project better, say which one you'd substitute and why it maps more faithfully.
