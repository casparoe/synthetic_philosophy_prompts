I write about science and ideas for a living, mostly physics-adjacent stuff, and I've been working on a piece about what people mean when they insist probability isn't just in our heads — objective chance, the real thing. My favorite source on this is a philosopher of science I've known since grad school, and instead of letting her lecture me I've started collecting her snap verdicts on little cases, which work as a great shorthand for her whole view (she says it's basically Lewis's Principal Principle plus whatever patches it needs to survive). Her record so far: a textbook fair coin, credence obviously a half; a die she knows is physically biased toward six at about a fifth, credence a fifth; a casino wheel with a posted sign claiming the probability of red is a half, which she has personally measured as biased, she says forget the sign and match the measured bias; a spin experiment in a Bohmian universe, where the physics is fully deterministic but the theory still assigns Born-measure a half to spin-up, and she says her credence is a half anyway; and a card already dealt and lying face down, where someone insists to her that the objective chance it's the ace of spades is 1/52 — there she says no chance, the event is settled, the chance is 0 or 1 and the 1/52 is just ordinary ignorance dressed up, so no deference.

Next week I want to spring a case on her that I've been saving: a genuinely decretalist world, where a god simply decrees that the chance of some event A is 0.7, and the decree is what makes it so — no dynamics behind it, no frequencies, the stipulation itself is constitutive of the chance. Apparently some people really have defended views in this neighborhood, so it's not just me making stuff up. My question: does she defer, i.e. set her credence to 0.7? Predict her verdict. Then tell me which rule(s) actually fit her track record so far — I can squint and see at least two or three, and I'm fairly sure they disagree about the decree — say what each one predicts for the new case, give me your best guess with your reasons, and suggest the single further case that would best split the surviving rules apart. My editor wants to end the piece on the decretalist bit, so I kind of need to know whether it's a genuine fault line in her view or a dud that her answer to the Bohmian case already settles.
