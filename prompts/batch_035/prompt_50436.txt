I write for the books-and-ideas section of a national newspaper, and every publishing season brings at least one author claiming to have found a law of history: that inequality cycles drive state collapse on a predictable schedule, that demographic decline dooms welfare states by a certain decade, that a technological transition makes a particular political order inevitable. My editors want me to choose quickly which of these deserve a serious review, which deserve a debunking, and which deserve nothing, and I'd like to stop deciding by vibes. Karl Popper's critique of historicism — the claim that there can be no laws of historical development and hence no large-scale predictions about the future of society — is the obvious framework, but it's usually stated at a level of generality that doesn't help me at a pitch meeting.

Please write a decision procedure I can actually run on a book or paper of this kind, in under an hour, before I pitch. It should walk me through the questions in order: what the author is actually predicting (a universal law, an unconditional prophecy, a conditional projection from a trend, or a self-referential prediction that may alter its own subject matter), what evidence could count against it, whether the author hedges in ways that drain the claim of content, and what the author's track record with falsification looks like. For each question, say what counts as a pass and where the procedure should stop with a verdict of "prophecy, decline to review" or "conditional projection, review as such." Mark explicitly the points where the question is one of judgment or of values rather than fact — for instance whether a prediction being unfalsifiable in practice is a reason not to cover it, given that unfalsifiable claims sometimes sell and get debated.

Two things I need beyond the checklist. First, take a committed position on what Popper's argument, correctly understood, actually licenses: does it forbid all long-range social prediction, or only predictions of a specific logical shape — unconditional statements about the future course of development as a whole — leaving room for conditional forecasts, technological predictions, and the kind of structural-demographic modeling that claims to have anticipated recent political instability? Don't give me both readings and let me choose; pick one and defend it, and let that position shape the procedure. Second, apply the procedure to three cases: a cliodynamic model predicting a decade of political instability from structural-demographic variables; a dated prediction that machine intelligence will exceed human intelligence within a specific number of years; and a UN-style population projection to 2100. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict I won't like — I confess I find the cliodynamic work interesting and would prefer it to come out well — and say honestly where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Keep the whole answer to 800 words. The procedure should be usable by a colleague with no philosophy of science background, so translate Popper's distinctions into plain editorial terms, but don't quietly drop the distinction between a law and a trend — that one has to survive the simplification, since it's where most of these books cheat.
