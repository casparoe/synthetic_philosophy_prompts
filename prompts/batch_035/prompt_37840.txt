I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, applying for a small internal award — a term-long, £2,500 scheme meant to fund a self-contained side project before the candidate returns to the thesis. The half-formed idea comes out of a reading group on collective action problems. Members kept using "race to the bottom", "tragedy of the commons", "arms race" and "multipolar trap" more or less interchangeably, but the cases seem to come apart in at least one respect. In some of them, each agent's choice makes the others worse off in absolute terms (overfishing, or fiscal competition eroding a tax base everyone relied on). In others, the harm is purely positional: everyone would be better off if all remained where they were, yet no one is worse off than in a world where nothing happened at all — think of elaborate courtship displays in nature, or advertising wars that simply reshuffle a fixed pool of customers. My suspicion is that the escape routes differ across these two kinds of case: absolute-harm cases yield to familiar coordination devices, whereas positional ones may be structurally inescapable, or escapable only by changing what the agents are optimising rather than by getting them to coordinate on what they already want. I'd like to know whether that split is real, whether it carves the terrain more usefully than the existing grab-bag vocabulary, and what follows for the deep pessimism one often encounters in commentary on races to the bottom — the sense that these traps are fated rather than merely unfortunate.
