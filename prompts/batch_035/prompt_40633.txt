At my decision-theory reading group we spent last Thursday evening on the absent-minded driver, and two members, Daniela and Felix, argued for two hours without either giving ground. I took notes and stayed out of it, but I would like to know who was right, and about what. Let me set the problem up, then give you the exchange as it happened, then tell you the format I want the answer in.

The problem: a driver's route home passes two intersections that look completely identical; by the time he reaches one, he can never remember whether he has already passed the other. At each intersection he can exit or continue. Exiting at the first is worth 0 to him; exiting at the second is worth 4; continuing past both leaves him on the long highway loop home, worth 1. Because the intersections feel identical, any procedure he uses at one he uses at the other, but he can randomize — say, by drawing from a bag of marbles. Assume risk-neutral expected-value maximization throughout.

They agreed on the ex ante arithmetic: a plan of continuing with probability p at each intersection is worth 4p(1−p) + p², which is maximized at p = 2/3 for an expected payoff of 4/3, beating always-continue (1) and always-exit (0). The dispute was over what this calculation is worth to the actual driver.

Felix opened: everything the driver will ever know at an intersection he already knows before starting out, so at the wheel there is nothing to update and nothing to decide. He should do all his reasoning in the armchair, fill a bag with two continue-marbles and one exit-marble, and treat himself at the intersections as a mere executor of the draw. The ex ante calculation is the only calculation, and 4/3 is simply what he gets.

Daniela objected that the wheel's-eye view has its own arithmetic. If the 2/3 plan is in force and he finds himself at an intersection, the odds he is at the second one are 2/5, since he reaches a second intersection only if he continued at the first. So exiting on the spot is worth (2/5)·4 = 8/5, which is more than the 4/3 the whole journey was projected to be worth. A plan under which his actual situation at the wheel is worth less than impulsively exiting looks broken precisely where it matters.

Felix called the 8/5 a conditioning illusion: the only driver not at an intersection is one who already exited for 0, so of course the intersections look lucrative. More basically, there is no action of exiting here iff this is the second one — he cannot tell them apart. Any procedure he could apply at the wheel he could have written into the plan at home, and the best such plan is 2/3. And if overruling the draw tempts him at one intersection, it tempts him at both, since they feel the same — so the procedure of overruling and exiting is just always-exit, worth 0.

Daniela replied that this assumes deviations must be all-or-nothing, when the driver can swap plans rather than abandon them. Standing at an intersection, he can commit, then and there, to a new probability q of continuing — and whatever reasoning fixes q now is the same reasoning that fixed it at the first intersection, if there was one, since it is the same state with the same information. So the odds he is at the second intersection are q/(1+q), not 2/5. If this is the second intersection, following q from here is worth 4 − 3q; if it is the first, following q here and again at the next one is worth 4q − 3q². Weighted by those odds, the wheel's-eye value of settling on q is (8q − 6q²)/(1+q), which is maximized at q ≈ 0.53 and is worth about 1.67 from the wheel — better, from the wheel, than the 8/5 his 2/3 plan is worth from there. Granted, ex ante q ≈ 0.53 is worth only about 1.27, less than 4/3; but he is not ex ante, he is at the wheel, and the wheel's-eye optimum should govern.

Felix closed: then a driver who can predict his own wheel's-eye reasoning should pre-commit against it — seal the bag, write the plan down, make the executor's job mechanical. If the interim perspective is genuinely rational, the ex ante optimizer should be able to price it in; if it cannot be priced in coherently, that is a reason to think it confused, not a second rationality. Either way, lock the wheel and take the 4/3.

Neither would budge. Please adjudicate, in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Arithmetic check (roughly 150 words): verify each computation in the exchange — the ex ante optimum, the 8/5, and Daniela's wheel's-eye optimum together with both of its claimed values — and flag any error you find.
2. Verdicts, move by move (roughly 350 words): a short verdict on each of the five moves in the order they were made, 2–4 sentences each, saying what is right, what is wrong, and what is equivocal in the move as actually made.
3. What both are missing (roughly 120 words): the one issue or distinction neither of them raises that most changes how the dispute should be seen.
4. Bottom line (roughly 100 words): what the absent-minded driver should actually do, and why.

Judge the exchange on its own terms: general decision-theoretic ideas are fair game if they help, but do not bring in outside literature by name, and do not suggest readings.
