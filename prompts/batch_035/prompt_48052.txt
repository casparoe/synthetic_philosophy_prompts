Wikipedia lets anyone contribute, but only under a strict rule: every claim in an article must be footnoted to a published source, and editors may not add facts from their own observation, expertise, or argument — what the site calls original research is barred. Wikipedia itself takes no position on whether the cited sources are true; it cares only that they exist and count as reliable, where reliability is decided by community norms and lists maintained through editorial discussion rather than by further citation. The chain has to end somewhere in published material outside the encyclopedia, and the encyclopedia is itself publishable material — editors periodically discover sources whose claims trace back to Wikipedia articles, closing the loop.

What theory of justification does this machinery presuppose, if any? Reconstruct its implicit answer to the regress problem: does the practice require a foundationalist picture on which certain sources terminate justification without needing support themselves, is it better read as coherentist — a large network of sources and editors propping each other up, with the loop-closing cases revealing coherentism's cost — or does the indefinite extensibility of footnote chains amount to a working infinitism? Distinguish what the citation rules strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, identify where the practice is underdetermined among the theories, and say what concretely would have to change if the implicit theory were swapped for one of its rivals — for instance, what an explicitly foundationalist encyclopedia, or a proudly coherentist one, would do differently about which sources may anchor a claim and about what happens when the loop closes.
