i work at a mid-size AI lab (ops side, not research, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabularly) and something about how we handle safety has been bugging me for months. On one track, a whole team does what they call alignment work: RLHF, constitution-style training, carefully curating the feedback so the model internalizes the right values — the framing is that we're shaping what the model wants, making it genuinely helpful and honest. On a completely seperate track, there's the safety systems people: they build monitors that read the model's outputs looking for scheming, tripwires that shut things down if the model does something weird, sandboxes, staged rollouts where a more capable model only gets to take real-world actions after weaker versions behaved for a while, and so on. Nobody on that second track ever seems to ask whether the model is good. It's all designed around the assumption that it might not be. So what view of the model — its mind, its goals, whatever — does each half of the practice presuppose? The alignment track seems to presuppose the model has something like values that can be installed; the control track seems to presuppose it has goals we can't observe and can't trust, which is a much darker view.

And here's the part that really confuses me. My manager, when I asked about this, said the control stuff is just like fire safety — we install sprinklers without believing the building wants to burn down. But that analogy feels off to me: the monitors are specifically watching for deception and goal-directed subversion, not hardware failure. Is the practice actually consistent — can you believe the model is basically a fancy autocomplete with no goals at all while also paying a team whose whole job is catching it in a lie? Or does running control measures quietly presuppose agential premises that contradict the tool-flavored story everyone tells about the model? And going the other direction, if you genuinely believed the alignment training worked, would any of the control apparatus still make sense, or would it be like running a fraud watch on a business partner you've decided is trustworthy — which seems to undermine the trusting?

So basically: reconstruct what each practice has to assume about the model's mind for it to be coherent, whether the two halves can be welded together under a single view, and if they can't, which one should give. I'm also curious where the whole thing is underdetermined — like, maybe several different theories of what the model is would license this exact same setup, and the labs are just not forced to pick one yet. That would explain why nobody at work seems bothered.
