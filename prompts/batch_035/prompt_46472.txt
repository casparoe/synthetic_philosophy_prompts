I am midway through my fourth novel and have become preoccupied, to the detriment of the actual writing, with a question I cannot settle: whether my characters could have had different origins, and whether whatever answer I give for them extends to me. I have written down my verdicts on a set of cases, and I should like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether some of them must simply go.

Here are the verdicts. Clara, my protagonist, could have been born in Lisbon rather than Vienna, raised by her aunt rather than her grandmother, and become a cartographer rather than a cellist: still Clara, and still the same character. If I had instead conceived the book about a child born to an entirely different couple in Oslo, I do not think I could have called that character Clara; she would have been someone new. When the hospital in the novel swaps two newborns and each is raised by the wrong family, I side with the child who came from the zygote and not the one who came from the cot: origins fix identity, rearing does not. So far, so consistent, and rather Kripkean, I believe.

But now the difficulties. My mother used to say that had her wedding been delayed by a fortnight, I should have been conceived a fortnight later, and I find I agree with her — that later child would still have been me — even though a fortnight's delay almost certainly means a different sperm and perhaps a different egg, in which case origin essentialism must be wrong about me. I also think my brother could have been conceived with a different sperm from my father — my parents tried for some months, after all — and still be my brother, whereas he could not have had a different father. And there are the twins in the second chapter, who came from one zygote that split: each, I feel, could have existed without the other, though I cannot see how a view on which the zygote is the origin allows this, since they share an origin exactly.

Then there is the matter peculiar to my trade. Clara did not, in fact, originate in the current novel: she first appeared, under another name, as a minor figure in a book I abandoned a decade ago. Yet I am sure she could have debuted in the present book without the abandoned one ever existing — so her actual origin is not essential to her. At the same time, if another novelist had written a book with a character answering to Clara in every detail, I would not say that character was Clara. Characters, it seems, are tied to their authors but not to their first appearances, while people, on my verdicts, are tied to their gametes and to nothing else I can name.

So: is there one principle here, or two, or none? Please state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can; motivate each clause by reference to a specific case above; say what the principle or principles predict for cases I have not listed — a frozen embryo stored for years before implantation, say, or a character carried over into a sequel written by my literary executor after my death — and be honest about which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated by any tolerably simple view, and which of them you would advise me to abandon. I would prefer that you respond in British English.
