hey! so quick context: im a journalist, I cover mostly science and ideas, lately a lot of AI stuff, and I've somehow gotten roped into running a workshop at a student conference on AI futures. 75 minutes, ~18 people, mix of grad students and sharp undergrads. they all know the prisoner's dilemma and basic expected value but no formal game theory. the conference theme this year is "agents that can bind themselves" and my session is on commitment races.

I want you to act as an expert game designer with a decision-theory background -- think someone who has actually run classroom games about bargaining and deterrence, not just theorized about them. Please think carefully before you design, and start with a short brainstorm of 2-3 candidate mechanics before committing (ha) to one.

here's the idea I need the game to make them FEEL, not just hear about: when agents racing against each other can make binding commitments -- irrevocable threats or promises, posted publicly before the race resolves -- there's pressure to commit EARLY because the first credible commitment can deter or shape everyone else's behavior. but committing early means committing with bad information: you lock in your threat before you know whether you're actually going to be the stronger side, and once it's locked, it's locked. so you can get outcomes where both sides have mutually committed to a fight, both would pay alot to unwind their own commitment, and nobody can. the bindingness is exactly what made the threat credible, and it's exactly what turns it into a trap. the deep point is the tradeoff between deciding earlier (more deterrence power, first-mover advantage) and deciding with better information (knowing who's actually going to win the race).

the game needs:

- roles for ~18 people (maybe 2 sides of ~9, or several small teams, your call -- justify it)
- a payoff scheme in points, no real money
- a physical/visible mechanic for commitments being public and IRREVOCABLE -- something like signing and flipping over a card you can't take back, whatever works
- some hidden information about relative strength / who's winning the race that only gets revealed AFTER commitments lock, and a round structure that forces people to choose between committing in an early round (blind) or waiting (better info, but maybe too late)
- an outcome where mutual committed conflict is reachable and the people in it viscerally wish they'd stayed flexible -- the "we locked ourselves into this" moment should be audible in the room

then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the idea (first-mover advantage, credibility-through-irrevocability, commitment as deterrence vs commitment as trap, the information problem). be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people not taking commitments seriously? a side folding instantly and deflating it? dominant strategy discovered in round one?) and how to patch it. and be frank about which part of the real commitment-race problem no classroom game can reproduce -- e.g. real racers are computational agents modelling each other in a loop, which humans around a table just can't do -- so I know what I have to supply verbally in the debrief.

structure your answer as: quick brainstorm -> chosen design with full rules -> round-by-round timeline for the 75 minutes -> debrief script -> what goes wrong and what the game can't do.

very last line, on its own, one sentence starting with "Bottom line:" summarizing what the game teaches.
