Our department has two people who've been at each other for years about causation by omission, and we finally convinced them to do an adversarial collaboration over the summer. One of them (call her the absences-are-causes person) thinks the gardener who doesn't water the plants really does cause them to die, full stop, and that the counterfactual dependence is enough. The other (the no-omissions person) thinks absences can't be causes because there's literally nothing there to do any causing — no event, no spatiotemporal location, no energy transfer — and that "the gardener caused the death" at best reports something else, like responsibility or a norm-violating default.

I've been drafted to write up the joint statement they'll both sign, except I'm bad at this, so you're doing it. Here's roughly what happened in their arguments, so you know the terrain. The no-omissions guy keeps pressing the profligacy problem: if the gardener's not watering caused the death, then so did the not-watering of every other human on the planet, since the flowers counterfactually depend on all of them too, and that's absurd. The absences person responds that yeah, causes are cheap, and ordinary language deals with the profligacy fine because of norms — the gardener was *supposed* to water them, some random guy in Osaka wasn't, so we only *say* the gardener caused it. The no-omissions guy then says that if which "causes" you pick out depends on what people were supposed to do, you've mixed the normative into the causal, and that shows you're not talking about causation at all anymore. There was also a long fight about whether absences have locations (where *is* the hole?) and whether that question even makes sense.
