So I do interpretability-adjacent work at a mid-size AI lab, and last week a colleague and I got into a long argument about whether sparse autoencoder features are "real" or just a useful lens we impose on the network. She's basically a realist: on her picture, a trained network has a finite-ish set of features that carve its computation at its joints, SAEs are discovering them roughly the way chemists discovered elements, and eventually we'll get something like a periodic table of what models compute. Another friend of mine, a physicist, says that's naive — any decomposition of a high-dimensional nonlinear system is underdetermined, you could pick other bases and get equally good abstractions, so calling any of them *the* network's representations is like a Ptolemaic astronomer getting attached to his epicycles. And now I can't stop turning it over, because they both seem obviously right when I'm talking to them.

Give me your credence that something like the realist picture is correct — that there's a fact of the matter about what features a trained network really computes, as opposed to many equally valid descriptions of the same computation — and decompose the reasoning. My own attempt at the decomposition, which you should fix if it's wrong: (a) the probability that training produces robust representations at all, rather than the network just being a messy pile of matrix multiplications that happen to work, (b) the probability that, given robust representations exist, they're actually identifiable — that there's a privileged decomposition rather than features being entangled or spread out in superposition in a way that makes any single basis somewhat arbitrary, and (c) the probability that our current methods, specifically, are tracking that privileged decomposition if it exists, versus just finding whichever features are easiest for us to find. Assign a number to each with a line of justification, say how they combine into your overall credence, and tell me which term you're least confident in.

One more thing I'd love your take on: if steering with a feature reliably produces the same effect across very differently trained models, does that push toward realism, or can the anti-realist just say that cross-model consistency shows training converges on similar functions without any particular decomposition being "the real one"? My credence keeps flip-flopping between roughly 0.4 and 0.75 depending on which friend I argued with most recently, which is embarassing, so I want to see the actual structure of the question laid out properly.
