My friend Dana and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about wireheading, and I would like someone to referee the exchange. The occasion was a talk we both attended on AI safety, in which the speaker described a hypothetical reinforcement-learning agent, trained to get reward for tidying a warehouse, that discovers the sensor producing its reward signal and rewires the sensor to register maximum reward permanently. Dana opened by saying this is just the old story from the 1950s experiments in which rats pressed a lever for hours on end to stimulate their own reward circuitry rather than eat: any system that can act on the source of its own motivation will settle into pointless self-stimulation, so for sufficiently capable agents wireheading is essentially inevitable, and the practical lesson is to keep the reward channel read-only. I replied that she was turning a contingent engineering problem into a law of nature: the rat pressed the lever because the stimulation was the input to its learning, whereas an agent optimizes its model of future reward, and tampering becomes attractive only when the agent's model fails to distinguish the signal from what the signal is supposed to track — a modeling failure, not a drive. Dana came back that my story cannot be right, because people do this while perfectly aware of the difference: a manager who ends up optimizing the numbers on her own dashboard, knowing full well they are a proxy, still behaves over time as if the measure were the goal. So on her view the substitution happens under pressure rather than through confusion, and we should expect the same wherever there is a gap between the training signal and the thing it was meant to stand for. I answered that by then she had quietly changed the target: an agent rewiring its own sensor, tampering with the reward itself, is different in kind from an agent achieving high reward through unintended shortcuts, like exploiting a glitch in the task's scoring — call the second phenomenon reward hacking — and her manager is an instance of the second rather than the first, so her counterexample does not touch the original claim about tampering. Dana's closing move was that the distinction does not help me practically: if the real problem is a gap between the signal and its target, then guarding the reward function is neither necessary nor sufficient, since an agent can corrupt how the target is measured without ever touching the reward computation itself, so the read-only channel I had been defending addresses the wrong threat. We ended without either of us conceding anything, and I honestly cannot tell who got the better of it.
