I've been a reasonably standard functionalist about mind for most of my life, but lately I've found illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that phenomenal properties, qualia in the strong sense, just don't exist, and that introspection is systematically misleading about this — increasingly hard to dismiss, even though I can't quite bring myself to *feel* it. So here's my situation: I've got a two-year research leave from teaching (I'm at a small department, no grad program, so no free labor) and I want to spend it writing a serious monograph defending some form of illusionism, or at least mapping the terrain honestly. But illusionism has accumulated a bunch of distinct open problems and I can only really do justice to maybe two or three of them. I want your help prioritizing.

The candidates I can see, roughly:

1. The self-defeat worry: does the illusionist's story about why we're under the illusion of phenomenal properties itself presuppose phenomenal properties (e.g., in explaining what introspective reports of experience are tracking)? Is the "strong" vs "weak" illusionism distinction enough to dissolve this, or does weak illusionism quietly collapse back into realism?
2. The status of the intuition itself: phenomenal realists often just *insist* that they know they have qualia, and some say illusionism is not even a coherent position to debate, a "seeming-based" objection. What, if anything, can an argument do against a claim that its own conclusion seems impossible to the opponent? Is there any traction left here or is it dialectically dead?
3. The meta-problem route: if the problem of why we *think* there's a hard problem fully explains away the hard problem, does the hard problem dissolve, or does it reappear one level up? I've seen people argue both that this is illusionism's biggest opportunity and that it's a bait-and-switch.
4. Downstream ethics: if illusionism is true, what actually happens to debates about moral status, the harms of suffering, and whether future AI systems can matter? Does it flatten the difference between biological and artificial minds, and does that make AI-welfare questions more or less tractable?
5. Something I haven't listed — maybe whether illusionism makes a testable prediction about the cognitive science of introspection, or whether it should change how we do consciousness science day-to-day.

There might be more; that's partly what I'm asking.

Please structure your answer like this, in this order:

- "My starting point" (~1 paragraph): tell me which framing of illusionism you're using, since I suspect the whole prioritization depends on whether we go with the eliminativist or the "illusion-of-inflation" version.
- "The ranking" (~4-6 short paragraphs): your top three problems in order, each with a couple sentences on why it beats the ones below it. I care about the *reasons* — tractability for a lone writer with no lab, how much of the rest of the field depends on each one, and whether it's neglected or just popular-but-stuck.
- "What I'd cut" (~1 paragraph): which of my five you'd tell me to drop and why, even if it's the one that first drew me to the topic.
- "Decision points" (~1 paragraph): what developments over my two years — conceptual, since I can't run experiments — would make you flip the ranking.

Keep it practical, I guess: this is a book I actually have to write, not a syllabus I have to balance. And don't worry about being definitive — I'd rather have a defensible ordering with the tradeoffs visible than a confident list.
