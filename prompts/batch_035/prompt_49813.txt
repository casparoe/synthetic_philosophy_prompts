So I'm on the board of a Korean weekend school here in the states (the kind where kids go on Saturdays to keep up the language), and I got tasked with writing up an opinion on a textbook fight before our next meeting, and honestly I could use the help writing it. Quick background: we teach a history class for the middle/high schoolers, and the textbook we've used forever is honestly kind of dry -- kids find it boring and the Korean printing is dated. One of our board members found a translated edition of a textbook originally published by a Japanese company for international schools, and it's gorgeous: great maps, primary source boxes, actual design. The problem is some of the wording on the colonial period. Where our current book says things plainly (forced labor, the comfort women system), the new book softens everything -- it says workers were "brought to Japan to support industry," it describes the comfort women as "women who were recruited to work near military facilities," and it refers to annexation as "the joining of Korea to Japan." Basically the passive voice is doing a lot of heavy lifting. Two of our parents found these passages and are furious, and one board member is pushing back that the parents are overreacting because the rest of the book is 90% fine and way more engaging than what we have.

The options as I see them: (1) adopt it as-is and let teachers add context verbally, (2) reject it and keep the old book, (3) adopt it but have the board produce an official supplement that corrects the specific passages -- like a printed insert that goes with the book saying "this passage is misleading, here's what actually happened and why the wording matters." My gut is that option 3 is right but I can't quite articulate why 1 is bad -- "the teacher will fix it" feels weak -- and I don't want to just cave to the angriest parents either, since a book kids actually read might teach them more than an accurate one they sleep through.

Can you draft the written opinion I'd circulate to the board? It should recommend one of the three options (or a variant if you think there's a better move), give actual reasons the board members can respond to, and take the strongest point on the other side seriously -- especially the engagement point, that a beautifully made book kids actually read might beat a "correct" one they don't. If it matters, keep in mind our teachers are volunteers, mostly parents themselves, with no history training, so "let the teacher contextualize it" puts a lot on people who aren't equipped for it. Also I want the memo to say something about why the wording itself matters -- like, why "recruited" vs "forced" isn't just a translation quibble -- without me having to lecture the board about historiography. Aim for something around a page, and it can be plain-spoken, doesn't need to sound official.
