I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science, and for two semesters now a reading group I help run has been stuck on the same fight about chaos theory and determinism. One side (mostly the physicists) insists that chaos matters philosophically: sensitive dependence on initial conditions means that even if the world is deterministic, no embedded agent could ever predict it in practice, because any finite agent's measurement error gets amplified exponentially, so the Laplacean picture of a demon who reads off the future from the present reduces to an empty metaphysical slogan — a precision that exists nowhere in the actual universe. The other side (mostly the philosophers) says this entirely misses the point: determinism is an ontological thesis about what fixes what, not an epistemic thesis about what anyone can know, and the difficulty of prediction tells us nothing about whether the future is genuinely open. What frustrates me is that everyone in the room agrees about the underlying mathematics — the logistic map, Lyapunov exponents, the distinction between determinism and predictability as concepts — and yet nobody has moved an inch in months.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about what a thesis has to do to earn its keep: the physicists implicitly demand that determinism be cashed out in terms accessible to a resource-limited agent embedded in the very system being predicted (so a thesis that no in-system agent could ever actually use counts as empty), while the philosophers treat it as a perfectly respectable modal claim about how the state of the world at one time relates to its state at later times, with no obligation to be usable by anyone. But I can't resolve several complications on my own. The self-prediction arguments — the Popper-style point that a computer trying to simulate a system that includes itself runs into something like a fixed-point or halting-problem obstruction, and the related suggestion that an embedded predictor faces a limitation in principle rather than merely in practice — seem to give the physicists something stronger than "prediction is hard." Yet the philosophers reply that even the undecidability of self-prediction is fully compatible with determinism proper, and I can't tell whether that reply is a decisive point or a change of subject.

Please take the role of a philosopher of science who has watched this dispute play out for decades and has no stake in either side winning. Think carefully, and consider brainstorming the possible diagnoses before committing to one. Then tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal (about the word 'determinism'), substantive (about the world or about what formal results show), two different questions traveling under one name, or a disagreement about the standards a thesis must meet before it counts as meaningful. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, not with a survey of chaos theory. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a sharper distinction, an agreed-upon toy model of an embedded predictor, a formal result about self-prediction, a reframed question — or make the case that no progress should be expected, and explain why.
