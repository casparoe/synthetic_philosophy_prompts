I'd like you to compare foundationalism and infinitism as responses to the epistemic regress problem, setting coherentism aside. On the usual telling the two are polar opposites: foundationalism halts the regress at basic beliefs, while infinitism (in Peter Klein's version) demands an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons. But the two may converge once each side makes its standard concessions. Infinitists typically insist that only finitely many of the infinitely many reasons need ever be actually deployed — the rest merely have to be available — which arguably blunts the objection that no finite being could be justified that way. Foundationalists, meanwhile, increasingly describe basic beliefs not as indubitable but as defeasible defaults that stand unless actually challenged, so that the regress halts only relative to a challenge someone raises. With those moves on the table, does anything substantive still separate the two positions, or do they collapse into a shared picture of justification as defeasible, context-dependent, and asymmetric? Where they genuinely come apart — say, on whether merely available reasons do justificatory work, or on what a foundationalist should say once a supposedly basic belief is in fact challenged — argue for which side gives the better account, rather than merely cataloguing the differences.
