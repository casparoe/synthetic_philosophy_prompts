Two of my grad students (call them Ana and Ben) got into it at our reading group last week about coherent extrapolated volition -- the old proposal that a friendly AI should do what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up farther together. They kept going at my place after and I couldn't referee it, so maybe you can.

Ana's opening move: the whole thing is hopelessly underdetermined. "Knew more" -- how much more? If you know literally everything, you already know what the extrapolation outputs, and it's not clear the counterfactual even makes sense. And small differences in how you run the idealization (which facts you add first, how far you extend each person's reflection) can produce wildly different outcomes, so there's simply no fact of the matter about what "we" would want.

Ben replied that this sets the bar absurdly high. The point was never to compute a unique fixed point; it's to do better than taking anyone's current, confused wishes at face value. We already treat people's idealized preferences as real all the time -- we say the recovered alcoholic "really" doesn't want the drink even while he's reaching for it -- and nobody complains that we can't predict his exact future preferences to the last decimal.

Ana's comeback: the alcohol case works because there's a fairly specific causal process (recovery) with a roughly shared endpoint. But that's just not true for values. Factual disagreement shrinks as people learn physics; moral disagreement among equally informed and reflective people doesn't obviously shrink at all. So idealization would leave all the interesting conflicts exactly where they were.

Ben then leaned on the "grown up farther together" clause: that's exactly why it's in there -- the idealization isn't each person reasoning alone, it's stipulated as a shared, mutual process, which is supposed to soften the conflicts. Ana said that just relocates the problem: "together" means you have to aggregate across people, aggregation needs weights, and no amount of extrapolation hands you the weights. Maybe the aggregation question was the real problem all along and the whole idealization story is a sideshow -- nice for an individual's AI assistant, maybe, but not for one AI acting for everyone.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step? And if there's something both of them are missing, I'd like to hear that too.
