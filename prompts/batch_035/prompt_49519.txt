I'm a PhD student in bioethics writing a dissertation chapter on how clinical ethics bodies reason from their own precedents, and as part of that I sit as graduate observer on the clinical ethics consult service at a large teaching hospital. Before each meeting, the service asks observers to circulate a short memo, and I have one to write for next week's consult. I'd be grateful for your help thinking it through.

The service issues advisory rulings on whether patients have died — typically to settle whether organs may be procured or treatment withdrawn without anyone being "killed" in the process — and it tends to follow its own earlier rulings closely. Its track record, as I read it:

(1) A patient with irreversible loss of all brain function — no cranial-nerve reflexes, a flat and confirmatory EEG, a positive apnea test, no confounding sedation or hypothermia — whose heart still beat on a ventilator: ruled dead, and organs were procured while the heart was still beating.

(2) A patient in a permanent vegetative state — cortex extensively and irreversibly destroyed, but breathing without a ventilator, with sleep-wake cycling and intact brainstem reflexes: ruled not dead, though the service separately held that the surrogate could withdraw nutrition and hydration, so that the patient could be allowed to die without ever having been declared dead.

(3) A patient otherwise as in (1), but exhibiting spinal automatisms — twitching and reflex withdrawal in response to stimulus: ruled dead all the same, the service noting that spinal reflex arcs are not brain function.

(4) A patient in asystole after thirty minutes of failed resuscitation: dead, unremarkably.

The new consult: a 54-year-old with a catastrophic brainstem hemorrhage. Two independent neurological examinations and imaging show complete and irreversible destruction of the brainstem — no cranial-nerve reflexes, and no respiratory effort whatsoever when the ventilator is paused under the standard apnea protocol. The unusual part is the cortex: it appears largely intact, and the EEG shows organized background activity with sleep-wake cycling; evoked potentials confirm cortical responses to stimulation. The neurologists say this pattern is exceedingly rare — it is, as it were, the mirror image of the patient in ruling (2). The surrogate (who, in the manner of ruling (2), has authority to refuse treatment) wants the patient's organs donated, and the transplant team is asking whether he can be declared dead now, with the heart beating, or whether the service must instead withdraw the ventilator and wait for asystole first.

What I need from you: What ruling should the service issue if it wants to be consistent with itself? Which precedent is the closest analog, and is it actually dispositive here? More importantly, I want the memo to be honest about the implicit criteria our precedents leave on the table — whole-brain, brainstem-only, higher-brain, circulatory — what each of those predicts for this patient, and which of them our earlier rulings have already eliminated. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the ruling, I want to state plainly what principle the service would be adopting with each available choice. And I'd like you to assess whether the apparently neutral route — withdraw the ventilator under the surrogate's authority and then determine death circulatorily after some minutes of asystole — really is neutral, or whether choosing it over immediate procurement quietly commits us to one of the criteria. Finally, name one further case we could realistically face that would best separate the surviving criteria from each other.

Please close your response with a final line of the form \verdict{...} giving the advisory ruling itself in a short phrase (for example, dead now versus not dead), so that I can extract it mechanically for my chapter's case table.
