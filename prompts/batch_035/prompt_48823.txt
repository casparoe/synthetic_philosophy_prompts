I am a high school student working on my extended essay for the International Baccalaureate program, and I would appreciate your comments on the argument I have developed so far. My research question is: Are harms that occur inside virtual worlds real harms? Below is a summary of my essay's central argument, followed by my worries about it. Please tell me where the argument is weakest, whether the objection I raise against myself can be answered, and whether any part of what I am doing is philosophy rather than psychology or sociology.

The argument, in brief. Everyone agrees that if a player is harassed through the game's chat function, a real harm has occurred, because the harm does not live inside the virtual world at all: it is an ordinary harm between two real people that merely uses the game as a medium. The hard cases are the ones where the harm seems to be constituted by the virtual events themselves. My main example is what I call the "double theodicy" problem, by analogy with the problem of evil: suppose a game company designs a world in which, through no fault of the players, non-player characters suffer famine, and players are invited to watch and even intervene. If the company had written a novel describing the same famine, we would not say anyone was harmed by the famine itself, even if readers felt distress; the distress would be the only harm, and it could be waived by consenting to read sad literature. My claim is that the difference we intuitively feel between the novel and the simulation has to be found in one of two places, and both places cause trouble.

The first possible difference is interactivity: in the simulation, what happens to the virtual people depends on what I do, so I can wrong them in a way an author cannot wrong characters, or cannot in the same way. The second is persistence and independence: the simulated famine runs whether or not I attend to it, whereas the novel's events exist only as contents of acts of reading. I argue that if the moral difference is supposed to come from interactivity, then it evaporates when I interact with something that has no moral status, just as my steering a radio-controlled car toward a mannequin does not become an attempted assault when I sincerely believe the mannequin is a person; sincerity affects my blameworthiness, not whether anyone was wronged. But if the difference comes from persistence and independence, then we are committed to saying that unobserved computational processes have some kind of welfare, and that seems to prove too much, since the same processes run on my laptop when it renders a screen saver.

I then try to rescue the intuition that virtual harms are real by proposing what I call the "double-counting test": a harm inside a virtual world is real exactly when counting it as real does not double-count anything that is already counted at the hardware or player level. Distress, lost time, stolen virtual property converted to real money, and broken friendships all fail the test — they are already real harms under other descriptions. This suggests, more radically than I expected when I started, that there are no virtual harms, only harms with virtual causes, and that our feeling that the virtual events themselves matter is a kind of perceptual illusion that game designers deliberately exploit, since the illusion is what makes games engaging.

Here are my three worries. First, my double-counting test may be circular: I explain the non-reality of virtual harms by appeal to a test that assumes the only real harms are the ordinary ones. Second, I am not sure whether the view that virtual events are never themselves harms can survive the case of virtual property that is stolen and then sold, since it looks like the theft creates the later real harm, and it is odd to say a non-event causes a real one. Third, my conclusion seems to conflict with the common view that things can be real in different ways, so that virtual objects might be genuinely real as virtual objects, rather than unreal as physical objects; but I do not understand what work the word "real" is doing in that claim, and my essay may simply be equivocating between "real" meaning causally efficacious and "real" meaning morally considerable.

Please comment on the essay's argument as summarized above. In particular: does the disjunction between interactivity and persistence exhaust the options, or have I missed a third place the moral difference could come from? Where exactly is the argument weakest, and what would I need to fix or concede? Finally, do you think my conclusion is interesting enough to sustain a four-thousand-word essay, or should I narrow the question?
