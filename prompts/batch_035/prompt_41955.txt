I'm giving a talk next month to a philosophy of science reading group defending what I'll call interpretability realism: the claim that when interpretability research attributes a "feature" to a neural network -- say, a dictionary element from a sparse autoencoder that fires selectively on images of the Golden Gate Bridge, and that makes the model talk about the bridge unprompted when amplified -- it is not just summarizing model behavior in a convenient vocabulary but tracking real internal structure that exists independently of our probing. My argument has three prongs: (1) interventions on single features produce the specific behavioral counterfactuals their labels predict; (2) dictionaries trained independently, with different seeds and sparsity penalties, recover substantially overlapping feature sets; (3) features seem to compose systematically rather than being arbitrary slices of activation space. My own twist, which I suspect is where I'm most exposed, is a deliberately modest realism: features are real causal variables, but their individuation is method-laden -- the ontology is real while our inventory of it is partial and shaped by what we probe for.

The audience: about a dozen philosophers of science, most of them instrumentalists about unobservables who regard the history of science as a graveyard of theoretical entities that everyone once took to be real, plus two visiting researchers from an interpretability team. The philosophers will see features as the electron of the decade, and the ML researchers think the whole framing is a category error: the network is an engineered artifact whose full microphysics we possess, so "discovering real structure" in it is like claiming to discover real structure in a spreadsheet you compiled yourself. I can't satisfy both audiences at once, and I suspect the ML researchers' objection does more damage than the philosophers' does.
