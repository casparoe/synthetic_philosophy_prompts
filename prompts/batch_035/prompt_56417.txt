I've spent a long time on the rationalist side of the internet, where consequentialism is the default operating system and deontology gets treated as a rounding error, so I've seen the paradox of deontology argued to death — and never resolved. The setup, as it usually gets run there: a villain says he will wrong five innocent people unless I myself wrong one of them. Deontological constraints forbid me to wrong anyone, even for the greater good; but if constraints are absolute, then I must allow five wrongs rather than commit one, and if I follow that rule while knowing the villain's threat was chosen to exploit it, I seem to be doing evil arithmetic with my own hands — honoring a distinction between my agency and the world's outcome that my adversary gets to weaponize. The consequentialist in the thread says this is a reductio; the deontologist says it's the whole point. Fifteen years of threads and I still don't think either side has written down what they'd actually accept as a loss condition.

So here's my ask: write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two characters — call them D, a constraint deontologist who holds that agent-relative prohibitions on harming can bind absolutely, and C, a consequentialist who holds that the right act is a function of outcomes only, agent-neutral — who have been arguing about this for years and have finally been locked in a room with a facilitator and a shared document.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about the case itself, about what each theory straightforwardly implies in it, about what does *not* follow from the paradox (e.g., that deontology is self-defeating in iterated settings, or that consequentialists never face an analogue), and about what a satisfactory resolution owes both sides.

2. A table of exact disagreements, each row written so that the named side would sign the wording of its own position: is the doing/allowing asymmetry a fact about agency or a fact about framing? Does the constraint-bearer's clean hands have value that a victim is permitted to weigh, or only value that the agent is permitted to weigh? Is the villain's threat an ordinary cost in the moral ledger or a change in what the options even are?

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, with, for each, a concrete case, argument, or formal condition both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I'd particularly like them to face a hybrid view that neither has fully considered before, which I'll propose and they should take seriously rather than dismiss as a cheap compromise: call it Lexical Compounding — the view that each additional violation *I commit* carries a superlinear penalty, so one self-committed wrong is never worth even many prevented wrongs, but at some threshold (five? fifty? a continent?) the agent-relative penalty compounds fast enough that committing the wrong becomes permitted. D should say whether her view actually forbids Lexical Compounding or merely re-describes it; C should say whether he can accept superlinear agent-relative penalties at all without collapsing into D's position. Predictions in advance, please — I want to be able to score this later.

4. A closing section stating whether any part of their residual dispute is merely verbal (e.g., whether "I am responsible for what I do, not for what I allow" is a substantive moral claim or a definition of "responsible"), and if so, which part and why.

Both characters should be maximally competent — D is not a disguised consequentialist having second thoughts, and C is not D's foil for easy points. They should respond to each other's strongest formulation, not the slogan version. Please write in American English, in the register of a polished joint statement rather than a transcript of bickering. The very last line should be a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states, in at most 35 words, the deepest crux between the two camps as you see it.
