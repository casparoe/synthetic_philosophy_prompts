I've been keeping a list of cases where I have a gut verdict about whether something is an honest signal — whether the act or trait really carries information about the sender, or is just bluff — and I want to know whether a single principle underlies my verdicts. My working hunch, from the handicap idea, is that a signal is honest when it's costly, but I can already see my verdicts don't line up with it, and I don't know whether to repair that principle or throw it out. The cases, with my verdicts:

1. Peacock's tail — enormously expensive to grow and to drag around, and it advertises to predators. Verdict: honest; the tail really does tell the peahen something.
2. Watch on credit — a man on a modest salary buys a luxury watch on a credit card and spends three years paying it off with interest. Verdict: dishonest; it signals wealth he doesn't have, even though his cost is real and large.
3. Old-money shabbiness — a woman from a genuinely rich old family wears worn clothes, drives a twenty-year-old car, and mentions this casually at dinner. Verdict: honest; her shabbiness really does tell people she's secure, and it works as a signal, though it costs her nothing.
4. Diploma mill — a "doctorate" for two thousand dollars, no coursework, certificate by return post. Verdict: dishonest; it signals competence that was never demonstrated, even though it costs money.
5. Vacuous ad campaign — a firm spends an enormous sum on a slick national campaign that says almost nothing about the product. Verdict: honest; the sheer waste persuades me the firm is confident and solvent, like the peacock.
6. Fraternity hazing — a brutal initiation week before membership. Verdict: honest, as a signal of commitment to the group — the pain is real and can't be faked — but I hate saying it, and I half-suspect this is the verdict my principle ought to overturn.
7. Diamond engagement ring — the case I keep going back and forth on. The cost is real, sunk, and effectively unrefundable, which points toward honest; but it is paid to a jeweler rather than endured, which feels like the watch in case 2.
8. The same shabbiness on a lottery winner — a man who got rich last year adopts exactly the style of case 3, worn clothes and all. Verdict: on him it doesn't signal security, it signals poverty. Same act, opposite message, and I can't see how any principle about the sender's costs allows that.

Please give me the entire analysis as a single JSON object with nothing outside it, using this schema:

- "principle": the best principle you can construct, stated precisely in a sentence or two — repair the cost principle or replace it, as you judge best;
- "clauses": an array with one entry per clause of the principle, each an object with "clause" (the clause), "motivated_by" (which of my cases it exists to save), and "cost_in_complexity" (what the clause costs the principle in simplicity or scope);
- "case_by_case": an array with one entry per case 1–8, each an object with "case" (the short name as above), "principle_says" (honest, dishonest, or indeterminate, plus a phrase of explanation), and "matches_my_verdict" (true or false);
- "contested_case": what the principle says about the diamond ring, and whether that settles my wavering or shows it to be two intuitions pulling apart;
- "new_cases": an array of two or three cases I haven't listed, invented by you in the same concrete style, each an object with "case", "predicted_verdict", and "why";
- "overturned": an array of any of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, each an object with "case" and "why" — be honest here rather than saving my face;
- "bottom_line": one sentence on whether cost turns out to be sufficient, necessary, or neither for honesty, and what does the work instead.
