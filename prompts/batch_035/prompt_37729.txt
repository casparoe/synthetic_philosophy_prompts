I help run a small decision-theory reading group, and our most reliable subject over the years has been a behavioral economist I will call Dana. Dana insists she holds no theoretical commitments — she just answers the bets — but her track record is stable enough that we treat her verdicts as data. I have two new bets planned for Friday's session, chosen because I suspect the standard models of ambiguity aversion come apart on them, and I want help predicting her choices and pinning down the rule she is implicitly running.

Her track record, all with real money and stakes she prices as roughly risk-neutral:

1. A known urn (50 red, 50 black) versus an ambiguous urn (100 balls, red/black split unknown). For $100 on a color of her choosing, she picked the known urn when choosing red and again when choosing black. She will pay $50 for either known-urn bet and $32 for either ambiguous-urn bet, and she is exactly indifferent between the colors on the ambiguous urn.

2. A single urn with 30 red and 70 balls split somehow between black and yellow. She prefers $100 on red to $100 on black, and $100 on black-or-yellow to $100 on red-or-yellow.

3. On the fully ambiguous urn (any number of red from 0 to 100), she accepted, at no entry fee, a wager paying $100 if the drawn ball is red and costing her $10 if it is black.

4. An urn known only to contain between 40 and 60 red balls among 100: she will pay $45 for $100 on red. When I mentioned that whoever prepared the urns knows the exact splits, she shrugged — whether the split is known to anyone else makes no difference to her prices.

5. A fair coin selects between an 80% shot at $100 and a 20% shot at $100. She prices that package at exactly $50, the same as a straight 50% shot — she reduces two-stage objective risk without any discount.

The two new bets:

A. The hedged ticket. One fully ambiguous urn (any red/black split) plus a fair coin. Ticket M: flip the coin, then draw one ball; you win $100 if the coin came up heads and the ball is red, or if the coin came up tails and the ball is black. Ticket N: flip the same coin; you win $100 if it comes up heads, nothing otherwise. As best I can calculate, both tickets pay with probability exactly one-half no matter what the urn contains — and yet I do not expect her to treat them as interchangeable. (Verdict 5 covers mixtures of risky bets; M is a mixture of ambiguous ones, and I cannot decide whether that difference should matter to her.)

B. The rare prize. Lottery K: a known 1-in-1,000 chance at $10,000. Lottery L: draw a ball from the fully ambiguous urn; if it is red, you get a known 1-in-500 shot at the same $10,000, and if it is black, you get nothing. Her win probability under L lies somewhere between 0 and 1-in-500 depending on the urn's composition, with a midpoint of 1-in-1,000.

Please tell me: (i) your predictions for A and B; (ii) which rules actually fit her track record — I have in mind the usual suspects (maxmin expected utility, possibly with a restricted set of priors; its α-mixture; Choquet expected utility with a capacity; the smooth ambiguity model), but I would genuinely like to hear of others, and of anything in the track record that one of these families cannot reproduce; (iii) what each surviving rule predicts on A and B, and precisely where the predictions diverge; (iv) your best guess about which rule she is running, with reasons — the certainty equivalents in 1 and 4 should let you fit parameters rather than talk qualitatively; and (v) the single further bet, described concretely enough that we could run it at a later session, that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. If nothing fits her numbers exactly, say so and tell me what the misfit is telling us. I care less about the label for Dana than about whether our models can be behaviorally separated at all.
