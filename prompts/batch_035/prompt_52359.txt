I'm taking a second-year course on Greek historiography, and I've run into a clash between two framings of the whole enterprise. At a family dinner, my cousin dismissed my syllabus by saying that everything we know about the ancient world has come down to us like a game of Chinese whispers: a story told and retold, copied and recopied for centuries before anyone produced anything like a critical edition, so whatever reaches us is basically whatever the last careless link in the chain felt like saying — and historians of the distant past are just annotating noise. My lecturer insists on the opposite picture: she describes sources as witnesses to be cross-examined — sources can be caught contradicting each other, caught contradicting themselves, and caught out by inscriptions, coins and archaeology — and she claims that the competing versions of an event Herodotus lays side by side are precisely the material out of which cross-examination can build knowledge. Both analogies are doing argumentative work: the whispers one is meant to license blanket scepticism about the distant past, the courtroom one a kind of methodological confidence, and I'd like to know which, if either, can carry that weight.

Please audit them, focusing especially on the Chinese whispers analogy. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the message, the teller, the individual transmission event, the chain, and the distortion at each link. Then say where the mapping genuinely breaks — the points that strike me are that a scribe copying a manuscript has the exemplar open in front of him rather than a half-remembered phrase from a neighbour, that transmission is usually branching and parallel rather than a single chain, and that later scribes and editors could and did compare copies against one another, so errors don't accumulate in one direction the way garbling does. Then tell me whether the wholesale sceptical conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy can be patched so that some restricted scepticism still follows — perhaps by relocating the distortion to the phase before anything was written down at all, when oral tradition really was a chain of retellings, which would make it do real work against, say, confidence in the exact wording of the speeches Thucydides admits to arranging himself, or in Herodotus' figures for the size of Xerxes' army, while leaving the skeletal narrative of events better supported. Finally, assess whether the courtroom analogy earns its confidence or merely relocates the problem: a witness can be pressed, but our witnesses are dead, and everything we would use to press them — the parallel accounts, the inscriptions — comes to us through the very copying process the whispers picture worries about, so does the cross-examining historian ever escape the chain, or just become another examiner of retellings of retellings? Please answer in British English, keep the structure of correspondence table, disanalogies, verdicts, and finish by saying what a better analogy for the transmission and criticism of ancient texts would look like if neither the game of whispers nor the courtroom can do the job.
