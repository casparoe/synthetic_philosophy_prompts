My reading group is reconstructing Orwell's editorial rules from his verdicts in "Politics and the English Language" (1946) and testing them on new material. His track record, as we've compiled it: he condemned worn-out metaphors used without a thought behind them, pretentious and foreign diction, padded and passive constructions, words so overused they name nothing at all, and above all political euphemism — bombing villages described as pacification, atrocities dressed in dignified phrase — which he treated as language whose purpose is to keep the writer's real thought from surfacing. What earned his praise was fresh, concrete, vivid prose; his diagnosis of the worst specimens was "staleness of imagery" combined with "lack of precision," a writer who either has no meaning or is indifferent to whether his words mean anything. What we can't agree on is the master rule. Is it a freshness rule (prefabricated language fails no matter what is in the writer's heart), a sincerity rule (language fails only when it conceals or replaces thought), or a political-evasion rule (only language that launders political reality is culpable, and dead metaphors in a love letter are merely bad craft)?

Three new cases from one company's announcements. (1) A layoff email assembled entirely from stock phrases — employees let go so the company can stay focused on what matters most — but the executive who drafted it is visibly, genuinely devastated, and means every worn-out word of it. (2) A freshly minted euphemism in the same email: the layoffs are described as "releasing talent back into the ecosystem," original and vivid and plainly designed to make ten thousand job losses sound like ecological benefaction. (3) A chatbot's condolence message to a bereaved user: fluent, freshly phrased, warm — and with no one behind it to be sincere or insincere. For each case, predict Orwell's verdict and his reasoning; lay out which of the candidate rules fit his track record equally well; give your best guess at the rule he is actually running and say why; and propose the further case that would discriminate most cleanly between the surviving rules. Please also distinguish what is settled by the essay itself from what is genuinely contested among careful readers of Orwell — for instance, whether his ultimate complaint is aesthetic, epistemic, or political — and don't present any one interpretation as the consensus.
