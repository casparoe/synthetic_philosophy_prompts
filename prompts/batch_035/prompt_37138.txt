I've come to hold a broadly Browning-style view about the perpetrators of the Holocaust and mass atrocity more generally: that Reserve Police Battalion 101 and others like them are best explained by situational dynamics -- conformity, deference to authority, gradual habituation through increasingly brutal assignments, concern for comrades, and simple careerism -- rather than by deep ideological commitment, national political culture, or sadistic character. On this view the men of the battalion were, as Browning's title has it, ordinary men, and the lesson generalizes: given the right structure of pressures, most human populations would do what they did. Daniel Goldhagen's rival account, in which a pervasive "eliminationist" antisemitism embedded in German culture does the real explanatory work, strikes me as an artifact of hindsight and moralism.

Tell me what would count against my view. I don't want a rehash of the 1996 debate; I want the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest sorting of which ones actually bite. Some candidates I'm aware of but haven't weighed: the archival critiques of the classic social psychology -- Le Texier's work on the Stanford Prison Experiment recordings, and Gibson's reinterpretation of the Milgram transcripts as showing engaged followership rather than blind obedience. If the laboratory findings don't mean what the situationist story assumed, how much of the case survives on the historical evidence alone? Then there is the historical material Goldhagen marshaled that Browning had to confront: the minority who took up Trapp's offer to step out, versus the killing that went beyond any order -- the local massacre at Jedwabne, where Poles murdered their Jewish neighbors largely on their own initiative; auxiliary units like the Trawniki men who volunteered; the leisure culture of killing reflected in soldiers' photographs. And the comparative evidence: if situations are doing the work, why did Denmark ferry its Jews to Sweden while the Netherlands, under comparable occupation, lost a far higher proportion? Can a situational account absorb that variance, or does variance itself smuggle culture back in as a cause?

I'd also like you to address what Browning and other defenders of the view have themselves conceded rather than only what critics allege, and to assess whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if any ideological commitment can be redescribed as "the situation made them believe it," what is actually at stake between the two camps?

One more thing: if you think the dichotomy I've set up -- situation versus culture/character -- is itself confused, or that the two explanations can't be weighed against each other the way I'm asking, say so plainly rather than answering on my terms. I'd rather have my question taken apart than answered politely.
