Been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for like a decade now (LessWrong since the Sequences days, ACX open threads, the whole ecosystem), and one thing I've noticed is that whenever the "does science actually track truth or just keep not-getting-falsified" debate flares up, somebody eventually name-drops structural realism and then everyone argues past each other. I never had a grip on it myself, but a friend of mine who's deep into phil of sci tried to give me the gist after reading some book review (can't remember the book, sorry). Here's more or less what he told me, written down the way I remember it:

Structural realism says that when a scientific theory gets replaced, what survives into its successor is the mathematical structure — the equations and the relations between things — while the descriptions of the objects themselves get thrown out. Newton's theory was a limiting case of relativity, so the structural content carried over even though absolute space turned out to be wrong. And since objects like the electron keep getting radically re-described from theory to theory (particle, wave, field excitation), the conclusion is that objects don't really exist at all — only relations are real, and the objects are just a way of talking about the relations. The motivation is the no-miracles argument: the success of science would be a miracle unless something in our theories was getting at reality, and the historical record shows it's the structure that survives theory change, not the object-talk, so be realist about the structure and drop the objects. Basically it's the "the map is real but the territory isn't" position. Anyway, the whole thing got refuted back in the twenties by a logician who showed any structure can be preserved by arbitrary relabeling, so saying "the structure is preserved" turns out to say nothing.

I'm inclined to believe him because he reads way more than me, but the "objects don't exist at all" part sounds suspiciously strong, and the map/territory comparison feels off in a way I can't put my finger on.

Can you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a philosopher of science working on this would basically accept, which parts they'd wince at as conflating things the view keeps distinct, and which parts are just wrong? In particular: does the eliminative-sounding conclusion about objects follow from the parts that are actually right, or is he merging two versions of the view that its defenders carefully seperate? And is the relabeling objection really a refutation or more of a live problem people are still arguing about?
