I'm putting together a session for my philosophy of religion reading group, and I want to try something with real friction in it instead of the usual theist-vs-atheist matchup we've done to death. The idea: both characters already believe in God, but they got there by different roads, and each thinks the other's cosmological argument is a mistake — maybe worse than useless, since a bad argument for a true conclusion can do more damage than no argument at all.

Write a dialogue between a defender of the kalam argument and a defender of the Leibnizian argument from contingency. The kalam person runs the temporal version: whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist (no actual infinities in reality, no traversing an infinite past, plus current cosmology as icing on the cake), so the universe has a cause, and that cause has to be personal, immaterial, and timeless. The contingency person runs the modal version: contingent beings demand an explanation, the whole collection of contingent things can't be explained by anything inside the collection, so there has to be a necessary being grounding it all. Crucially, they should go at each other, not at atheism. Have the kalam person press hard on the Principle of Sufficient Reason — it seems to demand explanations for everything, including God and including the PSR itself, and once the contingency defender starts carving out exemptions, why can't the atheist just carve out one exemption for the universe and be done with it? Have the contingency person press back that the kalam is hostage to whatever physics says this decade (what happens to the argument if cosmologists decide the universe is past-eternal after all?), that the premise about things that begin to exist is basically the PSR wearing a disguise, and that the whole move where the cause turns out to be personal looks bolted on rather than earned.

Both should be sharp and neither should be a strawman — they should actually engage with each other's points, concede the good ones, and adjust, rather than lecturing past each other. Somewhere in the middle I'd like them to hit the question of whether these are even two different arguments or the same argument in different clothes, and whether the choice between them is really about strength or about what you're willing to be accused of assuming. Let it end unresolved but with each of them having said something the other genuinely had to think about. For what it's worth I lean contingency myself, so don't let the kalam guy be the dumb one — if anything I want to know what the best version of his case sounds like. Please write it in American English, maybe 900 to 1200 words, and dont worry about stage directions beyond a light setting like two philosophers stuck at an airport bar waiting out a delayed flight.
