I review books and the occassional art exhibit for a mid-size weekly -- three or four pieces a month, 800 words each, and usually less than a week with the actual book before deadline. I'm tired of pretending my judgments are anything more than vibes, so I've been reading about Hume's essay on the standard of taste, and I like the core move: instead of defining beauty, you define the competent judge, and the joint verdict of good judges over time becomes the standard. The problem is Hume gives me qualities of the ideal critic (practice, comparison, delicacy, freedom from prejudice, good sense) but not a workflow, and I need something I can actually run in seven days while also writing the piece.

Turn Hume into a procedure a working critic could follow: the ordered steps, what counts as passing each one, where failing a step means I shouldn't publish a verdict at all (versus writing about the book while withholding the verdict), and where the procedure has to hand things back to plain judgment. Also be honest about the circularity objection people raise against Hume -- if we pick out the good judges partly by their verdicts, then use the judges to validate the verdicts, my procedure may just be laundering my existing taste -- and build in whatever protection against that you can.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a much-hyped literary novel that my industry friends all loved and I found hollow; (2) a grim thriller I devoured in one night and suspect is bad for reasons I can't articulate; (3) a genre I'm basically a stranger to -- say a romance novel or a collection of instagram poetry -- where the "practice and comparison" requirements seem to fail before they start. Case 2 should be one where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, whatever it turns out to be, and case 3 should show me what the procedure can't do rather than papering over it.

Structure your answer as: "What Hume actually gives you" (about 150 words), "The procedure" (numbered steps, about 300 words), "Three cases" (about 100 words each), and "Where it breaks" (about 150 words).
