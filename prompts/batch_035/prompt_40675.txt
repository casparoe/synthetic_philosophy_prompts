My public policy seminar this term is on paternalism and nudging, and my professor keeps reaching for the same analogy to defend libertarian paternalism: a nudge is like a GPS. It suggests the route, you're free to ignore it and drive wherever you want, and nobody would call a GPS paternalistic even though it's literally guiding your behavior toward someone's idea of the best route. The conclusion he wants us to draw is that nudges — auto-enrollment in pensions, healthy food at eye level with the candy by the register, defaults for organ donation — can't be an affront to autonomy because they only advise. The steering wheel stays with you.

I left class feeling the analogy is doing alot more work than it should. My GPS gives me reasons — "traffic ahead, saving 20 minutes" — and I weigh them as reasons, like an argument I happened to accept. But a lot of nudges seem to work precisely by not engaging my reasoning at all: nobody deliberates about the pension default, they just stay in it, and the choice architect knows they'll stay in it. Some nudges (the shelf placement ones) don't tell me anything new, they just exploit where my eyes go. A friend in the class thinks the whole problem is that my professor picked the wrong gadget — she says a nudge is really like a supermarket layout, designed by people who know how attention moves, and nobody calls a shelf decision "information."

Can you audit the GPS analogy properly? I'd like the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the route, the suggested destination, the recalculation when you go off-route, the fact that it's your car and your trip — then where the mapping breaks, and specifically which disanalogies actually matter for the autonomy conclusion and which are just noise (I don't care that one is a box and the other is a form, obviously). The one I keep circling: does "you can always ignore it" carry the autonomy conclusion once you notice that many nudges work because people aren't paying attention, not because they were persuaded? And if the GPS comparison fails, does the supermarket one do better — or does it have its own problem, since a supermarket isn't usually pretending to act in your interest, while a choice architect is?
