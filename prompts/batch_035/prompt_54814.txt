I chair the bylaws subcommittee of our faculty senate at a mid-sized state university (~600 voting faculty), and after the disaster that was last spring's named-professorship search, we're finally writing an actual voting rule. The bylaws currently just say elections "shall be by ballot," which turns out to mean different things to different people. The problem: our elections often have three or more options (chair elections routinely get 4-5 candidates, plus the option to reopen the search), and Prof. K from econ, who teaches social choice, has been invoking the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem at every meeting — her position is that ANY ranked-ballot rule we adopt with more than two options can be gamed by strategic voting, so we should just stick with plurality and stop pretending otherwise. Prof. R from statistics wants Borda counts. And one of the grad reps keeps pushing this homemade procedure he found on some internet forum, which is roughly: everyone scores the candidates 0–3, anyone averaging below 1.5 gets dropped, then a plurality runoff among the survivors, with ties broken by head-to-head results among the tied candidates. Nobody else on the subcommittee knows what to make of any of this and they keep looking at me.

I need a decision memo I can circulate before our April meeting, with an actual recommendation in it. Things I want it to grapple with: whether Prof. K is deploying the theorem correctly — my vague understanding is that it needs specific assumptions (deterministic rule, ordinal preferences, something about all preference profiles being possible), and that cardinal systems like approval or score voting might fall outside its scope entirely, but I don't want to hand-wave because the committee will smell it. Whether Borda is really as burying-prone as Prof. K claims (she says it's the single worst offender for that tactic). Whether the grad rep's franken-rule can be salvaged or should be put down gently. And honestly, whether the right move is to concede that some manipulability is inevitable and pick the rule on other grounds — ballot simplicity, explainability to an electorate that votes by email, resistance to *accidental* strategic distortions rather than theoretically perfect ones. One extra wrinkle: the bylaws require that chair elections go to whoever "recieves a majority of votes cast," which I suspect interacts badly with several of these proposals when there are four candidates splitting the vote, but I'm not sure which ones or how.

So please draft the memo: a clear recommendation (one rule, or a small set for different election types — we have single-winner chair elections, a slate of at-large committee seats, and occasional yes/no/"no, reopen the search" questions), the reasoning behind it, a serious and non-dismissive treatment of Prof. K's position including where she's right and where the theorem does less work than she thinks, and something concrete about the majority clause. Assume the readers aren't social choice theorists but several of them are skeptical of anything that sounds fancy, so be precise where you state the math and hedge where you're unsure. Under 1000 words if you can manage it, our meetings run long enough as it is.
