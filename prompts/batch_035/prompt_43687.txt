My spouse and I have spent the past year trying to decide whether to have a child, and last weekend a dinner with two friends turned into a long argument about whether the decision can even be a rational one. I want to say upfront that I found both of them convincing at different points, and I am not asking you to settle our family question — only their argument.

Dana, who studied philosophy, opened by explaining L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience. Becoming a parent, she said, is transformative in two ways at once: epistemically, like the color scientist who has never seen red, you cannot know what it is like in advance of living it; and personally, it rewrites your core preferences and values, so the person who would evaluate the outcome is partly created by the choice. Expected-utility reasoning therefore breaks down in principle, not just in practice — you would be assigning utilities on behalf of a person who does not yet exist. Marcus, who works in public policy and is more comfortable with decision theory, replied that this proves far too much: we save for retirement selves whose tastes we cannot imagine, choose careers before knowing the work, and marry people who will change us. If transformative experiences defeat rational choice, then almost nothing is ever chosen rationally, which suggests the argument has mislabeled ordinary deep uncertainty.

Dana answered that the disanalogy is one of kind rather than degree, and that Marcus had missed the deeper problem: the post-choice self's endorsement of the choice is manufactured by the choice itself, in the way a convert's testimony about a cult is manufactured by joining the cult. We do not count that as evidence the recruit chose well. This also contaminates Marcus's aggregates, she said: surveys showing that parents are overwhelmingly glad they had children are surveys of people whose very preferences were reshaped by parenthood, so summing their utilities and presenting the total to the pre-parent self is a category error. Marcus granted that preferences are always causally shaped by the lives that produce them — his fondness for his own career is exactly such a downstream preference — and argued that if Dana discounts caused preferences she can justify nothing, including the values she used to decide what to study. He then added that Paul's problem targets a specific, rather demanding model of choice, and that decision theory has resources for uncertainty: subjective probabilities need not be highly certain to support a rational decision, and one can act on the best available frequencies while acknowledging they are rough.

Dana's closing move was to accept that revelation is not rational deliberation and to embrace the consequence: Paul herself, Dana said, ends up suggesting that such choices be made by weighing the value of discovering who you will become rather than by justification, and the honest conclusion is that some life-defining choices cannot be made rationally at all — only blindly, or in something like faith — and that laundering them through expected value is a comforting self-deception. Marcus's rejoinder was that this concedes too much: if the endorsement of your post-choice self is worthless as evidence and the pull you feel beforehand is written off as non-rational, then Dana's position leaves nothing at all with which to choose, and a recommendation to follow what grips you is one a cult recruiter could give just as well. A view that eliminates every basis for a defining choice, he said, is more likely a reductio of its premises than wisdom.

They ran out of wine before I could sort out who had the better of it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each step, say who is right about the specific claim they advanced and why. I would also like to know whether any part of the disagreement strikes you as merely verbal — for instance, whether they are using rational to mean different things — and, separately, whether there is something both of them are missing that a careful reader of Paul would have put on the table.
