I write about science and ideas for a living, and every few years I end up doing a piece that brushes up against the Kant lying affair, and every time I come away feeling the whole thing is stuck in cement. You probably know the set-up: in 1797 Benjamin Constant baited Kant with a case — a murderer asks you where your friend is hiding — and Kant's short reply is remembered as saying you must tell the truth even to the murderer, which for most of the last two centuries has functioned as a punchline about where principle shades into fanaticism. Constant's own challenge, as I understand it, was roughly that a duty to always tell the truth would make society impossible — nobody could trust anybody's word about anything — so no such duty can exist. Then over the last few decades a whole revisionist literature has grown up insisting the punchline is a caricature. Some of these folks argue the essay isn't a general claim about lying at all but a narrow point about whether you ever have a *right* to lie — a juridical point about which duties could be coercively enforced, not a sermon about what a good person does in a crisis. Others lean on the details of Kants own version of the case, where things go wrong *because* of the lie: you lie, but unbeknownst to you the murderer has already left, the friend slips out the back and runs straight into him, so the harm gets imputed to you as the author of the deception — hence the real claim is supposedly about imputation of consequences, not about a duty to hand your friend over. And then a third group just bites the bullet: he really did hold lying is always wrong, that's the view, defend it or drop it.

What frustrates me as someone who has to compress all this into 800 words: the camps have stopped talking to each other. Popular writers keep using the murderer case as a gotcha, the revisionists keep publishing ever more detailed readings, and I can't find anyone who's changed their mind in either direction in twenty years. So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this even one disagreement, or several stapled together — a seperate textual one about what the essay claims, a philosophical one about whether lying admits exceptions, and maybe a verbal one about what counts as lying in the first place (would a misleading-but-true answer to the murderer even be in play for Kant, and if not, why not)? My working hypothesis, offered as just a hypothesis, is that the gotcha people and the scholars aren't actually disagreeing — they're answering different questions (is the view absurd? vs. what did the man actually write?) — but I'm sceptical of my own line here, because some revisionists clearly want the interpretive point to do philosophical work, i.e. to rescue the Kantian position rather than just Kants sentence. Is that rescue attempt legitimate, or is it motivated reading dressed up as philology? And what would count as progress here — a better reading of the text, a cleaner split between the exegetical question and the normative one, or an admission that Kant was doing something narrow and the case tells us nothing much about lying in general? Also, honestly: is the popular gotcha fair, unfair, or fair to Kant but unfair to Kantians? I'd like an answer I can build a column around, because right now the only honest thing I can tell my editor is that clever people have been at loggerheads over this since before my grandparents were born.
