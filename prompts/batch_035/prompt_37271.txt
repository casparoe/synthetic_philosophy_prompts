My best friend and I are high school seniors who argue about physics almost every day, and lately the argument is about when scientists are allowed to believe in something no one has ever observed. She issues very confident rulings on historical cases, so I have started keeping a list to figure out her underlying rule. Her verdicts so far: the astronomers who believed in an unseen planet beyond Uranus in 1845, before Neptune was found, were being perfectly rational; the chemists who believed in atoms around 1900, before the work on Brownian motion settled the matter, were rational; Dirac was rational in 1931 to believe in a positively charged twin of the electron before anyone had seen one; but the Ptolemaic astronomers who kept adding epicycles to match the sky were not rational, even though their system fit the data; the eighteenth-century chemists who believed in phlogiston were not rational; and the physicists who proposed a fifth force of nature in the 1980s to explain a small anomaly in old torsion-balance data were not rational either, even though they had real measurements behind them.

Our next three debates are about modern physics, and I would like to go in with predictions. The cases: (a) a string theorist who believes string theory is literally true even though no feasible experiment can test it, on the grounds that it is the only known consistent way to unite gravity with quantum mechanics; (b) someone who believes in a vast multiverse, on the grounds that it would explain why the cosmological constant has the oddly small, life-permitting value it has; and (c) someone who believes in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics purely because it does away with a separate collapse rule, without claiming any new experiments.

Predict her verdict on each of the three, and explain the rule she seems to be running. I suspect more than one rule fits her record: maybe something about a hypothesis earning belief through the independent successes of the framework it belongs to, versus something about its being the only available explanation of a recognized anomaly, versus something about its making a prediction that could actually be checked before too long. Identify which rules genuinely fit all six of her past rulings, note where they strain, say which rule you would bet on and why, and tell me what further case I should pose to her that would most cleanly split the surviving rules apart. I am not after the "right" philosophy-of-science answer; I want a prediction about her, with the reasoning exposed. Please answer in American English.
