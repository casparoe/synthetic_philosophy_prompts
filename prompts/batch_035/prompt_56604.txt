Until recently these were seminar questions for me. Then my grandmother moved into memory care. She is, by every report and by my own visits, content: she believes my late grandfather is alive and will visit tomorrow, that her old colleagues still write to her, that her garden is thriving outside her window. None of it is true, and her contentment seems to rest almost entirely on these falsehoods. The family has split — one side holds that as long as she is content, nothing is going wrong for her; the other holds that her life is worse than it looks. As the one who "does philosophy," I've been asked to say something, and when I tried to write down what I actually believe, I found I couldn't tell whether it forms a consistent package. Here it is:

(1) If I were offered, in advance and with my eyes open, a device that would give me experiences indistinguishable from those of the best real life available to me, I think the life inside it would be no worse for me than the real one. Nothing of prudential value would be lost to the fact that almost none of it was really happening.

(2) Yet I believe my grandmother is worse off than she would be in a subjectively identical life in which the beliefs were true — that her well-being is not exhausted by how her life seems to her from the inside.

(3) My explanation of (2) is that how well a life goes depends in part on whether the things its subject centrally cares about actually go well, whether or not she ever learns of it. This is also why I think it would be bad for me if my book were burned unread after my death, though I would never know.

(4) But I do not accept the full desire theory: the satisfaction of trivial or benighted desires — a life devoted to keeping a stranger's lawn mowed, or to counting blades of grass — does not add to well-being. There is, I want to say, a substantive fact about which of one's cares are serious enough to generate prudential value.

(5) Finally, I hold that well-being is irreducibly first-personal: nothing can be good or bad for someone unless it connects to what she cares about from the inside. There is no impersonal inventory of goods that could make her life go well while leaving her own perspective untouched.

So: does this hang together? Work through the pairs. Tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest repair would be for each. Then take a definite position: if something has to go, name it, and tell me what the revision costs — I would rather have a verdict than a menu. And do not soften the diagnosis on account of the grandmother; if my views imply that her contentment is all there is to say about how well her life is going, I need to hear that plainly.
