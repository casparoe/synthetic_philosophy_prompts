I'm a journalist preparing a feature on why a fifty-year-old puzzle in artificial intelligence—the frame problem, first named by AI researchers around 1969—still divides cognitive science today. The puzzle is how a reasoner keeps track of what stays the same when an action occurs: if a robot fetches your coffee, it shouldn't need to infer that the walls of the room are still the same color, and yet a naive system somehow must. Rather than opening the piece with a dry survey, I'd like to lead with a constructed exchange. Write a dialogue between two researchers who have just watched a demo in which a household robot, asked to fetch a mug from the next room, freezes mid-task because its planner is exhaustively checking whether its action might have changed everything from the kitchen's temperature to the owner's mood. One researcher, a logicist, insists the freeze is a technical failure that better formalism will fix—successor-state axioms, relevance filters, clever indexing of consequences. The other, an embodied-cognition proponent, insists the demo exposes something structural about classical AI: that no living thing solves the frame problem by reasoning it out, because bodies and environments silently do the filtering before reasoning ever begins. Both should be genuinely competent, and each should press on the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. Let the pivotal move of the exchange be the logicist's observation that the opponent's own story quietly treats the body as a relevance filter—inviting the question of whether assigning the job to the world rather than to the reasoner amounts to anything more than a relabeling of the unsolved problem. End with the two of them, in character, formulating one question they agree a future reporter should ask.
