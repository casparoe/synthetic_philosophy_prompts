I would like to know whether the following phenomenon has an established name in philosophy or psychology and, if it does not, I would appreciate help in defining it precisely, since a bare label will not be of much use to me.

Here is the pattern. The large question — whether my life, or life in general, amounts to anything — almost never occurs to me while I am doing something. Mid-task at work, absorbed in a game with my nephew, hiking with a route to follow: the question does not merely receive an answer; it does not arise. It appears almost exclusively in dead time — lying awake at three in the morning, a delayed train, the empty week between jobs — and at such moments it arrives with full force, as though the engagement had been suppressing it rather than answering it. Friends report the same. I have also noticed a second layer stacked on top: from inside the dead-time standpoint, engaged life looks arbitrary. A friend of mine, a committed furniture maker, once said from that standpoint that she could not see why she had spent ten years on it, though from inside the work the question had never once come up. Each standpoint dismisses the other: absorption makes the detached question look like a mood artifact, while detachment makes the engagement look like flight from the question.

A few details convince me this is not simply distraction. Anxiety also fills time and leaves no attention spare, yet it does not silence the question; only absorbed engagement seems to. Moreover, the silencing does not appear to require that the activity be objectively worthwhile, which is part of what unsettles me about it.

So my question is this: is there an established concept for the fact that the question of life's meaning seems to be generated by, and only fully available from, the disengaged standpoint, and extinguished by engagement? I am aware of the psychological literature on skilled absorption, but that concerns the quality of experience, whereas I am after the structure: which standpoint, if either, has the authority to answer the question, and whether anyone has treated the question's dependence on idleness as the important fact rather than an embarrassment. If nothing established covers it, I would ask you to coin a term, define it, and draw its boundaries — for instance, whether meditation, which is idle but concentrated, should count as engagement, and whether absorption in an activity one later judges to have been worthless still extinguishes the question.
