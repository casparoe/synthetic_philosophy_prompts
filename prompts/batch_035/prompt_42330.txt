I got deep into the whole moloch / multipolar trap way of thinking a while back — the idea that when lots of competitors can't coordinate, they all get dragged into whatever strategy wins even if it makes everyone worse off, and nobody's really "choosing" it, it's just the selection pressure. Like evolution but for institutions. I don't have any academic background, I just think about this stuff on my own, so bear with me.

The case I keep coming back to is doping in pro cycling in the 90s and 2000s. Everyone sorta knows the outline: the Festina affair in 1998 when police found a team car stuffed with EPO and the riders went on strike mid-Tour, then Operación Puerto in 2006 blew up a blood-doping ring with hundreds of client codenames, Floyd Landis got stripped of his 2006 Tour win for testosterone, and eventually Lance Armstrong lost all seven of his Tour titles and admitted everything to Oprah in 2013. Basically an entire generation of winners was doping.

So here's what I want you to do: take that era and actually run it through the moloch framework, carefully. Who are the "players" — is it the riders, the teams, the sponsors, the UCI itself? What was the defection strategy each of them was stuck with? What were the payoffs such that a clean rider was worse off than a doped one, and — this is the part I want you to be honest about — what did the payoff structure look like from the point of view of the fans and the race organizers who sort of knew and didn't want to look too hard? I have a suspicion that moloch-talk always frames it as riders trapped by competition, but honestly the trap also needed an audience that wanted to believe superhuman performances. Is a multipolar trap still a multipolar trap when one big player (the market for the spectacle) is kind of the reason the race to the bottom has a prize at the bottom?

Also here's my own little idea I'd like you to poke holes in. I've started distinguishing between "stiff" traps and "squishy" ones. Stiff = defecting costs the individual a lot even if nobody else defects (say, a CEO who unilaterally refuses to cut corners just loses money, forever). Squishy = the trap is only held up by everyone expecting everyone else to keep doing it, so it can collapse fast once a few people bolt. My feeling is cycling was squishy — the omertà (the code of silence) was doing most of the work, and once Landis and a few others broke it, the whole thing came down in a few years. Does that distinction hold up? Is it just a redescription of coordination games vs. something else, or is there a real difference in how the traps die?

And then two places I think the moloch lens might actually break down, and I want your honest read rather than the framework being stretched to cover everything:

1. The biological passport, introduced around 2008 — instead of testing for specific substances, they track each rider's blood values over time and flag suspicious deviations. That's a measurement/technology fix, not a coordination fix. Nobody had to trust anybody. Does moloch theory predict that kind of exit at all, or is "the trap was defeated by a better test" an anomaly it has nothing to say about?

2. Floyd Landis himself. By the trap's logic, whistleblowing looks like irrational unilateral disarmament — he burned his career and sued everyone and everyone hated him for years. But it arguably worked, or at least cracked the thing. So is he a counterexample to the idea that individuals in a multipolar trap can't escape, or was he just not playing the game the framework assumed (i.e., once you've lost your title anyway, your payoffs change and the framework was never really about you)?

Last thing: dont just conclude "moloch explains it, how tragic." I want to know where the framework is silent, where it misdescribes the actual history, and whether a cycling insider from 2004 with no theory at all would have understood the situation better than someone with the moloch framework. If the answer is basically "the framework is a vibe, not a model," say that.
