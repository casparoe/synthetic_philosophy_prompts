Write an essay on whether the conceivability argument for property dualism actually works. You know the basic setup: I can coherently conceive of a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of me that has no phenomenal experience at all, and (so the argument goes) whatever is ideally conceivable is metaphysically possible, so physicalism is false. But I want you to grapple with the wrinkle that's bugged me for years: my zombie twin would write this very essay, insist he's conscious, maybe even become a famous champion of property dualism — while getting all the physics right. So either (a) the causal chain from qualia to my beliefs about qualia does literally no work, which lands you in epiphenomenalism and makes it mysterious how I know anything about my own experience, or (b) my conviction that zombies are conceivable is generated by exactly the same cognitive machinery my zombie twin has, in wich case it's hard to see how that conviction carries any anti-physicalist weight. Take both horns seriously, bring in whichever physicalist reply you find strongest (a posteriori identity, phenomenal concepts, whatever), but don't hand me a survey and leave the verdict to me: commit to a bottom line — the argument succeeds, fails, or succeeds only given some specific extra premise that you should state explicitly and defend.
