"A long tradition models self-deception on interpersonal deception: just as a liar gets someone else to believe p while knowing that not-p, the self-deceiver gets herself to believe p while knowing that not-p. This model appears to generate two paradoxes, one concerning the state of the self-deceiver's mind and one concerning the process of self-deceiving. Present both paradoxes, and explain how Alfred Mele's deflationary account of self-deception is supposed to dissolve them. Illustrate with examples, including at least one case of twisted self-deception."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"On the traditional picture, self-deception works like lying to another person, except that both roles are played by the same person. The deceiver has to know that not-p, and the deceived has to believe p, so the self-deceiver must hold contradictory beliefs and must also intend to produce the false belief in herself. This generates two paradoxes. The static paradox concerns the time dimension: since nobody can believe p and not-p in one and the same state of mind, the deception has to be stretched out — first you know the truth, and then you gradually crowd it out of mind, while all along intending that outcome. The dynamic paradox concerns the strategy: a deceitful strategy works only if the victim doesn't know it is being run on her, but in self-deception the victim and the strategist are the same person, so the project seems doomed before it starts.

Mele's deflationary account denies that self-deception is ever intentional. On his view it is just motivationally biased belief: you come to believe something false because desire tilts the way you handle the evidence — you avoid the sources that would disconfirm what you want to believe, ask only the questions that will come back reassuring, and interpret ambiguous news favorably. Mele follows Davidson here in holding that the mind is divided into semi-independent subsystems, and he lets the biased subsystem play the deceiver while the rest of the mind stays in the dark. Since the biased processing is not a project anyone intends, neither paradox arises: there are no contradictory beliefs to be held at once, and no strategy that has to be kept secret from oneself.

The account works best for straight cases, where you wind up believing what you want to believe — for example, the person who never opens the letter with the biopsy results. But it cannot handle twisted self-deception, where someone comes to believe what she hopes is false, like the insecure partner who talks herself into thinking her spouse is unfaithful. In such cases there is no desire to believe the proposition, so there is nothing that could bias the treatment of the evidence; twisted cases must therefore arise from intentional self-manipulation after all. They show that Mele's deflationary account cannot be the whole story."
