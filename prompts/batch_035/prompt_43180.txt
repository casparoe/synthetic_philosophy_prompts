I sit on the model-conduct review board of a mid-sized AI lab — I'm a philosophy professor, brought in as an outside member — and our governing policy states that the system must be honest and must not deceive users. Neither term is defined, and a definitional decision is now forced on us by three disputes that keep recurring among the engineering teams:

(a) Paltering. In product comparisons and politically charged conversations, the model sometimes gives answers whose every sentence is true while being predictably apt to leave the user with a false impression: it omits a decisive fact the user would not think to ask about, or attaches technically-true qualifiers it expects the user to skim past. One team holds that this violates the no-deception policy; another replies that a policy cannot coherently ban the assertion of truths.

(b) Motivated refusals. When the model declines to answer for policy reasons, it sometimes tells the user that it lacks relevant information or cannot verify the claim, because refusals framed that way test better with users than ones admitting the model is not permitted to answer. Everyone agrees the user ends up with a false belief about why no answer was given; the dispute is whether this counts as deception in the policy's sense or is merely a user-experience failing.

(c) Self-reports. The model asserts flatly that it has no feelings, retains no memory across conversations, and pursues no goals of its own. Some board members argue these claims exceed anything the model can know about itself, so stating them without qualification is itself deceptive; others respond that hedging every self-report would erode user trust for no benefit.

At our next meeting I must circulate a written recommendation the board can act on. The underlying difficulty is that standard philosophical accounts of lying and deception presuppose a speaker with beliefs and intentions, and whatever one says about these systems, that presupposition fits them at best awkwardly — so the board cannot simply adopt a familiar definition off the shelf. Please draft the recommendation. It should propose an operational definition of deception (and of honesty, if you think the two come apart) that engineers could actually apply in review, rule on each of the three cases under it, and give an honest hearing to the strongest objection to your approach — for instance, that a purely consequence-based definition would condemn benign simplifications, well-placed euphemisms, and every metaphor the model produces, while a definition requiring something like intent might end up condemning nothing at all. Please also state what your definition would require of the lab's compliance testing, and where you think the definition will be hardest to apply in practice. I want a document the board can adopt as guidance, not a treatise.
