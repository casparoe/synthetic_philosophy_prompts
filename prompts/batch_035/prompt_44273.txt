I'm a graduate TA grading final essays for a third-year course, "Consequentialism and Its Critics," and one answer has me rereading it repeatedly without settling on a mark. The essay question, reproduced exactly as it appeared on the exam paper, was:

"Critics of utilitarianism have argued that it fails to take account of the separateness of persons. Explain this objection, taking care to distinguish the claim that interpersonal aggregation of welfare is impossible or meaningless from the claim that it is possible but morally constrained. Illustrate the objection with the contrast between trade-offs within a single life and trade-offs across lives. Then present the strongest utilitarian reply you can, and assess whether the objection, if sound, refutes utilitarianism outright or only limits it."

Students had roughly an hour and about 700 words. The marking scheme distributes 25 marks across four headings: reconstruction of the objection (8), the distinction between the comparability claim and the normative claim (6), engagement with a utilitarian reply (7), and overall verdict and quality of writing (4). Here is the student's essay in full:

"Utilitarianism holds that the right act is the one that produces the greatest total welfare, counting each person's welfare equally. The objection from the separateness of persons, pressed most famously by Rawls and Nozick, is that this treats many lives as if they were a single life. Within one life it can be rational to accept a loss now for a greater benefit later — a painful operation, an unpleasant degree — because it is all the same life, and the good and the bad land on the same person. But when utilitarianism licenses imposing a loss on one person for a larger gain to others, there is no single person whose life goes better overall: the cost falls on one person, the benefit on others, and no one receives the compensating balance. The theory has taken a principle of prudence for one person and applied it across persons, where it has no purchase.

The deeper point, I think, is that interpersonal comparisons of welfare are not possible. Each person's welfare registers on a private scale, and my units of satisfaction cannot be added to yours, any more than readings from two thermometers that have never been calibrated against each other can be summed. Because we are separate persons, the total that utilitarianism tells us to maximize does not exist. If that is right, the objection is devastating: utilitarianism is not merely false but unable to state its own directive, since the instruction to maximize presupposes a total, and there is none. It follows, too, that the state may not redistribute from the better off to the worse off for the sake of overall welfare — no such overall welfare exists — and Nozick draws exactly this conclusion, holding that redistribution in pursuit of a pattern treats some people as means to others' ends.

A utilitarian might reply that we manage rough interpersonal comparisons all the time, and that policy would be impossible without them. But this concedes what the objection needs: if the comparisons are only rough, the directive to maximize is only approximate, and wherever the comparison is uncertain the separateness of persons blocks the sacrifice. Alternatively the utilitarian can retreat to the level of rules, saying that institutions which respect individuals as a matter of fact maximize welfare. That seems to concede the objection at the level of principle while retaining the doctrine at the level of practice. On balance the objection succeeds: the utilitarian cannot answer it from within the theory, only by giving up aggregation, and at that point the theory is no longer utilitarian."

The comments should be specific enough that I could transfer them onto the student's paper as marginal notes, and I'd like a short breakdown of the 25 marks across the four headings at the end.
