I'm a final-year student taking an upper-level module on the limits of obligation, and our seminar leader has invited students to propose one candidate essay question each for the take-home paper on supererogation. Over the term we covered the standard trichotomy of the obligatory, the permissible and the forbidden; Urmson's old argument that the trichotomy leaves no room for the praiseworthy-but-not-required; Heyd's later attempt to systematise the category; and, most recently, the mirror category that philosophers call the suberogatory — acts that are bad, perhaps mildly blameworthy, yet not wrong. My supervisor was clear that the question must not be answerable by reciting the trichotomy and listing examples of heroic sacrifice: it has to make students take a position on something.

The tension I want the question to exploit is this. Supererogation seems to presuppose a threshold view of duty: there is a line such that everything above it is at most permitted and praiseworthy, and that line is not simply "wherever the best available outcome lies". But Singer-style demandingness arguments appear to erase the line. If the cost to me of pulling the drowning child from the pond is trivial, the rescue looks obligatory, and the same reasoning pushes outward until much of what we pre-reflectively call supererogatory giving becomes required — at which point the category starts to look like a device for protecting the comfortable. So the question should force students to say where, if anywhere, a non-arbitrary threshold for duty could sit (a fair-share norm? a reasonable-cost norm? the point at which further sacrifice would be admirable for me but compulsory for someone else?), and to say what becomes of the category if no non-arbitrary threshold exists. I would also like the question to touch on whether the suberogatory helps here: if duty is genuinely threshold-like, we should expect a mirror category of the bad-but-permissible just below the line, and some philosophers treat the fact that we recognise such a category as evidence for the threshold structure. I want students to assess that inference, not merely assert it.

Please give me: (i) the essay question itself; (ii) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (iii) a marking scheme consisting of five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, where for each point you spell out what earns the full 4, what earns 2, and what earns 0. One caution before you start: if you think my framing presupposes something false — for instance, that the demandingness argument and the conceptual point about thresholds are in tension in the way I have assumed, or that the suberogatory inference can carry less weight than I am placing on it — please say so plainly and adjust the question rather than building it on my possibly confused premise. Finally, so that I can log the question in our question-bank template, please end your response with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{[total marks], [word limit]} — for example, if you set the question at 20 marks with a 600-word model answer, the final line would read \answer{20 marks, 600 words}.
