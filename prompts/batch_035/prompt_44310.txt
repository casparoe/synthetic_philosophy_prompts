I do research-and-briefing work for a mid-size education policy nonprofit. Every month or so someone hands me a claim that leans on a heritability number -- a think-tank report saying the heritability of IQ is 60-80% so extra school funding mostly can't help poor kids, a journalist asking whether a new study "proves reading disability is genetic," a board member forwarding something about how twin studies show shared environment doesn't matter for adult outcomes. I have no genetics background, neither does anyone else here, and I typically get an afternoon per claim before I have to say something quotable. I need a procedure, not an essay.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run on these claims, in order: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with "this claim is fine as stated," where it stops with "can't tell from here, ask the person for more," and where it has to admit a step is a judgment call dressed up as a step. It has to work with the stuff I can actually get: a percentage from a paper abstract, a sample size, maybe a study design name if I'm lucky. Say what I do when I can't get the numbers at all.

The concepts I gather it needs to handle, from my own reading -- correct me if I'm framing any of these wrong: heritability being a fact about a population in an environment, not about a trait; the slide from within-group to between-group claims (the seed-and-soil thing); the equal environments assumption in twin designs; shared vs nonshared environment, including the awkward finding that shared environment comes out near zero for adult IQ, which I want to handle honestly rather than dodge; gene-environment correlation and interaction; and the point that heritability going *up* over time can mean environments got more equal, which cuts both ways politically.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the 60-80%-heritability-so-funding-doesn't-help report; (2) a claim that rising heritability of BMI over the twentieth century shows the obesity epidemic is environmental, since the gene pool didn't change; (3) a district contractor saying that because shared environment explains almost none of the variance in their adult outcome data, differences in school quality within the district (which are honestly fairly small) don't matter much. One of these should be a claim I *want* to call wrong and the procedure won't let me -- you pick which one and make me feel it. Also flag what the procedure can't do: where it would give false comfort, where it looks like it settles something it really just moved one step over.
