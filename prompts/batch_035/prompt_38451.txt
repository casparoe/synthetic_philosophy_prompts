I run public programming at a mid-sized regional history museum, and each season we receive proposals for events — lectures, traveling exhibits, panel evenings, private collections on loan — a growing fraction of which involve contested history. In the past year these have included a docent-led "mysteries of the ancients" evening that treats Atlantis as an open archaeological question, a local enthusiast of Fomenko's New Chronology who wants to argue that our conventional dating of medieval documents is a scholarly construct, a collector asserting that artifacts in his possession prove extensive pre-Columbian transoceanic contact, and — harder — a proposal on the peopling of the Americas, where the scholarly consensus has itself shifted within living memory. Our refusals have been ad hoc and inconsistent enough that the board has asked for a written procedure. The committee that would apply it consists of volunteers with roughly a semester of college history each, and they can spend perhaps an hour per proposal. Where a proposal is refused, the refusal may be appealed to the board, so the procedure needs to leave a record showing its reasoning.

Please write that decision procedure. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of pseudoscience and the study of pseudohistory actually offer on demarcation — the difference between an unorthodox hypothesis and a pseudo-inquiry, what it takes for a claim to answer to evidence rather than merely coexist with it, the role of consilience across independent lines of evidence, the standard warning signs such as claims that explain everything, hostility to falsification, and appeals to suppression by the academy — but operationalize all of this so a lay committee can run it. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask about a proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure terminates in a "no"; the points where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts (for instance, whether to host a talk the committee judges false because it draws a crowd and is honestly labeled as entertainment); and, crucially, how to distinguish a fringe-but-live scholarly question from a closed one. Because the committee cannot itself evaluate radiocarbon dating or statistical arguments about manuscript chronologies, the procedure must say which proxy indicators — how a claimant treats counter-evidence, whether the claim connects to anything outside itself, who is engaging with it — can stand in for the expertise the committee lacks.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, the Atlantis evening, which is popular with donors. Second, a talk presenting Norse presence in North America as established fact — include this case because it is the procedure's hardest test: that claim was itself considered fringe until excavation vindicated it, so the procedure must say what would have allowed a committee in the skeptical era to host such a talk without opening the door to every claim of the same shape, and it must not achieve this by knowing the answer in advance. Third, a talk arguing that a local carved stone is evidence of pre-Columbian contact, promoted by its supporters as a persecuted minority view. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do: whether it can really distinguish a claim that is wrong from a claim that is not even being investigated, and how the committee should handle proposals that are wrong in ways the committee considers harmless but that bring in visitors who then trust our other labels a little less.
