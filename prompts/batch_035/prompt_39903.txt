I've been arguing with myself about the ethics of having children, and I've collected my honest reactions to a set of cases without trying to force them into a system. I'd like to know whether some principle captures them all, or whether I have to give some of them up.

A couple learns in advance that, for genetic reasons, their child would have a life that is genuinely not worth living — nothing but pain. I think conceiving that child would be clearly wrong.

A couple simply decides not to have a child who, had she existed, would have had a wonderful life. I don't think they've done anything wrong. There's no one who is worse off because of their choice.

A couple knows their child will have a serious chronic illness and a lot of suffering, but a life that is clearly worth living; the child will grow up to be glad to exist. I think conceiving is permissible.

A woman on a medication is told by her doctor to wait one month before trying to conceive, because the drug causes limb deformities in embryos. She doesn't wait, and her child is born with malformed limbs but is, by all accounts, happy to be alive. I still feel she did something wrong — I'd blame her for not waiting. Yet if she had waited, she would have had a different, healthy child, not a healthy version of this child, so it's not obvious the child she actually had is worse off than the alternative.

A couple who are both carriers of a painful hereditary condition conceive anyway rather than adopting or using a donor, knowing their child will have decades of suffering but a life worth living. Structurally this looks the same as the medication case to me — conceive now and get a suffering child, or take another route and get a healthy one — but my verdicts differ: here I feel no blame at all.

During IVF, a couple has two viable embryos, one that will be deaf and one that will hear, both expected to have good lives. They choose the deaf one, for reasons connected to their community. Choosing that embryo feels wrong to me, in a way conceiving a child who will be deaf doesn't. I can't explain the difference.

Finally, a couple knows their child would have ten good years and then a painful death. I go back and forth on this one constantly, and I suspect my answer depends on where exactly the line between worth living and not worth living sits — but I can't see why a line in that location should be doing moral work at all, since the child on either side of it is glad, or would be glad, to exist.

Is there a single principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or close to it? If one of them has to go, I'd like to know which and why, and what the surviving principle says about the ten-good-years case. If more than one candidate principle is live, tell me what each one predicts for a case I haven't mentioned.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
