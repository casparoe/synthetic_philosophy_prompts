I'm a philosophy PhD student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and animal ethics, and I'm putting together an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship. I have a half-formed idea I can't seem to make precise on my own, and I want someone to turn it into an actual proposal document -- problem statement, method, deliverables, the whole thing. No citations needed, I'll slot those in myself later.

The topic is the scale argument for insect welfare, and what I think is a hole in how it gets formulated. You know the argument: there are something like ten quintillion insects at any moment, so even if you assign only a small probability -- say one percent -- to insects being sentient, the expected number of insect experiences is astronomically larger than the expected number of vertebrate experiences, and if suffering badness scales linearly with the number of sufferers, insect welfare dominates practically everything else in practical ethics. People in the effective animal advocacy world treat this as close to decisive.

My worry, which I can't quite nail down, is about the kind of uncertainty the argument is using. It treats "is sentient" as a binary fact about which we hold a crisp credence, and then multiplies that credence through the arithmetic. But my dissertation work on the neuroscience side has me convinced that the underlying question is more like a graded, multidimensional thing -- where an organism falls on dimensions like valenced experience, temporal integration, affective richness, whatever -- and nobody thinks the graded story maps cleanly onto a sharp cutoff we're merely ignorant of. If the uncertainty is dimensional rather than binary, I suspect the expected-value computation doesn't just give a wrong answer, it doesn't even have a well-defined input. But I also suspect there's an opposing move where you rescue the scale argument by treating the dimensions themselves as credence-weighted, and I honestly don't know if that rescue works or just pushes the problem back a level.

So the proposal I need should: state the research question precisely enough that it has an answer (something like: under what models of sentience-attribution does the scale argument go through, and is one of those models one we have reason to accept?); explain why the existing positions -- the straightforward EV version on one side and the various "small probabilities of huge stakes are suspect" replies on the other -- leave it open, since both sides seem to just assume the binary-credence framing; describe what I'd actually do over the year, including some engagement with decision theory since I have a feeling there's a formal result lurking about when EV reasoning is licensed over vaguely constituted quantities; say what a positive result would look like for people at animal welfare orgs who are currently allocating real money partly on the strength of this argument; and be honest about what would count as the project failing, e.g. if it turns out everything I want to say reduces to "use a sensitivity analysis and pick a defensible credence," which would be embarrassingly boring.

One more thing: my advisor is a resolute defender of the scale argument and a fan of expected-value maximization generally, so if the proposal could frame the project as sharpening the argument's foundations rather than attacking it, that'd help me at home as well as with the fellowship committee. That's a bit cynical maybe, but there it is.
