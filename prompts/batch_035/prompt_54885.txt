I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of decision theory and formal epistemology, and I'm planning a dissertation chapter on whether coherence arguments actually succeed in showing that rational agents must maximize expected utility. Before I criticize particular versions, I want to be clearer than I currently am about what success would even amount to. Write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate coherence argument would have to meet if it is to license the normative conclusion that a rational agent's preferences must conform to expected utility theory.

By coherence argument I mean the whole family: synchronic and diachronic Dutch book arguments, money pump arguments, and representation theorems in the style of Savage and of von Neumann and Morgenstern — arguments that try to extract the full expected-utility structure from something more primitive, such as the requirement not to be exploitable, or the bare requirement that preferences admit of a representation at all.

The desiderata I can formulate for myself include, at least: that the argument derive probabilities, utilities, and the maximization rule from premises that are independently more plausible than the conclusion, so that it does not simply restate the independence axiom in diachronic dress; that it be explicit about the modality of the exploitation claim — exploitable by whom, under what market and trading structure, and at what cost to the exploitee; that it say something about agents who are money-pumped but end up no worse off, or worse off only by the lights of the very preferences that generated the cycle; that it confront the reinterpretation problem, since a representation theorem seems at best to tell us how to read an agent's preferences as probability-and-utility talk, not how the agent is to choose among them; and that it take seriously agents with incomplete or context-dependent preferences, since completeness and partition invariance look like idealizations rather than norms.

Extend and correct that list. For each desideratum, give the motivating case — the agent, scenario, or theorem that puts pressure on arguments lacking it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for reaching the normative conclusion and which a defender could waive as harmless idealization. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; if a subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so, since then the list itself is the interesting result. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the arguments actually on offer. That last part is what I most want, because my hunch is that no version of the argument both satisfies the primitive-premises requirement and covers agents with ordinary attitudes to risk — but I would like to know whether that hump is real or an artifact of the requirements I am imposing.

If useful, close with a brief pass at how the main families — Dutch book, money pump, representation theorem — fare against the list. But keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring I can do myself once the spec is settled.
