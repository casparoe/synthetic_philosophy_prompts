I co-run a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent the past two semesters on the extended mind, from the original Otto-and-the-notebook thought experiment through the second wave's talk of complementarity, and we have hit the same wall the literature hit years ago. The strange thing is that the empirical claims are all agreed on. Nobody denies that the man with the notebook and the woman with intact biological memory behave alike in all the relevant respects; nobody denies that the internal machinery is hasty and opportunistic, cobbled together from whatever worked rather than optimally designed; nobody even denies that phones and laptops now do for many of us much of what memory, attention, and calculation once did. What nobody grants is the conclusion. The critics keep pressing the coupling-constitution distinction: a process's being causally dependent on some external resource is not the resource's being a constituent of the process — my stove's being coupled to the gas main does not make the pipeline part of the cooking. The defenders keep replying that this begs the question, since it assumes the very mark of the cognitive the thesis was meant to call into question, namely that cognition must be realized in the old biological core, and they note that the critics never say much about what the inner mark is supposed to be, beyond an appeal to non-derived content whose own origins are murky. By now the objections and replies are so predictable that our group could script each side's next move in advance.

My working hypothesis — which I want you to take seriously but not simply ratify — is that the disagreement is not about any fact at all: both sides accept all the same facts about Otto and his notebook, and the fight is over where to draw a line that resembles a national border, drawn by convention and revisable, rather than a coastline, fixed by nature. A comparison along those lines, which I half-remember from a foreword to one of the books and can no longer place, has stuck with me for exactly this reason. But I am not comfortable resting on this either, because the dispute does not feel verbal in the way a fight over whether a tomato is a vegetable is verbal. Both sides behave as though something real is at stake — about what a person is, about where a thinker stops and the world begins, perhaps about ownership of one's own thoughts.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute over the phrase "cognitive system"; a substantive disagreement about facts that no one has yet managed to state cleanly; or two different questions being run under one heading — say, a descriptive question about where psychological processes in fact happen, and a constitutive or normative question about what belongs to the thinker, with the parties lining up on opposite sides of that divide without noticing? Ground the diagnosis in what the parties actually say: the parity principle, the coupling-constitution fallacy as the critics deploy it, the retreat some critics made to a weaker thesis of embeddedness, and the second wave's quiet abandonment of parity in favor of complementarity. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper mark of the cognitive, some specific kind of empirical work, an open stipulation that the boundary is conventional, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why that is so.
