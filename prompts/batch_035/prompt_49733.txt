I'm a network engineer with about fifteen years in protocol design and operations. My native vocabulary is the world of layers, headers, payloads, handshakes, and agreed-upon encodings — the OSI stack and TCP/IP in practice, from the physical layer up through application protocols. For fun I've been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I've reached chapter 6, The Location of Meaning, where he proposes that every message really involves three messages: a frame message, which just flags the carrier as decodable and invites you to try (a book looks like a book; a record looks like a record); an outer message, the information about which decoding scheme to apply, which you have to crack before you can extract anything at all; and an inner message, the actual content. His punchline, as I understand it, is that meaningfulness is smeared between the artifact and the receiver, with the decoding machinery living largely on the receiving side.

Translate that trichotomy into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Map frame, outer, and inner onto the stack: which part of a packet exchange plays each role, and where the pre-agreed protocols — the thing both endpoints must already share before any communication is possible — sit relative to his three categories. Then give me the residue report, because I suspect it is where the real content is. In my world, every layer works only because both ends agreed on it in advance; there is no packet whose header teaches you, from nothing, how to read headers. Hofstadter, by contrast, seems to hold that outer messages can be recognized without prior agreement — that is why he thinks a record dropped by aliens, or DNA read by machinery that evolved alongside it, or a book in a language you don't know still carries all three messages. Is that difference expressible in my vocabulary at all, or does it fall outside the stack precisely because the stack assumes the very thing he is analyzing? Relatedly: his claim that meaning is partly located in the receiver looks to me like the observation that decoding is not in the message — and if so, I can't tell whether that is a deep point or a truism that my field quietly bakes in and never thinks about.

Structure the answer in four named sections, in this order, and keep the whole thing under a thousand words. First, The translation (roughly a third of the answer): term by term — frame, outer, inner, and the prior agreement — stated in stack vocabulary, without smuggling in Hofstadter's assumptions and calling them translation. Second, What survives: the places where the mapping is clean and a network engineer would nod along. Third, The residue: what has no counterpart in the stack vocabulary, with a plain verdict on whether the untranslatable part reflects a blind spot in my vocabulary or an overreach on his part. Fourth, Bottom line, three or four sentences: is the engineer's instinct — that this is all convention-sharing, which we happen to call protocols — correct, or is it missing something? Don't hedge the residue, and don't pad the translation with stack trivia I already know.
