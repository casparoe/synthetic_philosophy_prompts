"The Mytilene episode is the clearest case of structural failure. When Mytilene revolted in 428, the assembly, on Cleon's motion, voted to execute the entire male population and sell the rest into slavery, and a trireme was dispatched to carry the decree out. The next day the Athenians regretted it and held a second debate. This time Diodotus argued against Cleon and won: his point was that clemency was the smart policy, since sparing the Mytileneans would deter other allies from ever revolting in the first place, whereas the massacre would only make future rebels fight to the bitter end instead of surrendering. A second trireme rowed day and night and arrived just in time to stop the decree, so in the end only the ringleaders were executed. This shows the structural feature most clearly: the dēmos could reverse itself overnight — Thucydides even has Cleon complain that the assembly is at the mercy of whatever the last speaker said — and an institution that flip-flops like that is unstable by design.

The Sicilian debate shows the opposite, that the flaw was individual. Nicias spoke against the expedition and warned the assembly plainly that it was far too dangerous, but Alcibiades won the day with promises of easy conquest and support from sympathizers in Sicily, and the assembly voted to go. So the institution worked as designed here: a serious warning was heard and weighed, and the dēmos simply picked the more persuasive man. That's a failure of judgment about a person, not of the machinery, and it could happen in any system that votes.

The trial of Socrates is the strongest structural case of all. Demoralized by the Sicilian disaster, the assembly put Socrates on trial in 399 for criticizing the democracy and corrupting the young, convicted him by a comfortable majority, and, when he refused to propose a serious alternative penalty at the penalty stage, voted for death. A democracy that executes its most famous dissident for asking questions has failed structurally.

In sum, two of the three episodes (Mytilene and Socrates) reveal structural flaws, while Sicily looks more contingent, driven by Alcibiades' ambition."
