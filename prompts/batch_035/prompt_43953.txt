I run a training workshop for human-rights advocates and journalists on the politics of naming atrocities, and I need a decision tree that takes a user from a bare factual description of a mass atrocity to the right label for it — not the emotionally resonent label, the defensible one. The point of the exercise is to show that these labels are not synonyms for "very bad mass violence": each one carries distinct legal consequences and each one is contested for reasons that are historical and political, not merely semantic.

The tree must begin from facts alone (who did what to whom, at what scale, in what context, with what apparent purpose) and end at one of these leaves:

- genocide as defined in the 1948 Convention;
- genocide in Raphael Lemkin's original, broader sense, which treated the destruction of a group's culture — its language, religion, and institutions — as part of the crime;
- politicide, Barbara Harff's term for the destruction of groups defined by political status or opposition rather than communal characteristics;
- democide, Rudolph Rummel's term for mass killing by governments that need not target any group at all;
- ethnic cleansing, understood as expulsion from a territory rather than destruction of a group;
- crimes against humanity;
- and an "unlabeled" leaf for cases that fit none of the above.

Specifications:

1. Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the labels second; do not simply chain together a list of definitions. Branch questions must be answerable from facts about the case, phrased so that someone with no legal training can answer without knowing which label the answer leads to. The following distinctions must appear as branch points somewhere: (a) whether the victims are defined by nationality, ethnicity, race, or religion — the only categories the Convention protects, a restriction that was itself the product of drafting-stage politics, since political groups were deliberately excluded, which is why politicide must live at a different leaf; (b) whether the perpetrator's plan requires the purpose of destroying the group as such or only knowledge that destruction will be the near-certain byproduct of pursuing another objective — the live interpretive dispute between purpose and knowledge standards for the Convention's special intent; (c) whether the harm extends beyond killing and serious bodily and mental harm to the imposition of destructive conditions of life and to cultural destruction, which mattered to Lemkin but did not survive into the Convention; (d) whether the acts are widespread or systematic, which crimes against humanity requires but genocide, strictly, does not; (e) whether the violence occurs in the context of an armed conflict.

2. No node may stipulate intent as a bare fact. Somewhere the tree must show — or honestly admit it cannot show — how, if at all, genocidal intent can be inferred from conduct like the pattern of the killings, the selection of victims, or the words of officials.

3. Each leaf must carry three things: the label; one sentence on the practical consequence of applying it, which for the genocide leaf should note that naming it triggers duties to prevent and punish under the Convention — and that this obligation bites once a state knew or should have known of a serious risk, which is precisely why states able to act are so reluctant to say the word, a dynamic made vivid when the International Court of Justice's 2007 judgment in Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro found Serbia liable not for committing the Srebrenica genocide but for failing to do what it reasonably could to prevent it; and one sentence on that leaf's most awkward commitment.

4. Flag at least one pair of leaves the tree cannot separate from facts alone. I suspect purpose-genocide and knowledge-genocide are one such pair, since both can present identical conduct on the ground; if you disagree, say why and find another genuinely inseparable pair instead.

5. After the tree, add a section on unoccupied combinations: identify at least two regions of your logical space where no accepted label lives, and diagnose for each whether the region is empty because the events are hard to imagine, because the label was politically blocked at some drafting table, or because the category itself is incoherent.

6. Close with a short analytical paragraph on the gap between Lemkin's concept and the Convention's, arguing for or against the claim that the narrowing of the concept during drafting was itself a political act with identifiable victims, and one on why "ethnic cleansing" so often functions as a euphemism — a way for third-party states to acknowledge atrocity without acknowledging obligation.

Present the tree as a nested outline with clearly numbered branch questions, keep the total response under roughly 1,200 words, and write for an audience of educated non-specialists. Do not simply reproduce the Convention's articles in order; the tree should be usable by someone holding a single-page factual summary of an atrocity and wondering, in the hardest cases, what they are permitted to call it.
