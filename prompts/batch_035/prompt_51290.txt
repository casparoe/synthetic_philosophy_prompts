My logic prof gave us the classic surprise exam setup but with a twist that I cant get out of my head. Five-day week, exam on exactly one day, and the announcement is worded so that "surprise" is precise: on the morning of the exam day, you will not be able to prove, from the announcement plus the fact that the exam hasn't happened yet, that the exam is that day. My friend says this is dead on arrival and walked me through it: (1) If the exam hasn't happened by Thursday night, it must be Friday, and thats provable from the announcement, so Friday can't be the surprise day. (2) With Friday eliminated, the same reasoning kills Thursday, and so on backwards through the whole week. (3) So the announcement is provably false — no day satisfies it. (4) But here's the part that wrecks me: if we accept (3), we've proven the announcement false, so we can't prove anything from it, which means the exam can happen on literally any day and the announcement comes out true. It's false if we believe it and true if we don't. (5) My friend now insists this generalizes: no one can ever announce anything at an unpredictable time — not even the delivery company text saying your package arrives this week at a time you wont be able to deduce. That feels like too much to concede. You're a professional philosopher of logic, please think this through carefully, maybe brainstorm a couple of readings of what the announcement commits the teacher to before committing yourself: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep the backward-induction reasoning in (1) and (2) than bite the generalization in (5), but tell me honestly what I'm paying for that.
