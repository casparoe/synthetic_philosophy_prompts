I'm the data governance lead at a mid-size agritech company, which in practice means I chair the fortnightly ontology review meeting where our different teams try to keep a shared crop/pest knowledge graph from falling apart. Every few weeks we get the same fight in a different costume, and it's eating our sprint time. Latest round: one team models a barley variety as a class whose members are the individual plants, another models the variety as a single historical lineage that individual plants are *parts* of, and both are convinced the other side has made a factual error rather than a modeling choice. Similar blowups about whether a "field" is the legal parcel or the planted area when the two diverge (we lease half of one field to a neighbor who farms it differently), and whether a "drought event" is a disposition a region has or a process that unfolds over months. I've started to suspect that at least some of these are what philosophers would call merely verbal disputes — that nobody actually disagrees about which plants exist or what data we'd write down, and we're just using words differently — but some of them are genuinely substantive, because the two models produce different query results or different predictions, and I can't currently tell which fights are which.

I took one philosophy course in college, so I know just enough to be dangerous: I remember there being a literature on how to tell whether a dispute is verbal or substantive (some kind of substitution or elimination test? swapping the word out and seeing if a disagreement survives?), and something vaguely Carnapian about internal questions you can answer inside a framework versus external questions about the framework itself, and I gather there are also serious metaphysicians who would insist some of these questions are perfectly real and not verbal at all. That's about the depth I have. What I need is this stuff turned into something my review board — six engineers, one hour, no philosophers — can actually run when one of these fights breaks out: an ordered set of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure can stop and say "this one is just words, pick a convention," where it has to say "no, this one is substantive, you actually have to go look at the data," and crucially where it has to admit the question is real but not resolvable by us before the sprint ends, so it hands the decision to a named person on values/utility grounds rather than pretending the procedure settled it.

A few constraints. Whatever tests you give us have to be runnable by engineers with the entities in front of them, not by people who've read the literature — so if a philosophical distinction won't survive that translation, tell me and drop it rather than smuggling it in as jargon. The procedure also needs to handle the awkward middle case I keep running into: a dispute that IS verbal in the sense that nothing in the world turns on it, but where the convention we pick still has real downstream costs (query performance, who maintains the ontology, what the field agronomists are used to) — a verdict of "verbal" can't just end the meeting, it has to route us to a principled way of choosing between equally "correct" options. Finally, run the procedure on the three cases above (variety-as-class vs variety-as-individual, the leased field, the drought event), and don't go easy on me: I've been insisting the variety is a class for four years, and if the procedure says I was having a verbal dispute this whole time and just didn't notice, I want it to say that. Also flag honestly where you think the procedure is most likely to misfire — I'd rather know now than find out from a support ticket.
