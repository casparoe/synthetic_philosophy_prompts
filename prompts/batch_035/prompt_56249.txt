I am a teaching assistant marking a second-year philosophy essay, and I would be grateful for a detailed and fair assessment of it. The essay question set for the class was as follows:

"Empirical psychology appears to show that human behaviour is governed far more by trivial features of immediate situations than by stable character traits. Does this threaten Aristotelian virtue ethics? Defend your answer."

The student submitted the following essay:

"Situationism is the view, supported by empirical psychology, that what people do is determined mostly by their circumstances rather than by their characters. In Milgram's experiments, most subjects obeyed instructions to administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks, and in the famous Good Samaritan study, seminary students hurrying to give a talk about helping largely ignored a stranger slumped in a doorway. Studies of schoolchildren's cheating likewise found that how often a child cheats varies enormously from one classroom task to the next. Taken together, this evidence suggests that people do not possess robust character traits such as honesty or compassion.

Aristotelian virtue ethics holds that the good life consists in the exercise of the virtues, which are stable dispositions to feel and act well, acquired through habituation. Aristotle himself stated that an act is right if and only if it is what a virtuous person would characteristically do in the circumstances. He also believed that virtue is common: anyone brought up properly would come to possess the virtues, and the fully virtuous person is an idealisation rather than a realistic human possibility. If the situationists are right that such stable dispositions are largely absent from ordinary people, then Aristotle's empirical anthropology is refuted, and virtue ethics should be abandoned in favour of normative theories, such as consequentialism, that make no appeal to character.

The virtue ethicist has several replies. First, Aristotelian virtues are not mere behavioural frequencies: honesty, as Aristotle understands it, involves judging how and when truth-telling is appropriate in particular circumstances, so variation across situations is exactly what we should expect of a genuinely virtuous person. Second, since even situationists concede that people differ somewhat — some are slightly more helpful than others across situations — character traits do exist after all, and the challenge dissolves. Third, one can concede everything the situationists say about people as they actually are, and simply note that the situationist findings concern the average person, not the rare person of practical wisdom.

In conclusion, the situationist evidence is serious but not decisive. It does not refute virtue ethics, but it does show that virtue ethicists must take seriously the social conditions under which character is formed — something Aristotle, with his emphasis on habituation within the polis, would himself have welcomed."
