I'm a CS PhD student sitting in on a philosophy of language seminar, and the course reader has a chapter on indicative conditionals that ends with a paragraph on Lewis's triviality results that I keep going back and forth on. The chapter puts it like this: Lewis showed that if an indicative conditional is the kind of thing that can be true or false, and so can appear wherever a probability function can see it, then its probability has to be the corresponding conditional probability, P(if A then B) = P(B|A). But he then proved that this identity can only hold in degenerate cases: by repeatedly conditionalizing you can force conditional and unconditional probabilities to coincide, so the identity only survives where the antecedent makes no difference to the consequent. Since the identity plainly does hold for ordinary conditionals, something has to give, and the chapter says the upshot is that conditionals have no truth conditions at all — they don't express propositions, they just carry an assertibility given by conditional probability, the line Adams and later Edgington took — so that uttering 'if A then B' is really just reporting that your P(B|A) is high. Is this a fair rendering of the results? Please tell me which parts a philosopher of language would sign off on, where the simplifications start, and whether any part is just wrong. Im especially unsure about the last move, from the formal result to 'conditionals just signal high conditional probability' — to me it smells like its sliding from an epistemic thesis about how confident you should be into a semantic thesis about what conditionals mean, but I don't trust my nose here.
