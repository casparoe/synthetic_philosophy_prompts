I work at a local benefits office — no academic background at all, just someone who reads and writes official letters all day, and I've been chewing on something on my own for a couple of years and want somebody to actually think it through with me.

A while back our government brought in a plain-language rule: every letter we send to the public has to be understandable on one reading. There's software that checks it. It wants short sentences, active voice, everyday words, roughly a grade-8 reading level or under. We all got a half-day training on it. Text that's legally binding gets a carve-out, and there's a small team that rewrites the templates.

The reason I keep thinking about this is Orwell, who I read once in school. The bit that stuck with me is where he says "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible." So the rule we work under seems to be built straight on that idea: bad official language is a habit, habits spread by imitation, fix the surface of the language and the thinking (and eventually the government behind it) gets cleaner.

But here's what I see from my desk, and it doesn't fit. The clearest letter we send out is the one saying "your benefit will be reviewed" — which everyone in the office knows means it's going down. Short sentences, active voice, everyday words. Scores perfect on the software. And honestly it works better than the old jargon version ever did at getting people to miss the appeal deadline, because now they actually read it. Meanwhile the letters where the person genuinely needs to understand their options — reconsideration rights, hardship payments, all that — are exactly the ones with the legal carve-out, so they stay unreadable. Plain language got attached to the letters where being understood helps the department, and the jargon survived where being understood would help the claimant. It's like we mandated plain language but never plain dealing, and it turns out you can lie in short active sentences just fine.

So here's my question. What does a rule like this actually presuppose about language and politics? As far as I can work out it seems to assume at least three things: (1) that bad official language is a habit — something that spreads accidentally and can be fixed with a checklist — rather than a tool that gets used on purpose when someone wants to hide what they're doing; (2) that clarity is a surface property of a text, something a readability score can measure, so it's separable from what the writer is up to; and (3) that the reader and the department have the same interest in the letter being understood. I also half remember Orwell saying somewhere that the real enemy of clear language is insincerity, not long words — I might not have that word for word. If that's roughly right, then a readability score can't tell a clear truth from a clear trap, and our rule quietly assumes he was wrong about that, even while citing the part of his essay that sounds like it supports the rule.

So tell me: which of those three presuppositions does the practice really need, and which could it drop and still function? Is the practice actually consistent with Orwell if you read him carefully — he did think clearing the habits was a first step, not a guarantee — or does it take the reversible-process part and quietly drop the insincerity part, and is that a distortion? And then the thing I really want to know: if you built the rule on the insincerity view instead, what would it even look like? My guess is it'd be less like a readability score and more like a duty to put the unwelcome part first — the thing the reader least wants to hear in the first sentence. But is that the kind of thing you can make a measurable rule out of at all, or is it more like honesty, where the moment you try to score it you've turned it into something else? And be straight with me about which parts of what I've said hold up and which parts I've gotten carried away with — I've had nobody to check any of this against.
