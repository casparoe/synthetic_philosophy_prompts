My closest friend and I spent four hours last night on the statue and the clay, and we ended precisely where we began, which is why I am writing to you. The setup, in case it matters: a sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue. The standard puzzle is that the statue and the lump seem to share every physical property — same location, same mass, same atoms — and yet differ modally, since the lump could survive being squashed flat while the statue could not, and the statue arguably did not exist before the shaping while the lump did. By the indiscernibility of identicals, they cannot be one thing; yet it strikes many people as absurd that a single region contains two distinct objects, and worse, that it contains billions once you count every statue-shaped lump, every hair-covered cat alongside Tibbles, every aggregation of atoms with a slightly different persistence profile.

My friend took the one-object side. She said that admitting the statue and the clay as distinct is a metaphysical bookkeeping trick: no observation, no physics, nothing in the world could distinguish them, so the "extra" object is a shadow cast by language. I took the two-object side, or tried to: I argued that if identity is a real relation, the modal facts settle it, and that her view owes us an account of which of the two survival conditions the single object has — it cannot have both, since a thing cannot both survive squashing and not survive it. She replied that the modal facts are not about objects at all but about our ways of counting and tracking them, so there is nothing there for the identity relation to be about. I accused her of changing the subject from metaphysics to semantics; she accused me of mistaking grammar for ontology. We ran through the familiar escape routes — temporal parts, sortals as individuators, relative identity, the elimination of the lump, constitution as a primitive non-identity relation — and each of us found the others' favorites unmoving for reasons neither of us could articulate beyond appeal to intuition.

Here is what I actually want, and it is not a verdict on the statue. What struck me afterward is that this debate has been running in essentially this form for half a century, with the same handful of arguments on each side, and that positions seem to be almost perfectly predicted by antecedent temperament rather than by any argument. That suggests the real disagreement is not where we thought it was. So: diagnose the stalemate. Is the dispute substantive — a genuine difference about how many objects occupy that region — or verbal, hinging on whether "exists," "identity," or "same object" is being used univocally? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under one heading, one about counting and ontology, the other about persistence and individuation? Or is the puzzle a case where our concept of objecthood simply runs out, so that every answer is coherent and none is correct? My tentative hypothesis is that the fight over indiscernibility is a proxy for a deeper disagreement about whether identity conditions are discovered in things or imposed by sortals, and that neither side can argue for its view without presupposing it — but I would like that hypothesis examined rather than confirmed. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, drawing on what the parties to it actually say, and then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a test case, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no progress is possible here and why. I would especially like to know whether the proliferation of coincidence cases in the literature — the cat, the house, the person and the body — has taught us anything, or whether it is merely the same intuition pump with new furniture.
