my brother-in-law works in supply-chain optimization for a huge retailer, and lately he's gotten really into the old socialist calculation debate. his take: Mises and Hayek were right for their era that no planning bureau could do the math, but the whole thing is obsolte now. The market, he says, is basically just a big analog computer that iterates on prices until supply and demand clear. He points out that Lange himself came around to something like this near the end of his life -- wrote roughly that the market had been a primitive computing device and that electronic computers could now take over the job. So on my brother-in-law's story, the market is a distributed solver, a slow and lossy one, and modern optimization is just a better solver for the same problem: his team runs ML-driven demand forecasts and pricing across tens of thousands of SKUs, and he figures if that works for one firm, it would work for a whole economy given the hardware. Case closed.

Meanwhile a friend of mine who's very into the Austrians makes the exact opposite move with what sounds like the same analogy: prices are a messaging system, a kind of telecom network that aggregates information no central node ever has. On his version no computer can replace the market no matter how fast, because the relevant information isn't written down anywhere -- it's local, tacit, of-the-moment, and half the people who know it would misreport it if you asked, since they're strategic actors with no reason to tell a planner the truth.

Both of them are leaning on "the market is a computer" and drawing opposite conclusions, and honestly i can't tell which one is cheating. Can you audit this for me? I'd like a correspondence table first: in the market-as-computer mapping, spell out what plays the role of the processor, the memory, the input data, the objective function, and the iteration step -- done once for the Lange side (trial-and-error price adjustment by a planning board) and once for the Hayek side (decentralized price signals). Then lay out where the mapping breaks, and be clear about which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions being drawn versus which ones are just interesting: where do the preferences come from, who generates the new products that aren't in anyone's dataset yet, what makes anyone report their local conditions truthfully, and is the problem solved once and for all or does it have to stay solved as everything keeps shifting. And then give me the verdict on both: does my brother-in-law's "case closed" survive once you notice that what his company optimizes is a fixed catalog of SKUs with demand curves that somebody else's market process paid to discover -- or was the analogy carrying his conclusion the whole time? And does my friend's "no computer ever" survive, or is he doing the same trick in reverse by smuggling tacit knowledge in as if it were an eternal fact about computation rather than a claim about what gets written down and when? If neither conclusion survives, tell me what a better version of the analogy would be, or whether the whole framing is the problem.
