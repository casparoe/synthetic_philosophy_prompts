Last month a colleague asked me, in the corridor, whether the committee had reached a decision on her case. The provost had denied her tenure that morning; the letter had not yet gone out. I told her, truthfully, that no decision had been communicated to me — and I chose those words precisely so that she would conclude the committee was still deliberating. She did conclude that; she went home and told her family she was hopeful, and made plans on that hope. Every sentence I uttered was true, and at the time I took a certain satisfaction in that, as though I had honored a scruple rather than dodged a duty. Since then a friend has been pressing an argument on me, and it has unsettled me more than my guilt did, because it refuses to stop at my case.

His argument runs as follows. (1) If lying and merely misleading are morally different, the difference must lie in some feature the two acts do not share. (2) They share everything that seems to matter for responsibility: both speakers intend the hearer to come away with a specific false belief, both design their words to produce it, both foresee her acting on it, and both could prevent it by saying more; in both cases the hearer reaches the falsehood by her own inference from things that are, strictly, true. (3) What they do not share is only this: the liar asserts the false proposition, while the misleader asserts only truths and lets the hearer draw the conclusion. (4) But a moral difference between two acts must be a difference in what is done to the person — and the same thing is done to the hearer in both cases: she is brought to believe what is false and to act on it. (5) Therefore lying and misleading are morally on par. (6) Moreover, the reasoning is a template: wherever two courses of conduct differ only in the route by which the same intended effect on the same person is produced, they are on par. (7) So the business partner who lets his colleague go on believing the firm is solvent — intending that belief, saying not a word — is on all fours with the partner who tells him it is solvent; and the expert witness who answers every question with truths so arranged that the jury draws the intended false conclusion is on all fours with one who commits perjury outright. (8) And so, he tells me, what I did to my colleague was exactly as bad as lying to her; my truth-telling scruple was theater.

I cannot make myself accept (7), at least as it concerns the silent partner, and yet I cannot see where the chain breaks. My suspicion keeps landing on (4), which presents itself as a neutral principle about where moral differences can live but seems, on reflection, to mean that moral differences must be differences in outcomes produced — which is precisely what is at issue, and which would beg the question against anyone who thinks a speaker can do something to a hearer in the act of asserting. But when I try to say this carefully, I find I am just announcing that moral differences can attach to the type of act rather than to its effects, which is the very thing the friend is denying; and if I reject (4) outright, I owe him an account of why the route to an outcome can matter here without mattering everywhere, which he will immediately test against cases like refilling a poisoned glass. So I am asking you to do what I cannot: walk through the argument, tell me which step to reject, and explain why that step looked innocent when I first read it. Then price the alternatives — including the friend's, which is to accept the template and treat my engineered truth, the silent partner's silence, and the outright lie as the same wrong. And tell me, finally, whether my satisfaction at having spoken only truths was the residue of a real distinction or, as he claims, self-congratulation. I would most like to keep both (2), which strikes me as an honest inventory of what I did, and my conviction about the silent partner; I am beginning to suspect I cannot keep both, and I want to know which one to surrender and what that surrender costs.
