Six months ago I became the daytime caregiver for my mother, who has advancing dementia. Several times a day she asks where my father is; he died in 2020. I have stopped telling her, because each time she receives the news as if for the first time, grieves, forgets, and asks again within the hour. Instead I tell her he is at the office and will be home for dinner. I had always thought of myself as someone who does not lie, and that self-image did not survive the first week.

In an attempt to think this through properly, I have been reading Augustine's On Lying, where he argues that every lie is wrong — including the charitable kind — and that when someone asks a question they have no right to have answered, the correct response is to refuse to answer or to remain silent, never to deceive. I want to believe he is wrong, but not merely because his conclusion is inconvenient, so I have been cataloguing my actual verdicts on cases, to see whether some principle is hiding in them. Here is the catalogue.

Telling my mother my father is at the office: fine. I will do it again today, and I do not intend to stop.
Telling a woman I know to be searching for my undocumented neighbour that he moved away in the spring: fine, and I would not lose sleep over it.
Telling my aunt I love the sweater she knitted: fine, though I feel slightly uncomfortable about it.
Saying to a chronically late friend, as he walks in forty minutes late, that his punctuality is a marvel of the age: not a lie at all. Though on Augustine's definition — saying something contrary to what one has in mind — it seems to count, so something has to give either in my verdict or in the definition.
Telling a creditor the check is in the mail when it is not: not fine.
Telling my nephew that his dog, which had to be put down, went to live on a farm upstate: I keep going back and forth, and I have noticed that I want to say it is fine when my sister does it and not fine when a stranger does.
Answering my mother's question about my father not with a falsehood but with a redirection — asking whether she would like to help me make lunch: not merely fine but not even a candidate for lying, so far as my gut is concerned.
Telling a phone scammer that the elderly man he is asking about does not live at this address: fine.

What I would like, then, is an audit of my own views rather than a lecture on Augustine's. Specifically: Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts — and if so, state it precisely enough that it delivers a verdict on cases I have not listed. Two things particularly bother me and I cannot tell whether they should. First, the therapeutic fib to my mother and the farm story I might tell my nephew look structurally alike — both are benevolent falsehoods told to someone with little ability to check — yet my confidence in the verdicts runs in opposite directions. Second, I am tempted to explain the scammer and neighbour cases by saying that a person who asks in bad faith forfeits any claim to my truthfulness, but my mother has forfeited nothing, and my verdict in her case is at least as firm, so that cannot be the whole story. Finally, please tell me whether any of my verdicts has to be overturned rather than accommodated, and be honest about which ones the best principle will not save — including the uncomfortable possibility that Augustine's blanket prohibition is right and I simply do wrong twice a day.
