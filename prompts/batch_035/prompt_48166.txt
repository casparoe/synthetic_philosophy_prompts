Every argument I see about the institutional critique of effective altruism ends the same way — critics say that donating to a deworming charity while earning at a bank leaves the systems producing the underlying deprivation intact, while defenders reply that talk of systemic change is an applause light with no tractable plan attached. Rather than take sides, I want the specification first. Set out the desiderata that any adequate principle for dividing one's time and money between systemic interventions and incremental, measurable ones would have to meet: the cases it must adjudicate (earning to give versus working inside an institution to reform it, say, or a systemic gamble that could plausibly backfire), the distinctions it must respect (between changing a system and merely declining to reinforce it; between what is measurable and what matters), and the traps it must steer between — the regress on which you must always fix the deeper cause first and so may do nothing, and the measurability bias on which the unquantifiable drops out of the ledger. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and say which are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp. Most importantly, tell me whether the desiderata are jointly satisfiable — if the list itself forces a trade-off, that is the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria; there is no need to score particular organizations, authors, or interventions.
