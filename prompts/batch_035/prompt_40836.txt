I just got back from a four-hour argument with my friend Dana about whether consequentialism is too demanding, and I'm still kind of annoyed, so I want someone to actually make a case properly.

Here's where we ended up. Dana conceded early that the simple version of the objection doesnt work — you can't just wave "giving away everything would ruin your life" at a theory that says maximize wellbeing, because a ruined life is bad for total wellbeing too, and a person who collapses from overgiving helps no one. Fine. So she retreated to what she thinks is the real problem: the issue isn't the total amount of sacrifice, it's that consequentialism makes your obligations unconditionally indexed to how badly off other people are, while the morality we actually practice treats beneficence as conditional on other people doing their share. If a billionaire refuses to give a cent, consequentialism says my obligation goes UP to cover the shortfall. That seems backwards to her: the worse other people behave, the more I'm on the hook, and there's never a point where I'm allowed to stop and say this part is your mess, not mine. Dana's line was that once you see this, the demandingness objection was never really about demandingness — it's a fairness objection, and it still sinks consequentialism.

Please argue that Dana is wrong about this last step: that once the objection gets reformulated her way, as a fairness/compliance point, it actually loses most of its force. I want the strongest version of that argument, not a survey, and no reading list — I can find the literature myself if I want it. For what it's worth, the argument I half-formed during dinner but couldnt finish was something like: the fair-share idea smuggles in a bargaining perspective that only makes sense when you're dividing a fixed pot among contributors, but beneficence toward strangers in need isn't a cooperative scheme anybody opted into, so there's no baseline of "my share" against which giving more than my share counts as unfair. The trouble is that when I said it out loud it came out sounding like "fairness doesn't apply because suffering people aren't in a position to bargain," which just sounds cold, and Dana pounced. Can you build the argument so it doesn't have that hole?
