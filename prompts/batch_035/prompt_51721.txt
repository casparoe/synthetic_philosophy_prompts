"In 'Of Cannibals,' Montaigne describes the Tupinambá practice of eating captured enemies and argues that Europeans are in no position to call it barbarous, given what Europeans do to one another in the wars of religion. Does his argument depend on cultural relativism — the view that there is no objective standpoint from which one culture's customs can be judged better than another's — or does it only need something weaker, like a standard of reason or nature that all cultures alike fail to meet? Discuss with attention to how he handles (i) judging by custom versus judging by reason, and (ii) the comparison between ritual cannibalism and European torture. Is his position stable, or does it slide between the two?"

And here's the student's answer:

"It's clear Montaigne is a cultural relativist. He says that each of us calls whatever is far from our own habits barbarous, and this is meant to show the label has no objective content — barbarism just is deviation from whatever customs you happen to hold. Since the Tupinambá could just as easily call the Europeans barbarous, neither side can claim the higher ground. This is also why Montaigne travelled to Brazil to observe the Tupinambá first hand — he wanted an objective ethnographic record showing that their customs function perfectly well for them, so by their own standards they are every bit as civilized as we are by ours. He doesn't endorse the cannibalism though; he notes they eat their enemies out of honor and vengeance rather than appetite, and that eating a dead body is really less bad than the way Europeans, with their racks and instruments, tear a living body apart. So the conclusion is relativism with a preference: cannibalism is barbarous by our lights, torture by theirs, and there is nothing to adjudicate between them. In this he anticipates Rousseau's noble savage — the thesis that people closer to nature are morally superior — and Montaigne is essentially the first to formulate it. One might object that Montaigne now and then appeals to universal reason or nature as a measure, but that is just rhetorical flourish to keep his reader on side."
