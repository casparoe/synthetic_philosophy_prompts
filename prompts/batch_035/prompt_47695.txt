Two people from my translation study group (we meet on thursdays, mostly working translators) got into it last week about whether machine translation "understands" anything, and I want a referee because I honestly couldn't pick a winner. It started because Dana ran a modern MT system through her personal gauntlet of nasty sentences.

(1) First, Dana said the system passed a test she claims requires real understanding: she gave it "the plant on my windowsill finally flowered" and "the plant outside town opened in March," and it picked the botanical sense in the first and the industrial one in the second, even though both sentences are just about an entity located somewhere doing something in a season. Correct sense choice from context = understanding context, she said. Marco said no: "windowsill" and "flowered" are overwhelming statistical cues for the botanical sense, and you don't need understanding to exploit co-occurrence any more than a spam filter understands spam.

(2) Dana then played her ace: a sentence with a made-up word, something like "after eleven hours in the airport I was completely grozzled," and the system rendered "grozzled" as a state-of-exhaustion adjective in the target language, with the right morphology. That word occurs nowhere in its training data, she said, so it can't be regurgitation — it grasped the sentence as a whole and re-expressed it, which is what people like Hofstadter keep saying actual translation requires (deep reading, then re-expression, not surface mapping). Marco conceded the example was impressive but said generalizing from morphology and context is exactly what statistical systems are built to do, and that humans also just guess at neologisms from context — so if guessing-from-context counts as understanding the word has stopped doing any work, and if it doesn't, Dana owes him a definition.

(3) Marco counterattacked with a poetry line where the only decent translation required dropping the literal image entirely and inventing a different one that lands the same feeling (the target language has a pun in the spot where the source has none, so you have to pun somewhere else, or not at all). The system's output was metrically dead and semantically fine, which for Marco proved there's nobody home: surfaces mapped to surfaces. Dana called that a goalpost move — most human translators fail that test too, she'd like to see Marco's colleagues pass it — and said demanding poetry before granting understanding is like demanding a cat fetch before granting it sight.

(4) Marco's closer: fine, then name ANY test the system could pass that would make her say it understands. Dana said yes, definately — if it asked her a clarifying question about a genuinely ambiguous sentence, or offered two alternative translations with a note about what each one loses, she'd grant understanding. Marco said that just relocates the problem: a system can be *trained* to ask clarifying questions as a behavior, it's trivial, so her test is passable by the very surface-mapping machine she denies understanding to, which means her criterion was never tracking understanding in the first place. Dana shot back that by his logic nothing would ever count, so it's HIS position that's unfalsifiable, not hers.

By the end they were both pretty smug and I'd lost the thread somewhere in (4). Please start with your overall verdict — who's more right, if either of them outright wins — in the very first sentence, then go through the four moves one at a time, rating each from clearly wrong to clearly right with your reasons, and finish by telling me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I have a suspicion they're both smuggling in some assumption about "understanding" being all-or-nothing that neither of them ever says out loud, but I can't put my finger on exactly where it does the damage.
