My department runs a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and a colleague who leans toward the psi-epistemic camp keeps invoking an analogy that I'd like to see properly stress-tested: the quantum state is like a classical probability distribution over underlying states of affairs. On this picture, collapse is just Bayesian conditioning on the outcome of a measurement, preparation is the assignment of a prior, and the fact that you can never observe the wavefunction directly is exactly what you'd expect of a bookkeeping device rather than a physical object. She uses the analogy to argue that psi-ontologists are reifying an artifact of our ignorance — that the wavefunction is a map, and insisting it is part of the territory is a category mistake of the same kind as treating probabilities in a weather forecast as physical quantities.

I'd like a systematic audit of this analogy rather than the usual hand-waving in both directions. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what in the quantum formalism is supposed to play the role of the sample space, the prior, the evidence, the conditioning rule? Second, identify where the mapping breaks. Some of the disanalogies I've heard about — interference terms that don't behave like ordinary ignorance of a classical alternative, the fact that the wavefunction lives in configuration space rather than physical space, the observation that generic states of a composite system aren't probability distributions over product states — but I don't trust my command of the details, so please spell out which of these actually bite and why, and add any I've missed. Third, and most important, classify the disanalogies: which ones merely show the analogy is imperfect (as all analogies are) and which ones threaten the conclusion it was recruited to support? For instance, I gather the PBR theorem and related no-go results put pressure on the idea that the quantum state is *merely* a representation of ignorance — but I've also read that they presuppose an independently existing space of ontic states and shared preparation procedures, so it's not clear to me how much dialectical work they do against my colleague's position, and I'd like you to be careful about what they do and don't establish, including what their assumptions cost. Consider also whether Wigner's-friend-type scenarios, where different agents assign different states to the same system at the same time, help her case or actually complicate it.

Finally, close the loop on the original argumentative purpose: once the disanalogies that matter are on the table, does the conclusion — that there's no good reason to treat the wavefunction as part of the physical furniture, and that psi-ontology amounts to reifying a bookkeeping device — survive, or was the analogy doing all the work? And if the probability-distribution analogy is ultimately a poor fit, what would a better comparison be for the anti-realist's position — something in the vicinity of a law, a coordinate system, or a state of information that still respects the quantum formalism's peculiar features like noncommutativity and interference — or is the honest answer that our classical stock of analogies simply runs out? Please distinguish throughout between what's established by theorems, what's live interpretive debate, and what's your own judgment, so I can take it back to the group without misattributing anything.
