I serve on the steering committee of a small interdisciplinary initiative on the ethics of autonomous systems — two engineers, one legal scholar, one philosopher, and me — and we can fund roughly four projects of two years each on the problem of responsibility gaps: the worry that when a sufficiently autonomous system (a self-driving car, a content-ranking algorithm, a lethal autonomous weapon, a medical triage assistant) causes harm, the familiar conditions for holding anyone morally responsible fail to be met by any identifiable agent. The operators didn't foresee the outcome, the engineers couldn't predict how their design choices would interact with the environment, the executives are too far from the causal chain, and the system itself isn't an apt target of blame. Harm occurs, but responsibility seems to evaporate rather than merely diffuse.

We have to decide within a few months which lines of work to prioritize. I'll sketch the candidate questions as we see them, but treat this list as provisional — add or discard as you see fit:

1. Are responsibility gaps real, or artifacts? Perhaps the appearance of a gap comes from wrongly assuming responsibility must be held by a single human individual, when in fact it can be legitimately distributed across a network of agents, or reattributed to institutions, collectives, or the system's designers in retrospect. On this view the "gap" is a gap in our theorizing, not in the world.
2. Are they new? Some argue gaps are just the old "problem of many hands," long familiar from engineering disasters and organizational wrongdoing, now given a shiny new name. If so, the existing literature on distributed and organizational responsibility already contains the resources, and our money should go to applying it rather than to conceptual innovation.
3. If gaps are real and at least partly novel, what is the remedy — preventing them through system design (so-called meaningful human control, keeping a human in the loop with genuine understanding and authority), filling them legally (strict liability, collective liability schemes, or mandatory insurance that decouples compensation from blame), or accepting and redefining them (dropping the assumption that every harm needs a blameworthy agent)?
4. Do the answers depend on the domain? A gap in weapons systems, where the point of the technology is to cause outcomes, may differ categorically from one in medical decision support.

Give me a single defended ordering of these questions — or of a better set if you'd revise ours — as it should guide our four-project portfolio. State the criteria you're using (conceptual priority, tractability for a group with our composition, neglectedness, practical payoff for regulators and designers), say explicitly which question your top pick depends on having answered and which can be worked in parallel, and identify which of the four candidates above you would refuse to fund and why. Commit to a position on each question you rank — including whether gaps are real, whether they're novel, and whether the remedy lies in design, law, or acceptance — rather than mapping the options and leaving the choice to us. We can tolerate being told our framing is wrong; we cannot tolerate another survey.

Lead with your bottom line: the first sentence of your reply should state your single top-priority question and your overall verdict on the field, and the reasoning should follow immediately, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief.
