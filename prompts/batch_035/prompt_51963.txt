Retired biochemist here — thirty-five years of enzyme kinetics, and about three years into philosophy of mind since. I've hit a wall I'd like you to diagose for me rather than pick a side in. The debate is illusionism about consciousness. The illusionists — Dennett, Frankish, that crowd — hold that our ordinary conception of phenomenal consciousness, the raw feel of red or the intrinsically painful feel of pain as we naively conceive it, private and subjective in a way that can't fit into a physical world, describes something that couldn't exist, so the real explanatory target isn't the feels but our conviction that they exist: the brain builds a model of its own states and misdescribes them. Frankish even has a technical distinction for this, phenal seemings versus real seemings — it can seem to you that you have phenomenal properties without it being true that you do. The critics, Chalmers and Goff and most people I actually talk to, reply that this explains consciousness by explaining away the only datum we started with: if the feel of pain is an illusion, the illusion is either felt or not, and either way the feel is still there to be accounted for. This has run for decades, every move is known, and nobody budges an inch. My tentative hypothesis, from watching model disputes resolve themselves in science, is that it's not a factual disagreement at all but a disagreement about what counts as a datum: the illusionists file introspective reports under output-of-a-model, to be explained but not believed, while the critics file the same reports under data, to be believed and accommodated. And if that's right, no amount of neuroscience settles it, since each side just files the new findings under its own heading. Is that diagnosis right, or too simple, or actually unfair to one side? And tell me honestly wether anything — a distinction, an experiment, a reformulation — could ever move this, or if I should just expect to be reading the same papers in twenty years.
