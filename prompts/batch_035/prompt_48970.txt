I am writing a survey chapter on the epistemology of markets: whether, and under what conditions, market prices can be treated as a body of knowledge that an outside observer — a forecaster, a central banker, an intelligence analyst using prediction markets — is entitled to read as evidence. Before I grade any of the existing models, I want the grading key itself. Please write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of how markets aggregate dispersed private information into prices must meet.

The requirements I have in mind so far, roughly, are these, but I want them made precise, motivated, and if necessary corrected. First, the theory must discharge the no-trade baseline: as Milgrom and Stokey showed, if traders are expected-utility maximizers who start from a Pareto-optimal allocation and share a common prior, no private information can rationalize speculation on a common-value asset — so the enormous volume of speculative trade we actually observe must be explained by relaxing one of the assumptions, and the theory must say which relaxation is principled (liquidity needs, hedging, heterogeneous priors, noise trading) and which is ad hoc. Second, it must say what a price is a belief about: the average trader's, the marginal trader's, a sufficient statistic over all of them, or something that is not anyone's belief at all. Third, it must survive the informativeness paradox familiar from Grossman and Stiglitz: if prices fully reveal the market's information, no one has an incentive to acquire information, in which case there is nothing for prices to reveal — so full informational efficiency and a functioning price mechanism appear jointly unreachable. Fourth, it must handle the recursion: traders learn from prices, which are themselves aggregates of that learning. Fifth, it must accommodate the persistent, non-vanishing disagreement one finds in the off-market world, given the agreement results that suggest rational common-prior agents should converge. Sixth — and here I suspect I am smuggling in a camp prejudice, so tell me — it must say something about bubbles and crashes, or else explain honestly why episodes where prices decouple from fundamentals for years should not count against it.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one school dressed up as demands. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — and I suspect that any two of the no-trade baseline, informative prices, and costly information acquisition cannot all hold — say so explicitly, since an impossible list is itself a result I would want to feature. Tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories. Finally, a brief scorecard of the main families against the list: the rational-expectations-plus-noise-trading models, the heterogeneous-priors approaches, the behavioral programs, and the Hayekian view of prices as communicating dispersed knowledge without anyone possessing it. Skip citations and reading recommendations; I only want the specification and the reasoning behind it.
