I'm a journalist who writes long-form pieces on science and ideas, and I'm currently at work on a feature about the intellectual ancestry of today's liberal internationalism — the idea, familiar from the United Nations and the European project, that commercial interdependence and legal order will gradually tame war. Most accounts trace this back to Kant's 1795 essay Perpetual Peace, so I've gone back to the primary text, in the older Hastings translation. I managed the preliminary and definitive articles well enough, but in the First Supplement, "Of the Guarantee of Perpetual Peace," I ran into a passage that has stopped me cold. Kant writes:

"The guarantee of Perpetual Peace is furnished by no less a power than the great artist Nature herself: Natura Daedala rerum. The mechanical course of Nature visibly exhibits a design to bring forth concord out of the discord of men, even against their will. This power as a cause working by laws which are unknown to us, is commonly called Fate; but in view of the design manifested in the course of the world, it is to be regarded as the deep wisdom of a Higher Cause directed towards the realisation of the final purpose of the human race, and predetermining the course of the world by relation to it, and as such we call it Providence."

A little later he adds that "The employment of the term 'Nature' rather than 'Providence' for the designation of this power, is more proper and more modest in view of the limits of human reason, when we are dealing with it merely from the theoretical and not from the religious point of view."

What I can't work out is how this fits with everything that precedes it. The definitive articles seemed to argue that a federative arrangement is something we owe — a matter of right and duty, not prediction. But now Kant seems to be saying that nature, through its "mechanical course," will deliver peace anyway, "even against their will" — and he goes on, in the surrounding text, to treat war itself as nature's instrument for scattering peoples across the globe and forcing them into commercial relations. Can you help me unpick this? Specifically: what work is the word "guarantee" doing here; whether Kant is smuggling in a theological claim about providence while insisting he is only speaking of nature within the bounds of possible experience; and how the mechanism of self-interest and war is supposed to harmonise with the moral argument rather than render it superfluous. I'd be grateful if you could write your answer in British English, as my editor insists on it, and keep it accessible enough that I can crib from it for a general readership without losing philosophical precision.
