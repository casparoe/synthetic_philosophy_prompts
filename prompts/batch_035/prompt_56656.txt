I'm a novelist at work on a book in which a retired physicist of broadly skeptical temperament holds forth to his granddaughter about the vanity of academic philosophy, and in one scene he offers G. E. Moore as his prize exhibit: the famous proof of an external world. His summary, which I have adapted from a popular history of philosophy I read last winter, runs roughly as follows: Moore stood before an audience, raised his hands, and declared that he had thereby proved the existence of the external world, since a raised hand is exactly the sort of thing the skeptics doubt; that in doing so he refuted the skeptics of his day; that he showed common sense to be a stronger criterion of truth than any philosophical argument; and that Descartes' dream argument is answered once and for all by the plain fact that we can never be wrong about such things as our having hands.

I suspect the book I read, like most popularizations, compresses something, and I would like to know how much of this my character could say without a philosopher in the audience wincing. Which parts of the summary would a scholar of Moore accept as accurate, which are simplifications a defender might let pass, and which are outright misattributions? I have a vague sense, for instance, that proving an external world and refuting skepticism were, for Moore, distinct tasks, and that his own standard for what deserves to be called a proof was unusually strict, even while he made some concession to his critics about what such a proof can accomplish. And does the physicist's punchline, that Moore established we can never be wrong about such things, follow from anything Moore actually claimed, or does it convert one of his careful distinctions into a boast he never made? Since my scene requires the old man to sound authoritative while being subtly off, I need to know precisely where and how he goes wrong, so that the reader who knows the subject smiles at his confidence rather than at my research.

Please answer in continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
