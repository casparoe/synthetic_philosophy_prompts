I'm a retired civil engineer with no academic background in history, archaeology, or philosophy -- everything below comes from popular books and a lot of thinking on my own, so please treat me as an intelligent layman rather than assume technical knowledge.

For the past two years I've been reading about what people call "collapse": the end of the Late Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, the end of the Classic Maya city-centres, and Easter Island. One thing I notice is how much of the popular storytelling leans on the same underlying picture without ever stating it: a society is a living organism. It is born, grows, matures, falls ill, ages, and eventually dies, and there is such a thing as its cause of death. When a book says a civilization "collapsed," this organism picture is quietly doing the work: it makes collapse look like one dramatic event with one cause (invasions, drought, ecological ruin), and it makes the end of a civilization look like the death of a single thing -- a body -- rather than a reshuffling of many things that individually carry on.

My son-in-law, who studied classics, thinks this framing is basically a mistake. He points out that when the palace-centres of the Bronze Age world went under, ordinary people kept farming, kept their gods, kept speaking versions of the same languages; that when the southern Maya cities emptied out, life in the region didn't end and other cities carried on; and that on Easter Island the descendants of the "collapsed" society are still with us. His line is that nothing dies, so nothing collapses; societies merely change. But I notice he is using a version of the same organism picture in reverse -- he accepts that if collapse is real, it must be the death of an organism, and since there's no organism there, there's no collapse. That seems too quick. Something on Easter Island really did end, in a way that doesn't seem true of, say, the shift away from Bronze Age palace administration -- and I'd like to know what.

I'd also like to put my own alternative picture on the table, because I suspect it's not much better. When I try to think of a metaphor that fits what actually seems to happen, I land on an old-growth forest after a fire: the forest isn't dead, it's in an early successional stage, and in a century it will be a forest again -- but not the same forest. The other metaphor I keep reaching for is a company that dissolves: the legal person dies, but the employees, machines, customers, and know-how scatter and re-form into new firms. I can't tell whether these are genuinely better or just fashionable pessimism about one metaphor traded for a rosy view.

So here is my request. Include at least the mappings for birth, organs, illness/aging, death, corpse, and cause of death -- add others if they occur to you.

2. A prose audit of where the organism analogy breaks, separating the breakdowns that are fatal to its use from the ones that are merely loose. Pay particular attention to what it smuggles in about identity: what exactly is supposed to be the "body" -- the political system, the population, the way of life, the built landscape? -- and whether several of these can come apart, so that one dies while the others carry on. Use the three cases I mentioned as your test cases, treating Easter Island as the hardest case for the "nothing collapses, societies merely change" view and the Bronze Age as the easiest.
