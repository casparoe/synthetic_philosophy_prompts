Look, I've been teaching philosophy of physics off and on for fifteen years now, and I have to vent about the Bell's theorem situation. There's this fight that has been running basically since the 70s about what Bell's theorem actually proves, and I've come to suspect it can't be settled because the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about a theorem at all. I want you to diagnose the fight, not referee it.

The setup, as I see it: on one side you've got people like Tim Maudlin and Travis Norsen who insist Bell's theorem, combined with the Aspect-style experiments, refutes locality itself. Full stop. They think the phrase "local realism" is a weasel word that was retrofitted into the story, that there was never any independent assumption of "realism" smuggled into Bell's reasoning, and that ordinary quantum mechanics with its collapse postulate is straightforwardly nonlocal anyway. On the other side you've got a large chunk of the physics community, and especially the Everettians like Deutsch and Wallace, who say Bell's theorem only rules out local hidden variable theories, and that the Everett interpretation is fully local — the correlations are explained without any action at a distance because both outcomes happen. And then there's a third move where people say yes the correlations are nonlocal in Bell's sense, but since you can't signal with them, relativistic causality is untouched, so who cares. My grad seminar hit this wall last month and we've basically been going in circles since, which is what prompted this.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and nothing more: the disagreement is mostly about what "locality" is even supposed to mean. Bell-locality (the factorization condition on joint probabilities), signal locality, no superluminal causal influence, Lorentz invariance of the theory's structure — these come apart, and each camp may just be answering a different question under the one word. But I half suspect there's something more than verbal going on too, namely a fight about whether quantum mechanics without hidden variables is even the kind of theory Bell's theorem touches. If the theorem's premises are about what a theory must assign to unperformed measurements, and one side thinks nothing must be assigned, then the two sides are disagreeing about the conditions of applicability of the theorem, not its content. Is that right? Or is there a genuine formal or empirical question hiding in there that we're circling?
