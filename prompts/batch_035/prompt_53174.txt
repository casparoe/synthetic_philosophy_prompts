I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and I'm writing a term paper on what semantic externalism implies about memory. Construct a Twin Earth–style thought experiment to the following specifications. A subject's natural-kind term (you choose which one, and invent the Twin-Earthly counterpart substance) changes what it refers to without the subject or anyone around them ever being able to notice: no change in the subject's use of the word, in what they would sincerely report, or in how their environment seems to them from the inside. The change is to be gradual, spread over years, with no sharp cutoff the subject could in principle detect. The case must be engineered so that an internalist about meaning and a Putnam-style externalist agree about what the subject's word refers to today, but disagree about whether the subject meant the same thing by it twenty years ago, before the transition began. Note that stipulating any verdict about meaning directly is cheating: each verdict has to be derived from the theories given the setup. After presenting the scenario, do three things: state what each theory says about the subject's word at each stage of the transition, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and then give the externalist's best response to the charge that the case saddles externalism with an implausible result — namely, that the subject's sincere memory reports were true when they were formed but have silently become false in the meantime. Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
