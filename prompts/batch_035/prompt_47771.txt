I'm taking an upper-level epistemology seminar this term, and this week we discussed the KK principle — the idea that if you know something, you must be in a position to know that you know it — together with Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument against it. Afterward, two classmates of mine, Maya and Dev, carried the argument from class out into the hallway, and I followed it for the better part of an hour without being able to tell who was winning. Maya opened by defending KK on intuitive grounds: if you know that p but are not in a position to know that you know, then you can't rule out that you merely believe p while actually being wrong — yet if you can't rule that out, it's obscure how you count as knowing p in the first place. Knowing, she said, is supposed to be a state that settles the question for you, and a state that leaves you unable to certify itself as knowledge looks unstable. Dev answered with the anti-luminosity case: take someone standing in a cold room whose temperature rises by imperceptible degrees over an hour. At the start they know they're cold; by the end they don't (it's warm); at no moment is the change detectable. If knowledge of being cold were always self-certifying in the way KK demands, Dev said, you could keep certifying your way down the sequence and knowledge would never quietly lapse — but it plainly does lapse, without your noticing. So the condition of being cold is not luminous, and knowledge generally isn't either, which sinks KK.

Maya made two replies in sequence. First, she said the argument only works because it assumes her evidence and abilities stay fixed while the facts drift — but knowing that you know plausibly allows for a way of coming to know, and if your discriminative abilities were sharpened, the lapse would be visible; KK, on her favored reading, says you're in a position to know that you know, not that you already do, and a position you fail through imperceptiveness to cash in is still a position. Dev's response was that this makes "in a position" do no work: at the borderline moment, the difference between knowing and not knowing that you're cold is smaller than any difference you can register, so no amount of sharpened attention tracks it — the retreat to "position" either names an ability you don't have or collapses back into the claim KK was supposed to establish. Maya then shifted ground, allowing for the sake of argument that KK fails for borderline cases like temperature, but insisting this costs more than Dev admits: if he's right that knowing doesn't put you in a position to know you know, it's mysterious why we hold each other to knowledge-claims, correct ourselves by saying "I thought I knew," and organize inquiry around the question "how do you know you know?" — practices that presuppose at least some self-certifying knowledge. Dev's last word was that those practices are explainable without KK, since they trade in high confidence and justified belief, and that Maya was smuggling in a much weaker principle under the KK name, one he'd happily accept.

I'm supposed to write a short response paper adjudicating a dispute from the seminar discussion, and I'd rather use theirs than manufacture a fake one, but I honestly can't rate the moves myself — some of them seem to slide between genuinely different claims, and I can't tell which claims are load-bearing. Could you go through their exchange move by move and evaluate each of the five or so substantive moves they make, saying not just who comes out ahead overall but who is right about which specific point? Also, please flag for me which parts of this terrain are close to settled among epistemologists and which are genuinely open — for instance, I can't tell whether the "position to know" reading of KK is a standard refinement or a motivated goalpost-shift, and I don't want my paper to present some contested reconstruction as the received view. Finally, tell me if there's anything important both of them missed that I could add as my own contribution.
