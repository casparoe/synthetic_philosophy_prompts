I teach a graduate seminar on eighteenth-century French philosophy, and for our final session on Émilie du Châtelet I want to stage a controlled thought experiment: suppose du Châtelet were shown the contemporary empirical psychology of well-being, in much the same state of mind in which she wrote her Discourse on Happiness. On the basis of her actual arguments — her claim that illusions are not merely tolerable but necessary for a happy life, her treatment of passionate engagements such as love, gambling, and the pursuit of intellectual glory as renewable sources of happiness, and her remarks on reputation and self-esteem as things a rational person must actively manage — predict how she would respond to three developments she never witnessed: (1) hedonic adaptation and set-point research suggesting that people tend to revert to a baseline of contentment regardless of circumstance; (2) social media, which simultaneously inflates the pursuit of reputation and exposes every comforting illusion to instant, collective debunking; and (3) modern therapeutic culture, which tends to treat misaligned or flattering beliefs about oneself as symptoms to be corrected rather than as props for happiness. For each of the three, state whether you think she would regard it as vindicating, complicating, or refuting her position, drawing on her reasoning rather than on generic speculation about the Enlightenment. Then rank the three developments by how much pressure they put on her overall view of happiness, and justify each position in the ordering in a sentence or two. Finally, indicate whether you think her framework, transposed to the present, would counsel a retreat from digital life or a strategic exploitation of it.
