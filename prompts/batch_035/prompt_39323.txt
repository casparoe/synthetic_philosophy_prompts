I'm a program officer at a mid-sized foundation that three years ago adopted effective-altruist-style evaluation: every proposal gets an estimated cost-effectiveness figure, and grants are ranked on it. I review about two hundred proposals a year at two hours each, and the board signs off on whatever the ranking supports. I've watched the portfolio drift toward whatever is easy to count, and I want a procedure that resists this measurability bias without just telling me to use judgment — that phrase, by itself, has no content. Please write a decision procedure I can actually run in the two hours: the questions to ask in order of priority, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and three possible outputs at each stage — proceed on the numbers, treat the estimate as decoration and decide on grounds I must state openly to the board, or stop with a no. Somewhere in it, include a test for whether the cost-effectiveness estimate is load-bearing or merely decorating a decision already made on other grounds — a question that can come back with a negative answer, not just a report that we can't tell. Then run the procedure on three proposals: a school-based deworming program with a decent trial behind it; a small group building citizen oversight of municipal budgets, whose downstream effects no instrument of ours could ever trace; and a governance proposal with a suspiciously precise cost-per-outcome figure that I would like to fund on institutional grounds — the procedure must handle that one honestly even if it hands me an answer I won't like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, including whether it can do anything about a board that wants numbers, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
