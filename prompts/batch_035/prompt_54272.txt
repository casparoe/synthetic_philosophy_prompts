Last autumn I was appointed rapporteur to a national working group charged with advising our government on how to handle the commemorations of our mid-century civil conflict: the fate of certain monuments, whether an official day of remembrance should be instituted, and what should become of the testimonies collected from now-elderly survivors, among them my own grandmother. Over eight months of sessions the group has hardened into two camps that argue past each other. The first, led by a descendant of victims, holds a view close to Margalit's ethics of memory: it is communities, not only individuals, that can remember or forget; relations of thick mutual attachment among members of a shared polity generate a genuine ethical duty not to forget wrongs done to their own, and letting that memory lapse would be a kind of betrayal of the dead. The second, led by our archivist, holds something like Rieff's case for forgetting: the modern consensus that remembrance is always healing deserves suspicion, since ritualized public memory often entrenches grievance, freezes identities around historical wounds, and hands each generation a script of enmity; keeping meticulous historical records in archives is not the same as ceremonial commemoration, and a measured amount of forgetting, Renan's insight about nation-building, may be a civic achievement rather than a moral failure.

As rapporteur, I must produce a final document, and I have concluded that the only useful one is not a compromise verdict but the kind of joint statement an adversarial collaboration would issue — a text both camps could sign. Please draft it. It should contain a numbered section of claims both sides can accept, and these must be substantive rather than empty (e.g., about what we actually know of survivor testimony, about what archives already preserve, about the distinction between commemorating victims and commemorating a victory); then a disagreements section, ideally a table, in which each position is phrased so that its own holders would recognize the wording as fair; then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a duty of remembrance exists at all for communities as opposed to individuals, another is whether remembering wrongs makes recurrence more or less likely, and a third is who has standing to release a community from remembering, if anyone does. For each crux, name the concrete case, piece of evidence, or observable consequence both camps would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, so that reality could later arbitrate between them. Finally, mark explicitly which parts of the residual dispute you judge to be verbal — for instance whether remembering, record-keeping, and commemorating have been quietly conflated — and explain why the wording, not the substance, keeps the two sides apart there. Write it so that either camp could circulate the document to its own constituency as an accurate account of both its view and its opponent's.
