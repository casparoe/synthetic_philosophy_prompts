Two classmates of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument after our cognitive science seminar, which this semester has been working through Hofstadter's ideas about analogy and fluid concepts — the Copycat program and its letter-string microdomain, where the system answers puzzles like "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?" by navigating a network of letter categories and relationships (successor, predecessor, sameness, leftmost/rightmost, alphabetic groupings) under the pressure of building a coherent mapping. Dana's position was that Copycat is a genuine demonstration of fluid concepts: the program doesn't have a fixed representation of the problem or a stored rule for what to do with ijk, and concepts like "rightmost" or "successor" can slip into "leftmost" or "predecessor" when the mapping demands it, which is exactly the context-sensitivity that fixed-representation, logic-chopping AI of the 1970s and 1980s was missing. Marcus wasn't having it: the domain has twenty-six letters and maybe a dozen relationships, all hand-built by Hofstadter with hand-assigned concept depths, bond strengths, and temperature dynamics; the "slippage" is just Hofstadter pre-deciding which substitutions are legal and how attractive they are, so the fluidity is his, borrowed, not the system's. Dana replied that no simulation escapes this objection — any model has to have its substrate built by someone — and that the real claim is architectural: the answer emerges from stochastic competition between partially built structures rather than from a rule that says "when you see ijk, output ijl," and the system can even dither between plausible answers like ijl and ijd in a way that mirrors human hesitation.

Marcus's comeback was the sharpest thing said all evening, I thought, though I couldn't tell if it was actually right: he said "emergence" and "stochastic competition" are doing the same laundering job as before, because the weights that make one answer beat another were tuned until the program produced answers Hofstadter found elegant — the preferences are Hofstadter's preferences routed through randomness, and nothing inside the system could ever tell him a slippage was bad, since the judgment that an answer is graceful always came from outside, from him. A system with fluid concepts, Marcus said, would be one whose concepts rebuild themselves from experience, and Copycat's concepts never touch their own weights, so the fluidity is in the eye of the observer. Dana's final move was to bite a bullet: fluidity is always relative to a stable substrate, because you can't have slippage without something firm to slip from — a chess novice's concept of a "fork" is fixed vocabulary, but deploying it under time pressure against a real opponent is fluid, and by Marcus's standard of self-modifying concepts, humans only qualify after years of learning, so the standard would exclude the very novices whose cognition the microdomain is meant to model. Marcus said that analogy proved too much, since it would make any system "fluid" relative to its fixed parts, which would drain the term of all meaning, and there the argument stopped because the seminar room was closing.

They've asked me to referee it by email, and I honestly can't — I followed each move but have no sense of which ones actually land. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each of them concedes too much or too little, and what (if anything) both are missing about what would settle the question of whether a microdomain program can show fluid concepts at all?
