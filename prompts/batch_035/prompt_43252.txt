You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in aggregative ethical theories and suffering-focused ethics. Please think carefully and brainstorm several candidate scenarios before committing to one, since the hardest constraint below is easy to fake but hard to satisfy genuinely.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic — a health ministry or hospital administrator allocating a fixed budget between two programs, not a trolley, a deathbed, or a hypothetical extermination machine. The two programs should be such that one prevents or relieves suffering while the other produces a large aggregate of modest pleasures or well-being gains. The case must be engineered so that:

1. Classical hedonic utilitarianism and a suffering-focused negative utilitarianism (on which relieving suffering has lexical or near-lexical priority over producing happiness) deliver opposite verdicts on which program to fund.
2. The considered judgment of ordinary people sides with the negative utilitarian.
3. The divergence is generated by the suffering/happiness asymmetry itself — not by smuggling in deontological side-constraints such as rights, consent, or fairness, and not merely by the diminishing marginal utility of welfare. In practice this means the case should also strain the distinction between negative utilitarianism and prioritarianism (Parfit's priority view, under which benefits to the worse-off get extra weight but still aggregate).

Present the scenario in one paragraph, then demonstrate that it meets each specification. Walk through the verdict each of the three theories delivers and why. Identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work, and show what happens to the verdicts if that feature is flattened or removed. Report honestly on whether the case cleanly separates negative utilitarianism from prioritarianism or whether a prioritarian with steep enough weighting can reproduce the negative utilitarian's verdict; if the latter, say whether that shows the case is doing less work than it appeared to, or that the two views differ less than their proponents claim. Finally, give the classical utilitarian's strongest counterattack — the version of "this case cheats" they would actually press — and assess whether it succeeds.
