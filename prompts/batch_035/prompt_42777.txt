**Exam question (25 points).** Consider the hypothesis H: all ravens are black. Hempel's raven paradox purports to show that the observation of a white shoe confirms H.

(a) (6 points) State the principles of confirmation that jointly generate this result, and derive it: show precisely how they entail that the observation report "this object is a white shoe" confirms H.

(b) (7 points) What was Hempel's own assessment of the result, and why, on his view, does the result merely appear paradoxical?

(c) (12 points) Explain the background-knowledge (Bayesian) diagnosis, on which the white shoe confirms H but only to a negligible degree in realistic settings. Does this diagnosis show that Nicod's criterion is false as a general principle of confirmation? Justify your answer.

---

**Student's response.**

(a) Two principles do the work. First, Nicod's criterion: a hypothesis of the form "All F are G" is confirmed by any F that is found to be G, and refuted by any F that is found not to be G. Second, the equivalence condition: whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms anything logically equivalent to it. "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "All non-black things are non-ravens." A white shoe is a non-black thing that is a non-raven, so by Nicod's criterion applied to the contrapositive, the shoe is a confirming instance of it, and by the equivalence condition it is therefore a confirming instance of H. All of this was worked out in Hempel's "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation" (1945).

(b) Hempel's own view was that the result is correct: the white shoe really does confirm H. He held that the appearance of paradox is a psychological illusion. When we judge that the shoe is irrelevant, we are tacitly adding in background knowledge — in his parallel case, the knowledge that the substance held in the flame is ice and that ice contains no sodium salt. Once that extra information is bracketed, the paradox vanishes. So for Hempel the paradox is only apparent, and the right reaction is to accept the white-shoe result.

(c) The Bayesian diagnosis agrees that the shoe confirms, but adds that the amount of confirmation depends on background knowledge. Since non-black things vastly outnumber ravens, sampling a non-black thing at random and finding it not to be a raven only marginally lowers the probability that some non-black thing is a raven; so the confirmation of H is real but negligible. Importantly, this holds whatever our background knowledge happens to be: the white shoe always confirms H at least a little, never disconfirms it. Since the shoe result is what generated the paradox in the first place, and the Bayesian treatment shows that Nicod's criterion was refuted by it, Nicod's criterion has to be abandoned as a general account of confirmation. A parallel with Goodman's grue makes the point vivid: the grue problem is just the raven paradox in predicate form — the same problem about when positive instances confirm. One alternative way out is Quine's idea of restricting confirmation to natural kinds, which Hempel also adopted in response to Goodman; but that restriction looks ad hoc, and in any case the Bayesian framework already gives us everything we need.
