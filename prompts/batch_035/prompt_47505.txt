I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy of science reading group that has just finished a term on causation, ending with the contrast between Lewis's counterfactual analysis and Woodward's interventionist theory. A graduate student in the group has produced an argument that I cannot fault step by step, though its conclusion seems to prove too much. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Both theories share a difference-making requirement: C causes E only if there is some appropriately specified setting in which C takes a different value and E takes a different value as well. For the counterfactual theorist the setting is the nearest possible world in which C does not occur; for the interventionist it is a possible intervention on C.

(2) Consider a world governed throughout by deterministic laws — Newtonian mechanics, say, the physics I worked in — and any two events in it, C earlier than E.

(3) Let I be any event that changes C while leaving the laws and the entire state of the world before C's time exactly as they actually are. Since the laws plus that state determine C's actual occurrence, no such I is consistent with the laws. So every difference-making setting for C must either break the laws or alter the past.

(4) Lewis's own resolution of this difficulty — the nearest ¬C-world matches particular fact up to shortly before C, then diverges by a small miracle, then follows the laws — takes the first route. But an exception to the laws is not a possible event, so the counterfactual theory's settings are secured by impossibilities.

(5) Woodward's intervention on C must be exogenous to E. But every event in a deterministic world has causes of its own, and those causes are fixed by the same total past that fixes everything else, E included. So no exogenous event exists, and any intervention is the same miracle in different clothing.

(6) Hence every difference-making setting for C either violates the laws or rewrites the past. And if we soften "possible" to "not ruled out by the model," then which background we hold fixed becomes a choice, and causal facts become artifacts of that choice.

(7) Therefore all causal claims in a deterministic world are false or vacuous, or else causation in such a world is conventional. Since Newton's celestial mechanics is full of causal claims and is presumably not a work of fiction, something has gone wrong.

I cannot see which step to give up. I am reluctant to abandon (1), because difference-making is the one thing both theories agree on, and (2) is simply the physics I did for a living. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
