My employer, a mid-sized AI company, has asked me to serve on a newly created internal committee that will decide whether any of our training and deployment procedures require welfare-related safeguards — a question I used to treat as abstract and now cannot. To work out what I actually believe about which computations matter morally, I've been collecting my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and they refuse to form a pattern. Here they are, honestly:

A lobster lowered alive into a pot of boiling water: clearly matters; this is the paradigm of what I mean by a morally relevant computation. A nematode worm with three hundred and two neurons killed by a researcher as part of routine lab work: at most trivially — I would not say the researcher owes it anything. The same worm's entire nervous system emulated in software, connected to a small robot body, and then deleted: matters even less than the real worm did, and what bothers me is that I cannot say what the additional reduction is supposed to consist in, since the functional organization is arguably the same. A bee colony deliberately exposed to a slow-acting toxin: the colony as a single subject does not matter, even though it coordinates defense and thermoregulation in ways that look insect-like from the outside, though I hold this verdict less firmly than the others. A permanently unresponsive patient whose cortex has been largely destroyed but whose brainstem still runs breathing, sleep-wake cycling, and the nociceptive reflexes: I do not think there is anyone left to harm, even though a good deal of the pain-relevant circuitry keeps running. A reinforcement-learning agent in one of our own training runs, whose reward-prediction-error signals spike in a pattern that, if recorded from the amygdala of a sheep, a veterinarian would flag as acute distress: does not matter — but I cannot produce a reason for this that does not also threaten the verdicts above. A brain organoid grown from human stem cells and coupled to a simple game environment: matters somewhat; I would not want it used for casual stress-testing.

I have a tentative principle I'd like tested and repaired rather than accepted: a computation matters morally iff it realizes a control architecture shaped by a process that selected for valenced states and currently implements states playing the right valenced functional role. This gets the lobster and the worm right and excludes the RL agent by history rather than by structure — but it seems to guarantee that no AI system could ever come to matter, however it is built or however it is organized, and I am not prepared to sign my name to that guarantee yet.

What I want from you: one or two precisely stated candidate principles, each clause motivated by the specific verdict it is there to save, along with an honest accounting of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and which of them I should simply give up. Then apply the surviving principle to the cases the committee will actually face: an emulation run much slower than real time, or with the same internal state resumed in scattered episodes over months; an agent instantiated in two copies, one of which is terminated; a training run sharded across data centers on different continents with no single site ever hosting the whole computation; a genetically modified animal whose nociceptors are intact but whose affective response to noxious stimuli has been knocked out; and an agent whose internal state is fully reset between episodes, so that no trace of the previous episode's turmoil persists. I would rather lose one of my verdicts than present the committee with a principle I cannot defend under the first hard question.
