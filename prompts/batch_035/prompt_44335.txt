My roommate and I have been going back and forth about whether things still have the powers we credit them with when something's standing in the way, and I wrote down my gut verdicts, and now I want to know if there's a real principle behind them or if I'm just improvising.

- A wine glass wrapped in three layers of bubble wrap, inside a box, on the top shelf: fragile. Obviously.
- A book of matches that got soaked in the rain: still flammable. Wet, sure, but flammable.
- A viper at the zoo, behind glass, nowhere near any prey: venomous. Yes.
- A bridge engineered so that if any one support cracked, the load would shift to the others and nothing would happen: NOT fragile, even though its supports could crack. That one's engineering, not bubble wrap, but I can't quite say why the difference matters.
- My uncle, who used to have a terrible temper, but after years of training, whenever something would set him off the training kicks in first and he goes calm instead: not hot-tempered anymore? But the glass in the bubble wrap is still fragile, and the uncle case looks structurally the same, so something has to give.
- A poison slipped into a cup, where the intended victim happens to take a daily antidote that neutralizes exactly that poison: I keep flip-floping. Part of me says still deadly, it's the same poison. Part of me says if it can't kill anyone, calling it deadly is weird.
- A friend who would absolutely betray me for a million dollars, though nobody's ever offered: disloyal. I feel this one strongly even though it's all about what he'd do, not what he does.

My tentative principle was the simple conditional thing: a thing is disposed to do M in circumstances C iff, were it in C, it would M — if the glass were struck it would break, and so on. That seems to flunk half my list, so I want it tested against the cases and repaired if it can be. I know the dispositions literature has terms like 'finks' and 'masks' (the bubble wrap masks the fragility? or does it?) and you're welcome to use that machinery if it helps, but explain it as you go, don't just name-drop. So: what's the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, which of my verdicts does the best one have to throw out, and what does it predict for two cases I haven't decided? First, a poison whose victim would take the antidote only if she learned she'd been poisoned — does the order of events change anything? Second, a fragile sticker on the glass that makes everyone who sees it handle it gently.

Last thing: where the metaphysics is settled versus genuinely fought over, flag it clearly. I don't want to walk away thinking there's a consensus account when the people who work on this are still arguing about whether dispositions reduce to conditionals at all, and I'd rather know which parts of your answer are the minority report.
