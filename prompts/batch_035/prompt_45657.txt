I am a software engineer by training and trade, with no formal background in philosophy, though I have recently been reading about the puzzle of material constitution: the statue and the clay. If I understand it correctly, the statue shares every physical constituent with the lump of clay it is shaped from, and yet the two appear to differ in their persistence conditions — the lump can survive being squashed into a ball, while the statue cannot — so they cannot be identical, even though they seem to occupy exactly the same matter at the same time. I also gather that this is supposed to be genuinely puzzling rather than a confusion, and that philosophers have a range of standard responses: accepting that there really are two coincident objects, denying that one member of the pair exists at all, treating constitution as a relation distinct from identity, and so on.

Here is what struck me. My profession confronts what looks like the same problem all the time, and we do not consider it puzzling — we consider it a design decision, with standard resolutions already on the shelf. When two variables refer to the same object, we distinguish identity from equality (== versus is, reference semantics versus value semantics). When two filenames refer to the same data, Unix gives us hard links: two directory entries sharing a single inode, where the file's persistence condition is literally a reference count, so deleting one link does not delete the file. And when we need two objects that share all their state but must be able to diverge, we use copy-on-write: they are effectively one thing right up until a write forces them apart, after which the history of the fork alone determines which is which.

Please compare the philosophical treatment of the statue-and-clay problem with the engineering treatment of these analogous cases, in both directions. Specifically:

1. Map the standard philosophical responses onto the engineering repertoire. Which software resolution corresponds to accepting coincident objects, which to denying that one of the pair exists, and which to the view that constitution is a relation distinct from identity? If some engineering practice has no philosophical counterpart, or some philosophical response has no engineering counterpart, say so rather than forcing the mapping.

2. Assess where the analogy is deep and where it is shallow. The aspect I most want examined is this: a language designer gets to stipulate identity criteria, and there is a runtime — memory addresses, reference counts — standing outside the system as an arbiter of the facts. Does the statue-and-clay debate have any candidate for such an arbiter, and if not, is the absence of one the actual source of the puzzle?

3. Finally, tell me whether you think the comparison supports the view that the philosophical debate could be dissolved by adopting the engineer's attitude — treating identity conditions as something we adopt for purposes rather than discover — or whether it instead shows that the engineering cases are disanalogous in a way that makes the philosophical puzzle harder rather than easier.

Assume I can follow technical detail on either the engineering or the philosophical side; rigor matters more to me than accessibility.
