Rather than diving straight into the Blackburn/Gibbard literature and the back-and-forth it spawned, I want to get the specification right first, because my suspicion is that half the disputes in that literature are really disputes about what an expressivist theory is for. So: write me the desiderata. What does an expressivist account of moral language have to actually deliver to count as adequate? I'm thinking of things like: it has to explain the validity of the classic argument -- if lying is wrong, getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong -- without treating the moral vocabulary as plain truth-conditional, and the same goes for embedding under negation, disjunction, belief reports ("she thinks lying is wrong"), and straight questions. It needs compositionality in some form, since the whole problem is that "lying is wrong" seems to have one meaning inside a conditional and outside it. And it presumably still has to earn its keep on the things that motivated expressivism in the first place -- explaining genuine moral disagreement without disagreement about facts, and the connection between sincere moral judgement and motivation.
