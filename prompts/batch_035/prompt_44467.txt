Two friends of mine who volunteer at a maritime museum got into a proper row about the ship in the main hall, and I couldn't referee it. Background: the ship's been in dry dock since the sixties, and over the decades the conservators have replaced rotted planks a few at a time, always storing the retired planks in the basement. Nothing was ever swapped out all at once — in any given year only a plank or two changed. The museum just renovated, and they've used the stored old planks to assemble a second, rickety hull, now displayed next to the first. The row: which one is the ship? (1) Anna said obviously the continuously maintained one — it never stopped being the ship, and the reassembled hull is just a reconstruction made of old parts. (2) Ben said the original matter has to win: continuity of form is a bookkeeping convention, and the museum's own labels call the recovered planks "the original material", so the reassembly is the real ship and the maintained one is a replica in the old ship's clothes. (3) Anna then played her trump card: about a third of the stored planks were themselves patched before retirement, some with epoxy and new wood — so the "reassembled original" is partly replacement material, and by Ben's own criterion neither hull is the ship. (4) Ben said that just shows there's no fact of the matter, the whole dispute is about labels, and the museum board could settle it however it liked without being wrong. Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justifications, and say whether Ben's deflationary ending actually follows from Anna's twist or is just a dodge. Oh, and please answer in British English — I'm in Edinburgh and the american spellings set my teeth on edge.
