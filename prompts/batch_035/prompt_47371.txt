I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been invited to apply for a nine-month fellowship that would let me step back from the daily grind and pursue a single ambitious project. I have a half-formed idea and I need you to turn it into the written proposal the fellowship committee expects. Here is the idea in its current, messy state.

The public case for "institutions explain national wealth" — the argument that whether a country is rich or poor depends above all on the rules its elites play by, not on climate, resources, or inherited culture — leans heavily on a small set of showcase natural experiments: the city straddling the US–Mexico border where average income changes abruptly within a few blocks; the two halves of Korea diverging after 1945; Germany's postwar division and reunification. The rhetorical force of these cases comes from the fact that geography and culture are held roughly constant while institutions differ, so the divergence must be institutional. But I have two suspicions I've never seen developed together, and I think they may amount to a serious weakness in the popular version of the argument.

First, a selection problem. The twentieth century produced far more partitions and border splits than the famous ones — colonial boundaries, post-imperial divisions, federations that broke apart — yet only the divergent cases get cited, precisely because divergence is what makes a case memorable. If divided places that stayed similar are just as informative as divided places that diverged, then the showcase sample is biased toward supporting the institutionalist story, and nobody has systematically checked how large the bias is. Second, a bundling problem. The dramatic splits did not just drop different rulebooks on otherwise identical populations; they moved people. There was selective flight and expulsion, differential mortality, who-got-educated-where, decades of propaganda. "Institutions" is being asked to do the causal work of a whole bundle, and the standard narrative never says which component is doing what. And lurking behind both worries is a third: geography's defenders can always redescribe institutions themselves as geography's slow-acting mechanism — coasts and disease environments shaping who settled where, who set up extractive rules — which makes me unsure whether the three-way contest (institutions vs. geography vs. culture) is even a well-posed question or a dispute about where to stop describing one causal chain.

Please turn this into a complete proposal of roughly two pages, written in my voice, covering the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — decide whether it's better framed as an evidential question about natural experiments, a conceptual question about how to individuate the candidate causes, or both, and defend that choice. Explain why existing treatments, popular and scholarly, leave the question open rather than settled. Describe what I would actually do, month by month, being honest about what a working journalist without formal training in econometrics can and cannot execute — though I can, for instance, assemble a comprehensive list of twentieth-century partitions and code the outcomes, and I can interview historians and social scientists. Say what the deliverables would be and why that form suits the finding. State frankly what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that my two suspicions, once developed carefully, turn out to be already-standard caveats that change nobody's mind. Finally, include a short passage answering the skeptical committee member who asks why a magazine journalist rather than an academic should be the one to do this. Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will handle sourcing and references myself.
