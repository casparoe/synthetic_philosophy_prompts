I do flood-response volunteering with a neighborhood team, and last week my friend Ben and I had a knock-down argument about a hypothetical that came up in training and I'm still stewing about it. Scenario: there's a flood, you have one boat and fuel for exactly one round trip, and you know about two households that need evacuating. One house has five people stranded on the roof. The other house, a few minutes further on, has one person. All six are strangers to you, all in equal danger, and you have no reason to think anyone in either group is elderly or a kid or whatever — assume it's a total wash on individual facts. You can only get to one house in time.

I said obviously you go to the five. Ben said no — he'd literally flip a coin between the two houses, and he wasn't trolling, he had arguments. His main one: put yourself in the shoes of the single person in the second house. Why should her chance of being rescued drop from 50% to basically zero just because five other people happen to be stranded together somewhere else? She didn't do anything, she just got unlucky alone instead of unlucky in a group. He also said something like: there's no one on that roof who suffers "five deaths." There are five people who each suffer one death, and each of those individual losses is exactly as big as hers. Counting heads, he said, only makes sense if you think the group is one giant organism with one giant loss, and it isn't.

I pushed back that the coin flip is rigged in her favor — she walks away with a 50% shot when any fair accounting would give her one sixth of the rescue. Ben said one sixth of a rescue isn't a thing anyone gets to claim, since the rescue isn't a pie. I also tried the "wouldn't you change your tune if it were 500 people vs 1?" move and he just... said no, actually, coin flip again, though he admitted that answer felt cold even to him.

We're supposed to actually make calls like this in a real deployment someday, so I want to settle it in my head. Who's being more reasonable here? And is there some principled position between "numbers count fully" and Ben's coin flip, or do you have to pick one of those extremes once you take his objection seriously?
