I got into a huge argument with my roommate last night that went on for like three hours, and it started over something so dumb — she said I was being creepy by messaging a new coworker the way I did, and I asked her to actually explain WHY it was creepy, and she got annoyed and said "can't you just trust my judgment?" And I said no?? If you can't articulate it, why should I just take your word for it?? And she said I take experts' word for it all the time on other stuff. And then we were off to the races about whether you can ever justifiably believe something is wrong (or do something, or not do something) purely on someone's say-so.

Anyway I teach an intro ethics discussion section and I want to channel this fight into something useful for my students. I want a "which view on moral testimony do you actually hold?" quiz, like those personality quiz things, around 8 questions, each one a concrete little scenario with 2-3 answer options. Here's what I care about: the questions have to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which labels they've heard of — none of my students know any of the literature, so every question has to be answerable by a normal person with no background. Some scenarios I want in there: the roommate one (someone whose judgment you trust says your action is wrong but can't explain why), the historian one (you learn some atrocity happened centuries ago and there's basically no way for you to investigate it yourself — can testimony alone give you the belief that it was wrong?), the doctor analogy (if I can rationally take my doctor's word on a medical fact, is moral expertise or good moral character the moral analogue?), and some case where you'd defer on a factual premise of a moral issue but maybe not on the moral verdict itself (like trusting a climate scientist's data but not someone's conclusion about what we owe future generations).

For the scoring key, build it from the distinctions first rather than from a list of names. The way I see it (and tell me if I'm carving it wrong), the tree turns on at least these questions: can testimony transmit moral knowledge at all, or only evidence; even if it can, is there something defective about relying on it, and is the defect epistemic (like you're benighted, you don't really understand) or is it more like a character/conduct thing (you're being a moral freeloader); and does the answer come out different for forming beliefs versus deciding what to do — my roommate's complaint was really about my conduct, and my comeback was really about belief. Then locate the actual positions in that space: the full pessimist (no moral knowledge by testimony), the moderate (knowledge yes, but relying on it in your decisions is somehow off), the view that what you need is understanding rather than mere knowledge so it's fine to defer exactly when you could have worked it out yourself, and the optimist who thinks moral testimony is just testimony. If any combination of answers has no real occupant, say so and say why you think nobody's there — incoherent, or just seems bad, or maybe nobody's thought of it. Also flag honestly which pairs of positions your questions can't actually separate, because a couple of them might only differ on cases weirder than the ones I listed. Keep it usable for a 50-minute section, so the scoring key has to be something students can run on themselves in five minutes.
