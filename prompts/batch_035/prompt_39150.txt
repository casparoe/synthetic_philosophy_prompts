I'm a journalist who writes long-form essays on science, technology, and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about path dependence: the claim that history can be locked in by small, contingent events early on, and that the world we ended up with is only one of several worlds that could plausibly have persisted instead. The stock example in this debate is the QWERTY keyboard, and my editor is, rightly, tired of it. I want to build the essay around a different and richer case: the three-way contest between gasoline, electric, and steam automobiles in the first two decades of the twentieth century, and the century of internal combustion that followed it.

So that we work from the same record, here are the facts as I'll present them. Around 1900, cars sold in the United States ran on all three power sources. Electric cars were quiet, clean, easy to operate, and needed no cranking; they were especially favored by urban drivers and marketed heavily to women. Gasoline cars were noisy, dirty, and dangerous to start — the hand crank could and did break arms — but they had range, refueled quickly, and drew on cheap, abundant oil. By the 1910s the contest was effectively over: mass production of the Model T drove down the price of gasoline cars, a network of fuel depots and repair trades grew up around them, and the electric self-starter, arriving around 1912, removed gasoline's single worst drawback. Electrics became a curiosity; steam faded as well. What followed was a hundred years in which the internal combustion engine was locked in — not merely as a technology but as an infrastructure: filling stations, road-building priorities, supply chains, a trained workforce, and eventually political and regulatory commitments too heavy to dislodge. Only in roughly the last decade, with mature lithium-ion batteries, have electric vehicles returned in force and begun to displace it.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions.

First, map the episode onto the theory. Identify what path dependence would count here as the contingent events, the self-reinforcing mechanisms (on both the manufacturing side and the infrastructure side), and the point — to the decade, if the theory lets you say — at which lock-in became effectively irreversible. Be explicit about the counterfactual: had electric taken the lead in, say, 1910, which of the century's downstream developments would plausibly have differed, and which would have arrived anyway?

Second, tell me what the theory misses or misdescribes. Does path dependence actually explain why gasoline won, or does it only describe the aftermath? How much work is being done by ordinary price competition — cheap oil, cheap assembly line — rather than by increasing returns and network effects? And where the episode doesn't fit the theory's categories, say so rather than bending the case to fit.

Third, and this is the part I most want: the century-later twist cuts both ways, and I'd like you to adjudicate. Critics of the strong path-dependence view hold that efficiency converges in the end — the same outcomes arrive anyway, just later — and the electric revival looks at first like a clean win for them: after a century of lock-in, the alternative technology finally won, so was the lock-in ever really binding? But the same facts can be read as the strongest lock-in case on record: the internal combustion engine ruled for a hundred years, and only gave way when an entirely separate technological frontier — batteries — matured, which suggests the alternative was unavailable all along and the "wrong" technology was entrenched for ten decades. Which reading does this episode better support, and, more importantly, what would distinguish the two readings empirically? If a reader wanted to know whether some present-day lock-in is real or temporary, what does this case teach them to look for?

Finally, be candid about where the theory is silent. If there are parts of this story path dependence simply has nothing to say about — the roles of taste, status, and marketing, say, or of the oil discoveries that happened independently of the car market — flag them as outside the theory's remit rather than shoehorning them in.

One request about register: don't build the answer out of the QWERTY literature or the standard secondary debate around it, don't give me a reading list, and don't lean on attributions I'd have to verify. The analysis should stand on the facts as stated and on the theory in its general form. I'd like something I could adapt, with compression, into the analytical spine of a four-thousand-word essay.
