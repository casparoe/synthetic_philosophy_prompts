The exam question was:

"Set out the argument from illusion for sense-data (or, more cautiously, for a 'highest common factor' shared by veridical and non-veridical experience). Then explain the disjunctivist's response. What does the disjunctivist gain by responding this way, and what is the view's biggest cost?"

And here's the student's answer:

The argument from illusion starts from cases where something appears other than it is. Classic examples: a straight stick looks bent half-submerged in water, or you press on your eyeball and see double. In the bent-stick case, the stick appears bent, but there is no bent stick in the world — the stick is straight. Still, something is genuinely appearing to you, and it's appearing bent, so there must be something bent that you're directly aware of, call it a sense-datum. Ayer makes the same point with the table in Language, Truth and Logic: seen from an angle, a rectangular table presents a trapezoidal appearance, and since the table itself isn't trapezoidal, what we're directly aware of must be some mind-dependent item that is. The crucial move is generalization: for all the subject can tell, any veridical perception could have been an illusion, and things seem the same to the subject either way. So even in the good case, what we're directly aware of is a sense-datum, and we never directly perceive physical objects at all.

The disjunctivist denies the common factor. Veridical perception and hallucination (or illusion) do not share a common mental component — they're fundamentally different kinds of state. In the good case the experience is partly constituted by the external objects themselves: seeing the stick just is standing in a relation to the stick, not to some inner item. That's why it's called disjunctivism: any perceptual experience of the relevant sort is either a veridical perception of the world or a mere appearance, and there's no single kind of state common to both that would ground our concept of experience.

The disjunctivist gains a lot here. First, the argument from illusion is simply dissolved: since there are no sense-data, there is no argument left to give. Second, they get an answer to skepticism about the external world: veridical perception is factive, so if you're in the good case you're genuinely in contact with the world, which means you can know you're not hallucinating — the skeptic's challenge never even gets started. Third, on this view hallucination differs from veridical perception not just in its remote causes but in what it's like from the inside, so with sufficiently careful introspection the two are in principle distinguishable from the subject's own point of view.

The cost is that the disjunctivist owes us a story about the bad cases. If hallucination shares nothing mental with veridical perception, it's hard to see how the concept of experience can apply univocally to both, and they need to account for hallucination's phenomenal character without borrowing the resources they just denied themselves.
