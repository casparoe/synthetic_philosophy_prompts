I'm a high school junior taking an elective on the history of the Holocaust, and we have just finished reading Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men, about Reserve Police Battalion 101. Our teacher distributed a one-page handout meant to situate the book in its famous dispute with Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust. The handout says, in substance, the following:

"Browning's explanation is essentially the Milgram experiment applied to history. He studied a battalion of middle-aged, working-class policemen from Hamburg who were ordered to shoot and deport the Jews of occupied Poland, and concluded that they killed because they were obeying authority: the men knew that refusal would bring the death penalty or the camps down on them and their families, so conformity under coercion won out. Since almost every man in the battalion participated, Browning titled his book Ordinary Men — his message being that under comparable pressures, ordinary people almost anywhere would have done the same. Goldhagen rejected this as letting the killers off the hook. Working from the same material, he argued that the men were not coerced at all: they killed willingly, sometimes volunteering, and the reason was eliminationist antisemitism — a hatred of Jews so pervasive in German culture that virtually the entire nation approved of the elimination of the Jews. For Goldhagen, therefore, the perpetrators were ordinary Germans rather than ordinary men, and culture, not situation, does the explanatory work. Both historians relied on the same postwar interrogation records of the battalion's members, and both treated that testimony as straightforwardly reliable, which was possible only because it was collected decades later, when the men could no longer be punished."

The handout closes by declaring that the case is now closed: because Goldhagen cannot explain why the battalion's commander gave his men the option to step out of the shooting, most historians regard his book as refuted, and the field has settled on Browning's situational account.

I am inclined to accept the closing verdict, mainly because I have also been taught about Milgram in psychology, and the handout's version of Browning fits that story very neatly. But that neatness is exactly what makes me nervous. Would you go through the handout claim by claim and tell me which claims a historian working on the perpetrators would accept, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong? In particular, I would like to know: (a) whether Browning's account is correctly described as obedience under threat of punishment, or whether that flattens what he actually found about the handful of men who did refuse; (b) whether the claim that both historians treated the testimony as straightforwardly reliable is fair to either of them, given what each was trying to do with those sources; (c) whether the handout's description of Goldhagen is a fair statement of the eliminationist-antisemitism thesis or a distortion of it; and (d) whether the conclusion that the case is closed follows from anything that survives your audit — or whether the debate, once the handout's errors are removed, looks like something other than a simple situation-versus-culture contest with one winner.
