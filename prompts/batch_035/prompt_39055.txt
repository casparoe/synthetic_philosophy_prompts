I never went to college, so take this as just a guy who's been chewing on this stuff for a couple of years, but I got deep into Thomas Szasz a while back. The Myth of Mental Illness, his whole thing: that "mental illness" isn't like a broken leg, it's a metaphor, and most of what we call mental illness is really just problems in living -- life situations, moral conflicts, bad habits, people behaving in ways society doesn't like. And when society changes its mind about what it doesn't like, the "illness" disappears, which for him was the tell. No bacteria or tumor ever had to be found or lost.

So here's the episode I keep coming back to and I want you to actually analyze it through Szasz's lense, properly, both directions. The removal of homosexuality from the DSM. Quick facts so we're on the same page: it was in there from the first DSM in 1952, as a "sexual deviation" under the "sociopathic personality disturbance" heading. Then gay rights activists, especially after Stonewall, started showing up at APA conventions around 1970-72, disrupting talks, sometimes heckling psychiatrists who called them sick. Also Evelyn Hooker's research from the 50s showed gay men who weren't patients scored the same on tests as everyone else, and Kinsey's numbers had made homosexuality look a lot less rare. In December 1973 the APA board of trustees voted to drop it, the membership confirmed by referendum in 1974 (by a pretty thin margin from what I remember), and the manual was quietly updated -- though they kept "ego-dystonic homosexuality" (basically: you're only diagnosable if being gay distresses you) all the way until 1987. The psychiatrist Robert Spitzer, who'd been more on the anti side, brokered a lot of the compromise.

So the obvious Szaszian reading: a "disease" was created by a committee and deleted by a committee, after protests, heckling and a vote. Nobody found the virus. That looks like his smoking gun, no? If it was ever medicine, the cure wouldn't come from a picket line and a referendum.

But then I start doubting myself, because I can read it the exact opposite way: psychiatry took in new evidence (Hooker's studies, Kinsey's numbers), argued about it, and self-corrected, the way science is supposed to. The wrong diagnosis got removed because it failed empirically. On that reading the episode shows the APA behaving like a real medical field correcting a real error -- the opposite of a myth.

And there's stuff I genuinely can't fit either way, which is where I want you to do the work. The vote. Medicine doesn't usually settle diagnoses by referendum at all, so what does it mean that this one went to a vote? The ego-dystonic compromise -- is that the myth shrinking, or just finding a cleverer hiding place (still medicalizing, but now the distress of living in a homophobic society gets labeled the symptom)? And Szasz himself was obsessed with coercion, the state locking people up -- but this fight was mostly about a label, activism from the outside, nobody was involuntarily committed for the DSM code number itself as far as I know. Does his framework even have anything to say about an episode that's about stigma and classification rather than force?

So: walk me through it as a case study. Map who the players were and what Szasz's theory says each side was actually doing. Then be honest about what the theory explains well here, what it misdescribes, and where it's just silent. And then run it the other way -- does this episode confirm Szasz, refute him, or is it a case his theory never anticipated and doesn't really fit? I'd especially like your take on whether the 1973 removal is the strongest evidence FOR his view that exists, or actually one of the strongest against it, because I keep flip-flopping and I'd like to know which reading survives contact with the details.
