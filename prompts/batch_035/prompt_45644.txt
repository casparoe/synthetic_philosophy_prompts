I'm a PhD student in social policy writing a dissertation chapter on benefit conditionality, and I've got a puzzle I can't resolve on my own. In most welfare states I study, unemployment benefits come wrapped in obligations: claimants must sign job-search agreements, document their applications, accept offers of "suitable" work, and attend mandatory employability training, with payments suspended for weeks or months when they fail to comply. The system is designed on the assumption that the claimant's unemployment is, at least in part, something they are responsible for managing through their own effort. Yet the very same states handle other luck-dependent conditions quite differently: nobody's disability pension is sanctioned for non-attendance at physiotherapy, nobody's old-age pension is reduced for a lifetime of poor dietary choices that raised public health costs, and a worker made redundant is compensated as the victim of circumstance rather than its author. So the same administrative apparatus seems to treat the unemployed person as an agent answerable for their situation while treating the sick person, the old person, and the redundant person as patients of fate. What I want to know is what theory of moral responsibility and desert this pattern actually presupposes. Is it something like a luck egalitarian view holding that the state compensates misfortune but not choices? A reciprocity or contractualist view about what members owe the cooperative scheme? A purely behaviorist view in which conditionality is just an incentive device with no desert commitments at all? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several of these?

There's a second layer to the puzzle that I suspect is doing a lot of work. Conditionality is deliberately insensitive to local labour market conditions: the job-search obligations and sanction regime are essentially identical whether unemployment in the claimant's region is 2% or 20%, even though the causal contribution of the claimant's own effort to finding work obviously differs enormously between those two settings. If the implicit theory is that effort rather than luck determines outcomes, the flat sanction regime seems to betray it precisely where it matters; if the implicit theory is instead something more coercive or disciplinary, the desert language that surrounds the policy in public debate looks like window dressing. I'd also like you to address the contrast case that got me started: the enormous administrative and political effort devoted to detecting benefit fraud by the poor, relative to the far more casual treatment of tax fraud by the better-off, which involves much larger sums. Is that asymmetry expressive of the same underlying commitments as conditionality, or does it presuppose something further, say a distinction between the state's money given and the state's money not taken?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires a theorist to believe from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identifying which rival views of responsibility for circumstance the practice quietly rules out. Then tell me what the sanction regime and the fraud-enforcement asymmetry would each have to look like if the state instead built them on the rival view — for instance, if it took seriously the claim that unemployment in a depressed market is no more choice-dependent than illness. I'd like continuous prose only, the sort of thing I could paste into a dissertation literature review: no headings, no bullet points, no tables.
