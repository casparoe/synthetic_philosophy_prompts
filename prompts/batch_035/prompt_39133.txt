I took a Chinese philosophy course last semester and we spent basically the whole second half on the Mencius-Xunzi thing about whether human nature is good or bad, and my reading group has been going around in circles on it since. What strikes me is that the two sides agree on way more than you'd expect from how different their slogans sound: both grant that humans have selfish appetites that run wild without training, both think almost nobody becomes a decent person without cultivation, both think ritual and habit matter enormously. Mencius isn't saying babies come out virtuous and Xunzi isn't saying they're little monsters. And yet whenever someone brings up Mencius's examples — the person who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well and feels alarm without calculating anything, or the way anyone shown a corpse treated like garbage would feel revulsion — the Xunzians in my group say that's just a feeling, not the seed of virtue, and the Mencians say if it's not a seed then where does the sense of shame even come from. Nobody has budged an inch in months.

My working theory is that at least half of the standoff is about the word "nature" itself, since the term the two of them share is doing different jobs: Mencius seems to mean something like the distinctive tendencies that make humans humans and that flourish if you nurture them, while Xunzi seems to mean whatever's in you prior to any deliberate shaping, which is exactly the raw material that needs to be carved like crooked wood. On those readings they might not even be disagreeing — Xunzi could accept that we have proto-moral reactions and still insist they belong to our untutored nature and are too weak to count. But if it's just verbal, I can't explain why the practical payoffs diverge so much: Mencius leans toward the authority coming from something already in you that you extend outward, Xunzi toward moral order being essentially an artifact, something sages constructed and we sustain. That feels like a real disagreement about where the source of normativity sits, not a translation quibble, though I'm honestly not sure whether it's that or a difference in what counts as evidence about human nature in the first place.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of the two answering different questions under one heading — say, one doing something like empirical developmental psychology and the other doing a theory of where standards come from? Is some hidden question doing the work, like whether moral goodness has to grow from inside or can be imposed from outside? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here: would a sharper distinction (some third account of what "nature" could mean), or evidence about what small children are actually like, or recasting the whole debate in terms neither of them used (e.g., the modern innate-bias-plus-institution story, which gets floated in our group a lot) genuinely dissolve the standoff, or would it just relocate it? And if nothing should be expected to settle a dispute this old, say so and explain why.
