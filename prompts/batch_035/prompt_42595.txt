So I'm a philosophy grad student and in a few weeks I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on the boundary (or lack of one) between moral psychology and metaethics. My paper defends a fairly specific claim: the empirical literature on cross-cultural variation in folk morality — the whole WEIRD-psychology stuff, where industrialized Western samples turn out to be global outliers on things like harm norms, fairness intuitions, and how much weight people put on intentions versus outcomes — doesn't by itself threaten moral objectivity. My argument, roughly: a lot of the documented variation concerns thick, institution-dependent norms (purity, loyalty, deference, hierarchy), the thin core of harm and fairness judgments shows striking convergence once you control for factual beliefs (people who think witches cause crop failures aren't disagreeing with us about whether causing crop failures is bad), and the remaining disagreement mostly tracks disagreement about non-moral facts rather than about moral principles themselves — the way disagreement about the shape of the earth doesn't undermine the objectivity of geography.

The audience is going to be tricky though. It's mostly metaethicists and moral philosophers, but two of the invited speakers are empirically-minded moral psychologists who are frankly kind of contemptuous of philosophers who hand-wave the cross-cultural data, and there's also a couple of self-identified relativists in the room who've been waiting years for someone to make my kind of argument so they can tear into it. I'm worried the metaethicists will come at me with the "abstraction is cheap" objection — that you can always rescue objectivity by redescribing disagreement as merely apparent, at which point the claim does no work — and the psychologists will come at me with the data itself (some of which I only know secondhand), e.g. that supposedly "universal" harm norms get swamped by punishment norms and community-level factors in small-scale societies, or that the intention/outcome asymmetry flips in predictable ways across populations, which sounds a lot more like deep disagreement than factual error to me when I stare at it too long.

Act as an experienced philosopher who has sat through many hostile Q&As and prepare me a realistic prep sheet. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this specific audience, ordered by how much damage they could do, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them (the psychologist's version of a point should sound different from the relativist's version). For each, give me a two-minute reply I could deliver under pressure and an honest note about where the reply is soft — I want to know what to concede up front rather than get caught backpedaling. And crucially, tell me which question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, the one that if asked well I don't have a good answer to, so I can at least have something prepared instead of just praying. Think carefully about the actual workshop dynamics too: who pipes up first, who waits to pile on, and which objection becomes lethal only when two of them are asked back to back.
