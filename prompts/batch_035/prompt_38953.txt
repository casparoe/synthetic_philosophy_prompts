I'm circulating a short position statement on moral luck ahead of a workshop where it will be dissected, and before I send it I want an audit of whether the commitments actually cohere. Here is the set, roughly as I hold them:

1. A strong control principle: no difference in how blameworthy or praiseworthy two agents are can be grounded in any difference outside their control. If two agents exercise the same care, choose for the same reasons, and differ only in how the world responds, they are equally assessable.
2. What makes an act a proper object of moral assessment is what it expresses about the agent's character and quality of will — and character, even character one did not choose and could not easily have had, is genuinely the agent's own. I take it there is no regress here: there is no further self standing behind character that selected it, so the buck stops at character.
3. Resultant luck does not affect blameworthiness. Two contractors skip the same safety inspection on the same kind of scaffolding; a gust collapses one scaffold and kills a pedestrian, while the other is never tested by wind at all. They are equally blameworthy, full stop.
4. Circumstantial luck does not either. The junior diplomat posted to the Buenos Aires embassy in 1927 and his colleague, identical in outlook and resolve, posted instead to Berlin — the one is not a better person than the other, whatever relief he may have earned.
5. Reactive attitudes: I think the pedestrian's family would be right — fitting, not merely understandable — to feel toward the unlucky contractor a resentment they do not and need not feel toward the lucky one, and I do not think this is a bias our practice should be corrected out of.
6. On the praise side, I admire the naturally gentle person more than the equally well-behaved person who acts gently only against a steady drag of sour impulse. The ease of the first one's kindness seems to me genuinely more admirable.
7. Although the two contractors are equally blameworthy, only the unlucky one owes compensation to the family — and I do not think that difference is an injustice.
8. Punishment: I hold that deserved punishment must track blameworthiness. Yet I cannot bring myself to think our courts act unjustly whenever the completed harm is sentenced more harshly than the identical attempt, and I equally cannot accept that the extra years are desert. Something seems to me to be going right in that sentencing gap, not merely something pragmatic, and I don't know how to say what.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look to be, and for each real tension what the cheapest repair would cost in plausibility elsewhere. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave, and tell me whether any single revision would clear up most of the mess — or whether the whole set is rotten at the root, i.e., whether the control principle as I've stated it in (1) can survive contact with any of (2), (5), (6), and (8) at all. Feel free to use Nagel's fourfold carve-up (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) only where it earns its keep, not as a template for the answer. Two further requests. First, I've tried not to signal which commitment I'd give up, and I'd like to keep it that way — diagnose the set, don't flatter my probable favorites. Second, if you think one of the theses is straightforwardly confused, or that a case I've described smuggles in a substantive assumption, say so plainly rather than finding a charitable reading by force; I'd rather hear the audit is of a muddle than get a polite tour of one.
