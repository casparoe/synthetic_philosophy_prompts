My friend and I co-organize a graduate reading group on AI safety, and for the past year our meetings have reliably ended with the two of us arguing about corrigibility and the shutdown problem — whether an artificial agent can be built that would let itself be paused, edited, or switched off without resisting, and what exactly stands in the way. The reading group has started to find it tiresome, so we've agreed to do what adversarial collaborators do: produce a joint document we could both actually put our names to. She holds roughly the anti-naturalness view — that corrigibility is in tension with capable agency as such, since a goal-directed agent treats shutdown as a threat to its goals, so the more capable the system, the more pressure against being turned off, and apparent corrigibility is either shallow mimicry or capability we've sacrificed. I hold roughly the design-problem view — that corrigibility is unusual but achievable, for instance by building agents that are uncertain about their own objectives and treat human interventions as evidence about them, so that a sufficiently uncertain agent can prefer to be shut down, and the standard off-switch setup (in which an agent that defers to the human about whether to act or be disabled does better than one that barges ahead) shows the logic can work rather than merely asserting it.

Please draft the joint statement. It should have a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — covering what the shutdown problem actually is, what instrumental reasoning does and does not imply about shutdown resistance, what the off-switch result does and does not establish, and what both sides would count as progress. Then a disagreements table, with each remaining disagreement phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording: not strawmen, not truisms. Then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether corrigibility survives out of distribution, and another whether goal-uncertainty is a stable equilibrium or erodes as the agent learns and its uncertainty shrinks, but identify whatever you think the genuine cruxes are — and for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or line of argument that both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome written down in advance.

One more request: my friend and I have come to suspect that part of our own dispute is verbal — for instance about which of shutdown-friendliness, deference to correction, and indifference to goal replacement deserves the single word corrigibility. If you think part of the dispute between these two camps is likewise verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why; if you think none of it is, say that too, since we'd want to know whether we've been arguing about the world or about a word.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with recorded predictions. Please be exact rather than diplomatic — a bland statement neither of us would dispute is a failure, not a success.
