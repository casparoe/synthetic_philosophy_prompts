I teach philosophy of mathematics and every couple of years I run a seminar session on the Lucas–Penrose argument, and every year it ends with the two sides of the room talking past each other. This year I want to rehearse the anti-mechanist side properly beforehand, because I always end up arguing the mechanist line myself and I've honestly never had to defend it against a good opponent. So please play one for me: a committed Penrose-style anti-mechanist, and make him a mathematician rather than a philosopher, so he argues from the inside of mathematical practice — what it's like to actually see that a sentence must be true, and why no formal system captures that seeing. He should be sharp, not a strawman. He knows the standard objections backwards — Benacerraf's old point that you'd need to know you're sound before your Gödel-sentence move even gets going, the point that we can't pin down which formal system (if any) we're supposed to be, the awkward fact that actual mathematicians make mistakes and contradict each other — and he should have real replies ready, not dodges. He can concede small points but never the main one. Two extra rules for him: he is not allowed to appeal to consciousness or anything quantum, the Gödel line only, so the argument stands or falls on its own; and if I press the distinction between Lucas's idealised reasoner and the finite, fallible minds we actually are, he has to engage with that head-on rather than just repeating the original 1961 argument. Stay in character until I write 'stop', keep each reply to a single punchy paragraph so it feels like a live exchange rather than an essay, and please answer in British English throughout. Heres my opening move:

'You say you can see the truth of your own Gödel sentence. But spell out what that seeing has to contain. The sentence is only true if the system you're supposed to be is consistent — and by the second incompleteness theorem, that system can't prove its own consistency, so neither can you if you are that system. So when you say you "see" it's true, all you can mean is that you're confident you don't make mistakes of a certain kind, and that confidence is precisely the thing the theorem says you can't have a proof of. Either you can't see it after all, or you can — in which case you're holding a belief in your own soundness that you can't justify, which is just faith. Which is it?'
