So my doctor put me on the spot last week — she offered me a low-dose antidepressant to get me through the next year, which is going to be rough because I'm the one caring for my mom as she declines, and I realized I can't decide because my gut verdicts about happiness and what makes a life go well dont actually add up to anything coherent. I need someone to figure out what I actually believe, and I'd rather you give me a real committed answer, not a menu.

Here are my gut calls on a bunch of cases. Nozick's experience machine: not plugging in, and it's not close — you get a perfectly blissful simulated life and never know the difference, and I still think I'd be getting cheated. A guy who dies content believing his kids adore him, his charity saved lives, and his wife was faithful, when none of it is true: bad life, robbed, even though he never noticed. A pill that just makes an already genuinely good life feel better (the friends, the job, all of it are real): totally fine, kind of the point. The same pill given to a guy whose actual life is bad — failing marriage, dead-end job — so he feels fine about it: I hate this one, it feels like cheating, which is wierd because it's the same pill. My grandma with advanced dementia: mostly happy, doesn't remember the losses, likes her nurses and her shows, and when I'm with her I honestly believe her life is going well for her right now. My brother, who spends five hours a night in his VR rig, knows perfectly well its fake, and calls it the best part of his day: bugs me way less than the machine case, even though he's choosing the illusion with eyes open, which should count against it more. And a scientist whose life objectively goes great — real friends, real accomplishments — but who feels flat about all of it: I still want to say her life is going well, just with something wrong in the experiencing of it.

Is there one theory of wellbeing that captures all of these, or close? Give me your actual best answer, pick one, state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop and why that one, and tell me what it predicts on cases I didn't list. Then run it on my real decision: the pill that would blunt the worst of this year and probably some of the good of it too, while the facts of the year stay exactly what they are. And tell me honestly if the answer it gives there is one I should trust or a sign the principle's broken. Please answer in American English, and dont hedge into "it depends on what you value" — I know that, that's why I'm asking.
