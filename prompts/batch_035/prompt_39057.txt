Retired diplomat here, 28 years in the foreign service, mostly arms control and bilateral trade negotiations. I recently stumbled on something called Aumann's agreement theorem — the claim, as I understand it, that two rational agents who start with the same prior beliefs, and who each learn different things, and who then honestly tell each other what they now believe, cannot end up disagreeing once they both know the other's estimate. Supposedly rational people literally cannot agree to disagree. This sounds alien to me, so I want a translation.

Here's the vocabulary I actually use, and I'm fluent in it: positions vs underlying interests, signaling and reading signals, face-saving exit ramps, credible commitments, back-channels, escalation ladders, confidence-building measures, constructive ambiguity, "reading the room", the difference between what a delegation says at the press stakeout and what the envoy says over dinner. I have no formal training in probability or economics — "posterior" is not a word I've ever used in a sentence.

Please restate the theorem and the argument around it in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. So: a prior would be, what, the shared working assumption both capitals start from? Common knowledge would be the situation where each side knows the other has seen the cable, and knows the other knows, down the chain — I want to know whether that chain, which we gestured at but never spelled out, is doing real work in the theorem or is just mathematical decoration. And the "telling each other your estimate" part — map that onto signaling, and be honest about whether the theorem assumes the signals can't be faked, because in my world half of signaling is exactly about faking.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the theorem translate cleanly, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no expression at all in how negotiators talk. I have a suspicion about the residue, tell me if it holds: in my world "we agree to disagree" almost never means two people with the same beliefs looking at the same evidence — it means the sides want different things, or trust each other so little that no declaration of belief would be believed. If that's right, then the theorem might translate fine but be about a narrower slice of diplomacy than it first appears: the intelligence-assessment conversation between allies, maybe, but not the bargaining itself. Or is that me smuggling in my framework's assumption that beliefs and interests are cleanly separable, when actually they bleed into each other? That last question is the one I actually care about.
