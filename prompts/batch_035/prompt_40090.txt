A long book review I read last weekend contains a paragraph-summarizing Edmund Burke and Chesterton's fence that I mostly nodded along to, but a couple of moves in it struck me as too tidy, and I'd like to know which parts to trust. The review's paragraph runs, roughly: Burke's objection to the French Revolution was that societies should never change anything quickly; institutions carry the accumulated wisdom of past generations, so any attempt at sweeping reform is doomed. Chesterton's fence — never remove a fence until you know why it was put there — is presented as the same idea in modern dress, which leads the reviewer to conclude that both thinkers are best read as defending the status quo on principle: whatever has survived for a long time must be doing something useful, so there is always a presumption in favor of keeping it. The paragraph also glosses Burke's use of the word prejudice (his term for inherited commitments handed down through custom) as the claim that inherited commitments deserve more trust than the reasoning of any individual, and it ends by saying that the position therefore amounts to the view that no one can ever justify changing anything — which, the reviewer notes, is why critics ever since Paine have dismissed Burke as an apologist for whatever power structure happens to exist.

Some background on me, so you can pitch the answer: I took one political philosophy course years ago and have read the Reflections only in excerpts, so I can't check the summary against the text myself, and my instinct is that it is roughly right in spirit. Please go through the paragraph piece by piece and tell me which claims a Burke scholar would accept, which they would accept only with qualifications, and which are genuinely misrepresentations. In particular I want to understand: whether Burke actually held that sweeping reform is always doomed, or whether his target was something narrower than the reviewer suggests; whether Chesterton's fence is really the same claim as Burke's, or whether the two come apart — for instance, whether Chesterton's point is a demand to inquire rather than a presumption about what inquiry will find, and whether it commits anyone to conservatism at all; whether the survival-based reading (whatever has survived must be useful) is Chesterton's, Burke's, or a third thing being smuggled in; and whether the gloss on prejudice is fair to what Burke was doing in reclaiming that word. Finally, tell me whether the reviewer's ending — the inference from all of this to the conclusion that no one can ever justify change — follows from the accurate parts of the summary or only from the inaccurate ones.

Throughout, please keep two kinds of claims visibly separate: what is broadly settled among people who work on Burke and on Chesterton's fence, and what is genuinely contested — for example, whether Burke's case for inherited institutions is ultimately an epistemic one about the limits of individual reason or a substantive one about the value of particular inherited arrangements, and whether that distinction even holds together in his writing. Where a point is a matter of live scholarly disagreement, say so rather than presenting either side as the consensus, and tell me how confident to be about each item in your audit.
