My research partner and I have spent the better part of two years arguing about what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain, and after yet another seminar that ended with us talking past each other, we've agreed to try to write up an adversarial-collaboration-style joint statement instead. The clinical picture we both accept: patients with pain asymbolia — typically following damage to regions such as the posterior insula or anterior cingulate cortex — report that they feel painful stimuli, can locate them and judge their intensity, and yet do not mind them at all; they may smile or laugh at pinpricks and offer no resistance to sustained noxious stimulation, while responding to other threats with normal fear and remaining otherwise rational and psychologically intact.

I hold that the unpleasantness of pain is a distinct affective-motivational component layered on top of a sensory-discriminative sensation, and that asymbolics have the sensation without the affect: on my view pain is not intrinsically bad, and what is bad about ordinary pain is the distress and aversion that usually accompany it — components that, as asymbolia shows, can come apart from the sensation itself. My partner holds instead that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the pain sensation: an affect-free "pain" is not genuine pain but a mere nociceptive sensation, so the asymbolic either does not genuinely feel pain, or feels pain that really is bad while their cheerful reaction reflects an anomalous expression of or access to their state rather than an absence of badness.

Please write the document we should have produced: a numbered section of claims both of us can sign (about the phenomenology, the clinical data, and what is or is not separable from what); a disagreements table stating exactly where we part ways, with each disagreement phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the asymbolic's experience counts as genuine pain, and another is whether the "badness" at issue is phenomenal badness, badness-for-the-subject, or mere disvalue — and, for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Where you think a residual disagreement between us is merely verbal, say which part and why. Please write in British English.
