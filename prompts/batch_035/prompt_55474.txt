In his 1758 Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, Rousseau argued against d'Alembert's suggestion, in his Encyclopédie article on Geneva, that the city should establish a playhouse. The argument is not a general philistinism: Rousseau thought theatre perfectly suited to monarchies and great capitals, where it amuses idle subjects and keeps them harmlessly occupied, but corrosive to small republics, because it converts active citizens into passive spectators who purchase the appearance of virtue for the price of a ticket while the real thing goes unpracticed. He also faulted Molière's Misanthrope for making candor ridiculous and smooth sociability win, and proposed in place of the theatre public festivals in which the citizens themselves would be the spectacle, invoking Sparta and suggesting that something as modest as a maypole crowned with flowers in a public square would do more for Geneva's morals than any stage.

Please read Lin-Manuel Miranda's Hamilton through the lens of this argument, and tell me whether Rousseau's critique lands. On its face the show is a prime exhibit for the prosecution: a spectacle about the founding of a republic, staged for metropolitan audiences at premium ticket prices, whose spectators leave having watched other people deliberate about politics rather than having deliberated. Yet several features complicate the charge. The casting and idiom are themselves a civic claim — the founding story performed by the America of the present — and the show's reception has been participatory in ways eighteenth-century playgoing was not: audiences memorize and perform the cabinet battles, rap battles have become a schoolyard genre, and a filmed version has put the performance in countless homes. The show also aestheticizes conflict itself — the cabinet debates as rap battles, the personal quarrels escalating to duels — which one might read either as Rousseau's nightmare of drama substituting for action or as deliberation made newly vivid. And in King George's numbers the show displays a comic knowingness about the pomps of monarchy, almost staging Rousseau's contrast between monarchical spectacle and republican self-display.

I would like a reading that takes both Rousseau's argument and the musical seriously. Where does the critique genuinely bite — for instance, does the show's fandom, for all its participation, remain consumption of a fixed artifact rather than the mutual self-display Rousseau envisioned? Where does it misfire because Hamilton is not the kind of theatre Rousseau was attacking — is his account of the spectator too passive to describe an audience that memorizes, remixes, and answers back? And does the show expose something Rousseau could not have seen, such as what happens when a would-be republican festival is recorded, licensed, and streamed, or when its consumers are also its performers? I am less interested in a verdict than in an account of where the musical resists the Rousseauian reading and where it confirms it, and of what the confrontation teaches about the difference between spectacle and festival that neither Rousseau's letter nor the show can articulate alone.
