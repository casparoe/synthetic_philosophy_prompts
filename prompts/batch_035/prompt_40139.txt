I teach a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and this term we paired Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial with material on negligence in both tort and criminal law. At our final colloquium, a student presented an argument she had built out of that pairing, and I confess it has been bothering me since. She began from Arendt's familiar claim that Eichmann's crimes issued not from demonic hatred or even from settled ideological conviction, but from thoughtlessness — an inability to look at his own deeds from the standpoint of those who suffered them. She then argued as follows: (1) A person is answerable for a wrong only if the wrong issues from what he willed — from a genuine exercise of choice. (2) What issues from an inability is not willed; the law marks this distinction wherever it finds genuine inability, which is why insanity, coercion, and infancy excuse rather than accuse. (3) Eichmann's deeds issued from an inability, on Arendt's own account, and so were not willed. (4) Therefore Eichmann is excused as an instrument rather than an author, and at most is guilty of careerism and poor character — faults that no one hangs for. (5) The only escape is to manufacture a new offence, the offence of failing to think, whose dangerousness she illustrated by observing that it would indict every conformist in every ministry in every state in history.

The student herself was happy with neither horn. Excusing Eichmann strikes her (and me) as intolerable — and, she noted pointedly, it was intolerable to Arendt too, who nonetheless endorsed the Jerusalem court's death sentence. But convicting every thoughtless clerk strikes her as monstrous in the other direction, since it criminalises a disposition rather than a deed and collapses the distinction between the man who schedules the trains and the man who stamps the visas that the trains require. She concluded that Arendt's whole account is incoherent: it cannot both explain Eichmann and condemn him. My own suspicion is that something in her argument is too strong — it seems to me to prove far too much from premises that each looked unobjectionable when she recited them — but I cannot for the life of me locate the slip, and I have noticed that the harder I press on step (2), the more it begins to look like strict liability for inner character, which is exactly the crime she says the argument manufactures. Please tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked as innocent as it did, and what rejecting it costs — both for Arendt's account and for the general theory of responsibility. For what it is worth, I would much prefer to keep (1), since I am unwilling to hold anyone answerable for what did not flow from his will at all; if you take that away from me, I want to know precisely what I am being asked to swallow in its place.
