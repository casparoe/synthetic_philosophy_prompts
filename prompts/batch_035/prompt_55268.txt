I'm a senior flight instructor — around 8000 hours, CFI-I/MEI, lots of time in 172s and Seminoles, some turboprop time — and I've been half-following the philosophy fight about "knowing how" vs "knowing that" (Ryle, and the people who pushed back on him around 2001, Stanley and Williamson I think). I understand it only at the airport-briefing level of detail and I want it in my vocabulary, because I think flight training is maybe the purest case of the whole distinction: my whole job is taking a student who can recite the POH cover to cover and turning them into someone who can actually land the thing.

First, so you translate faithfully instead of guessing, here's my vocabulary and how fluent I am: hand-flying, flows and flow checks, the scan (fixation is the enemy), stabilized approach criteria, the "step on the ball" rule, seat-of-the-pants flying, partial panel, unusual attitude recovery, the PTS/ACS standards, checking out in a new type, proficiency decay, the crosswind landing as the thing that separates pilots from owners, "fly the plane first," and the magenta-line kids — students who can fly the flight director beautifully and fall apart when you fail the vacuum pump. I know this stuff cold; treat me as fluent, and don't translate into it dumbed-down.

The debate as I half-understand it: Ryle said knowing-how is not a kind of knowing-that — skills are dispositions and abilities, and the claim that every intelligent act is preceded by consulting a proposition leads to a regress (applying the rule needs another rule for the applying, and so on, nobody ever gets to fly). The intellectualists say no, knowledge-how just IS propositional knowledge under a practical presentation — their case is the ski instructor who can no longer ski well enough to demonstrate but still knows how to perform a parallel turn in the way the student doesn't. The skier case is what I actually care about, because I'm the ski instructor every day: I'm 58, my own landings are no longer the thing I'd show a checkride examiner, and I still know how to land a light twin on one engine in a way a fresh commercial pilot reading the same AFM doesn't.

Here's the job. Restate both sides in my vocabulary, as honestly as you can. For the anti-intellectualist: what do skills-as-dispositions, and the regress, look like in terms of flows, the scan, muscle memory, proficiency decay? For the intellectualist: what does "propositional knowledge under a practical mode of presentation" even come to in my world — is there anything in my vocabulary that could be a proposition held a different way, or does the whole idea need to be smuggled in under a phrase I'd never use on a lesson? Use my skiing-instructor situation as the running case if it helps.

Then the residue report, which is the part I most want: what translates clean, what translates only with distortion, and what can't be said in my vocabulary at all. Be suspicious in both directions — maybe my vocabulary can't express what the intellectualists mean, but also maybe their position only sounds like a position, and I'd genuinely like a verdict on which it is. And there's one case I want handled explicitly: the student who can fly beautifully and explain nothing, versus the instructor who can explain everything and barely fly anymore. Which of those two has the knowledge-how, and does either theory actually predict the answer?

Finally, don't leave me with "both capture something important." Take a side. After doing the translation honestly, tell me which view you actually think is right and defend it in a paragraph I could argue with. If your honest view is that the whole debate mostly dissolves once you speak fluent instructor, say that and defend it — but you still have to tell me what's left over that my vocabulary genuinely can't carry. I'd rather get a definite position I disagree with than a both-sides summary I can't do anything with. Also, quick correction early on is fine — if I've garbled Stanley and Williamson or the regress somewhere above, flag it rather than translating my garbled version.
