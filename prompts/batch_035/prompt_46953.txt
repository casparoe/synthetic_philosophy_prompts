I'm a graduate student in philosophy of economics writing a paper on what official economic forecasting presupposes about probability, and I want help working out the reconstruction before I start drafting.

Here is the practice I'm studying. Central banks publish quarterly fan charts for inflation and output: a central projection, conditional on the announced path of the policy interest rate, surrounded by shaded bands drawn so that the darkest band is meant to cover the outcome with some stated probability, with progressively wider and lighter bands for larger probabilities. The bands widen steadily with the forecast horizon, the central line is presented as the single most likely path rather than the mean, and the forecasters openly use the asymmetry of the bands to communicate that the risks are skewed toward worse outcomes. Read literally, then, the chart encodes a full probability distribution for next year's inflation — not just an interval but a shape, with a mode and a skew. The same institutions also do two further things. First, in genuinely turbulent conditions they suspend probabilistic judgment: they decline to attach numbers to particular downside risks and instead publish scenarios — a small set of coherent stories about how the year could go, offered with no probabilities attached. Second, in bank regulation they run stress tests in which a severely adverse scenario is handed down to banks and capital adequacy is judged against it, with no probability assigned to the scenario, even though the same regulatory framework elsewhere requires banks to compute expected losses by attaching probabilities of default to each exposure.

Here is my puzzle. What theory of probability and uncertainty does this mixed practice only make sense under? Does publishing a fan chart strictly presuppose that uncertainty about next year's inflation is measurable — that a cardinal distribution exists to be estimated — and if so, is the presupposed view a degree-of-belief view, a frequentist view, or something in between? Is the crisis-time switch to unquantified scenarios coherent with the fan chart: does the practice presuppose a two-tier picture on which some uncertainties have determinate probabilities and others do not (which I take to be close to Keynes's own position in the Treatise on Probability, where some probabilities are non-numerical and some comparisons between them simply do not exist), or is it opportunism papering over an inconsistency? And a question about the method itself: a colleague of mine insists the fan chart is a communication device rather than an epistemic claim, so nothing is really presupposed — I would like the answer to address whether that move works, i.e., whether an institution can presuppose a view it never asserts.

Two constraints on the substance. Don't give me a history of probability theory or a survey of what various economists believed; the interest is the logical reconstruction. And don't treat presupposes as univocal: to the extent that some views are strictly required by the practice, others merely fit it well, and a third set are compatible with it, the answer should sort views into those tiers rather than declaring one implicit theory.

(~300 words); Rival theories that would license it (~250 words); What would change under a Bayesian and a radical-uncertainty view (~250 words, describing changes to the practice, not just to the theory); Verdict (~100 words).
