I'm writing a seminar paper on the nonidentity problem and want to test it against a novel rather than the usual thought experiments. The problem, roughly: Parfit observed that many of our present choices affect not just the welfare of future people but which future people exist at all. A person born because of some policy cannot coherently wish the policy away without wishing herself away, so she is not, in the ordinary comparative sense, worse off than she would otherwise have been — and yet the policy still seems wrong. The literature runs almost entirely on cases about future generations, depleted resources, and far-off centuries, which feel abstract and remote. I want a case where the person whose existence and whose grievance share a single source is present, narrating in the first person, and speaking for herself.

Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go appears to be built in exactly this shape. The students of Hailsham are clones created to donate their organs, and the crucial detail is that none of them would have existed at all had the donation program not existed: had the wrong not been done, there would have been no one for it to be done to. Tommy, Ruth, and Kathy owe their lives to the very arrangement that will kill them. Late in the book, Miss Emily effectively offers what I want to call the nonidentifying defense: these students, being the particular people they are, could not have had other lives, and Hailsham at least gave them a sheltered, comparatively humane version of a monstrous institution. The students themselves never rebel; Kathy narrates decades of donations and completions with something between resignation and tenderness, and the novel pointedly withholds any scene in which a character wishes the program had never existed.

Please read the novel through the nonidentity problem and reach some verdicts rather than staying neutral. Does the novel endorse Miss Emily's nonidentifying defense, or quietly undermine it — and what would undermining it even look like, given that no character's life goes worse than it would otherwise have been? Is the students' famous passivity best read as tacit acceptance of the nonidentity reasoning, or as something else — love, habit, self-deception, the narrowing of hope — and does that distinction matter for what the book shows about the argument? Finally, what does the novel give the debate that the standard cases cannot: does Ishiguro capture something the abstract literature misses about what it is like, from the inside, to be a person whose harm and whose existence have the same cause? Please don't quote the text; refer to scenes and characters descriptively. And be candid where the lens strains: if reading the book as a nonidentity parable flattens something Ishiguro is doing with memory, friendship, or narration, say so and explain where the reading breaks.
