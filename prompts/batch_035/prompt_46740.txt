I am a professor of philosophy, and I would like your help adjudicating an argument that broke out among my colleagues at a recent dinner — one I confess I could not settle to my own satisfaction. Please approach this as a professional philosopher with expertise in philosophy of mind, epistemology, and the paradoxes of belief and agency, and think carefully: before reaching any verdicts, take a moment to brainstorm the possible readings of each move in the exchange rather than announcing conclusions.

Some background. Our colleague M., a senior scholar in our department, has had his book manuscript rejected twice by the same press. He tells everyone the press will regret passing on it, that reviewers in his subfield chase fashion rather than quality, and that the manuscript needs no substantive revision. At the same time, he checks the press's portal obsessively, has quietly revised his introduction seven times, and has stopped reading the very journals he claims are run by fashion-chasers. Two of my colleagues argued over whether M. is self-deceived. The exchange went roughly as follows.

1. A. held that self-deception is impossible in principle. Deception requires a deceiver and a deceived: if M. knows the truth — that the book is being rejected on its merits — he cannot be the deceived party, and if he does not know it, then no one with access to the truth is doing the deceiving. Either way, the state we were all describing at dinner — M. both believing and not believing that the manuscript is flawed — cannot occur.

2. B. replied that this treats the self as a unified agent, which it is not. A single mind can register a threatening fact at a level that drives behavior — hence the obsessive checking and the endless revising — while keeping it out of the foreground of avowal, hence the cheerful public declarations. Information asymmetry within one person is real, B. said, and self-deception just is the management of that asymmetry: hold the belief where it steers action, out of sight where it would have to be admitted.

3. A. answered that on that description nothing remains of deception. What B. described is ordinary motivated reasoning wearing a grander name, with the paradoxical-sounding label doing all the persuasive work: substitute "wishful thinking" for "self-deception" in B.'s story and every fact goes through unchanged.

4. B. said the substitution is not innocent, because M. is not passively drifting into error — he actively arranges his epistemic environment. He curtails his reading, he steers conversations away from the manuscript when the topic turns threatening, and at dinner he preempts criticism by raising it himself and then dismissing it. That is strategy in the service of maintaining a false belief, and if that does not count as deception, B. said, the word has no application anywhere.

5. A.'s last word: strategy requires a strategist who grasps the point of the stratagem. M. cannot arrange things to avoid a conclusion he does not anticipate, and if he anticipates it, he already holds the belief he is supposedly keeping at bay. So B.'s "active" story does not dissolve the paradox; it merely relocates it from the belief to the plan.

I want three things from you, in order.

First, a move-by-move adjudication of the exchange as it actually occurred: for each numbered step, say whether the move is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify each rating. Engage the argument as made, not a nearby strawman, and be candid about which steps are doing real argumentative work and which merely sound decisive.

Second, an overall verdict: who comes out more right, and on which specific points is each of them right and wrong? Resist the temptation to split everything evenly; if one of them is simply closer to the truth, say so and say why.

Third — and this is what I most want from you — tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. Is there a principled position from which the whole exchange looks like two people talking past each other, each winning a different argument? If you think a deflationary treatment of self-deception renders the dispute tractable, explain how it handles the specific facts about M. (the revising, the checking, the preemptive self-criticism) rather than restating the position in the abstract. If you think the paradox survives deflation, say where it reasserts itself.
