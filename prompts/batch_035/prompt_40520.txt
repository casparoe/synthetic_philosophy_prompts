So I just got roped onto our institute's new "sensitive research" committee — the job is basically to write rules for when a single lab can go ahead with something risky without checking with the rest of us first. I've been reading about the unilateralist's curse and it feels made for this, so I want to stress-test it on the episode everyone brings up anyway: He Jiankui announcing the CRISPR babies in November 2018.

Here's the idea as I'm working with it (I'm no expert, correct me if I've mangled it): when several agents each get to decide on their own whether to do something somewhat risky, and each one's judgment is only slightly better than a coin flip, then the action happens whenever even a single one of them is wrongly confident. Groups of individually reasonable actors end up doing the risky thing way more often than any of them would endorse, and the proposed fix is a norm of conformity — when in doubt, don't be the one who acts. That's the curse, as I understand it.

Now map it onto the He Jiankui thing. The facts as I know them, keep me honest: in late 2018 he announces at the genome-editing summit in Hong Kong that twin girls had been born from embryos he'd edited, targeting the CCR5 gene, aiming at HIV resistance. The scientific community reacts with horror, the summit condemns it, and he's later convicted in China and sentenced to prison. And the clinical-use question wasn't live — the big academy reports had already said germline editing for reproduction was definately premature.

What I want is the careful version of the analysis, not the slogan. Who counts as the group whose unilateral member he was — the germline-editing-capable scientists, all biologists, Chinese regulators? Does the mapping even survive when the actor isn't an epistemic peer of the group but someone way below the median judgment (he apparently cut corners on the review and informed-consent side too)? Is this still the curse, or is it just misconduct wearing the curse's clothes? And here's the part I keep going back and forth on: the model treats the harm as flowing from the act itself, but a lot of what happened next — the moratorium talk, tighter oversight, a chilling effect on legitimate germline work — came from the community's reaction. If the reaction is endogenous, is the curse the wrong frame entirely? Should my committee be thinking of it as a governance failure (nobody had standing to tell him no) instead of an epistemic one?

I want it to run both directions: what the theory says about the episode, and what the episode shows about the theory — does it confirm it, is it an anomaly, or is it a case the framework never anticipated? And because I actually have to write policy: what would the curse framework tell us to recommend, some ex ante rule plus conformity pressure, versus what an ordinary research-misconduct framing would tell us, and which do you honestly think I should run with?
