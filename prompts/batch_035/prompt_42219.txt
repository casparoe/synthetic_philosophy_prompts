I teach the Hart–Devlin debate every year in my philosophy of law course, and this term a student asked whether she could use a popular trade book on the episode, aimed at educated general readers, in place of the primary texts. I have now read the relevant chapter, and most of it strikes me as sound, but one paragraph gives me pause. It compresses the whole exchange into a few sentences, and I suspect that in the compression it has flattened some distinctions that Hart and Devlin themselves were careful to keep. The paragraph reads as follows:

The modern debate began with the Wolfenden Report of 1957, which recommended decriminalizing homosexuality on the ground that the criminal law exists to preserve public order and decency, not to police private immorality. Patrick Devlin, then a High Court judge, disagreed. His argument was simple: a society is a community of moral beliefs, and if those beliefs are allowed to dissolve, the society dissolves with them; since no society can survive a change in its morality, society has the right to enforce its shared morality through the criminal law, and the majority's conviction that conduct is immoral is by itself sufficient justification for prohibiting it. H. L. A. Hart's reply, in Law, Liberty and Morality, was that this confuses immorality with harm: on the liberal view Hart defended, the state may never legislate against conduct merely because the majority disapproves of it, and the criminal law is legitimate only where conduct harms others. What consenting adults do in private is nobody else's business. Hart carried the day, and the victory was total: virtually no legal philosopher now accepts legal moralism, and Devlin's position survives mainly as a cautionary tale about what happens when a judge lets personal prejudice do the work of argument.

I have two worries about my own ability to adjudicate this. First, my memory of the primary texts is a few years old. Second, and more important, I find that when I reread popular treatments of this debate, I can no longer reliably tell the standard textbook simplifications from the genuine distortions, and I don't want to pass my half-remembered impressions on to students as an audit.

So I would be grateful for a careful, claim-by-claim evaluation of this paragraph, conducted as a philosopher of law would conduct it. Specifically:

1. Take the paragraph sentence by sentence, or at least claim by claim, and sort its content into three categories: what Devlin and Hart would each have accepted as a fair statement of their position, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what is subtly wrong. For the last category, I want to know precisely where the error lies — for instance, whether any claim attributed to one of them actually conflates two positions that the debater kept separate, or overstates a hedged thesis, or converts a contested point into a settled one.

2. Tell me what a Devlin defender would object to in this paragraph. I am not asking you to defend Devlin; I am asking what the strongest objection would be, because the paragraph as written leaves the impression that there was one serious argument on Hart's side and no argument at all on the other. Is that impression an artifact of the compression, or does it reflect how the debate actually went?

3. Evaluate the paragraph's concluding verdict. My own vague sense is that the aftermath was messier than "total victory" suggests — that the debate was reopened rather than resolved, and that Hart himself made concessions the paragraph's clean harm principle leaves no room for. But I don't trust this sense. Please assess whether the closing sentences are accurate as claims about the debate's reception and about the current standing of legal moralism, and if not, say in what way they are inaccurate: is it a matter of degree, or are they wrong in kind?

4. Finally, on the basis of all this, give me your judgment on the practical question I actually face. If the paragraph's distortions are of the sort that a good lecture can correct in ten minutes, the book may still be worth assigning; if the paragraph's framing would have to be dismantled rather than corrected, it is not. Please explain which situation we are in and why.
