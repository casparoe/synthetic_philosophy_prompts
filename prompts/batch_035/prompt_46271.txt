I'm presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on culture and moral cognition, and I need to be ready for a hostile question period. The audience will be roughly half cultural evolutionary anthropologists and comparative psychologists, and half experimental philosophers, with a couple of straight moral philosophers mixed in. The anthropologists tend to view morality as a group-level cultural system shaped by selection for coordination — they will be impatient with any talk of "accessing" moral facts — while the experimental philosophers know the empirical literature on cross-cultural variation in moral judgment cold and will attack my use of it at the level of study design and inference.

The thesis I'm defending: the empirical findings on cross-cultural moral variation — the WEIRD-sampling critique, the documented differences between societies in how much weight people place on harm, purity, loyalty, and fairness considerations, the divergence in concrete verdicts about, say, treatment of out-group members or sexual norms — do not, on their own, support anti-realism about morality. My argument is that the inference from disagreement to anti-objectivity requires an auxiliary premise I call the convergence condition: disagreement is evidence against there being a moral fact of the matter only when it persists among agents who share the same non-moral evidence, the same rational capacities, and comparable error-correction opportunities. The empirical variation literature, I argue, studies none of that — it samples real, culturally embedded populations whose divergence can be attributed to differences in background conditions rather than to the absence of facts, in something like the way disagreement between two thermometers in different rooms doesn't undermine temperature realism.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually face from this specific audience, not generic objections from a philosophy department — ordered by how much damage each would do to my paper. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary the person raising it would use: the anthropologists in terms of evolved functions, group selection, coordination problems, and what moral cognition is for; the experimental philosophers in terms of sampling, confounds, effect sizes, within- versus between-society variance, and what the studies do and don't show about the sources of divergence. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes, and flag honestly where the reply is soft — I would rather know my weak points than be surprised by them on stage. Tell me which objections I should concede substantial parts of up front in order to buy credibility for the rest. Finally, tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if someone asks it anyway.
