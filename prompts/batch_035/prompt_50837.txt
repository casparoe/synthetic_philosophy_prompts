My reading group just finished Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929), and I've been keeping a list of the verdicts he actually hands down, since the general principle behind them is way murkier than people expect. As best we can distill it: sex before marriage isn't wrong in itself when its part of a serious relationship, though drifting promiscuity drains life of the depth that love needs; a childless marriage should be dissolvable by either partner, without stigma or years of manufactured guilt; a spouse's affair doesn't automatically wreck the marriage so long as the love and the shared life survive it; jealousy is mostly a leftover territorial instinct that a civilized person should work to overcome rather than treat as proof of love; but once children exist, stability and routine become genuine duties, and parents owe the kids constancy even at real cost to themselves; and separately, he'd rather the young marry early and have families than postpone everything for career reasons.

Next week's session is on this case: a couple in their late twenties, married five years with two small kids, who decide together to open the marriage — both honestly consent, both say (and seem) to love each other more rather than less, and the kids' home life stays boringly stable throughout. If we're trying to predict what the Russell of our list would say, what's the verdict? My hunch is that a consent-plus-honesty rule and a children-need-constancy rule pull in opposite directions here, but there may be other rules that fit his track record just as well and land elsewhere. So: lay out the candidate rules that fit the verdicts, say what each one predicts for this case, give me your best guess at his call along with your reasons, and tell me which further case would best split the candidate rules apart.
