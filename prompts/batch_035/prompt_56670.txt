here's where i've landed in metaethics after a couple years of thinking it through, and i want a consistency check before i present it at our reading group. my five commitments:

1. the is-ought gap, in the strong logical form: no argument with a normative conclusion is valid unless at least one premise is itself normative. there's no way to get an 'ought' out of purely descriptive premises — you always need a bridge premise, and the bridge is normative. i teach this to intro students and it's never felt shaky to me.

2. moral naturalism, and not the watered-down supervenience version either. i mean straight identity: moral properties just are certain natural properties, the way water just is H2O. i'm honestly not sure yet which natural property 'right' turns out to be (something well-being-adjacent, i'd guess), but i'm confident the identity is there to be found.

3. Moore's open question argument is a bad argument. all it shows is that 'good' isn't synonymous with whichever natural term you plug in, and identity never needed synonymy in the first place — 'water' and 'H2O' aren't synonymous either. people teach the OQA like it's a knockdown and it just isn't.

4. a judgment i'm pretty firm on: someone who says "i can see this action maximizes well-being, but i'm suspending judgment on whether it's right" is making the same kind of mistake as someone who says "i know this is water, but i'm suspending judgment on whether it's H2O." doesn't matter how careful and epistemically humble the suspension looks — if they genuinely know the first thing, they're in a position to know the second. the mistake is structural, which is it's own kind of interesting.

5. persistent, fully-informed moral disagreement is real. two people can agree on every natural fact — including every fact about welfare, no empirical stone unturned — and still disagree about what ought to be done. and that disagreement is substantive, not verbal, not just them failing to have located the relevant natural property. it's genuine first-order moral disagreement.

what i want from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which ones only look like they are, and rank the real tensions by severity instead of flagging everything as equally bad. for each real one, say what would have to give and what it costs. then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. i genuinely don't know where the problem is, if there is one, so don't go easy on it — and if some tension dissolves on inspection, i want to see exactly how it dissolves, since that's the part i'd be most tempted to fudge in the Q&A. also worth noting the room has one person who thinks the whole naturalism/descriptivism package collapsed in the 40s and never recovered, so if any of my commitments look like they're re-running a refuted program, i'd want to know that going in rather than getting it sprung on me.
