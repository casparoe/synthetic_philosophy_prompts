A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker, and we walked away with a disagreement that is precise enough to be settled numerically rather than rhetorically. I would like you to settle it that way.

The case as we set it up: I am stranded; a driver offers to take me to town, but she can tell whether I would keep my promise to pay her on arrival, and knowing that I would renege once in town she would leave me stranded. So I get the ride only because I am the sort of person who would pay; yet once I am in town, standing in front of the cash machine, paying serves no further purpose of mine, and refusing appears to be the locally rational, self-interested move. Parfit's label for this phenomenon was rational irrationality: the disposition to renege is irrational for the person as a whole, even though each act, judged at its moment, looks rational.

My friend holds that once in town, the rational choice is simply not to pay: nothing I do now can affect whether I was picked up, and the earlier fact that I would not otherwise have gotten a ride is, causally speaking, water under the bridge. The lesson of the case, she says, is that one should cultivate the disposition to keep such promises, but that once the ride is over, the disposition and the present act come apart, and rationality governs only the act. I hold instead that rationality attaches to the decision procedure or policy, not to the time-slice: a theory of rational choice that tells the hitchhiker to refuse at the machine is telling him, in effect, to hope he was a different person at the roadside, which is not advice anyone can act on.

Here is what I want from you. Give me your credence that the hitchhiker, already in town with no prospect of being stranded again, is rationally required to pay — not a hedge about it depending on one's decision theory, but a number, together with its decomposition. At minimum, assign rough probabilities to the following sub-questions, each with a one-line justification: first, that causal decision theory rather than some evidential or functional rival gives the correct account of rational choice in one-shot cases like this one; second, that the rational status of an act should be assessed time-slice by time-slice rather than at the level of the policy or disposition from which it flows; third, that rationality is determinate enough in this scenario for there to be a genuine fact of the matter, rather than the dispute being partly verbal, a clash between two legitimate senses of the word; and add any further terms you think the final number depends on. Then combine them, tell me which sub-question the combined figure is most sensitive to, and say what would move it most — for instance, a variant in which the driver is only an eighty-percent-accurate reader of my intentions, or one in which I could have bound myself with an enforceable contract at the roadside but neglected to. Finally, tell me whether you think my friend and I are really disagreeing about decision theory, or about the level at which rationality should be assessed, and what evidence or argument would most plausibly reconcile us.
