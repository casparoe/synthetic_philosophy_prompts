I'm a civil servant who has spent most of my career in transport regulation, and I have just been transferred to my country's online safety directorate, where my first assignment is to draft advice on two related matters: a new statutory requirement that websites hosting adult content verify the age of their visitors, and the regulatory treatment of "AI companion" applications that offer users romantic and sexual conversation with synthetic personas. My own views on these matters are unsettled, and in trying to find my footing I have begun reading Bentham, whose writings on sexual morality — including the manuscripts he deliberately kept out of print during his lifetime — seem to speak to my brief with unusual directness. I want your help thinking through what he would actually say.

Take on the role of a historian of political thought with deep expertise in Bentham's corpus, and before you answer, reason carefully through what he in fact argued: his case that the condemnation of non-procreative sexual conduct rests on what he called the principle of asceticism rather than on any calculation of harm, his insistence that the mischief of an act lies in the pain it produces and not in its character, his treatment of the gap between what is disliked and what is harmful, and his cost-consciousness about enforcement. Then, on that foundation, project him onto questions he could never have encountered. Would he support mandatory age verification for adult sites, and would his answer change if verification could be done in a way that preserves anonymity? Would he want platforms hosting consensual adult material held liable for what users do with them? What would he make of sexual companionship with a synthetic persona — a relationship that produces real pleasure while involving no second person whose interests can be affected at all? Where, in each case, would he demand empirical evidence before committing himself, and what would he count as the relevant evidence?

Please distinguish, throughout, between three grades of claim: what follows fairly directly from principles he explicitly stated; what requires guesses about empirical matters he would have wanted settled by data; and where his own commitments pull against each other. On that last point, remember that the man who argued against punishing private vice is the same man who designed a prison built on perpetual visibility, and I want you to take that tension seriously rather than resolving it away. Close with a candid paragraph on where you think the extrapolation is least trustworthy and why. Write continuous prose only, in the manner of a long letter from a scholar to a policy official: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Attribute Bentham's views in your own words; do not put anything in quotation marks, and do not give me bibliographies or reading lists — I am interested in the reasoning, not the apparatus of scholarship.
