I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the last year reporting a long-form series on how the word "genocide" travels from Lemkin's original conception -- which explicitly included not just killing but the deliberate dismantling of a group's language, institutions, and way of life -- through the 1948 Convention's narrower legal definition, and out into ordinary argument. Somewhere along the way I accumulated gut verdicts on cases, and now I want to know whether they add up to anything. The Herero and Nama extermination campaign: genocide, clearly. The Holodomor, where the famine that killed millions of Ukrainians was engineered or at least weaponized by Soviet policy: my gut says yes, genocide, even though I know the intent question is contested and the group was targeted in part as a class of "kulaks." Boarding schools that took Indigenous children from their families to erase their languages, with deaths but not mass killing as the mechanism: my gut says this is genuinely genocidal, and I notice I'm relying on Lemkin's broader notion of cultural destruction rather than the Convention's. The Atlantic slave trade and the Middle Passage: the scale of death was foreseen and tolerated as a cost of profit, but the point was exploitation, not destruction of the group as such -- and my gut says not genocide, which disturbs me, because the death toll dwarfs cases I do call genocide. The expulsions and massacres of partition-era Punjab in 1947: not genocide in my gut, though the aim was, in a real sense, to remove a group from a territory, and I can't articulate what separates "removal" from "destruction." Pol Pot's killing of anyone with glasses or an education: morally the equal of any genocide I'd name, but the groups were defined by class and politics, which the Convention excludes, and my gut follows the exclusion while resenting it. And a case I keep chewing on: a rising sea slowly erasing an island people whose state is not the perpetrator of anything -- my gut says that's a catastrophe but not a genocide, yet I can't say why intent matters so much when the outcome is a group's disappearance.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible: state it precisely enough that it could be applied to a new case by someone who shares your framework, tell me which clauses are doing the work for which verdict, and then be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save -- I would genuinely rather revise a gut verdict than smuggle in ad hoc clauses, but you should take a definite position on which way to go rather than leaving the choice to me. I'd also like your prediction for a case I haven't listed: a counterinsurgency campaign that doesn't aim at a group's destruction but foreseeably destroys its economic base and thereby its ability to persist as a distinct people. Don't lay out the options and wave at the tradeoffs; commit. Please close with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a short phrase, the core of your principle together with the verdict you think I must give up (for example, something like "destruction-in-any-sphere, drop the slave trade carve-out" -- but use your own wording).
