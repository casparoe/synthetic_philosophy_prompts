Background so you know where I'm coming from: I retired a couple years back after about thirty-five years in pharmacology and physiology, mostly running enzyme assays and the occasional clinical study, and since then I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of science. Right now I'm working through the causation literature and the counterfactual vs. interventionist split keeps nagging at me. As an experimentalist, my whole career was basically "a cause is something you can wiggle while holding everything else fixed," so Woodward feels like home. But I also did a ton of but-for reasoning — investigating why a batch failed, writing adverse event reports — where nobody intervened on anything and the reasoning still seemed perfectly fine, so I dont want to just dismiss the other side.

Here's what I want you to build me. A scenario — realistic, the kind of thing that could plausibly happen in a lab, a hospital, or an industrial process, no quantum mechanics, no demons, no time travel — where the plain but-for counterfactual test ("if C hadn't occured, E wouldn't have") says C is a cause of E, but the interventionist test (would surgically setting C wiggle E, holding fixed everything off the path) says no. I do NOT want the barometer-and-the-storm case; that one shows up in every other chapter. Two routes I can imagine, pick either or find a third: (a) a case where the but-for dependence runs through a common cause, so it's really backtracking, but the backtracking is subtle enough that a careful investigator could miss it at first; or (b) a case where C partly constitutes E, or the two are otherwise so entangled that there's no well-defined surgical intervention — coupled variables, "fat-handed" as I gather the jargon goes. The entanglement should be real to the science, not stipulated ("the variables are non-separable, trust me" is cheating).

Finish with how a defender of whichever theory comes out looking bad would attack the case — what they'd claim we got wrong about the scenario or the variables. Keep the scenario itself down to one paragraph; the analysis can run as long as it wants. And be honest if some constraint turns out to be unsatisfiable — I'd rather hear that with an explanation than get a fudged case that only looks like it meets the specs. I'm trying to figure out whether the interventionist picture I find so natural actually loses something, so a case that quietly riggs the verdicts is worse than useless to me.
