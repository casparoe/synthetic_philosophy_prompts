Quick bit of background: I did thirty-five years at the British Geological Survey, mostly seismic hazard modelling, and since retiring I've fallen in with a philosophy group that meets above a pub. I'm the numbers person there, which is exactly how I got walked into this.

One of the members, a lovely chap but very pleased with himself, has discovered the Condorcet jury theorem and now thinks it settles everything. He laid it out like this:

(1) If n people each judge the same yes/no question independently, and each is right with probability a bit above one half, then as n grows the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty — with 50,000 people each at 51%, the majority is essentially never wrong.
(2) For any question you like, the average person is surely at least slightly better than a fair coin at getting it right — people may be ignorant, but they're not systematically worse than random.
(3) Their judgements are independent in the sense the theorem needs: nobody's copying anybody, each person makes up their own mind in their own kitchen.
(4) So on any question whatever, whatever a large poll says is right with probability indistinguishable from 1.
(5) So even when I'm ninety percent sure about something in my own field — say, whether a particular fault is capable of a magnitude 7 event — I should abandon my ninety percent and adopt whatever a YouGov sample of the general public believes, because 99.99-and-then-some beats ninety.
(6) And the same goes across borders: a large majority in one country believes Q, a large majority in the country next door believes not-Q, and the theorem apparently certifies both majorities at once.

I know the conclusion is daft — if the public polled better than my colleagues on fault mechanics I'd have spent my career doing something very odd — but when he walked through the steps I honestly couldn't put my finger on where it broke, and I've a nagging feeling that if I reject the wrong step I'll end up throwing out the theorem's legitimate uses too, like averaging independent expert reviews. I suspect the problem is somewhere in what "independent" or "better than a coin" quietly means once you move from his toy juries to actual humans with newspapers and shared biases, but I want it spelled out properly.

Please reply as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each — no intro, no wrap-up, just the list. Use them to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs the theorem elsewhere.
