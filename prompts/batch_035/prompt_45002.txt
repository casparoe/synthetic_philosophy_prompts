I'm a high school senior writing an extended essay on free will, and I have become stuck on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, which concludes that no one is ever morally responsible for their actions in the ultimate, desert-entailing sense — the sense in which one deserves blame or reward because of what one has done, apart from any good consequences of punishing or praising. As I understand it, the argument runs: (1) to be responsible for an action in this ultimate sense, you must be responsible for how you are, in the morally relevant respects, at the moment you act; (2) you cannot be responsible for how you are, because either you are the way you are for reasons you did not control, or you tried to make yourself a certain way on the basis of how you already were, which only pushes the question back a step; and the regress never terminates in a self that made itself. Hence no one is ever responsible in the ultimate sense.

My advisor thinks the whole thing is a trick of definition, while I am half-persuaded that the conclusion must be true even if the argument itself is flawed. Please give me your actual credence that the conclusion is true — that no person has ever been morally responsible in the ultimate, desert-entailing sense — with the reasoning decomposed. I want separate numbers for: the probability that premise (1) is correct, that ultimate responsibility for actions really does require responsibility for how one is; the probability that premise (2) is correct, allowing for every way out people have proposed, including an infinite regress of self-shapings, an uncaused self, and a self whose acts of self-making were themselves unchosen; and any further terms you think belong, such as the probability that the conclusion would still hold even if this argument fails, or that the ultimate sense of responsibility is incoherent, making the conclusion neither true nor false. Give one line of justification for each number, then show how they combine into your final credence, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to. Finally, say where the disagreement between my advisor and me most likely lives: does he reject premise (1), reject premise (2), accept the argument and deny its importance, or deny that the conclusion means what it seems to mean? I am not looking for "it depends on your concept of free will"; I want the numbers and the weak points marked.
