Something I keep chewing on: I'm German, and here the state literally collects the church tax on behalf of the Catholic and Protestant churches — the tax office keeps a small admin fee and forwards the rest, and if I want out I have to formally declare I'm leaving at a government office, I can't just stop paying. Meanwhile my kid's public school teaches religion as a normal graded subject (you can opt her out, and in some Länder you get ethics class instead). Then you look at France: the state funds nothing religious, but public schools ban conspicuous religeous symbols — which sounds like neutrality but is the state actively regulating religious expression. England somehow still has bishops sitting in the House of Lords by right, and the monarch is the Supreme Governor of the Church of England — yet nobody calls England a theocracy. And the US is fully disestablished but has In God We Trust printed on the money and tax-exempts churches and even parsonages.

So here's what I want to know: what does each of these setups actually presuppose about state neutrality or state–church relations? Does the German system presuppose that neutrality means treating the big churches as public-law corporations the state cooperates with, while the French one presupposes that neutrality means the state being blind to religion entirely — and the English one that neutrality is just unnecessary because one church is quietly part of the constitution? And are the American bits (motto, tax exemption, congressional chaplains) consistent with disestablishment, or do they presuppose some generic favoring of religion-as-such? For each system I'd like to know which parts strictly require the implicit theory and which parts any of several theories could license — my suspicion is a lot of this is historically frozen compromise that no single theory would justify from scratch, and I'd like to hear whether that's fair or whether there's a cleaner underlying idea in each case than I'm giving them credit for.

Please answer in prose, but afterwards give me a structured summary as a JSON object. One key per country — "germany", "france", "england", "united_states" — each with these fields: "implicit_theory" (one sentence on the view of state neutrality the practice presupposes), "strictly_needed" (the parts that only make sense given that theory), "ruled_out" (rival views of neutrality the practice is inconsistent with), "underdetermined" (parts that several different theories could equally license), and "tensions" (internal inconsistencies, if any). Plus one top-level field, "shared_core", listing anything all four systems presuppose in common — or an empty list if there really is nothing, which honestly wouldn't surprise me.
