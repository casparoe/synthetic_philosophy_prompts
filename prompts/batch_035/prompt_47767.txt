My grandmother died in March, and in the weeks since I have been reading whatever I can find about the philosophy of time, because a popular book by a physicist — I will leave the author unnamed, since it is the view rather than the book I want to check — offered a version of the "block universe" that I have found myself returning to almost daily. The book's summary of the position runs, in substance, as follows:

Modern physics has shown that time does not really pass. The universe is a four-dimensional block in which past, present, and future events all exist equally; nothing in physics distinguishes the present moment as special. Nothing is genuinely gone. My grandmother's life is still there in the block, just as real as my reading of this page, simply located at an earlier slice, in the way that Sydney is as real as London even though I am not there. Einstein himself reached this conclusion, remarking in a letter written near the end of his life that the distinction between past, present, and future is a stubborn illusion. And relativity did not just suggest this; it refuted presentism decades ago, since presentism insists on a single objective now and relativity denies that any such now exists. Philosophers call the block view eternalism, and it is now essentially the consensus among those who take physics seriously. So when someone dies they are not gone anywhere — they still exist, elsewhere in spacetime.

I want to be clear that I am not asking to be comforted, and I am not asking you to tell me whether my grandmother is, in some deep sense, all right. I am asking whether the summary is accurate. Part of me is inclined to believe the comforting part, which is exactly why I distrust my own reading of it. Specifically, I would like to know:

1. What the summary gets right — which parts would a philosopher of time accept as a fair statement of eternalism and of the physics it is usually said to fit?
2. Where it misleads — the summary moves quickly between claims that careful writers keep apart, and I suspect some of its confident steps are weaker than they sound, or trade on an ambiguity (for instance, about what it means to say the past exists). Please identify any conflations, overstatements, or points where a technical term is being used in its everyday sense.
3. How a presentist would reply — the summary treats presentism as already refuted by relativity. Is that fair, or are there recognized ways for a presentist to accommodate relativity that the summary glosses over?
4. What follows for my situation — whether the comforting conclusion, that my grandmother still exists in the way the book suggests, actually follows from whatever parts of the summary are accurate.

Please structure your reply in exactly those four sections, in that order, with the second section the longest (perhaps three to four short numbered points) and the first and fourth roughly a short paragraph each. I would rather have a sober correction of the book than a kind word, so do not soften the fourth section on my account; if the honest answer is that the accurate parts of the summary buy me nothing, I want to know that.
