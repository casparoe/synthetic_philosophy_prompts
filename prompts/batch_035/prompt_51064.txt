I help run a small interdisciplinary workshop that is, at this point, mostly two professors who have been politely feuding for fifteen years: one a philosophy-of-science type who thinks Hempel was basically right and that any adequate historical explanation is really an incomplete sketch of a covering-law argument (deductive, or at least inductive-statistical), and one a historian who insists that narrative explanation is a genuinely different kind of thing -- you explain a revolution by tracing it, not by subsuming it under even a probabilistic law, and she says that what the covering-law theorist politely calls an "explanation sketch" is really just an excuse for not looking at what actually happened. Their stock example is always the outbreak of the First World War, where the historian's whole point is that each step felt contingent at the time and the tracing of that contingency IS the explanation, while the philosopher says the contingency-talk just reveals that the relevant generalizations are probabilistic and unstated. We finally talked them into trying an actual adversarial collab -- a year of structured meetings, and at the end they want a joint statement both can sign without gagging.

Please draft that statement for them to work from. I want: (1) the claims both could sign -- I suspect they can agree on more than either will admit in public, e.g. that working historians almost never state laws, that the "because" claims historians actually make are mostly singular-causal rather than law-citing, and that a good narrative can be retrospective without being irrational; (2) the residual disagreements, each phrased so the relevant side would accept the wording of its own position -- the Hempelian gets to insist that explanation is still *conceptually* law-involving even when the law is trivial or left tacit, the narrativist gets to insist that narrative structure does explanatory work that no mere concatenation of singular causal claims adds up to; (3) the cruxes, with each side's prediction written down in advance -- they were explicit about wanting predictions recorded now so nobody can quietly retrofit later. My guesses for cruxes: one is whether a fully spelled-out causal chain plus trivial generalizations ("people tend to resent humiliation," " empires overextend") already constitutes the whole explanation or leaves something out; another is what to do with narrative conventions like where you begin the story, what counts as the outcome needing explanation, and the historian's hindsight -- but they may have better ones, so propose what you actually think divides them.

Two practical constraints: the statement goes to the whole workshop including grad students who havn't read any of this literature, so it has to be self-contained -- don't assume anyone knows what an "explanation sketch" is without a sentence of setup. And both of them have signing red lines: the narrativist will not put her name to anything that calls narrative "merely description," and the covering-law guy will not sign anything implying his view requires historians to produce laws the way physicists do, so the agreed claims section has to steer between those. Numbered sections for the agreed claims and then some kind of disagreements/cruxes table would match what they asked for.
