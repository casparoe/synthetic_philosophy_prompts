I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the point-of-view characters is a manufactured intelligence — not a robot story exactly, more like an engineered creature whose mind is legible enough that you can see its moving parts. At my last writing group, two members got into a proper argument about whether my character can plausibly be written as *choosing* anything at all, and they dragged in a wasp, of all things, and I'd love a referee who actually knows the philosophy.

The wasp, apparently, is a classic: the female digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it at the threshold, and pops inside to check the burrow first. If you nudge the cricket a couple of inches away while she's inside, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the threshold — and goes in to check the burrow again. Move the cricket forty times and she performs the whole routine forty times, like she can't notice the trick. Dennett called this "sphexishness." So Marta's opening move was: my character is sphexish by design — its "decisions" are just long, branching routines, and if the novelist (me) knows the branch table, nothing it does counts as a real choice, and I'm basically committing fraud if I write it deciding. Dev pushed back that by that standard *we're* mostly sphexish too — humans run on habit, he runs the exact same breakfast routine every morning, and genre writers (him, pointedly) are the most sphexish organisms alive, running the same plot routine every book, and yet we don't say Dev has no free will. Flexibility is a matter of degree. Marta conceded the degree point but said it wrecks his position too: if free will is just "more branches in the lookup table," then the wasp's re-check loop is already a fallback branch, so where's the line? Dev said the line is that the wasp can't *notice when her routine stops working* — she never clocks the experimenter — and the flexibility that matters for agency isn't having fallbacks, it's noticing failure and improvising past it. Marta's last word was that this just pushes the bump under the rug: a thermostat "notices" the temperature, an error handler "notices" an exception, so "noticing" is doing all the work while being left undefined, and meanwhile he still owes her an answer about whether a character in a *novel* can have free will when every one of its choices is something I typed on purpose.

So, if you'd take this on: think of yourself as a philosopher of mind who's used to adjudicating arguments between smart people who've read a couple of Dennett essays each, and please don't rush to an overall winner. Go through their exchange move by move — Marta's fraud charge, Dev's "we're all sphexish" defense, the degrees objection, the noticing criterion, the thermostat comeback — and say who's right about what at each step, where someone is using a word sloppily, and what (if anything) both of them are missing about sphexishness, flexibility, and free will. If it helps, brainstorm a bit first before you assign verdicts, and feel free to tell me whether the novelist-specific twist at the end (fictional characters and free will) actually changes the argument or is just a distraction. There's a chapter of my book riding on this, so I'd rather have honest adjudication than diplomacy.
