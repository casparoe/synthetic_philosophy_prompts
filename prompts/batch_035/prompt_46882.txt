I've been following the reasons-internalism arguments for years now — first on the philosophy-of-rationality corners of the blogs I read, then in the primary literature — and I've noticed the same thing happens every time: a committed Williamsian internalist and an equally committed externalist (of roughly the Parfit or Scanlon stripe) talk past each other for three hundred comments and neither party's credences visibly move. The usual suspects get trotted out — whether an agent indifferent to his own future suffering "has a reason" to care, whether sound deliberation has to be an actually available route or merely a logically possible one, whether the externalist's bare insistence that a reason exists does any explanatory work or just re-voices the conclusion under dispute — and then the thread dies. My reading group has decided to force the issue properly, in the style of the adversarial collaborations that get floated on the forums: lock the two positions in a room until they produce a document both would sign.

I'd like you to draft that joint statement. The internalist holds that all practical reasons an agent genuinely has must be reachable by some route of sound deliberation from that agent's actual motivational set, such that saying "he has a reason he could not in any way arrive at, care about, or be moved by" is either false or empty. The externalist holds that there are genuine reasons that outrun any actual agent's motivations — that the person who doesn't care one whit about his future agony, and couldn't be brought to care by any coherent deliberative route, still has decisive reason to avoid it. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, which must not be vacuous — include the genuinely shared ground, such as whatever both accept about how deliberation can transform motivations, about the difference between having a reason and being motivated, and about which crude versions of relativism both reject; (2) the residual disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would endorse as a fair statement of its position — I'm especially interested in whether the debate over whether aversion therapy, brainwashing, or far-fetched deliberative routes can create reasons in Williams's sense marks a real disagreement or a boundary dispute about what counts as "sound deliberation"; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — where I'd like you to consider at least these candidates and add others if you see them: (a) whether the externalist's claim is explanatory or merely normative, and whether that distinction does the work the internalist wants; (b) whether the near-universal externalism about epistemic reasons — that what you have reason to believe depends on your evidence, not your desires — puts pressure on practical internalism or whether the disanalogy between belief and action holds; and (c) whether internalism, consistently held, collapses into relativism or scepticism about normativity, or whether that is a price the internalist should cheerfully pay. For each crux, give one concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both sides would agree bears on it, plus each side's written-down prediction about what the case shows — the point, as with any prediction register, is to make falsification possible in advance rather than post hoc.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with a final short section flagging any parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal and explaining the verdict. Bear in mind the signability constraint throughout: nothing in the document may be phrased so cautiously that it says nothing, and nothing so tendentiously that one side would refuse to put its name to it. If you think one side's position is secretly unstable — for instance, that the internalist quietly needs a notion of rationality that smuggles in external reasons, or that the externalist cannot say what it is in virtue of which the reason-giving fact obtains — you may say so, but only in a section both parties would accept as an open question rather than a verdict, since the exercise fails the moment either signatory feels they've been ambushed.
