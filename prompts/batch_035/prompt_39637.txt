Construct a novel example of rational irrationality: a case in the same family as Parfit's hitchhiker and Kavka's toxin puzzle, in which an agent does better overall because she is disposed to perform an act that, at the moment of performing it, would count as irrational. Specify the case concretely enough that the payoff structure is checkable — actual figures for the costs and payoffs, and the order in which moves are made. Your example must differ from the familiar ones in one deliberate respect: the disposition should pay off only because it is genuinely possessed, not merely because it is believed to be possessed, as would be so if the counterparty can cheaply probe or test the agent rather than take her word for it. After presenting the example, explain what it is meant to show: what work the genuine possession of the disposition is doing, whether the "irrational" act really comes out irrational once the whole case is in view, and whether the example supports treating such dispositions as rational in themselves rather than as costly side effects of rational design. Throughout, please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the decision-theoretic literature — in particular, note that the verdict on whether the disposed act is irrational depends on which decision theory one accepts, and do not present any of those verdicts as the consensus view. If any part of your example only works on a contested reading of what rationality requires, flag that rather than building on it silently. Write in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
