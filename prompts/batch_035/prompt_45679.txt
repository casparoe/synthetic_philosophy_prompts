The assignment question was: "Grice distinguishes what a sentence entails from what a speaker conversationally implicates. (a) Explain the difference, using 'some' as your example. (b) Explain how, on Grice's account, the inference from 'some' to 'not all' is supposed to arise — which maxim is involved, and what the hearer is doing. (c) Explain the difference between conventional and conversational implicatures, with an example of each. (d) Presuppositions are also not entailments; describe one diagnostic for telling a presupposition apart from a conversational implicature, and explain why it works."

Here is the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted: "Entailment is when the truth of the sentence guarantees the truth of another proposition. If 'Some students passed' is true, 'At least one student passed' has to be true — entailment. The suggestion that not all passed is different: it's not an entailment, since 'Some students passed, and in fact every one of them did' isn't a contradiction. (b) On Grice's account, the hearer assumes the speaker is cooperative and follows the maxims. Since the speaker said 'some' when the stronger 'all' was just as easy to say, the hearer concludes she doesn't believe all passed, or she would have said so — that's the Quantity maxim at work. This inference is so automatic that it's really part of the fixed meaning of 'some': it can't genuinely be cancelled. 'Some students passed — in fact, every one of them did' reads as basically contradicting itself. So strictly speaking the not-all suggestion behaves like a presupposition of 'some' rather than an implicature, which is presumably why some later authors just treat 'some' as ambiguous. (c) Conventional implicatures are built into words but don't affect truth conditions. 'But' conventionally implicates contrast: 'She was poor but honest' is true under exactly the same conditions as 'She was poor and honest' — the only difference is that 'but' signals the two things are somehow in tension. Since conventional implicatures aren't truth-conditional, they pattern with conversational ones elsewhere too — e.g. they can be cancelled by continuing the sentence: 'She was poor but honest, though I'm not suggesting poor people are usually dishonest.' (d) A good diagnostic is negation: presuppositions drop out when the sentence is negated, while implicatures persist, because implicatures attach to what was meant rather than to the sentence itself. For example, 'She didn't manage to pass' — the suggestion that she tried is gone, which shows 'manage' contributes an implicature rather than a presupposition."
