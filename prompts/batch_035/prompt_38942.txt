Do you know the concept of safe Pareto improvements (SPIs)? Quick sketch in case it's fuzzy: when two parties are set on playing some costly noncooperative game -- war, lawsuit, price war -- an SPI is a way of modifying the game (arbitrator, randomization, side payments, whatever) so that each party expects more payoff than it would get in the equilibrium of the original game, without anyone having to settle the underlying disagreement. Toy example: instead of two nations fighting over a strip of land, they flip a coin and each keeps half the money they'd have burned on the war. Each side is confident it would have won, so each accepts the lottery; nobody gives up its claim, yet the costs of fighting just vanish. Ex ante everyone is better off by their own lights.

Suppose I'm advising a small funder who can support roughly two grad students plus a postdoc for two years on the conceptual side of SPIs -- decision theory, bargaining theory, normative questions, no lab and no empirics. Which open questions should that little team actually work on, in what order, and why? Some candidates I've jotted down, feel free to add to or kill any of these:

- How SPIs behave when the parties disagree about the underlying game itself. Divergent beliefs about who'd win the war is exactly what makes the coin flip attractive to both sides; but what if they disagree about the stakes, or about how much the other side values the outcome?
- Who even counts as a party? Nations aren't people -- whose preferences go into a state's SPI? That looks like it smuggles in a whole interpersonal aggregation problem that the literature mostly waves away.
- The dynamic / moral hazard worry: if SPIs become standard practice, do parties invest more in conflictual postures up front, since the downside gets cushioned? Does brinkmanship effectively get subsidized?
- How to actually generate SPIs. Is there a systematic recipe, or is it just clever case-by-case engineering (auctions, lotteries, escrowed side payments)?
- The AI angle: if autonomous systems end up negotiating with each other, does pre-committing to SPI routines make multiagent interactions meaningfully safer, and what would the arbitrator's incentives even be?

I care less about the list than about the reasoning. For each question you'd prioritize: why it's upstream of the others, whether it's actually tractable with philosophy and decision-theory tools versus needing empirical work we can't do, and what developments over the next couple of years would bump it up or down. Also tell me which of these you'd drop entirely -- either as a dead end dressed up as an open question, or as already basically solved by older bargaining theory (Coase, Nash bargaining, mechanism design, that stuff).
