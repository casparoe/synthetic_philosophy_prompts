I am a recently retired geophysicist — thirty-two years measuring strain in rock, and a career spent believing that whatever is real is, at bottom, something you can put an instrument on. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy with the enthusiasm of a convert and the habits of an old positivist, and this year I agreed to lead a monthly discussion group at our local lifelong-learning institute. The group has about fourteen members, mostly fellow retirees, and sessions run ninety minutes. Next month's topic, which I chose rather against my own instincts, is whether virtual things can be real things: whether a virtual object exists in any serious sense, and whether harms that befall a person inside a virtual setting — the destruction of something they built, the loss of something they owned there — are real harms, or merely minor disappointments attached to patterns of light. My training pulls me firmly toward the deflationary view — it is all bits and voltages, and no seismograph will register the destruction of a virtual castle — but I have noticed that people who lose years of work in a virtual world grieve in a way that does not feel counterfeit to them, and I would rather my group encounter that tension than hear me lecture about it.

Here is what I would like from you. Design a structured activity for that session — rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff or scoring scheme that does not require real money — in which the participants themselves build or accumulate something inside a small, self-contained game (paper, tokens, cards, a shared board; I can prepare whatever materials are needed), and in which the game's rules then inflict on some participants, by lot, the destruction or erasure of what they have built, while offering them a token real-world compensation to underline the thought that "it was only a game." The idea is that they should feel the intuition first and only have it named afterwards: no philosophical framing up front, no mention of virtual worlds or ontology until the debrief. The debrief should then map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical questions — what exactly was destroyed when the board was swept into the box, whether the compensation addresses the same kind of loss, what the physical substrate does and does not explain — and it should be constructed to give a hard time to someone with my own deflationary instincts, so that I am forced to see where my "it's just cardboard, and by extension just bits" line holds and where it embarrassingly fails.

Please also include a frank section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with a room of retired engineers and accountants, and how I should handle it — I anticipate, for instance, someone insisting the whole exercise is rigged because the stakes are trivially small compared to years invested in a real virtual world. And please be explicit about which feature of the actual philosophical problem no ninety-minute activity can reproduce, so I know what I must supply myself in the discussion afterwards. A note on where I should stop the activity if it runs over time would also be useful. Kindly write your response in American English.
