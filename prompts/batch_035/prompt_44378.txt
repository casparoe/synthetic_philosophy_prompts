I'm a retired geophysicist — seventy-one this year, spent most of my career shooting seismic waves into the ground and listening to what bounced back — and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy the way some people get into birdwatching, obsessively and slightly late. I just worked my way through Pascal's Pensées, and the passages about justice and force have gotten under my skin. The idea, as I read it, is that justice without force accomplishes nothing, force without justice is tyranny, and that in practice what actually holds up a legal order isn't justice but custom — law on one side of a river being robbery on the other, the weaker side giving in not because it's persuaded but because it can't do otherwise. In geophysics nothing is "unjust" — a wave just propagates, it doesn't deserve anything — and I'm starting to wonder if I believed my whole working life that law was more like a wave than like a moral fact.

So I've been testing my own gut reactions against Pascal, and I honestly can't tell whether he's right or whether I'm just a tired old man who's gone cynical. Here are my verdicts. Running a red light at 4am on a completly empty street: I don't do it, but it doesn't feel wrong the way stealing would, it feels like a habit, almost superstition. Taking an aggressive-but-legal tax position a few years back: didn't bother me in the slightest. A former colleague in the 80s who quietly shaved some safety data because the regulator had no teeth and would never check: I've never forgiven him for that, still think it was straightforwardly wrong. My grandson getting disciplined by his university over something he said — legal speech, but against the campus code: I thought the university was within its rights and I couldn't really work up any indignation about injustice, even though the "law" there is nothing but the university's power to expel him. When a court struck down a law I personally thought was just: I thought we all had to comply anyway, no question.

And then the one I keep going back and forth on: Nuremberg. Part of me thinks the tribunals were genuinely right — those men deserved what they got and "following orders" excuses nothing. But another part of me suspects it was the victor's force dressed up in justice's clothes, and that if the Axis had won, the exact same reasoning would have been aimed at us, and that this matters somehow, though I can't say why it should. So: is there a principle, maybe something Pascalian like "legitimacy is custom plus an enforcement equilibrium that's been stable long enough to get mistaken for rightness," that captures all my verdicts? Test it against my cases, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save and would have to overturn, and then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't decided yet — whether I should think a country with universally recognized legal claims but no army to back them up is more entitled to be obeyed than one with the army and none of the claims, or whether that whole framing is confused. Be honest with me, I've handled bad news from data my whole life.
