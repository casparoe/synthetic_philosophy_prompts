I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized newsmagazine, and by an accident of seniority I now chair its standards committee, a small body that sets editorial policy on contested usage. We are deadlocked on a live case and I need a decision memo to circulate before our next meeting — the deliverable is a formal recommendation the committee can vote on, not a survey of the literature.

The case: last month, during a recorded session of a county planning board, one of its members used a racial slur, in full, while denouncing an affordable-housing proposal, and the recording has since circulated widely. We are preparing a feature about the meeting and the backlash. Our current style guide, written decades ago, says only that "offensive terms are avoided unless essential to the story" — guidance too vague to settle anything. Three positions have emerged on the committee. Our standards editor wants the slur printed verbatim once, in the body of the piece, on the ground that the public record matters and that euphemism softens what actually happened. Our features editor wants the paraphrase convention ("the member used a slur for Black Americans"), arguing that our pages shouldn't be a vector for the word's circulation. A third colleague proposes asterisking — printing the word with some characters replaced — as a compromise, and a fourth has raised what I find the most interesting objection to that compromise: that asterisking may not be a middle path at all, since it obliges every reader to complete the word privately, so the reader rather than the writer performs it. Two further wrinkles. First, we will embed a clip of the meeting on our site, with captions and no bleeping, so a print-side paraphrase policy creates an internal inconsistency our readers have already begun writing in about. Second, our newsletters push subject lines and preview text directly onto phones, where readers cannot choose whether to encounter the content.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of language, with the temperament of someone who has advised newsrooms rather than someone chasing terminological purity, and to draft the memo I will circulate. Before writing it, think carefully and brainstorm the options — including ones the committee hasn't formulated, such as a tiered policy distinguishing where in our product the word may appear (body text versus headlines versus push notifications versus archival transcripts) — rather than assuming the three live positions are exhaustive. Then commit: the memo should recommend one concrete, actionable policy, stated so that a desk editor could apply it without consulting anyone. In the reasoning, engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side — in particular, give the use/mention distinction its due, and consider what follows if a slur's capacity to wound lives in something other than its literal content, so that quotation may not fully neutralize it — while explaining why your recommendation nonetheless wins. Please also address the captions inconsistency and the newsletter problem explicitly, since a policy that ignores them will not survive the vote. Aim for roughly a thousand words, formal register, addressed to the committee, and free of citations to particular authors or papers; the members are smart generalists, not specialists, and I want them persuaded by the reasoning rather than snowed by references.
