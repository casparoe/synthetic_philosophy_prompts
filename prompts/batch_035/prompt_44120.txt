I write novels for a living — midlist literary fiction, the kind that gets nice reviews and modest sales — and somewhere along the way I picked up a philosophical commitment I can't shake: that some books really are better than others, and that this isn't just a fact about my training or my class background. The version I've ended up with is basically Hume's from the 1757 essay on taste: aesthetic judgments aren't objective like arithmetic, but they're not pure preference either, because when you take people with practiced, unprejudiced, clear-sighted judgment and let them compare notes over time, their verdicts converge, and that convergence is what "this novel is good" tracks. It matters to me practically, not just in the abstract — it's why I'll spend two years getting a sentence rhythm right instead of chasing what sells, and it's what I tell myself when my agent says nobody can tell the difference anyway.

But lately the view feels shakier than I'd like, and for reasons coming out of my own trade. I've sat on prize juries where five extremely qualified readers — as close to Hume's ideal critics as you'll find in the wild — produced five completely different shortlists, and the winner seemed to turn on whose turn it was to be persuasive that weekend rather than on any converging verdict. I've watched the same manuscript get rejected forty times and then win a big prize once a famous name was attached. And I've noticed that most of the "timeless" works we treat as proof that taste converges were assigned in schools for two or three generations first, so readers were trained on them before they ever got a chance to judge freely — the convergence may be downstream of the curriculum, not the other way around. There's also the flip side: things sit in obscurity for a century and then get canonized, which cuts both ways for the convergence story.

So: what would actually count against the Humean view? Give me the full map of its exposure — the observations, arguments, or possible cases that should genuinely worry me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted by which ones the view's defenders would concede as real defeaters versus which they'd wave off as missing the point. I'm especially interested in whether the position has been quietly insulated: if every case of expert disagreement gets dismissed with "well, those weren't truly qualified judges," and every case of convergence gets chalked up as vindication, is there anything left that could falsify it — and if not, is that fatal for a view like this, or just an honest feature of what taste is? And be straight with me about whether the jury stuff and the canon-training point I described are real evidence against the view or just my bruised ego after a bad prize season — what would distinguish the two, hypothetically or in practice?
