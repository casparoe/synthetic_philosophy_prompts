okay so, context: i'm a novelist, mostly comic stuff, and my current book is built out of interstitial documents — paperwork from fictional agencies, stuck between the chapters. the world of the novel is one where epiphenomenalism is official government policy. qualia exist, the state is very clear about that, they're real and important and legally recognized. they're just not allowed to cause anything. (there's a backstory with the physics dept, not relevant here.)

write me one of these documents: the annual caseload report of the Bureau of Phenomenal Causation Audits, roughly 500-600 words, deadpan official register, numbered findings. the Bureau's job, in case it's not obvious: citizens file claims that some utterance of theirs — "i'm in agony," "the red of that sunset nearly knocked me over" — was caused by the experience itself rather than by their brain. auditors are dispatched, they investigate, and in every single case this year (pick a number, 4,000-something, and have the director note it's fully consistent with last year and the year before) they find that the brain did it.

some requirements:

- the comedy has to come from the actual structure of epiphenomenalism, not from exaggerating it into a strawman. the auditors are thorough and sincere. the Bureau believes in consciousness, it never once suggests the complainants are making things up — it just can never catch a quale in the act. one finding should mention that the complainant's sincere tears during the interview turned out, on inspection, to also just be neurons.
- include one case file where the complainant is a philosopher (invent her, don't name a real person) who argues her testimony is caused by her experience the way smoke is caused by fire. the Bureau's written reply should quietly concede that this is exactly the thing it can never, in principle, verify — without ever saying "in principle."
- somewhere the report should classify testimony about experience as admissible evidence that the experience occured, and inadmissible as evidence of any causation by it. this rule should be stated as boringly as possible, like it's about filing deadlines.
- end with the director observing that the report itself — the one you're reading, with its conclusion that qualia never cause anything — was likewise produced entirely by physical processes, and that the Bureau takes no position on whether anyone should find that troubling. don't wink harder than that, no exclamation marks, no "or was it??"

the thing i care about most is that a philosopher of mind reads it and says "yes, that's unfortunately the actual situation," and a civilian reads it and just thinks it's a funny bureaucracy. if you can get both, we're good.
