I'm an undergraduate taking a course in political science, and we've just spent two weeks on collective choice and voter behavior. When we covered social choice theory, the professor presented the Condorcet paradox: with three or more options and certain preference orderings among voters, majority rule can cycle, so that option A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A, leaving no option that defeats all rivals. Then, in a separate unit on turnout, she referred to "the paradox of voting" again — this time meaning something different: that a purely instrumental voter, whose only aim is to influence the outcome, faces odds of casting a decisive ballot so remote that the expected benefit almost never outweighs even trivial costs of voting, yet people turn out in enormous numbers anyway. I've also seen the label "the Downs paradox" attached to this second usage. I came out of the course with these two results blurred together, and I want to make sure I have them straight before the exam.

Here is the picture I currently have, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong. Since the Condorcet result shows that majority preferences can be circular, election outcomes in multi-candidate settings don't track any coherent "will of the people" — the winner is partly an artifact of agendas and procedures. Given that, it seems to me that the turnout puzzle answers itself: rational citizens stay home because the outcome doesn't reflect a genuine social preference in the first place, so there is nothing meaningful for a single vote to influence. On this reading, the low-turnout behavior that political scientists keep describing as puzzling is actually the correct instrumental response to cycling, and the move of adding a separate term for civic duty to the voter's calculus, in the style of Riker and Ordeshook, looks like an ad hoc patch designed to rescue the theory from an awkward fact. So my questions are: (1) are the cycling paradox and the turnout paradox genuinely two distinct problems, or is my reading right that the first simply explains the second? (2) if they are distinct, why exactly — doesn't a rational voter's expected-value calculation depend on whether the electoral outcome means anything? and (3) if a voter became convinced that majority rule cycles in their jurisdiction, would that give them a good instrumental reason to abstain?

Please be thorough but clear, define any technical terms when you first use them, and write your answer in American English.
