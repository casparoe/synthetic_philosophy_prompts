For the last twenty years I've read the rationalist blogs — the ones about akrasia, self-binding, and treating your future self as a somewhat different agent, to be managed rather than blindly trusted. In my late thirties I put that outlook into practice in a way most people would find strange. Having watched my father's mind come apart over a decade, I chose an afternoon when I was rested, unmedicated, and under no acute stress, and wrote a document with my wife and my oldest friend as witnesses. It provides that if I ever lose the capacity to make my own decisions, no aggressive measures are to be taken to prolong my life beyond a stated point; comfort is to be prioritized over extension. And it contains one further clause, which is the clause at issue: if the impaired future me turns out to be calm and content with his condition, and says he wants to go on living, that expressed wish is to carry no weight — because I judged, while I was still in a position to judge, that a contented, deteriorated version of myself would not be speaking for the person I took my life to be.

I am now in my early seventies with early-stage Alzheimer's. My memory is going, but I remain legally competent, and I have concluded that I was wrong. I am not frightened, my days still have a shape to them, I want to keep living, and I have asked to revoke the document. My wife, whom I appointed precisely because she would hold to it against my later objections, refuses: she says the entire point of the exercise was to win this exact argument, and that if she yields now, the document was never anything more than a sentimental gesture. My son tells her she is letting a dead man keep his grip on a living one.

I can still follow an argument, so give me one. Setting aside what the law permits, who is entitled to have their wishes prevail here, and what does the answer turn on? Is it whether the man who wrote the clause and the man who now regrets it are the same person in the sense that matters for this purpose? Whether a promise made to oneself can bind? Whether my present contentment counts as evidence of what I want, or only of what the disease has done to what I want? And is there any principled ground for my wife to stand on between the two of us, or is she simply choosing which of me to betray?
