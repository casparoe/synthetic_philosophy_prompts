In seminars I defend what I take to be Aumann's result and its orthodox reading: in a finite game of perfect information such as the centipede game, common knowledge of rationality implies the backward-induction outcome — take at the first opportunity — and when real subjects cooperate for many rounds, that merely shows the common-knowledge condition was not satisfied, not that the theory mispredicts. What would count against me here? I am less interested in the experimental fact that people cooperate than in the formal and conceptual results that should worry a defender: proofs that common knowledge of rationality fails to deliver backward induction, arguments (Stalnaker's, for instance) that beliefs at unreached nodes are not settled by the knowledge condition and that the standard argument smuggles in extra counterfactual assumptions, or demonstrations that those extra assumptions are inconsistent in some games. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them an Aumann-style defender would concede as counting against the view and which would be reinterpreted away, and give me an honest verdict on whether the fallback — "the prediction holds whenever the knowledge condition holds, and it didn't" — leaves the position with any empirical or formal risk at all. If it doesn't, say whether that insulation is acceptable for a solution concept that is supposed to ground game-theoretic prediction, or whether it signals the theory is answering a different question than the one we ask of it.
