I live in a cohousing community of about twenty adults that shares a large vegetable garden. Everyone eats from it, and for years the arrangement has been informal: whoever feels like it works in it. In practice the same four or five people do nearly all of the work, and there is growing resentment. Next month we vote on a formal proposal, and since I've been asked to open the discussion, I want to get my own thinking straight first. I have no background in philosophy or economics — everything below is just what I've worked out on my own while standing at the sink — so I'd like your help. Please take on the role of a philosopher who specializes in fairness, cooperation, and how groups divide up shared burdens, and work through my questions carefully rather than jumping straight to a verdict.

The proposal on the table: each household owes a fixed number of garden hours per month. A household can skip any of those hours by paying fifteen dollars per skipped hour into a fund that would pay a local teenager to do the work instead.

Here is what keeps tripping me up. The people who currently do most of the gardening mostly enjoy it. My neighbor across the path is out there nearly every evening, and for her the garden is as much her social life as a chore; if she weren't weeding, she'd be home doing something she likes less. Another member, by contrast, detests gardening, but he cooks nearly all of our shared Sunday dinners, which takes him several hours a week. Nobody has ever counted the cooking toward any shared duty, because it isn't "garden work," yet everyone eats those dinners just as everyone eats the vegetables.

Thinking about this, I've come to suspect that two different things get tangled together in our arguments, and I've privately started calling them "what you give up" and "what you put in." What you give up is the leisure, effort, or discomfort you sacrifice. What you put in is the useful work that lands on the pile for everybody. When the proposal counts hours, it seems to measure only the second thing — the pile. But our resentment seems to track the first. The happy daily gardener logs far more hours than the reluctant one, but it isn't clear she gives up more to produce them. Meanwhile the cook gives up a lot and puts a lot on a pile, and neither the hours rule nor the resentment notices him at all.

So I have three questions for you. First: when a shared duty is being divided, should a person's required contribution be measured by what they sacrifice, by what they add for everyone, or by some combination? Does it change the answer that the sacrifice is partly chosen — someone can take up gardening precisely because they enjoy it, and I find it odd in both directions: it seems wrong to reward people for happening to enjoy their share of the work, but also wrong to act as though the pleasure makes their effort less real. Second: is there a principled way to decide which tasks count toward the shared duty in the first place, or is the line between "garden work" and "everything else we quietly do for each other" just an accident of which chores we happen to formalize? Third: what about the fifteen-dollar opt-out? Part of me thinks it's a clean and humane solution. Another part worries that it quietly converts a community into a marketplace: the households with more money end up never touching the garden at all, and we stop being people who shoulder something together and become people who hire and are hired.

Before you answer, please spend a couple of paragraphs brainstorming freely: lay out the main ways one might measure a fair share of a shared burden, note where each runs into trouble given my specific facts, and mention any well-known lines of thought your analysis echoes, in plain language. Then tell me plainly which way of thinking you find strongest and why. Finally, close with practical advice for the meeting: what I should actually propose, what compromises I should be ready to accept, and what I should be on guard against saying, since I suspect several of the arguments circulating in our community sound persuasive but won't survive the analysis.
