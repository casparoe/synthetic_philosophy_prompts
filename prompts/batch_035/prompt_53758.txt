Before I commit to a side in the function wars, I want the specification first: what an adequate theory of biological function actually has to deliver. Fix the purpose in advance, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory needs to be good enough for two jobs: underwriting judgments of malfunction, as in medicine and veterinary practice, and handling traits in organisms whose history we partly know and partly designed, as in breeding and synthetic biology.

Write the desiderata. Among the cases the theory must resolve, include at least these: a heart that pumps poorly, where there should be a fact about what it is supposed to do even while it fails to do it; a vestigial structure like the human appendix; an exapted trait, such as feathers originally selected for insulation and later co-opted for flight, including the gene-duplication version where one copy retains the ancestral job and a duplicate acquires a new one; a trait fixed by a breeder's preferences rather than by natural selection; and a de novo part in a synthetic minimal cell, designed and working before any selection has operated on it. Among the distinctions the theory must respect: function versus fortuitous benefit, function versus current use, malfunction versus mere variation, and the function of a trait type versus that of a particular token — consider the pumping organ of the very first organism to have one, before any selection for pumping. Formal and empirical constraints: no presupposition of a designer, no circularity (defining function through selection while understanding selection through function), and consistency with how evolution actually proceeds — drift, pleiotropy, and structures that arise as by-products of something else.

For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it, and classify it as non-negotiable, negotiable given the stated purpose, or a demand that is really just a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a constraint. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I have one candidate tension in mind: whether a single theory can both underwrite malfunction, which seems to require history, and assign functions to first-time or engineered parts, which lack the relevant history — but find others if they exist. Say whether each tension is genuine or an artifact of how the cases are described, and whether hybrid or two-tier views escape the tension or merely inherit both sets of problems. Close with a brief scorecard: the etiological or selected-effects family, the causal-role or functional-analysis family, and one forward-looking view that ties function to propensities or dispositions rather than to past or present performance, each checked against the list, plus a verdict on which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. The list is the deliverable; keep the scorecard short, and do not spend time on the history of the debate or on attributing positions to particular authors.
