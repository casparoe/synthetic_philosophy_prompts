Draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued for years about the one-shot prisoner's dilemma.

Camp A holds that in a genuinely one-shot prisoner's dilemma between causally isolated agents with no communication, defecting is simply what rationality demands, and that every argument for cooperation smuggles in something the stipulations of the game exclude: repeated play, reputation, a causal link, or altruistic payoffs that are no longer the prisoner's dilemma at all.

Camp B, drawing on the superrationality tradition and on evolutionary and tournament-based arguments about the emergence of cooperation, holds that agents who know they are facing symmetric choices with symmetric information and symmetric deliberative machinery should treat their own choice as strong evidence about the other agent's choice, and that cooperation can therefore be rational even in a strict one-shot setting.

The document should contain the following: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — about the formal structure of the game, about what the folk theorem for iterated play does and does not establish, and about what Axelrod-style tournaments actually demonstrated (given that they involved repeated interaction and pre-submitted strategies rather than live one-shot deliberation); (2) a table of the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so the camp holding that view would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own position — including whether the evidential weight of one's own choice is a theorem of decision theory, a consequence of an additional empirical premise about how similar the two deliberators are, or an outright confusion of correlation with causation; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is whether any real-world interaction is ever genuinely one-shot, given shared culture, shared algorithms, or shared training; another is whether the causal isolation stipulation, if it could actually be enforced, would itself dissolve the cooperative intuition or merely make it irrational to follow; (4) for each crux, a concrete case, experiment, or argument both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal — for instance, about what 'rational' should mean in a decision-theoretic context — say explicitly which part and why, and separate it from the residual substantive disagreement.

End with a short section stating what, if anything, both camps agree would constitute progress on this question, and what would merely restate their starting positions in new vocabulary.
