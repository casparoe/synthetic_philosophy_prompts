I teach a 300-level metaphysics seminar (15 students, one 75-minute session) and we're two weeks into Naming and Necessity — they've read the first two lectures, so they've seen the modal argument and the Gödel/Schmidt stuff on the page, but honestly I don't think they feel it. It's all just moves to them. I want an in-class activity where the rigidity of names and the epistemic/metaphysical modality split show up in something they actually do, before I say a word about it.

My vague idea: a game where teams introduce names under conditions I control — some names fixed by a dubbing ceremony with someone physically present in the room, and some names handed over via a description I've rigged to be false (a Gödel/Schmidt setup: I tell them "Belson is the guy who proved the Reductio Lemma," and later they find out it was actually his grad student, whose real name nobody was ever told). Some students should recieve the name only secondhand, through other students, so there's a chain. Then the descriptions fail or get contradicted, and teams have to bet — actual points, a small prize at the end — on what their names refered to all along, both in the actual world and in counterfactual scenarios I feed them ("in the closest world where Belson plagiarized everything, who proved the Lemma?"). I want whichever description-theorists are in the room to feel their predictions about their own usage go wrong.

Second phase: I want them to fix a name onto a stuff or a kind without knowing what it is (some unknown clear liquid in a flask, a sealed sample of something), then get the a posteriori discovery partway through the session, and then be forced to answer counterfactual questions about it ("in a world where the lab tests had come back different, would the flask still contain tolu?"). My hope is they slide into treating their own a posteriori discovery as necessary without noticing they've done it, and then I catch them at it in the debrief.

So design the whole thing: rules, roles, materials, a timeline that fits 75 minutes, the scoring, and the debrief — with the debrief mapping specific moments in the game onto (a) fixing reference vs giving meaning, (b) rigid vs accidental designation, (c) epistemic vs metaphysical possibility. Also give me a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I assume students will try to lawyer the rules, and some will have half-remembered Kripke and will smugly "get" the trick early and try to lecture their teammates — I want to know how to handle both without giving the game away.

And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. My guess is it can generate the rigidity intuitions but not the metaphysical necessity half — "water couldn't have been XYZ" is supposed to be an insight about the world, not just a fact about how a dubbing sticks to a sample, and I'm not sure any classroom setup bridges from the second to the first. Tell me whether you agree and what I have to supply by hand at the end.
