I'm a journalist — science and ideas desk, mostly, which in practice means I write the pieces about memory and genocide scholarship every April when the anniversaries come around. Somewhere along the line I got hooked on Zygmunt Bauman's argument in Modernity and the Holocaust: that the genocide wasn't modernity failing or breaking down, but modernity working normally — the division of labor that lets each person hold one clean piece of a dirty process, technical rationality replacing moral judgment, the distance between the desk and the ditch, the state as gardener deciding what belongs in the garden and what gets weeded out. The first time I read it, it felt like it explained everything. Now I'm less sure, and the thing that keeps needling me is Rwanda, because I keep going back and fourth with a colleague about whether it fits the theory or wrecks it.

Here are the facts as I understand them, and tell me if I've got any of this wrong: in 1994 something like 800,000 people were killed in roughly a hundred days, a per-day rate that exceeds the Holocaust's, and it was done mostly with machetes and clubs, neighbor killing neighbor, face to face, on hillsides and at roadblocks — but also coordinated through militia chains of command, RTLM radio broadcasts that named targets and told people where to wait, and identity cards sorting people by ethnicity, cards that were themselves a bureaucratic artifact of Belgian colonial rule. So the assignment: run the Rwandan genocide through Bauman's framework. Map it carefully — what plays the role of the bureaucracy, what plays the role of the distance he thinks is essential, what plays the role of rationalization — and be disciplined about the mapping instead of forcing everything into slots. Some parts of the machinery will fit; the intimacy of the killing and the near-total absence of industrial technology don't seem to have any role in his theory at all, and I want you to say that plainly rather than pretending otherwise.

Then run it both directions, like you'd want from a real case analysis: where Bauman explains Rwanda, where he's silent, where he actively misdescribes it. And I want your verdict on what the episode does to the thesis — is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a kind of case his framework never anticipated? Two sub-questions I can't shake off. First: the western media at the time framed Rwanda as ancient tribal hatreds, premodern savagery — Bauman's lens rejects that framing completely, and I suspect the truth is weirder than either story, that Rwanda was simultaneously more modern than it looked (colonial classification systems, radio as a mass medium, the administrative logistics of the roadblocks) and the most intimate genocide on record. Second: the Historikerstreit fight was in part about whether the Holocaust should be treated as unique and incomparable, and Bauman's whole project of building a general theory of modern genocide out of it would have made the Nolte side and the Habermas side both uncomfortable in different ways. Does using the Holocaust as theory-fodder the way Bauman does change how we should compare Rwanda to it at all — or is Bauman's kind of comparability a totally different animal from Nolte's, and if so, why exactly?
