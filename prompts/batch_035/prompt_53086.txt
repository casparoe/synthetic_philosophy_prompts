i'm a phd student in philosophy of mind and i maintain our department's semi-popular blog. i want to build one of those sorting quizzes — "answer some cases, find out which account of self-deception you're actually committed to" — and i need your help designing it so it does more than sort people by which label they like the sound of.

the debate i'm targeting is the old one: can a person literally deceive themselves? on one side you need a deceiver and a deceived, but if they're the same person the deceiver has to know the very thing the deceived is being kept from, which looks like believing p and not-p at once. the leaves i need on the tree: (1) straight intentionalism — you somehow set out to fool yourself and succeed; (2) the deflationary / motivational story — no second-order intention, just desire pushing evidence around until you land on the comfortable belief; (3) divided-self views where the deception is real but the mind splits into compartments, and i'd like the tree to at least try to seperate the davidson-flavored version (semi-autonomous departments of belief) from the freudian one (an unconscious that does the work); (4) the sartrean move — you can't literally deceive yourself, consciousness is transparent to itself, so what we call self-deception is really something else you're doing while knowing full well you're doing it; and (5) the hardline "self-deception is impossible / the concept is confused, everything is just bias or motivated reasoning" leaf.

the actual work is in the diagnostic questions. i want around eight, each a short concrete vignette with two or three answer options, and none of them may ask "do you think you can fool yourself?" — that's the thing i'm trying to diagnose, not assume. a few requirements on which cases get used: at least two should stress the simultaneity issue, i.e. whether the self-deceiver has to hold p and not-p at the very same moment or whether the "knowledge" can be dispositional, background, something they'd assent to under anesthesia-free conditions but never avow; at least one should test whether timing does all the work, like the person who deliberately avoids looking at the test results now so that six months later they can sincerely, and by then genuinely, believe the comfortable thing — the "i just won't look" strategy, where the intending and the believing happen at different times; and at least one on gradations, akrasia and wishful thinking versus the real thing, since that's where my readers will most want a question to draw a line that maybe no question can draw for them.

then the scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, plus two honest notes at the end: which pairs of leaves the quiz genuinely cannot pull apart with vignette answers (i suspect the sartrean leaf and the divided-self leaves will blur, but tell me if i'm wrong and why), and for any combination of answers that lands nowhere, a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. audience is grad students and motivated undergrads, so assume they know what akrasia means but don't lean on any specific papers — paraphrase every position in your own words, no quotations from the literature, i don't want this reading like a book report. oh and keep the vignettes fresh — not the textbook dying-wife case everyone's seen a hundred times, give me variations that force the commitments out instead of triggering pattern-matching.
