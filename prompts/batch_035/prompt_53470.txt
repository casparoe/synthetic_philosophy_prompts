I'm one of the five members of the Amnesty Committee of our truth commission — we were set up under the peace accord that ended the civil war, and our mandate runs out at the end of June, after which anything we haven't decided goes into criminal process. The committee's job under the enabling act is to grant individual amnesty for politically motivated crimes, but only where the applicant makes full disclosure of all relevant facts and the act was proportionate to its political objective. In practice we've read "full disclosure" as requiring a truthful account of what happened, the names of everyone involved, the locations — and also honesty about the applicant's own degree of responsibilty, not just the facts he can hide behind other people's orders for.

The case I have to write our position on before Thursday: a former officer in the security forces who ran one of the detention facilities through which most of the disappeared from the eastern region last passed. His testimony is the single best source we have — he's given what look like genuine locations for three of the four remaining mass graves, and nobody else alive seems to know the fourth. But the families' lawyers cross-examined him for two days and he would not concede direct participation in anything: every order he gave he describes as relaying instructions from above, every beating supposedly happened while he was elsewhere, and for the one session where a surviving witness swears he was in the room, he claims he arrived only afterwards. Half the committee thinks this is textbook minimisation — self-exculpatory in exactly the way that defeats full disclosure — and that granting amnesty would effectively let the commission certify his sanitised version of events. The other half, me included sort of, worries that refusing him means the fourth grave is never found and his file goes to prosecutors whose evidence is thin, so we'd be trading the families' chance to bury their dead for a trial that probably collapses anyway.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the other four members before the meeting. It needs a concrete recommendation — grant, refuse, or something conditional like a further closed session, or amnesty conditioned on physically leading us to the graves — with the reasoning spelt out, and it has to take the minimisation objection seriously on its merits, not treat it as an emotional speed bump. Also please flag anything you think is muddled in how we've framed this: if you think full disclosure as we've been applying it just can't cleanly decide a case like his, or that the choice isn't really graves-versus-justice at all, say so directly instead of politely splitting the difference — I'd rather the committee spend Thursday arguing about the right thing.
