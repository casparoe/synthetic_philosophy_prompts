I teach a phil of language survey, and for the final seminar I want to pair Russell's theory of descriptions with Waiting for Godot. I know that sounds like a gimmick, but i think it's actually substantive, so I'd like a real reading of the play as a drama of empty reference. Godot is a name whose bearer never shows up, so sentences like Godot will come tomorrow look a lot like the present king of France is bald — yet the tramps use that name to organize their whole existence: they keep the appointment, defer the hanging, treat the road by the tree as a meeting point. The name is action-guiding for them without ever picking anyone out. So: does the Russellian paraphrase (there is exactly one godot-like entity, and it will come) capture what the name is doing in the play, or does Beckett come out closer to Strawson — the tramps never assert that anyone is coming, they just presuppose it, and presupposition is precisely what lets them go on waiting? Then look at the uniqueness and existence clauses: the play withholds exactly the commitments the analysis needs. We never learn whether there is one Godot or several, the boy who arrives at the end of each act insists he wasn't there the day before, and — if I'm remembering right, Vladimir at some point claims he once knew a family with almost the same name as Godot, so even the name's bearer-connection is a running joke. Dont reduce the play to an illustration of Russell: tell me where the play resists this reading, and whether Beckett dramatizes something the theory misses — a use of names that keeps working through total reference failure. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the reading overall and single out the one interpretive claim you're least sure of.
