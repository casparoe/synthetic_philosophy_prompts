I'm part of a small reading group that spends a few months each year on early modern travel and ethnographic writing, and our current pick is Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (Book I, Chapter XXX of the Essays). Most of the essay reads easily enough, but the paragraph our discussion got stuck on has been going round in my head all week, and I'd like to actually resolve it rather than leave it as a vague disagreement:

I find that there is nothing barbarous and savage in this nation, by anything that I can gather, excepting, that every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country. As, indeed, we have no other level of truth and reason, than the example and idea of the opinions and customs of the place wherein we live: there is always the perfect religion, there the perfect government, there the most exact and accomplished usage of all things.

"They are savages at the same rate that we say fruit are wild, which nature produces of herself and by her own ordinary progress."

In truth, we ought rather to call those wild, whose natures we have changed by our artifice, and diverted from the common order. In those, the genuine, most useful and natural virtues and properties are vigorous and sprightly, which we have helped to degenerate in these, by accommodating them to the pleasure of our own corrupted palate.

We may then call them barbaric, in respect to the rules of reason: but not in respect to ourselves, who in all sorts of barbarity exceed them. Their wars are throughout noble and generous, and have as much excuse and beauty as this human disease is capable of; having with them no other foundation than the sole jealousy of valour.

There are three things I can't untangle on my own. First, when Montaigne says we have "no other level of truth and reason" than the customs of the place we live, is this meant as a description of how human judgment in fact works, or as a diagnosis of a mistake — and either way, how does he seem to exempt himself from it, given that the whole paragraph is him judging another nation favorably by his own lights? Second, the fruit analogy: I take the point that "wild" fruit is simply fruit that grew without our intervention, so "savage" need not be pejorative; but he then reverses the analogy and says it is the ones we have changed by artifice that deserve to be called wild. Is this a neat turn of phrase, or is he making the stronger and stranger claim that cultivation is corruption — that "civilized" people are the real deviation from the common order? And if it's the stronger claim, doesn't it rely on a normative standard of what nature intends, the very sort of standard he seemed to deny anyone has access to a few lines earlier? Third, the closing sentences: he passes a pile of verdicts (their wars are "noble and generous," war itself is a "human disease," and we exceed them "in all sorts of barbarity"), which looks like the opposite of the relativist-sounding opening.

Please help me work out what this passage is actually claiming: what "level of truth and reason" is doing in that sentence, how the fruit analogy and its reversal are supposed to work, how the descriptive and the normative sides of the paragraph fit together, and whether there's a coherent position here or whether Montaigne is simply trading on an ambiguity between denying that custom is a *good* test of anything and reporting that it's the only test anyone has. I don't need suggestions for further reading or what to bring to the group next time — I want your own reading of this paragraph itself, including where you think it genuinely breaks down if it does.
