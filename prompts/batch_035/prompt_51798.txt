For the final session of our philosophy of mind reading group on intentionality — Searle's intrinsic-versus-derived distinction, the Chinese Room, and the replies it provoked — I'd like to close, as we've done each term, with a poem rather than a summary. The piece I want is a sestina built around Dennett's two-bitser.

The example, in case it helps: imagine a coin-operated jukebox somewhere in Canada, built to accept the local two-bit coin. Its coin mechanism is a genuine discriminator — it distinguishes the right coin from slugs, washers, buttons — and Dennett invites us to say, without embarrassment, that the machine detects quarter-shaped coins because it believes that's what they are. Now suppose a foreign coin comes into circulation that is physically indistinguishable from the two-bit piece (I have Italian lire in mind, but I may be misremembering the denomination; the poem shouldn't depend on it). The machine's innards are unchanged, yet whether its internal state counts as a true belief about one currency or a false belief about another depends on where the machine sits, what circulates there, and what the people who own it want from it. Content, the moral goes, is not sealed inside the physical state; it is fixed by the whole setting and by the interests of those who take the intentional stance toward the thing. Searle's rejoinder you know: the machine's aboutness is borrowed, like the meaning of marks on paper — real intentionality has a source, and the machine isn't one. And Dennett's escalation you also know: if all meaning must be lent by a comprehending mind, then natural selection was our first lender — competence without comprehension, all the way down. So either the two-bitser's belief is real, or ours is no better than its.

The requirements:

1. The sestina is in the voice of the two-bitser itself. This is deliberately paradoxical — per the objection, the machine has no voice of its own, only ventriloquism — so the poem should make the reader feel that problem without announcing it: the machine keeps speaking, and the reader keeps hearing someone else speak through it. Do not name either philosopher inside the poem.

2. The six end-words must be: token, quarter, slot, weight, belief, worth. The fixed rotation is the philosophical engine of the form: the same end-word returns each stanza with its sense migrated — token as coin, then as type-versus-token, then as a merely token gesture; quarter as coin, as a fourth of a year, as something torn in pieces; weight as what the coin exerts on a sensor, as the weight of a consideration; slot as aperture, as the slot one is fitted into; and so on. Each end-word should appear in at least two distinct senses across the poem, because this is exactly the two-bitser's situation: one physical state, different contents, depending on setting and use. If the form is doing its job, the sestina's repetition-with-drift should be an argument, not just a device.

3. A rough arc, with latitude for your own handling: early stanzas, the machine at home in its native circulation, honestly discriminating; middle stanzas, the currency shifting under it, its unchanged innards meaning something new; late stanzas, the objection that its words are not its own; final stanza and envoi, the escalation — who lent us ours? The envoi (three lines, all six end-words, two per line, in any placement) should deliver that you-too move without telling the reader whether it is a promotion of the machine or a demotion of the reader. Leave that hinge open; it is where our discussions always ended.

4. The poem must be right, not just decorated: someone who knows this literature should find each move recognizable — the real discrimination, the interest-relativity of content, the derived-intentionality objection, the evolutionary escalation — and someone who knows none of it should still have a good poem about a machine quietly insisting on what it takes to be true.
