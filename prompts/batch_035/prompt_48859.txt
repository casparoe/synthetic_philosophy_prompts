My sister's getting married in June and I'm giving the toast. Here's the complication: for the last decade she's been completely serious about a view she calls tranquilism — the idea that the only thing that finally matters is the absence of craving and suffering. Not that suffering matters more than other stuff, but that nothing else has final value at all. Positive feelings are at best the feeling of a headache subsiding, she says; joy is just suffering taking its hand off your throat. She's not a sobriety bore about it — she laughs constantly — it's more that she genuinely believes wanting is the disease and calm is the cure. Her fiancé is fully on board, they met at a meditation retreat, and roughly half the guest list will be people from that world who will absolutely notice if I say something dumb. The other half is family who think the whole thing is a phase and are expecting a normal toast about soulmates and burning passion.

So I need a toast that works for both rooms. It can't celebrate craving — no passion, no she-wanted-this-her-whole-life, no couldn't-live-without-him — but it also can't be a five minute lecture that loses the aunts. I want it warm and funny and actually a toast, not a TED talk with a raised glass. One angle I keep circling: wanting to be free of wanting is itself still a wanting, and my sister is the first person to admit that (she thinks it's funny, like a hand trying to grab itself), and maybe a marriage is actually a decent place to run that experiment — but do whatever you think works best, you don't have to use it.

Also, I'd like a short note at the end flagging where the toast takes liberties — anywhere it quietly smuggles in value a real tranquilist would deny, like treating satisfaction as good rather than just relief as good (my sister is big on that distinction, she says the difference between satisfaction and relief is like the difference between a dessert arriving and a pain stopping). And if you think the request can't be done honestly — like, if any wedding toast is so stuffed with desire-shaped sentiment that a consistent tranquilist would just refuse to give one — then say so plainly rather than papering over it, because honestly I've wondered the same thing myself and I'd rather know before I'm standing up there with a microphone.
