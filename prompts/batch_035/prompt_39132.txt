A friend of mine who reads a lot of popular physics insists the wavefunction is like a weather forecaster's probability map. There is a definite fact about the weather; the map just encodes our ignorance; and when new data comes in, the map is updated. On his telling, superposition is nothing but our uncertainty before we look, collapse is nothing but updating when we learn, and the whole weirdness of quantum mechanics evaporates once you stop confusing the map with the territory. He uses this to argue that quantum mechanics never made a strange claim about reality at all, and that people who say a particle is "in two places at once" are making a category error. My own rough thought is that the analogy dies at interference — two things each individually possible can conspire to be jointly impossible, which no forecast of rain can do — but I've never seen this worked out carefully, and I don't want to rely on a hand-waved objection. Could you lay out the comparison properly: what in the weather picture corresponds to the quantum state, to measurement, to collapse, and to the underlying "actual weather"? Then show where the correspondence breaks, and say which of the breakages actually matter for his conclusion and which are just loose talk that could be patched. In particular: does his picture quietly assume there is a definite underlying reality for the map to be ignorant of, and can it hold onto that assumption while also explaining interference? And if some patched-up version of "the state is just knowledge" does survive, what does it have to concede that his original, nothing-strange-about-reality conclusion cannot?
