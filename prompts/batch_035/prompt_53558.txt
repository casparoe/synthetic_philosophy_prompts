In my philosophy elective we have been discussing when two things are really one thing, and I have kept a list of my own judgments about cases involving objects and the stuff they are made of. I would like to know whether some single principle lies behind all of them, and I am prepared to hear that one or two of my judgments are simply mistaken.

Here are the judgments. (1) If a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue, I feel sure the statue is not just the same object as the lump of clay was: the statue would be destroyed if it were squashed flat, while the lump of clay would have survived that without any trouble. Since they differ in what could happen to them, they seem to me to be two different things. (2) On the other hand, I cannot actually bring myself to believe there are two things sitting on the pedestal. If someone asked me to count the objects in the room, I would count the statue once, not twice — once as a statue and again as a lump of clay. And when the sculptor squashes the statue, I want to say that exactly one thing was destroyed and exactly one thing survived, not that one object was destroyed and a second, hidden object was revealed. (3) I think the ship of Theseus survives having its planks replaced one at a time, so an object is not tied to the particular matter it started out as. (4) Yet if my grandmother's gold ring were melted down, I think the ring would be destroyed, even though every atom of gold survives. Being made of the same gold does not, by itself, keep the ring in existence. (5) Finally, the value puzzle: the statue might be worth millions, while the very same clay, sold as plain clay, would fetch a few dollars. I believe one and the same thing cannot be worth both millions and a few dollars, which pushes me toward the statue and the clay being distinct — and that is exactly the conclusion my counting instinct in (2) refuses to accept.

Is there a single principle that captures all five of these judgments, or at least most of them? If a simpler principle would force me to give one up, which one should go, and why that one rather than another? I would also like to know what your principle says about two cases I have not made up my mind about. First: a sculptor carves a statue directly out of a block of marble — did a block of marble exist right up until the moment the statue came into being, such that the sculptor destroyed one object in the course of creating another? Second: a piece of paper is folded into a crane and later unfolded and flattened back into a plain sheet — did the crane exist only during the folding, and did its destruction when the sheet was flattened count as the loss of an object? Please state the principle as precisely as you can, indicate which of my five judgments it preserves and what it predicts for my two undecided cases, and be honest about any judgment it cannot accommodate.
