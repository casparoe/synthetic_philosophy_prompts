I run a university reading group that pairs a short work of fiction with a philosophical framework each term, and next term's theme is collective action. For the session on multipolar traps and races to the bottom, I have chosen Dr. Seuss's The Lorax (1971), to be read alongside the Moloch framework — the idea, familiar from Allen Ginsberg's Howl and given its most influential recent statement in Scott Alexander's 2014 essay on Moloch, that competitive systems can destroy what everyone values without any individual villain: each actor does what is locally rational, and the aggregate devours the values that no actor chose to sacrifice. I would like a serious reading of the book through this lens, one that takes both the text and the game theory seriously.

The obvious obstacle is that the Once-ler appears to be a lone magnate, not one competitor among many, and Moloch's defining feature is multipolarity — traps that persist even when every agent behaves rationally. So the reading must confront this head-on: is The Lorax in fact a story of individual greed masquerading as a systemic fable, or can the multipolarity be located elsewhere — in the nameless consumers who all need a Thneed, in the pressure of the arriving relatives when the biggering begins, in the demand side the book barely acknowledges? Does the story need the Once-ler to be venal, or does it show a man half-aware of the trap, taking each expansionist step as a locally compelled response to family, market and sunk costs? Relatedly, I want the Lorax himself examined as a norm entrepreneur without enforcement power: he speaks for the trees, confronts the Once-ler repeatedly, and yet commands no sanction — what does the book suggest about protest unbacked by any mechanism, and what is the significance of his final exit through the smog?

The ending worries me most. The Once-ler hands the last Truffula seed to a child, and the moral of the book seems to be that the situation improves only if one individual cares a whole awful lot. Against the Moloch thesis, this is precisely the remedy that multipolar traps metabolise: individual conscience is what every competitor can afford to have only if their rivals have it too. So the reading must decide whether the ending is naive from the trap's perspective, or whether it gestures at something the structural literature underdescribes — the maintenance of a norm across generations, the seed as a focal point that creates common knowledge among future coordinators. Please tell me which scenes I should point the group towards, where the Moloch reading genuinely breaks down on the text, how an invisible-hand optimist would argue that the book actually vindicates markets (the Thneed is, after all, a voluntary purchase), and finally what the fable gives us that the game-theoretic arguments do not — whether making a child feel the loss of the Bar-ba-loots does philosophical work that diagrams of payoff matrices cannot.
