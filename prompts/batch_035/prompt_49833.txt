A friend of mine who's gotten really into ancient history tried to explain a theory he read about in a popular book on the collapse of civilizations: Tainter's idea is that societies are problem-solving machines — every new crisis gets answered with more complexity, more administrators, more soldiers, bigger infrastructure — and each round of complexity costs more and buys less, until the returns go negative. At that point any shock, like a drought or an invasion, tips the whole thing into collapse, which means the death of most of the population and the permanent loss of its knowledge. That, he says, is what happened to the Maya: they exhausted their soil, the returns on complexity turned negative, and within a few generations the great majority of them were dead. So the lesson is that collapse is essentially the penalty for living beyond your means, and modern industrial society is presumably heading for the same fate. I found all this pretty convincing at the time, but I've since heard that both Tainter's theory and the Maya story are more contested than my friend made them sound. Is his summary fair? Please take it part by part: what would a scholar of these societies accept, what gets Tainter's actual claim subtly wrong, what does it get wrong about the Maya specifically, and does his conclusion about our own society follow from whichever parts survive correction?
