Here is the position I hold. In the dispute between William James's "The Will to Believe" (1896) and W. K. Clifford's maxim — "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for any one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — I side with James, but in a deliberately narrowed form. James granted his permission for any genuine option, by which he meant one that is live, forced, and momentous, where the evidence cannot decide. I consider that too permissive. I hold instead that the permission applies asymmetrically, to what I call participatory beliefs: beliefs whose truth is partly constituted by their being held. Trusting that a friend is genuinely well-disposed toward me, confidence that I can pull off a difficult undertaking, faith that a partnership is worth staying in — in such cases the belief tends to help make itself true, and I claim that this is what makes believing on insufficient evidence permissible there. The mirror-image self-deceptions — the shipowner's comfortable conviction of his vessel's seaworthiness, the agitators' accusations — lack this feature, which is why they remain wrong; I take this asymmetry to be the datum that any adequate ethics of belief must capture. I also hold a subsidiary suspicion: that Clifford's own maxim cannot be accepted on the evidence Clifford would allow himself, so that consistent evidentialism undercuts its own basis.
