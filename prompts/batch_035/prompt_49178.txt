A close friend of mine is a condensed-matter physicist, and last weekend we had one of those arguments that runs from dinner past midnight. He insists that electrons are simply there — that his entire field is the manipulation of things whose existence no sane person doubts. I pushed back with the history of science: phlogiston, caloric, the optical ether — serious researchers once felt the same confidence about entities we now regard as mistakes. His comeback was the one I couldn't shake: that when the theory changes, what survives is the mathematical structure — that Maxwell kept Fresnel's equations even while discarding Fresnel's ether — so perhaps the honest conclusion is that structure is what our theories track, whatever else may or may not be underneath it. By the end we were unsure whether our remaining disagreement was about what exists or only about what we can know, and whether that distinction even does any work here.

I run a small reading group on philosophy of science, and I want to turn our argument into a proper diagnostic instrument rather than letting people shout labels at each other. Please write me a decision tree that takes a participant from a modest starting point — something like 'our mature scientific theories are strikingly empirically successful' — and leads them, node by node, to a named position on scientific realism.

Some specifications:

1. Every node must be a question a physicist or a student could answer without knowing any of the position names — questions about cases, not about schools. The obvious candidates should appear: what to say about the history of discarded theories (the pessimistic induction), whether the continuity of equations across theory change is real continuity or a lucky artifact, whether one can believe in unobservable entities while suspending belief in the theory that describes them, whether a purely relational description of nature carries non-trivial content or collapses into near-triviality (the worry usually raised against Russell and Newman), and whether observation is itself a notion that can be drawn neutrally enough to do this work.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and only then locate the actual positions within it: standard scientific realism, epistemic structural realism, ontic structural realism, entity realism, constructive empiricism, and an instrumentalist or pragmatist leaf. The structural realist positions must not collapse into one node; whether they can be genuinely separated is precisely one of the things I want the tree to reveal.

3. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, say so in a sentence: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply never been explored? And flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot in principle separate.

Please deliver the result as a single JSON object with the following schema. The field 'tree' is an array of node objects, each with the fields 'id' (a string), 'question' (the plain-language question), and 'branches' (an array of objects, each with 'answer' — the participant's answer — and 'next' — the id of the node or leaf it leads to). The field 'leaves' is an array of objects, each with 'position_name', 'diagnostic_answers' (the path through the tree that lands here), 'core_committment' (one sentence), and 'most_awkward_committment' (one sentence — the thing its defenders least like to be reminded of). The field 'unseparable_pairs' is an array of objects with 'pair' and 'reason'. The field 'unoccupied_combinations' is an array of objects with 'answers' and 'diagnosis'. Finally, the field 'settled_vs_contested' is an object with two array fields, 'settled' and 'contested', plus 'caveats': in these, please separate what is genuinely settled among philosophers of science from what remains a live dispute — for instance, whether the two structural realisms differ substantively at all, or whether the triviality objection admits of a clean reply — and do not present any contested reading as the consensus one.

Some background knowledge of the philosophical terrain would be helpful to assume, but please make every question answerable from reflection on the cases themselves, not from having memorised the literature.
