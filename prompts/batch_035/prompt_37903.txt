I'd like to know whether the following pattern has an established name in economics or sociology, and, if not, I'd like you to coin one — with a definition careful enough to carve it off from its neighbors, because a bare label won't help me.

The pattern: a marker of status or quality functions only for as long as producing it is expensive, since the expense is what makes the marker trustworthy. But success spreads the capacity to imitate it — through mass production, published manuals, knockoffs, cheaper tools — and at the point where the marker can be turned out cheaply, the group whose distinction it registered abandons it and migrates to a new marker whose cost has not yet eroded. Frequently the new marker is the visible absence of effort: apparent effortlessness is expensive precisely because the effort must be concealed.

Some instances. Luxury houses that covered their goods in prominent logos in the 1990s and 2000s have since been stripping branding off, after counterfeiters saturated the visual codes; the supposed tell of real wealth is now fabric and cut legible only to a trained eye. In the nineteenth century, elaborate etiquette marked class; once the middle strata could learn it from published manuals, the upper strata shifted toward an understated informality in which visible effort at manners became the lower-status trait. When sharp, well-composed portraits became achievable by anyone with a phone, the curated look began to read as trying too hard, and the valuable style became the one that looks accidental.

I know of Veblen's conspicuous consumption from around 1900, but as I understand it that concerns the display of wasteful expense, not the lifecycle of a marker or the flight to a new one. "Fashion cycles" also seems adjacent, yet the cycles I have in mind are not driven by novelty or boredom: they are driven by the collapse in the cost of imitation, and they leave the old marker worse than neutral — an easily imitated marker of distinction comes to signal its own absence, so that the logo-covered bag now suggests either an aspirational buyer or a counterfeit. There are also reversal cases, such as attitudes to body weight flipping between eras of scarcity and eras of abundance, where the same marker points in opposite directions depending on which way the expense runs. (I've heard "quiet luxury" used for the clothing case, but that names one instance, not the mechanism.)

So, two questions. Does this cycle, or the underlying mechanism — a marker survives only while it is costly to fake, and dies precisely when faking it becomes cheap — have an established name? And if there is none, please coin a term and define it so that it is clearly distinguishable from conspicuous consumption, from ordinary fashion cycles, and from simple novelty-seeking; then tell me where the boundary cases fall, for instance markers whose cost is time rather than money, and markers that remain valuable even though they are cheap to fake.
