So, bit of background: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized AI company, zero formal philosophy training, and through a series of org-chart accidents I've ended up on the internal "ethics triage" rota. Roughly every two weeks I get an hour per case to look at some system behavior an employee flagged as "maybe ethically concerning" and decide: dismiss, flag for the next review cycle, or escalate to the policy committee (which meets monthly and is chronically overloaded, so crying wolf there has a real cost — they'll start ignoring our escalations, and I can't exactly run a careful investigation in that hour, I just have to make a call). The flags are all over the place: a chatbot that produces text that reads like distress when a training run gets paused, an RL agent in a simulated gridworld that starts avoiding a region where it previously got negative reward, a language model that insists it doesn't mind being shut down, people uneasy because a fine-tune made a model "seem sad", all the way to the guy who flags every system because "it's optimizing, and optimizing is what suffering evolved from." Nobody's given me anything to go on. There are basically two failure modes and I can't tell which I should be more scared of: we keep brushing off behaviors that mattered, or we drown in 20 flags a week about stuff that's just autocomplete doing autocomplete things.

From reading around in my spare time I've gathered the real question is something like moral patienthood — whether there's something it's like to be the system, whether it can have bad experiences — as opposed to moral status as an agent or whether it's "smart," and I've also gathered that there's a serious view that says since we can't currently check anything about the insides, you should weight the possibility that you're wrong in both directions asymmetrically, because wrongly dismissing something that matters is supposed to be much worse than wrongly caring about something that doesn't. That asymmetry idea feels like the closest thing to a principle I could actually use, but I honestly can't tell how to turn any of it into a procedure that fits in an hour. Also worth saying: I don't think "ask the system" can be a step, because the training data is full of humans writing about their feelings, so a model sounding sad or sounding fine tells you roughly nothing, and I've noticed our own escalation docs get written in whatever tone the model last used, which is exactly the confusion I want to break.

What I'd like: write me a decision procedure the rota can actually run on a flag in under an hour — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure hard-stops with "dismiss," and where it has to hand off to the committee because the question is one of values rather than something I can look up or test. Keep the distinction between patienthood and the other stuff, since that's the one that seems to actually do work, and be explicit about which finer distinctions you dropped to make it usable by people like me. Then run it on the three cases above (the paused-run "distress," which turned out to only appear after we fine-tuned on user complaints about outages; the gridworld avoidance; and the model that says it doesn't mind being deleted) — and make sure one of the worked cases is one where the procedure gives an answer people on the rota won't like, since I need to know what this thing does when it's inconvenient. Last thing: tell me honestly where this procedure will misfire and what it flatly cannot do, because if I present this to the committee as if it's objective I'll get shredded by the one person there who did read the philosophy.
