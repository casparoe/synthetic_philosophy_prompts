I am a novelist by trade, and I have found myself in a situation I suspect has a well-worked philosophical structure to it, though I can't put a name to that structure. I would like your help in understanding it.

Fifteen years ago, a nineteen-year-old drunk driver killed my brother, who was twenty-two. This spring the driver, now thirty-four, comes up for parole, and I have been asked to speak at the hearing. The man who will sit before the board does not resemble the teenager who was sentenced: he has spent his imprisonment earning a degree, teaching other inmates to read, and by every account I can find, he has genuinely changed. My brother's best friend intends to speak in favor of release, which has split those of us who loved my brother, and I am the one caught in the middle.

Two arguments are being pressed on me, and I can render each of them from the inside — that is, after all, what I do for a living — which is precisely why I cannot choose between them. His counsel argues, in effect, that continued confinement protects no one: it costs the state tens of thousands of dollars a year, and it punishes a person who no longer exists, since the nineteen-year-old is gone and what remains is a middle-aged man who would be a danger to no one. Others in my family argue that none of this is to the point: a life was taken, and good conduct in prison is as irrelevant as it would be in any other matter of debt. A third friend put it differently again: she said that however long we keep him is the only sentence society will ever write about what my brother's life was worth, and that releasing him early is an editorial revision of that sentence. And my own motive worries me most of all: part of me wants him kept in because his release would feel like the world being permitted to move on from my brother, and I cannot tell whether that feeling is a desire for him to suffer — or whether there is some other way of wanting his continued confinement that is not that.

Please take on the role of a philosopher who works on punishment and the ethics of blame. Before answering, think through the situation carefully and, if it helps you, begin with a brief brainstorm of the distinct positions hiding inside these three arguments — I do not want them lumped into a simple two-sided dispute, because I already know it is not one. Then help me see the structure: what each argument assumes about what confinement after a crime is actually for; what work the claim that the punished person no longer exists is doing, and how someone could accept that he has changed while denying that this settles anything; whether there is a coherent way of wanting him to remain imprisoned that is not a wish for his misery; and what difference it makes, if any, that I am being asked to speak rather than to decide. I am not asking you to tell me what to say at the hearing, and I am not asking for comfort. Please reason with the strongest versions of each position rather than the convenient ones, avoid platitudes about closure and healing, and set aside the legal mechanics of parole boards — I want the philosophy, worked through honestly, from someone who will tell me where my own framing of the situation has gone wrong.
