I have no background in philosophy or economics — I work in freight logistics — but I read a lot of history of ideas on my commute, and something has been bothering me that I can't find a word for.

I read about Malthus, who argued around 1800 that population grows geometrically while food production grows arithmetically, so population will always press against the food supply and keep most people near subsistence. Two centuries later there are eight billion of us, and calories per person are higher than in his day, and yet I keep running into people who insist he was "right in principle, just early": the mechanism is still there — finite land, exponential appetites — it was merely delayed by synthetic fertilizer, hybrid crops, and so on. Then I noticed the same shape elsewhere. The 1970s predictions that oil and several key metals would be effectively exhausted by the 1990s: there was even a famous ten-year wager in 1980 between a biologist and an economist over the prices of five metals, which the scarcity side lost outright when the metals got cheaper, and afterwards the response was that the bet had been luck, the decade unrepresentative, the mechanism intact. Same with certain predictions of collapse or of a technology arriving by a stated year that now gets pushed back a decade at a time: the defenders never say the prediction was right, and never say it was wrong — they say it was early, that the clock, not the compass, was off.

So my question: does this epistemic situation have a name — a theory that made a genuinely checkable prediction, failed the check, and is then defended on the ground that only the timing was ever inessential, while the direction or mechanism stands? I can see it's somewhere near unfalsifiability, but the original prediction really was falsifiable and really was falsified; it's the rescue that can't be checked yet. It also isn't quite "moving the goalposts," because the defenders typically concede the missed prediction in full — they move the deadline, not the claim. And I want more than a label, because I suspect there's a real distinction hiding here between rescues that are legitimate (a warning that partially lands decades later, having changed behavior and so partly prevented itself) and rescues that are degenerate (any failure is compatible with the theory, so the theory is really explaining everything and predicting nothing). Is there a principled way to tell those apart at the moment of rescue rather than in hindsight, or is "right but early" always going to be indistinguishable from "wrong, but unfalsifiably so" until the thing either happens or doesn't? If there's an established concept for this exact configuration, tell me what it is and where its edges are; if it sits between several established ideas, tell me which fits best and what's left over; and if nothing fits, coin a term, define it precisely enough to exclude the neighbors, and give me a case on the boundary where it's genuinely hard to say whether it applies.

One more request: where you draw on things that people who actually study this disagree about — for instance, whether Malthus himself should be read as having made a dated prediction at all, or as having described a tendency that could be offset — please flag that as contested rather than presenting one reading as settled, and tell me which parts of what you say are standard and which are your own carving.
