Last night my closest friend and I had a long argument — the kind that starts over dessert and ends with a walk around the block — about Henry Sidgwick's decades of involvement in psychical research, and I have not been able to shake it since. My friend's position was brisk and, I suspect, the conventional one: that the Society for Psychical Research was a Victorian gentleman's hobby that happened to attach itself to a great philosopher, embarrassing in the way that a distinguished colleague's belief in astrology would be, and of no philosophical consequence for the reading of the Methods. I found myself defending the opposite, though only half-formed at the time: that the psychical research is not separable from Sidgwick's ethics, because the deepest problem he left us — the dualism of practical reason, in which rational egoism and utilitarianism each seem self-evident yet can dictate conflicting lives — was, for him, partly an empirical problem. My claim in the argument, reconstructing it this morning, was this: if death is annihilation, then the egoist's horizon is short and finite, and nothing guarantees that what is best for me coincides with what is best overall, so the dualism stands insoluble; but if personality survives death, the egoist's horizon stretches out until the two calculations converge, and the dualism dissolves. On that reading, the sittings, the trance mediums, and the census of hallucinations were not a diversion from the Methods but its unfinished empirical continuation: Sidgwick investigating, with the only methods he trusted, a premise his axioms could not supply. My friend's reply was that this is retrospective system-building — that I am crediting Sidgwick with a tidy architecture he never announced, and that plenty of the SPR's work (table-turning, thought-transference experiments) has no visible connection to any ethical question at all. We ended where we began: neither of us with a settled view, and me with a suspicion that the question is actually researchable.

I am preparing an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I want to turn this argument into the proposal. Please draft it. Specifically, I need:

1. A precise statement of the question — something like: did the psychical research function as a load-bearing premise in Sidgwick's attempt to reconcile rational egoism with utilitarianism, or was it a psychological fact about Sidgwick with no systematic role in his ethics? — stated so that it has a determinate answer, not a matter of interpretation endlessly deferred.

2. An account of why the existing positions leave it open. As far as I can tell, the field splits between my friend's dismissal, which treats the SPR material as biography, and a sympathetic reconstruction, my own instinct, which can start to look like it is finding whatever structure it needs. The proposal should say what evidence would discriminate between them — what in Sidgwick's texts, his treatment of the dualism across editions of the Methods, his stated reasons for taking the empirical evidence seriously as evidence, would count for or against the load-bearing reading.

3. A method section: what I would actually do, month by month, over twelve months. Assume textual and archival work rather than experiments: the successive editions of the Methods, Sidgwick's essays on the evidence for the supernatural, the memoir material. Do not include a bibliography — I will supply citations myself.

4. A statement of what the possible conclusions are, including the one my friend would favor: that the connection is real but weaker than I claimed, holding only at a particular moment in Sidgwick's development, or only for one strand of the psychical work. The proposal should make clear that this middling outcome is a result, not a failure — that the interesting question is where and how the connection holds, not whether I win the argument with my friend.

5. An honest account of what would count as the project failing: for instance, discovering that the textual evidence is too thin or too ambiguous to decide anything, or that the question cannot be kept distinct from the unanswerable one of whether the spirit communications were genuine. If the latter threatens, the proposal should say in advance how I will hold the line between reconstructing Sidgwick's reasons and evaluating his subject matter.

Keep the register of the proposal formal but not padded — the fellowship committee is philosophically serious but not Sidgwick specialists, and the document should be readable by them as a study in what happens when a rigorist epistemology meets a question it regards as decision-relevant but methodologically open. Roughly two pages of prose. Do not feel bound to vindicate either side of my argument; the proposal should be strong enough that I would submit it whichever way the evidence turns out to run.
