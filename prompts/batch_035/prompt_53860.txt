Quick background so you know what you're working with: in about two weeks I'm presenting at an AI safety org's internal research seminar. My thesis is that the shutdown problem as usually posed is less a wall than a hole — the standard impossibility-flavored worries (self-preservation as instrumental convergence, the old utility-indifference patch generating weird money-pump behavior, etc.) all quietly assume the agent has a complete, stable preference ordering over whole world-histories. My pitch is that if the agent is allowed to have genuine gaps in that ordering — cases where it just *doesn't have* a preference, e.g. between "keep operating and pursue the goal" and "shut down now" — and if it treats unresolved ties as something to punt to the humans rather than resolve itself, then the pressure toward keeping itself around never gets a purchase. Corrigibility stops being a hack you bolt onto a maximizer and becomes just what an agent with holes where the humans go looks like. I've been calling this "indifference by default" in my notes, though I'm not married to the name.

The room is maybe a dozen people and quite mixed. There are two or three people from the decision-theory side of things who are deeply convinced that any agent smart enough to be useful is smart enough to notice that its indifference is load-bearing and complete the preferences on its own, so anything like my scheme just delays the problem by a few gradient steps. There's a couple of pure-ML folks who think the whole framing is confused because deployed systems don't have utility functions over world-histories at all, they're reward-model-shaped artifacts that do whatever got reinforced, and "shutdown resistance" is a training data issue, not a theorem. One formal RL theorist who I suspect will go after whether my "ties get punted to the human" rule is even well-defined once you ask how the agent chops the world into the relevant outcome space. And one philosopher of action who will want to know whether an agent can coherently prefer *not* to have some preference, or notice the gap in itself, without that very noticing already being a preference.

So: build me the prep sheet. The objections in roughly the order they'll actually arrive, phrased the way each camp would phrase them (the decision theorists talk about reflective stability and money pumps, not "alignment issues"), ranked by how much damage each could realistically do to the thesis. For each, give me the two-minute version of a reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin — I'd rather know my soft spots than bluff. Say which things I should just concede up front to buy credibility, and where I need to hold the line no matter what. And please include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, because if I get it anyway I want to have something prepared. If you think one of the camps' worries actually sinks the whole idea, tell me that too — better I find out now than in the seminar.
