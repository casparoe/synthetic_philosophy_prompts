I'm finishing a graduate seminar on utilitarianism and its discontents, and for our final meeting each of us has to circulate a one-page position statement that the rest of the group will then tear apart. I've drafted mine, and rereading it, I genuinely can't tell whether it's a coherent position or just a stack of intuitions wearing a system's clothes. The label I've been using for it in conversation is a kind of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, though as you'll see I'm not sure the label fits what I actually wrote. Before this goes out to eleven people who will smell blood, I'd like an outside audit of whether the commitments hang together. Here they are, stated as plainly as I can:

(1) I'm a totalist about betterness between outcomes: to compare two worlds, you sum welfare over everyone in them, and the higher total wins.

(2) I think mild pains and pleasures are fully commensurable: enough mildly pleasant experience — concentrated in one life or spread across millions of lives — can outweigh any collection of mild aches, headaches, and itches.

(3) But I think there's a threshold of pain intensity above which this stops: no amount of pleasure, however vast and however distributed, can compensate a single instance of threshold-exceeding pain. One year of torture is not outweighed by any number of blissful lives, no matter how many there are.

(4) I accept that severe pains aggregate with one another: preventing ten instances of severe pain can be worth allowing one new instance, and the more you prevent, the easier that trade becomes.

(5) I'm relaxed about where exactly the threshold sits. Pain intensities form a continuum, the boundary is vague, and I take moral vagueness in stride elsewhere, so I don't see a special problem here.

(6) I reject side constraints. If one outcome is better than another, bringing about the better one is what ought to be done, and I take (3) to be a fact about the shape of the good rather than a rule sitting above the good — that's what separates my position from someone who just has an absolute rule against torture.

(7) On population, I bite the classic aggregationist bullets: a very large population whose lives are only just barely worth living is better than a smaller and much happier one, and I'm at peace with that.

(8) One clarification I keep having to make when I explain this: the threshold crossing isn't itself a morally momentous event in the experience of pain. The difference between a pain just below and just above the threshold is just an ordinary small difference in intensity — nothing like the difference between pain and its absence.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how badly. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damning; for each, say what would have to give, what that costs elsewhere in the set, and whether the cost is one I could realistically pay given how I've motivated the rest. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I don't want a tour of the literature or a lecture on utilitarianism's greatest hits — just the audit, and keep the whole thing under 800 words.
