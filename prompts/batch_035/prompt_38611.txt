A friend of mine, a psychology graduate student who reads philosophy on the side, has an argument that self-deception is impossible, and I cannot locate the flaw -- though I am confident there is one, because the conclusion seems to prove too much.

The argument, as she gives it:

(1) In every genuine case of deception, there is a deceiver who knows (or at least believes) that p is false, and who acts so as to bring it about that someone believes p.
(2) In self-deception, the deceiver and the deceived are the same person.
(3) So the self-deceiver believes p to be false while bringing herself to believe p.
(4) To bring it about that she believes p, she must succeed: she must actually come to believe p.
(5) Believing p and believing p false at the same time, in the same respect, is not something one person can do; where it appears to happen we are looking at dissociation or pathology, not self-deception.
(6) Therefore there is no self-deception.

She anticipates the obvious rejoinder -- that the knowing part can be unconscious, hidden, or partitioned off from the believing part -- and has a dilemma ready. If the partitions interact, then the knowing part must somehow keep the believing part from registering what it knows, and you have re-created the original paradox inside the partition: what does the regulating, and how does it do its work without knowing what it is holding at bay? If the partitions do not interact, you have not explained deception at all; you have merely described two minds, one of which happens to be wrong. She claims the same dilemma afflicts every level-splitting story on the market, including the ones people deploy to explain weakness of will.

Here is why I suspect the argument proves too much. If it worked, it would apparently also show that there is no such thing as turning a blind eye, since watching-without-seeing should be impossible by the same reasoning; that a con artist cannot exploit a mark's eagerness to be fooled, since the mark would have to know what he is buying into; and that motivated reasoning can never produce genuine false belief, only a performance of it. All of these look real. Something in the neighborhood of self-deception plainly happens: people end up believing convenient falsehoods in ways that are somehow their own doing, not merely something that happens to them.

So tell me: which premise or step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially interested in whether the dilemma she poses for the partitioning reply is as lethal as she believes, or whether there is a version of the split that slips between its horns. I would also like to know whether the analogy to weakness of will helps her or hurts her -- the Socratic puzzle about akrasia was also supposed to establish an impossibility, and we got out of that one somehow, or at least we think we did. Finally, tell me what her argument proves about the nearby cases -- the turned blind eye, the willing mark -- if it proves anything at all. I want to know whether the diagnosis I accept for self-deception will generalize to those cases or whether I will be forced to treat each one separately.

I would rather keep the claim that self-deception exists than keep any particular premise, but I want to know exactly what I am paying for it, and in which currency.
