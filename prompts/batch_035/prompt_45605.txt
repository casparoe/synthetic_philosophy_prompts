I'm a distributed-systems engineer and a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, where Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked so often, in the slogan form that rationalists cannot agree to disagree, that it has practically become local currency. My day job, meanwhile, is consensus protocols: Paxos and its descendants, Byzantine fault tolerance, the two-generals problem, the FLP impossibility result, vector clocks and causal message ordering. It recently struck me that the rationalists' notion of common knowledge and my notion of a replicated state machine that has actually reached consensus might be the same object seen from two sides, and I want to know how far that identification goes.

Restate the core results of epistemic game theory in the vocabulary I actually use — message-passing asynchrony, crash versus Byzantine fault models, quorums, eventual delivery, clock skew, the gap between a process's local view and the global state. Cover, in particular: common knowledge of rationality and its role in iterated elimination of dominated strategies; backward induction and what it needs agents to know about each other; Aumann's agreement theorem; and Rubinstein's email game, in which two agents who exchange a finite number of confirmation messages never reach common knowledge, and an arbitrarily small probability of message loss can unravel an equilibrium that common knowledge would support. Translate each as faithfully as you can into protocol-land, then give me the residue report: which of these claims map cleanly onto things my field already has theorems about, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all.

Three questions I especially want your view on. First, whether common knowledge as the epistemic logicians define it — the infinite nesting of agents knowing that agents know — is literally the same fixed point my protocols can only approximate in runs with reliable enough communication, or whether the identification is doing illegitimate work somewhere. Second, whether Aumann's theorem is best read as a claim about agents whose communication has already converged to a fixed point of message exchange, and what the protocol analogue of the common-prior assumption is. Third, whether the email game is just the two-generals problem with payoffs attached, or whether the payoff structure yields a genuinely stronger kind of collapse than my impossibility results give me.

Finally, tell me where the residue lies in both directions. Is there something in the epistemic game theory literature that distributed systems simply cannot express — and if so, is that a defect of my vocabulary, or evidence that the game theorists are trading on an idealization no realizable protocol could ever deliver? And conversely, is there something my field handles routinely — adversarial faults, equivocation, agents that lie about their local state — that the common-knowledge framework quietly assumes away by treating all knowledge as veridical and all agents as honest?
