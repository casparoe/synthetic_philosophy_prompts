I am a retired physical chemist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy of mind with an amateur's enthusiasm and, I hope, a scientist's patience for distinctions that actually do some work. Two camps have caught my attention, and I confess I cannot adjudicate between them, so I would like you to do something more constructive on my behalf.

The first camp, following Searle, holds that intentionality comes in two grades: original (or intrinsic) intentionality, possessed by conscious minds, and derived intentionality, which words, diagrams, and instruments have only because conscious agents ascribe content to them. The second camp, following Dennett, is suspicious of this distinction. Its emblem is Dennett's two-bitser: a soft-drink vending machine that accepts only American quarters, transplanted to a country whose coins are physically indistinguishable from quarters by the machine's crude detectors. Dennett uses the case to argue that the machine's "recognition" of quarters cannot be an intrinsic matter of what is inside it, and more broadly that if we trace every case of "derived" content back far enough, the original/derived contrast does no explanatory work: our own intentionality is as much a product of design history — evolution, then culture — as the vending machine's.

My instinct, from decades in a laboratory, is that the dispute will not be settled by repeating the Chinese Room or the two-bitser, so I want to force both sides onto common ground with a case of my own. Please treat the following as a fixed test case in the document you produce. An automated gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer in a pharmaceutical plant is run by software whose statistical model was retrained, on-line and without human review, to compensate for detector drift. It now reliably flags a contaminant that no chemist has ever isolated or named, and does so correctly. Its designers could not have anticipated the specifics of this classification; the training data came from the plant's own process stream; the acceptance thresholds were set by a committee of process chemists who are mostly retired. I would like to know whose intentions, if anyone's, the machine's aboutness is derived from — and whether that question is even well posed.

With that in place, please draft the joint statement that a committed defender of the original/derived distinction and a committed Dennettian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (i) a numbered section of claims both parties accept, including about what the two-bitser and my spectrometer actually show; (ii) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, being careful to mark which disagreements are substantive and which turn out to be verbal — in particular, whether "derived" is being used univocally by the two camps; (iii) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party — and, for each crux, a concrete argument, thought experiment, or empirical scenario both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that scenario recorded in advance. Both parties should be presented as equally competent: neither is to be a foil for the other, and the joint statement should read as a text either philosopher would be willing to put their name to.
