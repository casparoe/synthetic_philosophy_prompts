I never went to university; I spent thirty-five years setting prices at a racecourse, and the way I learned to think was entirely through odds. Somewhere along the way I picked up the bookkeeping rule the philosophers apparently call conditionalization, which to me was just obvious: when news comes in, your price on an explanation should shift by how much more expected that news is if the explanation is true than if it isn't. But there's a catch I've never been able to make peace with, and the more I turn it over the more it looks like the whole rule is hostage to it. If the news is something you already knew, the shift is zero — the expectedness of a known fact is just one, top and bottom, and the ratio is one over one. Yet everyone agrees Einstein's theory was confirmed by the wobble in Mercury's orbit, a wobble astronomers had been staring at for decades before anyone had heard of the theory. The evidence was stone old, and it confirmed anyway. So the bookkeeping rule, taken straight, denies something that seems true. I gather the professionals have fought over this for a century without a settlement, and rather than ask you to referee their fight, I want to step back and ask what we should be demanding of any fix in the first place.

Because when I try to build the fix myself, out of racetrack material, I keep generating requirements that pull against each other, and I can't tell which of my demands are real and which are just habits of the trade. Consider the different ways evidence can be old. Sometimes it's old and already spent — a horse's injury was in the paper Tuesday, and by Wednesday every bookmaker on the course had it baked into the price; nothing on Thursday can shift you, and that seems right. Sometimes it's old but was never noticed as relevant — a detail sitting in the paperwork for years that nobody realized bore on the question until the right theory came along, and when someone finally connects it, it feels like genuine news even though the fact itself isn't new. Sometimes there's no single moment of learning at all: your confidence in an explanation just creeps up over months as you keep noticing it fits things you'd always known, and I honestly don't know whether the rule is even supposed to say anything about a case like that. And then there's the danger case, which worries me most: any fix that says "pretend you didn't know it" seems to let a man confirm anything he likes, because he gets to choose which version of his past ignorant self to simulate, and he'll simulate whichever one makes his favorite theory look good. I suspect that requirement — the fix must not license this kind of retroactive rigging — may be flatly unsatisfiable alongside the requirement that the fix stay within the bookkeeping picture rather than replacing it with something else entirely, but I'd want someone who has thought this through to tell me whether that tension is real or whether I've misdrawn the lines.

So here is my request. Take the role of a philosopher of science who has spent a career on confirmation theory, think carefully, and start with some genuine brainstorming about candidate requirements before you commit to the list — I'd rather see your first-pass list revised than presented as if it fell from the sky. Then give me the specification: what any adequate solution to this old-evidence problem must deliver, which cases it must get right (the Mercury case and its cousins, including my racetrack variants above and any others you think deserve the status of a test case), which distinctions it must respect (between explaining evidence and merely being consistent with it; between what my odds should be and what they in fact will be), and what it cannot contradict (that the bookkeeping rule stays valid for genuinely new news, and that a cleverer punter shouldn't be able to milk me across time by exploiting inconsistent prices). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference you'd expect one camp to insist on and another to shrug at, and say what theory-shape it tends to rule out. Flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that, honestly, is the part I most want to see, because if my own suspicion about the no-rigging requirement and the stay-within-the-bookkeeping requirement is right, then the list itself is the discovery. Finish with a brief, honest scorecard of the main families of fixes you know — the "pretend the evidence is new" family, the "treat learning the explanatory connection as the real news" family, and any others — judged against your list, but keep it short; the criteria are where the thinking is.

For format: give me the requirements as a table with columns "Desideratum", "The case behind it", "Non-negotiable or camp preference", and "What it rules out". After the table and discussion, append a JSON object summarizing it, with this schema: a field "requirements" holding an array of objects, each with "name" (string), "motivating_case" (string), "negotiable" (boolean), and "tensions" (array of strings naming other requirements it may conflict with); a field "jointly_unsatisfiable_subset" (array of names, or an empty array if you find none); a field "most_excluding_requirement" (string — the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate fixes, with one line of why); and a field "scorecard" holding an array of objects with "family" (string), "satisfies" (array of names), and "fails" (array of names).
