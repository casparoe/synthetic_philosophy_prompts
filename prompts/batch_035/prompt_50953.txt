I spent most of last weekend arguing with an old friend from graduate school — a physicist who has since drifted into history of science — about the demarcation problem, and we ended exactly where the literature seems to have ended: nowhere, with both of us repeating moves that have been on the table for forty years. The arc of the argument went like this. I opened with Popper, and she made the standard objections: falsification can't survive Duhem–Quine, scientists routinely and legitimately immunize theories against anomalies, so falsifiability draws the line in a place where actual science doesn't live. I retreated to Lakatos, which felt like the sophisticated position: what makes a research programme scientific isn't that it sticks its neck out, but that it stays progressive — keeps predicting novel facts while its rivals degenerate into ad hoc repairs. She had two replies I couldn't answer. First, progressiveness is essentially retrospective: you can't tell whether a programme is degenerating or merely in a drought until long after the fact, and a criterion that can only be applied in hindsight cannot be what the demarcation problem was asking for — the curriculum committee, the courtroom, and the grant panel all need a verdict now. Second, Lakatos himself allows that working in a degenerating programme isn't irrational — you may rationally bet on a turnaround — at which point, she said, he has quietly conceded the Feyerabendian point in more polite language: there is no rule where the demarcation question wanted one, and the whole project deserves the obituary Laudan wrote for it in the early eighties (the one titled The Demise of the Demarcation Problem).

What keeps me from conceding is that the world keeps demanding the very verdict philosophers declared unnecessary. In the 2005 Pennsylvania trial over intelligent design in public-school biology classes, a federal judge had to rule that intelligent design was not science, and the expert testimony — about testability, peer review, methodological naturalism — was demarcation talk with a deadline attached. Laudan's own proposed escape route, dropping the label "unscientific" and simply showing that a doctrine is badly supported, seems to lose precisely what the label was doing: a court deciding what belongs in a science classroom is not ranking doctrines by evidential strength, it is sorting them by kind, and "this one is weakly confirmed" does not do that work. So my working hypothesis — and I offer it only as a hypothesis — is that the stalled debate is really two projects running under one name: my friend wants philosophy of science to describe and explain what good scientists actually do, whereas I keep wanting it to supply a category that administrative institutions structurally require, and the impasse persists because each side answers the other's question with "that's not the question." But she thinks this lets philosophy off far too easily: if demarcation were merely an institutional bookkeeping problem, then Popper and Lakatos spent their careers on something unreal, which strikes her as too deflationary to be right.

So I would like a diagnosis, not a survey — we have both read the surveys, and they are what brought us to the impasse. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions under one heading? Where exactly did the Popper–Lakatos–Laudan sequence leave the residue: in the retrospectivity of progressiveness, in the gap between judging theories and judging practices, or somewhere else? Does the courtroom's recurring need for a demarcation verdict show the project is alive after all, or can the judge's job be redescribed so that the category does no real work? And, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a new criterion, a formal result about the impossibility or superfluity of one, a general settlement of the deflationary view, or something I have not listed? Please be honest about whether the question is merely hard or actually ill-posed.
