The proposal to formalize the Anthropocene as a geological epoch has, if anything, moved backwards: after roughly fifteen years of work by the Anthropocene Working Group, which settled on a mid-twentieth-century start date marked by radionuclides and other industrial signals in the sediments of Crawford Lake, the relevant subcommission voted the proposal down in 2024 by a wide margin, and the International Union of Geological Sciences confirmed the rejection — while remarking, in its statement, that the term would remain in use as an "invaluable descriptor of human impact on the Earth system." Meanwhile the wider argument about when the epoch began, if it exists, has been running since Crutzen popularized the term: candidates include the Neolithic Revolution and the spread of agriculture, an apparent dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 following the death of most of the Americas' indigenous population, the industrial turn around 1800, and the mid-century Great Acceleration. And alongside the dating argument there is a second fight about naming and responsibility — those who prefer Capitalocene or Plantationocene want the period to indict a particular economic arrangement rather than humanity in the abstract.

I want a diagnosis of this stalled condition rather than a vote on a start date. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is not fundamentally about the stratigraphic evidence at all: the objects in the lake and the ice cores are what they are, and both sides read the same curves. What the stratigraphers' refusal reflects, I suspect, is a requirement of their discipline — that an epoch boundary needs a boundary that can be recognized at the same place in the same everywhere — running up against a concept whose point has never been chronostratigraphic: Earth system scientists, historians, and critical theorists who use "Anthropocene" are trying to periodize a process (an accelerating, diachronous transformation of the Earth by certain human activities), not to subdivide rock. On that reading, the dispute is partly verbal, but also partly about disciplinary jurisdiction — about who gets to say when geological time changes — and partly substantive, since the dating options really do encode different stories about what the human impact on Earth consists in.

But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and there are facts that cut against it: the stratigraphers' complaint about the Crawford Lake proposal was not only about synchronicity but about whether a seventy-year-old sliver of sediment can bear the weight of an epoch at all, which suggests a more ordinary methodological disagreement about what counts as evidence of a durable boundary; and the Capitalocene camp is surely not merely using a homonym. So tell me: is this a substantive empirical disagreement that better stratigraphy could settle, a verbal dispute about what a "period" is, two communities answering different questions under one name, or a jurisdictional and political fight about who narrates planetary history, with the rocks as a proxy battlefield? Support the diagnosis with what the parties to each strand of the dispute actually say and do, not with a survey of the start dates. And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a better boundary marker, an agreed division of labor between formal chronostratigraphy and informal periodization, a decision to hand the concept to historians and Earth system scientists altogether, or nothing — and, if nothing, why the coexistence of an officially rejected epoch and a scientifically indispensable term is a stable resting point rather than a failure.
