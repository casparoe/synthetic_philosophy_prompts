i teach a decision theory seminar, about 10 students, 75 minutes, one room. Design an in-class game based on the Death in Damascus setup where students feel for themselves why no plan stays stable: the moment they settle on fleeing to Aleppo, staying in Damascus starts looking better, and vice versa, so their deliberation just chases its tail and never lands. Build it over multiple rounds where "Death" (me, or a designated student) announces in advance where the meeting will happen, and has a track record of being right in earlier rounds, so students get the feel of a predictor they can't outguess. Payoffs should be cheap — points, candy, bragging rights — no real money. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal structure: the two conditional utilities, how each candidate plan undermines itself once you're about to commit to it, and how a ratificationist move (only ever deciding what to do *now*, in your current state) would break the loop. Be honest with me about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first run — my bet is someone just flips a coin and declares they've dissolved the puzzle, figure out how to handle that — and which part of the paradox a classroom version can't reproduce at all, given that the "predictor" is really just me rigging where Death shows up.
