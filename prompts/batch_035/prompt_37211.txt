I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about when a risk-scoring model treats two demographic groups fairly, and I'd like to know whether they add up to a coherent principle. Consider a lender deciding loan approvals, where the two groups genuinely differ in their default rates. My verdicts: (1) a model that approves both groups at the same rate even though their repayment rates differ: unfair, since it's denying safe borrowers in the safer group to satisfy a quota; (2) a model that, among people who would in fact repay, denies group B noticeably more often than group A: unfair, since the innocent members of one group are bearing the model's caution; (3) a model that is well calibrated for each group separately, so that of everyone labeled "20% chance of default," roughly a fifth actually default in both groups: fair; (4) a model that ignores group membership entirely and ends up denying group B more often simply because their average risk is genuinely higher: fair; (5) a model whose "high risk" label is right only half the time for group A but nearly always right for group B: unfair. My tentative principle is that fairness means the model's outputs track actual risk, with group membership set aside. Does that principle — or any single principle — deliver all five verdicts? If so, what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on, namely whether the lender may deliberately use group membership as an input when doing so improves calibration for each group? If no principle fits, I'd like to know which of my verdicts I should surrender, and why.
