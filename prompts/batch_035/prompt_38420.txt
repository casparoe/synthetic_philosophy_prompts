I spent thirty-one years as a physicist at a national laboratory, most of them on warhead surety and verification regimes, and retired two years ago into what my wife calls my second career: sitting in on graduate seminars in philosophy. Next month I present my first proper philosophy paper, at a university workshop on ethics and international security, and I want a preparation sheet for the question period.

My thesis, briefly: the standard moral assessment of nuclear deterrence — the just-war-style question of whether the threat is proportionate, discriminate, and directed at legitimate targets — targets the wrong object. Deterrence as actually practiced does not rest on a conditional intention to retaliate, which is what generates the old paradox that a rational actor cannot credibly threaten what it would be irrational to carry out. It rests on what Schelling described as leaving something to chance: deliberately maintaining a contested, only partially controlled escalation process whose expected value is stabilizing precisely because a small probability of catastrophe is priced into it. If that is right, the moral load-bearing element of deterrence is the retained probability of accident and miscalculation — not the intention, not the declared doctrine. And an institution that is stable only because it is somewhat out of control cannot be assessed with the categories we use for actions under an agent's control: it is closer to a managed hazard, like a reactor with an engineered failure rate, than to a threat in the ordinary sense. I want to argue that this reframing both rescues deterrence from the credibility paradox and exposes a moral problem the just-war framework cannot express — the substitution of luck for intention — so that the familiar debate about retaliatory intentions is, in a sense, about the least important feature of the system.

The audience: roughly two-thirds philosophers — several just-war theorists, one fairly orthodox Thomist who will want intentions to do real work, a consequentialist who works on risk imposition and will see a familiar structure in my reactor analogy — plus two political scientists who do formal crisis bargaining, and at least one participant who takes Schelling to have dissolved the credibility paradox rather than relocated it into the machinery of brinkmanship. They will not need deterrence explained to them. What they will need, and what I am least sure I can provide, is for the managed-hazard framing to be made precise rather than metaphorical, and I suspect the formal people will press exactly there.

So prepare me accordingly: the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased in that audience's vocabulary, with a judgment about which ones can actually sink the paper and which are noise. For each, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and an honest note on where the reply is soft — which concessions I should make up front to buy credibility for the parts I intend to hold. Include the objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises. And since I would rather discover my errors before the workshop than during it, tell me plainly what would change my mind: which single argument, if pressed successfully, would show that the managed-hazard framing is a metaphor rather than an analysis, and where, in your judgment, my reasoning is most likely to fail — the point at which a physicist's habit of treating institutional probabilities like engineered failure rates stops doing honest work.
