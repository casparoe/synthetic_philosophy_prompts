I'm doing a philosophy of mind course this term and we've just got to pain, specifically the weird dissociation cases, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about whether pain is bad for you and it's a mess. My tutor says to trust my gut on cases first and reconstruct the principle after, so here are my gut verdicts. The classic pain asymbolia patient — the ones who report that pinpricks and burns hurt in the ordinary sensory way but who genuinely don't mind, who'll joke around while a doctor is sticking pins in their hand — I don't think their pain is bad for them, it's like a fire alarm going off with no fire. Ordinary pain, like a pinprick or a stubbed toe with no damage done, is bad, even though it's tiny. Someone on high-dose morphine who says "it still hurts but I don't care about it" — same verdict as the asymbolia patient, the pain isn't bad for them anymore. Meanwhile grief or dread or humiliation is bad even though none of it is pain, which makes me think it's the minding that matters, not the hurting.

But then two cases where I go the other way. First, a depressed patient with chronic pain who's become so flattened that they say they don't care about the pain, don't care about anything — I still think their pain is bad for them, which seems to contradict my morphine verdict even though the report is the same. Second, the burn from hard exercise or eating very spicy food: hurts, I mind it while it's happening, and it still doesn't seem bad at all, maybe even good, which no version of "pain plus minding equals bad" seems to handle. And lurking behind everything: if you tortured someone but first gave them a drug that removed the aversive component so they reported the torture the way an asymbolia patient reports pins, I feel like something would still be wrong with doing it, though I can't tell if that's because the pain is bad for them or because of something else entirely.

So: is there a single principle that captures all of these, something like "the badness of pain lives in one component of it rather than the whole experience"? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts, and if so which one is most likely the sentimental stray? I'd also like to know what your best principle says about some cases I haven't got intuitions on — whether fish nociception would count as bad on it, and what it implies about a foetus or a patient under nitrous who says painful things happened but seems unfussed. And the one I keep looping on: if the pain-without-minding verdict is right, would it be fine to give someone a guaranteed harmless but intensely painful experience, say in a medical procedure they consented to, with nothing to numb the minding? Something feels off about saying yes, and I can't work out whether the feeling is a real problem for the principle or just leftover prejudice.
