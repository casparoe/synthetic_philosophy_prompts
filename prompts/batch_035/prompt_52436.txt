I teach a seminar on rationality for philosophy students (15 students, one 90-minute session), and I want a game that makes them feel the difference between maximizing and satisficing rather than just hear about it. Design a card-drawing search task: participants draw "options" one at a time from a shuffled deck, each draw costs them points, and they must eventually commit to one option. Assign different stopping instructions — one group told to find the best option in the deck, another asked to write down a private "good enough" threshold before drawing and to stop the moment a card clears it. Decide for me whether passed-over cards can be recalled, and specify the deck size, the distribution of card values, the search cost, and a payoff formula in points rather than money. Run it in two or three rounds, including one where the deck is stingy and the fixed threshold backfires, so students discover that whether satisficing wins depends on the environment, not just the rule. In the debrief, map specific moments from the game onto Herbert Simon's picture of bounded rationality as a scissors with two blades — the limits of the organism and the structure of the environment — and then pose the awkward question the game itself provokes: if the instructor chose the threshold that let the satisficers win, is satisficing just maximizing outsourced to someone else, and what actually sets aspiration levels outside the classroom? Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about the feature the game cannot reproduce — that real aspiration levels adapt with experience — so I know what I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
