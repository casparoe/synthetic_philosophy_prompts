You are a professional philosopher working on AI ethics and the philosophy of agency, and I would like your help auditing an analogy that keeps resurfacing in my reading group.

The context is a familiar framing of the "alignment problem": what is technically urgent and well-defined is intent alignment — getting an AI system to reliably pursue the intentions, including the informed and reflective intentions, of its user or principal — as opposed to value alignment, which would require the system to track what is genuinely good or right, independent of what anyone happens to want. One member of the group defends the narrow framing with an analogy. An aligned AI assistant, she says, would be like an elite executive assistant or butler: the butler anticipates the principal's needs, fills in what was left unsaid, gently corrects for the principal's fatigue and lapses of memory, and is utterly loyal — yet nobody demands that the butler share the principal's deepest values or possess systematic moral wisdom of his own. Enough situational judgment to serve well, and no more. So, she concludes, we should stop demanding that AI systems be moral agents and simply aim for superhuman butlers; intent alignment is not a pragmatic stopgap but a coherent ideal, and the demand for value alignment rests on requiring more of our tools than we have ever required of human staff.

Please think carefully and take your time before answering; begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list, without yet evaluating them, the features of the source domain (the human butler and his professional role) that are plausibly doing the work in this argument. Then present your main analysis in four parts:

1. A correspondence table: what plays the role of the butler, the principal, the household or organization, the butler's professional norms and training, the butler's own judgment, and the wider society in which the employment relationship is embedded. Fill any empty cells explicitly rather than glossing over them.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the intended conclusion and those that don't. Candidates worth weighing include: the butler is embedded in a society of moral and legal peers, so third parties are protected by institutions — law, reputation, enforcement — that do not yet exist for AI systems acting on a principal's behalf; the butler can resign, be fired, or refuse, which changes the power structure of the relationship; the butler is not so vastly more capable than the principal that the principal's oversight becomes nominal; the butler is himself a moral patient whose treatment gives others independent reasons for concern; and the stakes of butler-scale errors are bounded in a way that superhuman-assistant errors are not. You needn't accept these framings; audit them.

3. What the analogy quietly imports about the relation between intent and value. In particular: the good butler's skill of anticipating what the principal would endorse upon reflection — as opposed to merely obeying instructions, the way a literal-minded genie would — may already be saturated with substantive judgments about welfare, autonomy, and harm that the principal does not himself possess. If so, does the analogy smuggle a thin theory of the good into what presents itself as a value-neutral ideal? Does it also conflate intent alignment with corrigibility, or are those genuinely distinct in a way the analogy obscures?

4. A verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the butler argument survive, or does the narrow conclusion depend on a background of shared values and moral infrastructure that the analogy assumes rather than establishes? If the analogy fails, propose a better one — or argue that no comparison of this shape can settle the question and say what kind of argument would be needed instead.

Aim for genuine nuance rather than demolition or defense: the interesting question is precisely how much of the pro-intent-alignment conclusion this analogy can carry, and where the load path gives out.
