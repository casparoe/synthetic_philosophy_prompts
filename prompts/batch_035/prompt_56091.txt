I sit on the grant-renewal panel of a mid-sized private foundation that funds theoretical research. Our bylaws commit us to a decision this spring on a fixed five-year block: the money goes entirely to one of two rival research programmes in a field I will describe in simplified form, since the panel's dilemma is philosophical rather than technical.

The older programme — call it the Attractor programme — has run for about twenty-five years. It began with a compact hard core, a set of laws governing how its central quantities evolve, wrapped in a flexible protective belt of modeling assumptions. Early on it produced a striking novel prediction, a scaling relation between two measurable parameters, whose confirmation made its reputation. The last dozen years look different: four separate anomalies have each been absorbed by adding a free parameter or restricting the theory's intended domain, and its one recent novel prediction came out wrong and was rescued only by redefining what counted as a fair test. Its practitioners are the field's best trained, its mathematics the field's most admired, and its defenders add that no rival explains the original scaling law as elegantly.

The younger programme — the Sparse programme — is about a decade old. Its hard core is thinner and its positive heuristic partly improvised; its models often look stitched together. But in the past three years it has made two genuinely novel predictions, with stakes declared in advance and no adjustable parameters in play, of which one was clearly confirmed and one partially. It has also attracted most of the field's recent graduate students.

A minority wants to renew the Attractor programme anyway, on the ground that theoretical depth and the training of students count for more than a thin recent record; a minority wants to fund the Sparse programme and let the Attractor programme wind down; and a faction proposes splitting the block, arguing that judging a whole tradition by a few years of predictions is exactly the kind of instant verdict science should resist.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before our June meeting, of roughly 1,200 words. It should:

1. State a clear bottom line — renew the Attractor programme, fund the Sparse programme, adopt the split, or some alternative you can defend — together with the reasoning.
2. Spell out, in terms the panel can actually apply, operational criteria for what counts as a novel prediction and for distinguishing a progressive from a degenerating problem shift, so that our decision does not merely relabel our prior preferences.
3. Take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than restating our bottom line.
4. Be candid about wherever the Lakatosian apparatus underdetermines our choice — for instance, whether a methodology that appraises long historical series, permits rational persistence in a degenerating programme, and forbids instant verdicts can license a forward-looking funding decision at all — and say what the panel should do at exactly those points where the framework gives out.

Please write the memo in American English, and cite nothing: the panel wants the reasoning worked out on the page, not a bibliography.
