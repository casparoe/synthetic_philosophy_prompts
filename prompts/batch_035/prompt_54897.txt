I'm taking a seminar built around Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and we've just reached his question about where the meaning of a message actually resides. As I understand his framework, any message comes in layers: a frame message that just signals that it is a message and worth trying to decode; an outer message carrying the decoding information, which you have to grasp somehow before any content is available to you; and the inner message, the payload itself. Hofstadter's punchline, if I have it right, is that the outer layer can only be decoded by drawing on knowledge you already possess, so that no message is fully self-explanatory, and whether meaning resides in the artifact or in the interpreter is a matter of degree. Our professor challenged us to point at real engineering decisions and ask what theory of meaning they quietly commit you to, and I've become mildly obsessed with the ones humanity has actually made for talking to extraterrestrials.

Consider the Voyager Golden Record, launched in 1977: a phonograph record curated by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan, carrying 116 images, greetings in 55 languages, and a program of music, with an etched aluminum cover offering what its designers hoped were self-teaching decoding instructions — a drawing keyed to the hyperfine transition of hydrogen to fix a unit of time, a pulsar map locating the sun among pulsars whose rotation periods are written in binary, and instructions for playing the record at all. The reliance on physical constants as the calibration layer reflects a bet that any intelligence capable of retrieving the record will share our physics; the scales in the images are likewise defined by constants that hold everywhere in the universe. Meanwhile, radio SETI runs on a complementary bet: listen for narrow-band signals, on the reasoning that a sharply confined transmission frequency stands out against naturally noisy astrophysical backgrounds and so functions as a frame message — a marker of artifice detectable before any content is. There is a nice historical wrinkle here, too: the first pulsar was discovered in 1967, and its pulses were so uncannily regular that the source was half-jokingly labeled with a name implying little green men; once it turned out to be natural, that very kind of regularity got absorbed into the background, so much so that pulsars now serve as the neutral landmarks on the Voyager map. So the same sort of signal was first read as a sign of intelligence, then reclassified as noise, then conscripted as a decoding key.

My question is about the implicit theory. What view of the location of meaning does this whole practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that outer messages can in principle be universal — that mathematics and physics constitute a decoding key available to any sufficiently intelligent being, whatever its evolutionary history — or only a wager that any technology-building species will share enough curiosity and pattern-seeking to treat a found anomaly as a sign demanding interpretation? Can those two readings be separated, in practice or in principle? And if one held instead that meaning is conventional all the way down — that there is no fact of the matter making one decoding of the record correct rather than another — would the design of such messages have to change, and would the narrow-band search still be coherent, given that it presupposes the natural/artificial line is detectable from the receiver's side? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
