I write for a mid-size science-and-ideas magazine — the kind of outlet where a piece can be half psychology, half philosophy and nobody complains. I'm working on something about why we *seek out* sad stuff: the divorce playlists, rewatching that one devastating film for the tenth time, sobbing at a novel on a train. The classic puzzle, going back to Hume's short essay on tragedy, is that if sadness is unpleasant, paying money for it seems irrational. Hume's own answer was basically that the artistry somehow converts the unpleasant feeling into pleasure, which always struck me as hand-wavy — a label where an explanation should be.

My editor liked the piece better when I pitched my own theory, which I'm not sure survives contact with daylight. Here's the idea: the pleasure of sad art isn't in the sadness at all — it's in the discovery that the sadness *stops on cue*. The credits roll, you close the book, you blow your nose, and you're fine. So every good cry at a movie is a little piece of evidence that you're the kind of creature that can be shattered and then recover. Tragedy is less a feast of sorrow than a rehearsal, run at reduced stakes, for losses you haven't had yet. In my notes I call it the "off-switch theory," which is probaly too cute.

Her two main objections: (1) it's circular. The theory explains the pleasure by appealing to a satisfaction I've already smuggled into the setup — of course discovering that you recover feels good, if you just stipulate that this discovery is satisfying. (2) People who are actually grieving often reach for the sad songs *harder*, and they're clearly not practicing at reduced stakes — they're in it. That fits awkwardly with my framing.

What I want from you: your honest overall verdict in the very first sentence — is this theory load-bearing or just cute? — and then your reasoning, no preamble. Specifically I'd like you to address: whether the circularity objection actually sticks, and whether there's a version of the theory that dodges it; whether the grieving counterexample breaks it or could maybe be turned around into support; whether there's some obvious literature a smart reader will expect me to at least nod at (catharsis, benign masochism, whatever) that I'm not seeing; and whether the whole angle supports 3000 words or is really a paragraph's worth of idea stretched. Don't be nice about it — I'd rather find out now than in the comments section.
