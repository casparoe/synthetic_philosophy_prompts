I'm marking take-home exams for a third-year philosophy of mind course and I'd like a second opinion on one of the answers before I finalise the grade. The question we set was:

"Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal states are caused by physical states but have no causal influence on anything physical. Explain why this creates a puzzle about our beliefs and verbal reports concerning our own conscious experiences (the paradox of phenomenal judgment), and assess how well the epiphenomenalist can respond."

Here's the student's answer, copied as submitted:

"Epiphenomenalism says the physical world is causally closed: brain states cause phenomenal states, like the redness of seeing a ripe tomato, but the phenomenal states are like smoke trailing behind an engine - they don't push anything back down into the physical world. Frank Jackson coined the phrase 'paradox of phenomenal judgment' for the problem this raises, and his knowledge argument already established the causal inertness part. Mary, trapped in her black-and-white room, knows all the physical facts but learns something new when she sees red, which shows the red-quale has no causal powers.

The paradox is that I sincerely say 'I am seeing red' and this utterance is a physical event, involving my vocal cords or my typing fingers. If qualia are causally inert, the red-quale itself cannot be what caused that utterance. Instead it was caused by physical processes in my cortex. But then my belief that I'm seeing red is caused by something other than what the belief is about. So the belief is only true by coincidence, and the epiphenomenalist is committed to saying that most of our phenomenal beliefs are false, since the things they are about never touch the things that produce them.

The standard reply is the common-cause strategy. The same underlying physical state causes both the quale and the report about the quale, rather like how a disease causes both a rash and a fever, where the rash doesn't cause the fever. Since there is a lawful correlation between physical states and qualia, the physical cause of my belief reliably tracks the quale, so the belief can still be true. In my view this reply dissolves the paradox completely: once the correlation is explained by the common cause, there is nothing left to be puzzled about, and the epiphenomenalist's position is in as good shape as anyone else's. Our introspective beliefs are caused by brain states, and brain states are reliably correlated with qualia, so we get truth the same way perception gives us truth about tables and chairs."
