Retired physicist here (soft matter, mostly colloids, 34 years), got into philosophy about two years after I stopped going to the lab and it's been a riot. Lately I'm stuck on the St. Petersburg paradox -- the coin flip game where you keep flipping until it lands heads and the payoff doubles each time, so the expected value is 1/2 + 1/4*2 + ... = infinite, and yet nobody sane would pay even $100 to play. I've now read maybe a dozen "solutions" (diminishing marginal utility, finite bankrolls, risk aversion, truncation...) and my problem is I don't actually know what would COUNT as solving it. In physics you'd never debate an interpretation without first agreeing what the data says it has to reproduce. So before I read any more, I want the spec.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each one. To get you started, here's my own rough list -- extend, correct, and organize it:

1. It has to say what the rational ticket price is, or explain why there isn't one -- not just that "infinity is weird." A number or a principled refusal to give a number, either is fine, hand-waving isn't.

2. It has to survive what happens when you press on it. If the answer is "marginal utility of money is bounded," fine, but then someone constructs the Petrograd game where the prizes are adjusted in utils so the expectation in YOUR OWN utility function still diverges. Whatever the resolution is, it can't be something I can fix up the game to break again. A resolution that dies to adversarial re-parameterization is not a resolution.

3. It has to handle my pet worry, which I've never seen treated properly: the whole game assumes an unbounded sequence of coin flips with unbounded payoffs, but in the actual universe, payoffs are physically bounded (there isn't enough matter to pay 2^100 dollars, and entropy puts a floor on how small a probability a physically instantiable event can have). Does that finiteness dissolve the paradox or just relocate it? A resolution should tell me whether "the game as described can't be physically instantiated" is doing real work or is an accountant's cop-out, and why.

4. It has to deal with the aggregation point: if you play many independent rounds, the average payout grows without bound, just slowly and erratically. So "I'd never pay much for one round" coexists with "I'd pay a lot for a million rounds" without obvious irrationality. A resolution has to say what happens across many plays, not just one.

5. It can't just be a general distrust of infinite expectations, unless you're prepared to bite the bullet on cases where we're fine using unbounded expectations in science (I used heavy-tailed distributions my whole career without anyone calling me irrational).

Also mark which of the desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just what particular camps happen to care about (decision theorists vs. statisticians vs. people like me coming from physical limits), and tell me honestly if you think some subset can't be satisfied together -- if the spec itself is the theorem, I want to know.

Two asks on how you write it up: tell me how confident you are in each requirement actually being a requirement (some of mine are probably just physicist prejudices), and single out the one point in the whole answer you're least sure of -- don't bury it.
