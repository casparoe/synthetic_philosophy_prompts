My mother worked as an ICU nurse for three decades, and after watching her own father's final years she signed a detailed advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration should she ever develop advanced dementia. She reaffirmed it verbally once, about six years ago, when she was already showing the first signs of forgetfulness, which is itself something my sister and I argue about, since I'm not sure a reaffirmation made by someone in the early stages counts as fully hers. She has moderate Alzheimer's now; she rarely recognizes us, though she hums along to music and takes pleasure in her meals in a placid, unreflective way. She has begun aspirating, and her physicians say that within weeks we must either accept a feeding tube or transition to careful hand-feeding. I am her healthcare proxy, so the decision formally comes to me.

My sister thinks the directive should be overridden. Her argument is roughly that our mother wrote it while imagining a nightmare of suffering and indignity, but the person actually living this life is content; the directive's premise failed, and enforcing it means letting the preferences of someone who no longer exist prevail over a patient who never expressed any objection to being fed. When I try to say why I still feel bound, the best I can manage is that she took the trouble to make her wishes unambiguous precisely for this situation, and that if I picture her lucid, watching the scene from the doorway, I think she would be horrified by what my sister proposes. My sister replies that this picture is just me substituting a ghost of the former person for the one actually sitting in the chair, and I can't quite answer that. We have also combed the diary she kept for years, looking for guidance: nothing in it softens the directive, but nothing in it anticipates that dementia might be gentle rather than terrible either, so the document seems to rest on a prediction that came out half wrong, and I don't know whether that matters. A further wrinkle is that her doctors point out she is not otherwise dying; refusing the tube would make the aspiration and its complications the thing that kills her, which feels different to me than honoring a refusal of treatment for a terminal illness, though I can't say why it should.

What I want from you is help thinking the decision through rather than a simple verdict: where my sister's argument is strong, where it goes wrong, and what the strongest version of my own position actually is, stated better than I've managed to state it. I'd also like your view on how much weight the reaffirmation made during early cognitive decline should carry, and whether her failure to revisit or soften the document during her remaining lucid years counts as evidence that it still expressed her, or is just inertia that we shouldn't read meaning into. Throughout, I'd ask you to keep separate what is fairly settled among people who think carefully about these situations from what remains genuinely contested, and not to present one side's position as if it were the consensus; I'd rather know where the ground is uncertain. And please write in plain continuous prose, no headings, lists, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter, since I find I follow arguments better that way.
