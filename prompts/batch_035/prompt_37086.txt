I teach an upper-level seminar on collective choice and mechanism design — 14 students, comfortable with preference profiles and the vocabulary of social choice functions, but we have not yet touched any impossibility results. Next week I want to open the unit on strategy-proofness with an 80-minute activity, and I want to run it before the students ever hear the name Gibbard–Satterthwaite or see its statement. The idea is that they spend the session designing voting rules under a fixed list of constraints, and the constraints should be chosen so that every rule they can come up with fails — either someone's report of their preferences turns out to matter not at all, or someone gains by lying. I'd like the failure to be something the other students find and demonstrate, not something I announce: each design should be attacked by a rival group constructing an electorate in which some voter, under that rule, does strictly better by submitting a ranking that isn't their sincere one, or in which the rule silently reduces to one person deciding.

Concretely, design the session for me: group sizes, the exact constraint list I should post on the board (I'm imagining something like: at least three alternatives; the rule must be able to elect any of them for some profile of sincere rankings; no single voter's submitted ranking may always determine the outcome; and no voter should ever do strictly better by misreporting — but sharpen or reword these as you see fit), the format for submitting a rule, how the attacking groups get incentivized to find the manipulation (some scoring scheme that doesn't use real money — perhaps attacker points for a demonstrated manipulation, designer points for surviving a round, with a published scoreboard), and a timeline that fits 80 minutes including the debrief. I expect some groups will drift toward plurality or Borda and get buried by tactical voting, and that at least one group will try to smuggle in randomization or a tie-breaking "chair" and argue about whether that counts — build in how I should referee those two disputes, since both are actually load-bearing for the debrief.

Then the debrief: map each failed design onto the parts of the theorem — which constraint was violated and how, which manipulations the students found correspond to the familiar pathologies (compromising, burying), and where the "no single voter's ranking may always determine the outcome" constraint quietly became a dictatorship the moment they added a tie-breaker. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run — groups that spend the whole time relitigating the constraint list, attacks that fail because the attacker used a profile where the manipulation isn't strict, designs that are too vague to attack at all — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about the limit of the exercise: the students will have seen that every rule they proposed fails, but no finite session can show that every possible rule fails, so somewhere I have to stop refereering examples and supply the actual theorem, with at least a sketch of why the impossibility is general rather than a summary of their failures. Tell me where in the 80 minutes that handoff should happen and what I should say at that moment, and flag anything about the theorem the activity structurally cannot reproduce — e.g. that it concerns social choice functions defined on all profiles, not the handful of electorates the students will ever write down.
