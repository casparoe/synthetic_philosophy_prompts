The question (25 marks):

Raphael Lemkin coined the term "genocide" and drove the campaign that produced the 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide.
(a) On Lemkin's own conception, what is destroyed in an act of genocide, and how can a group be destroyed without killing its members?
(b) How does Article II of the Convention both reflect and depart from Lemkin's conception? Identify at least two features of the final text that were political compromises rather than clean applications of his idea.
(c) Assess one philosophical objection to treating "genocide" as a distinctive moral category at all, rather than a legal artifact or a subcase of crimes against humanity.

The student's answer:

Lemkin was a Polish-Jewish international lawyer, most of whose family was murdered in the Holocaust, and he had been trying to get an international crime against the destruction of peoples on the books since the 1930s. He coined the word in his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, combining the Greek genos with the Latin for killing, and then lobbied more or less single-handedly for the Convention, adopted in December 1948.

(a) For Lemkin the object of genocide is not primarily individual lives but a people as such. What gets destroyed is the group's pattern of life — its language, institutions, culture, religion, economic existence. So a group can be destroyed without mass killing: you can dismantle its institutions, suppress its language, destroy its livelihood, or impose another group's identity on it. He described genocide as having two phases: destroying the national pattern of the victim group and imposing the national pattern of the oppressor.

(b) Article II defines the crime as "any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such," so the protected groups are national, ethnic, racial, religious, and political ones. The intent requirement reflects Lemkin: it has to be aimed at the group as a group, and the mens rea is the perpetrator's motive — you must be motivated by hatred of the group's identity, which is what distinguishes genocide from ordinary murder at scale. The act list also reflects him, since it includes non-lethal acts like imposing measures to prevent births and forcibly transferring children, and the crime applies in peacetime as well as war. As for compromises, cultural genocide was in early drafts and got dropped to make ratification easier for states worried about forced assimilation policies. Also, "in whole or in part" is satisfied by any part — strictly, killing even a handful of members in one village counts as destroying the group "in part."

(c) The obvious objection is that the category is morally redundant. Because the final text was the product of political horse-trading — groups added and removed, cultural genocide dropped to please states — the category has no moral content of its own. Calling an atrocity "genocide" rather than "mass killing" is a rhetorical choice; it does not change the moral facts, which depend on the acts and the victims, not the legal box. And since every mass atrocity targets some group or other, genocide just collapses into crimes against humanity with a bigger label. So Lemkin's category adds legal utility but no distinctive wrong beyond what mass murder and crimes against humanity already cover.
