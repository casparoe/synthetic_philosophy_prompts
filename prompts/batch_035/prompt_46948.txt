I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on philosophy of language, and our next unit is Moore's paradox: sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it," where each conjunct is perfectly consistent, the conjunction itself is logically consistent too, and yet asserting it in the first person, in the present tense, sounds somehow defective. For a change of pace from the primary literature, I want to hand the students a satirical piece that they can dissect alongside the serious readings.

Write a parody of a DSM-style diagnostic manual entry for a fictitious disorder — call it Moorean Assertory Disorder — whose sole presenting symptom is the sincere first-person, present-tense assertion of Moorean sentences. Use the full diagnostic-manual apparatus: a codified name and number, diagnostic criteria (lettered and numbered, with subcriteria), specifiers, a Prevalence and Course section, a Differential Diagnosis section, Comorbidity, and a Treatment section. About 600–750 words, deadpan throughout; the parody should never wink, and the manual must treat the condition as though it were entirely ordinary psychopathology.

The humor has to come from the actual philosophy, and to that end the entry should quietly respect the following facts, exaggerating each into a symptom or a clinical puzzle rather than inventing anything:

1. The disorder cannot be a contradiction. "It's raining, but I don't believe it" is consistent — so the criteria must characterize the defect some other way, and the Differential Diagnosis section should explicitly rule out "Simple Contradiction (F03.1)" on the grounds that the patient's utterance could all be true.
2. Third-person and past-tense variants are unremarkable. "It's raining, but he doesn't believe it" is a ordinary report, and "It was raining, but I didn't believe it" is ordinary self-description — so the criteria need a specifier that somehow restricts the diagnosis to first-person present assertions, and the epidemiology section can marvel that the condition is somehow sensitive to tense and grammatical person, without ever saying why.
3. There are two presentation types: "p, but I don't believe p" and "p, but I believe not-p." The manual can list these as separate specifiers and note that they respond identically to treatment.
4. The only effective intervention in the Treatment section should be getting the patient to assert only what they believe — the norm of assertion doing, under a clinical name, all the work the manual attributes to therapy. Pharmacotherapy should be reported as ineffective at making the utterances sound better, though it may stop the patient from saying them.

The satire's target is the genre of medicalization itself — the manual's relentless confidence that anything deviant can be scored, staged, and treated — and it should be fair to that genre's real structure (its criteria, specifiers, differential diagnoses) while never stating the philosophical point. A student who knows nothing about Moore's paradox should still find it funny; a student who knows the paradox should find every joke load-bearing.

- "fidelity_check": an assessment of whether any joke risks misrepresenting either actual diagnostic manuals or the philosophy of Moore's paradox, and what the entry does to stay fair to both.
