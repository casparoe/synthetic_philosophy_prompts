I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I spend a lot of time arguing about fairness metrics for ML models at work, and someone told me our debates about what should be equal across groups are reinventing a decades-old fight in political philosophy. When I asked what fight, a colleague who did a philosophy degree summarized what she called the "equality of what?" debate for me over lunch, roughly like this: "The question was posed by Amartya Sen. There are three main answers. Welfare egalitarians think justice requires equalizing happiness or preference-satisfaction, and Rawls was essentially one of these, since the difference principle maximizes the position of the worst off and what ultimately matters to people is how well their lives go. But welfare egalitarianism is refuted by the expensive tastes problem: if I genuinely need fine wine to be happy and you're content with water, equality of welfare makes me entitled to more resources than you, and no one accepts that. Resource egalitarians like Dworkin instead say we should equalize resources, and Dworkin's imaginary auction shows what that means: everyone gets an equal budget and bids until no one envies anyone else's bundle. But Dworkin denied that people with handicaps or deficits of inborn talent deserve compensation, because compensating for those would just be welfare equality creeping back in. Sen's own answer is capabilities, meaning the basic functionings people actually achieve, like being nourished, literate, or healthy -- and this is what the Human Development Index measures. So the upshot is that both happiness and income are the wrong targets, and policy should aim at capability equality directly."

She said all this very confidently, and some of it sounded right, but a couple of things nag at me. I have a vague memory that Rawls is standardly classed with the resources people, not the welfare people, so her first historical claim may be off; and it seems odd that Dworkin would refuse compensation for handicaps, since that's the most sympathetic case for resource redistribution there is. But I don't know enough to say where else she went wrong, if she did. Please go claim by claim: which parts of her summary a careful defender of each of the three views would accept as a fair gloss, which parts conflate things the debaters deliberately keep apart (I'm told capabilities and functionings are not the same thing, for instance, and that Dworkin's position on internal deficits is more complicated than "no compensation"), and which parts overstate a contested objection into a settled refutation. Finally, tell me whether her practical upshot -- drop income and happiness as targets, aim at capabilities directly -- actually follows from the parts of her summary that survive scrutiny, or whether it outruns them.

Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence (e.g., roughly how much of her summary survives), then give the reasoning claim by claim, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Along the way, flag which of your own corrections you're least confident about and what would change your reading -- for example, whether there's a defensible interpretation under which Rawls really is a welfare egalitarian in substance, or under which Dworkin's auction story does imply what she said about handicaps and talent.
