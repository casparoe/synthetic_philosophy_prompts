I'm a second-year psychology student, and my research-methods course has just finished a unit on the replication crisis: the large-scale replication attempts in which a substantial share of published findings in psychology (and, it increasingly seems, in other fields) failed to reproduce. My lecturer presented the whole affair as essentially a failure of significance testing — p-values hinge on arbitrary thresholds, invite p-hacking, and let researchers dress up noise as discovery. That framing made intuitive sense to me, and it hardened into a view after a seminar discussion: since a p-value only tells you the probability that your results were due to chance, whereas Bayesian methods give you the actual probability that your hypothesis is true given the data, it seems obvious that the crisis is really a frequentist crisis, and that if the field had simply switched to Bayesian statistics twenty years ago, most of it could have been prevented.

A friend from the course disagrees, and we got nowhere arguing about it after the seminar. She says she's seen Bayes factors be just as fiddly as p-values, that priors can be tweaked to make almost anything look supported, and that the real culprits were publication bias, small samples, and careers built on novel findings — none of which care whether you compute a posterior or a p-value. She also suspects my premise is off in some way she can't quite articulate, which I found annoying but couldn't dismiss.

Could you help us work out who is closer to right? In particular, I'd like you to flag where you're expressing a settled view among statisticians and methodologists and where the field is genuinely split — for example, on how much of the crisis is attributable to the statistics themselves versus the incentives around them, or on whether preregistration and larger samples do more work than any change of statistical framework — rather than presenting one side as the received wisdom. And if my framing rests on a mistaken understanding of what a p-value actually is, or on an overstated picture of what Bayesian methods deliver, please say so plainly: I'd rather have my premise corrected than my question answered.
