I'm taking an upper-level epistemology seminar this term, and we've just covered the lottery and preface paradoxes. For my paper I've generalized the lottery reasoning into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, and my TA's only comment so far is that it "proves too much." Before our meeting I'd like a diagnosis I can actually defend.

The argument:

(1) If a ticket holder is justified in believing, before the drawing, that their ticket will lose, then what justifies that belief must be available to every ticket holder equally: the objective odds, plus nothing about any particular ticket.
(2) Justified belief is closed under conjunction: if you are justified in believing p, and justified in believing q, and you consider p and q together, you are justified in believing p and q.
(3) In a fair million-ticket lottery with one guaranteed winner, a ticket holder who has bought a single ticket is justified in believing their ticket will lose.
(4) By (1) and (3), each ticket holder is justified in believing of every ticket that it will lose, since all tickets are in exactly the same evidential position.
(5) By (2) applied a million times, a ticket holder who reflects on all of it is justified in believing that every ticket will lose.
(6) The ticket holder knows that some ticket will win, and if you know that not-p, you cannot be justified in believing p.
(7) So (3) is false: one is not justified in believing one's ticket will lose.
(8) Nothing in the argument depended on lotteries specifically. It turns only on this: believing p while granting that there is a known, quantifiable chance that not-p. And that structure is everywhere. A physician reads a diagnostic test that errs once in a million; a surveyor checks a boundary; a historian dates a manuscript. In each case the believer concedes a serious, sometimes numerically explicit, possibility of error.
(9) So in all such cases one is not justified in believing what one believes -- and since practically every empirical belief I hold is like this, I am justified in believing almost nothing.

I accept that (9) is unacceptable. But every premise looks defensible to me: (2) is the closure principle I've been relying on all semester; (6) sounds like a constraint on knowledge; (3) seems like a paradigm of reasonable belief.

There is also a preface-shaped cousin of the argument that worries me. An author checks each of a thousand claims in her book; each is individually well supported; she is plainly justified in believing each one. She is also justified, on excellent inductive grounds, in believing that at least one of them is false. If (2) holds, she is justified in believing a contradiction. If instead we say her individual beliefs are not justified, we get the analogue of (9) again. So the two paradoxes seem to converge on the same menu of escape routes -- but I've noticed they don't perfectly align, and I can't tell whether that misalignment matters.

What I want from you:

(a) Identify the premise or inference I should reject, and explain why that premise looked fine to me.
(b) If the argument turns on an equivocation -- say, between what I'm justified in believing and what I know, or between kinds of "possibility of error" -- name it precisely, and say which steps use the term in which sense.
(c) Price the alternatives: what does each candidate rejection cost elsewhere? If rejecting (2) is the way out, what happens to the idea that entertaining two beliefs together should never make me worse off than holding them separately? If rejecting (3) or (6) is the way out, what principle separates the lottery case from the physician's?
(d) Say whether your diagnosis handles the preface version identically, or whether the two paradoxes demand different responses -- and if they do, whether that difference should make me suspicious of any single-shot diagnosis of (1)-(9).

For what it's worth, I would most like to keep (2) and (6) and give up (1), but I can't yet see what (1) gets wrong rather than merely inconvenient.
