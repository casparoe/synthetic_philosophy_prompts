I teach a final-year undergraduate module on rational choice at a British university, and I'm writing this year's closed-book summer examination. I want one ninety-minute essay question on the Pasadena paradox and the problem of undefined expected values, but I've been burned before: two years ago I asked students to explain why the Pasadena game's expectation is undefined and to evaluate one proposed response, and the top scripts were almost indistinguishable from the reading notes. This year I want a question built around a variant of my own construction, so that nothing in the answer can be recited.

Here is the variant, which I've been calling the Wilmslow game. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n² units of utility. I've had the arithmetic checked: the probability-weighted sum of the payoffs is the alternating series ∑ (−1)^(n+1) / n², which converges absolutely to π²/12, so unlike the Pasadena game this one has a perfectly well-defined expected value. The twist I want the question to exploit is that the second moment still diverges — the variance is infinite, because the probability-weighted sum of the squared payoffs is ∑ 2^n / n⁴, which diverges. So the question I have in mind is roughly: does the well-defined expected value of the Wilmslow game settle how a rational agent should value and choose with respect to it, and does the contrast show that undefinedness was never the real lesson of the Pasadena paradox? Please sharpen this into a proper exam question — the phrasing is up to you — but it must be answerable only by someone who understands the machinery, not by someone who has memorised the Pasadena literature. Our lectures covered the Pasadena and St Petersburg games, the relabelling and reordering objections to conditional sums, dominance problems of the Pasadena-plus-a-pound variety, and the distinction between sample means converging in probability (what our lectures called weak expectations) and stronger senses in which a game can be good for you in the long run.

Please supply three things. First, the exam question itself, as it would appear on the paper, with any framing or instructions to candidates. Second, a model answer of about 600 words, which should show the convergence calculation briefly rather than belabour it, and should reach a definite verdict. Third, a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), each with a one-line note on how students typically miss or fumble it. The scheme should make the A/B boundary concrete: my expectation is that B scripts will show the series converges absolutely, note that this blocks the reordering objections, and conclude that all is well — and I want the scheme to award that only partial credit. A scripts must also confront what well-definedness leaves unsettled: whether an expectation with infinite variance is a usable guide to choice, in single-play and repeated-play settings, and what exactly the Wilmslow game reveals about which of the Pasadena paradox's symptoms were due to undefinedness as such. Please don't require any citations or references in the question or model answer, since it's closed-book; anything from the literature you want the answer to draw on should be paraphrased into its own terms.
