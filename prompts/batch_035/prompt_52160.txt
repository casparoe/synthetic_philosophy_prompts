I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a module on Marx and the philosophy of work, and I also hold a part-time internship in the people-analytics team of a large British insurance company. In the seminar we have been working through the early Marx on alienation: the claim that the worker is estranged from the product of her labour, from the activity of working itself, from her species-being, and from other workers, and that this estrangement is a structural feature of the wage relation rather than a feeling that might happen to accompany it. At work, by contrast, my main responsibility is the annual engagement survey: a battery of Likert-scale items asking employees whether they find their work meaningful, whether their voice is heard, whether they would recommend the company as an employer, and so on. Low scores are treated as a job-design problem to be fixed by line managers, whose bonuses are partly tied to their teams' results, and the aggregate score is reported to the board as a single index alongside attrition and productivity.

Here is what puzzles me. On the reading we've done in the module, an employee could answer every survey item enthusiastically and still be, in Marx's sense, completely alienated — indeed, the survey would treat her contentment as evidence against alienation, whereas Marx's own account of ideology seems to say that the alienated worker's self-report is precisely not authoritative about her condition. So the survey apparatus appears to presuppose that alienation, if it is anything at all, is a subjective state of dissatisfaction, reportable by the person who has it and remediable by managerial adjustments within the existing structure of ownership — a view that quietly rules out the structural reading entirely.

Could you help me think this through properly? What theory of alienation, or of work more generally, does the engagement-survey apparatus actually presuppose? Is that presupposition consistent across the practice, or do the different parts pull in different directions — for instance, the fact that the score is fed into a retention model alongside attrition data suggests the company also treats it as an economic variable rather than a measure of wellbeing? Does the practice strictly require the subjective view, or is it somehow compatible with the structural one? And what would have to change if the company took the structural view seriously: what, if anything, would an "alienation dashboard" measure, or is this precisely the sort of condition that survey methodology cannot capture? Finally, is it legitimate to criticise the practice on the ground that it presupposes the wrong theory, given that the survey may still be doing useful work under some thinner, purely predictive interpretation of its scores? I would be grateful if you could be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory of work could license it.
