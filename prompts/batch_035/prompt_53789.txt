I'm the program officer at a mid-sized research fund that grants roughly forty million a year across maybe thirty proposals, and every funding cycle we relitigate the same argument: one faction wants us to concentrate the budget on the single best-expected-value bet, another wants timid equal slices across everything so no one failure embarrasses us, and a trustee who spent two decades on a trading desk keeps telling us we're thinking about this completely wrong -- that what we should care about is the geometric growth rate of the endowment, not the arithmetic expectation of any one cycle, and that there's a whole literature (she mentions Kelly this, fractional Kelly that, and something called ergodicity economics that I only half follow) about sizing repeated bets. I've absorbed just enough of this to believe she's onto something and not enough to operationalize it. What I need is not a lecture on log utility but a procedure my team can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure for sizing our commitments to individual funding bets, grounded in the Kelly criterion and the geometric-rationality tradition but usable by a program officer with a spreadsheet and an hour, not a statistics background. It should specify, in order: what inputs we need before the procedure even starts (and what to do when a payoff is genuinely unquantifiable), how to compute or bound the recommended fraction, how to adjust downward for the fact that our edge estimates are themselves uncertain -- my understanding is that full Kelly on an overestimated edge is a classic way to ruin yourself, so the procedure has to say something principled about fractional Kelly rather than just "be conservative" -- and where the arithmetic-expected-value view and the geometric view actually give different answers, so the team knows which fights are real. It should be explicit about the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgment call has to be admitted as such, especially around how to weigh the fund's mission, which is not the same as maximizing the bankroll.

Then run it on three concrete cases: first, a proposal where a spinout company offers a 2.2x return on a grant structured as an equity stake, with our internal estimate of success at around 60%, competing against simply holding the endowment; second, a moonshot research program with perhaps a 3% chance of a payoff so transformative that the expected value dwarfs everything else on the slate, where I suspect Kelly will tell us to bet a rounding error and our charter tells us we exist for exactly this kind of thing; and third, a situation we've never faced but might -- a funder's clause that could force us to spend down the entire endowment within three years, so the premise of a long run of repeated bets that seems to underwrite the whole Kelly approach just isn't there. On the second case especially, don't soften it: if the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, I want to see that plainly, along with an honest account of what the procedure can't do and where following it faithfully would be a mistake.
