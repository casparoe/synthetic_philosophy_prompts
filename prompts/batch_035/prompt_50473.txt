I sit on the forecasting governance committee at a large software company that runs an internal prediction market with real bonus money. Roughly 2,300 employees are eligible to trade on questions like whether the flagship release will hit its target date, whether a particular enterprise contract will close by quarter's end, and how many paying customers a new product line will have a year after launch. Activity is thinner than we hoped — maybe 400 regular traders — and about sixty percent of the volume comes from a few dozen heavy traders whose track record, as far as our analytics team can tell, is no better than the casual traders'. The market correctly called one major launch delay that management had been in denial about, which won it some credibility, but it also badly missed a contract renewal that half the sales team apparently saw coming. At Thursday's meeting we have to decide what role the market should play in annual planning going forward: the head of operations wants to replace the current process (a consensus figure negotiated among managers in quarterly reviews, which everyone agrees is politicized and slow) with market prices as the single official forecast, on the theory that markets aggregate dispersed information better than committees ever will. Our CFO is dead set against it, and one of our members, who teaches economics part-time, keeps pressing an argument she calls the no-trade theorem: in an idealized market where traders share a prior, know each other to be rational, and start from a position where no mutually beneficial bet exists, new information alone should never generate trade at all — so if our market is humming with activity, something other than information aggregation must be doing the work, and what that something is determines what the prices actually mean.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday. Recommend a concrete course of action — adopt market prices wholesale, keep the consensus process, or a hybrid with specified rules for when each input governs — and defend it with reasons the other committee members could actually engage with rather than dismiss. The memo should take the no-trade argument seriously rather than waving it off as ivory-tower idealization: explain what the theorem does and doesn't show about a market like ours, which of its assumptions we're plausibly violating (heterogeneous priors, entertainment value, employees hedging against their own team's deadline being tied to their bonus, a small population of whales who enjoy trading), and what each violation implies for whether the prices are best read as aggregated belief, as belief distorted by position-taking incentives, or as something closer to noise on top of a signal. Where it helps, connect this to the older agreement literature: if traders disagree enough to want to trade, they can't all be calibrated, and persistent disagreement in a market raises the question of whose disagreement the price is registering.

A few constraints: the memo needs an actionable bottom line the committee can vote on, and it should include criteria we could apply at each annual review to decide whether to raise or lower the market's weight in planning (for instance, thresholds relating volume, trader breadth, or calibration performance to how much authority the prices get). Give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side — if you recommend against adopting the prices outright, say plainly what management would be giving up in speed and resistance to wishful thinking, not just what the risks are. And avoid assuming the committee has any economics background beyond what a curious general manager would have picked up; explain anything technical in the memo itself, since two of the six members will vote on whatever they can actually follow.
