A friend in the economics department has an argument that, if it works, destroys every welfare comparison I care about, and I cannot locate the false move.

Her argument runs as follows. (1) The observable facts about a person's preferences are exhausted by her ordinal ranking of the options she faces; granting the von Neumann–Morgenstern apparatus at most buys cardinality up to a positive affine transformation, which still leaves a choice of zero and unit that no observation supplies. (2) A statement whose truth is left open by everything we could in principle observe is not merely unknown but meaningless; there is no content left for it to be unknown. (3) Interpersonal comparisons therefore have no content: whether my migraine is worse than your stubbed toe depends on how our two utility representations are aligned, nothing fixes the alignment, and every alignment is observationally equivalent. So far she is on familiar ground, and I know the standard responses. The trouble is what comes next. (4) Now take a single person whose preferences change at some point in her life — she acquires a taste, or loses one, or converts. As far as preference orderings go, her post-change self relates to her pre-change self exactly as a stranger does: nothing inside the two orderings ties their utility representations to a common zero and unit, and any independent rescaling of the two is observationally equivalent to any other. (5) So comparisons across the change — that the painful retraining was worth it for the career it bought, that she is better off after the divorce than before — are exactly as meaningless as interpersonal comparisons. (6) Since nobody's preferences stay strictly fixed, the conclusion is not merely that interpersonal comparisons fail, but that every welfare judgment reaching across a change of taste — which is to say, nearly every judgment of prudence, regret, and "it was worth it" — is without content.

I am confident the conclusion is false, but not confident which step is to blame. Premise (2) smells like verificationism, but rejecting it seems to leave interpersonal comparisons true but unknowable, which is of little use for policy. Rejecting (4) looks like insisting that continuity of memory, or the body, or an extended preference over whole lives supplies the missing alignment — but she will ask what fixes the zero and unit of that extended representation, and whether, if something can, the same trick is not available for two strangers with sufficiently similar sensibilities. Please tell me which step you would reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: is there a way to reject (4) that does not thereby rehabilitate interpersonal comparisons, and is there a way to accept (4) together with the meaninglessness of cross-time judgments without collapsing prudential reasoning into nonsense?
