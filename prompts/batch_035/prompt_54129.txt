I'd like a careful philosophical reading of Borges's "The Library of Babel" through the problem of logical omniscience, written as a piece of literary criticism that takes both the story and the logic seriously. Please take the role of a professional philosopher working at the intersection of formal epistemology and philosophy of literature, and think through the reading before committing to it — do some internal brainstorming about which scenes and which formal idealizations actually make contact, and drop the pairings that don't work rather than forcing them.

By way of background: standard epistemic logic models an idealized agent whose knowledge is closed under logical consequence — the distribution axiom and the rule of necessitation jointly ensure that if such an agent knows p, and p entails q, the agent knows q, and that the agent knows all logical truths. The introspection axioms pile on further: the agent knows what it knows (axiom 4) and knows what it fails to know (axiom 5). Real agents — humans, institutions, perhaps language models — are nothing like this, and the mismatch between the ideal agent of the formalism and actual bounded agents is the problem of logical omniscience. Borges's story seems to me to dramatize this problem from a strange angle, and I want to know whether the appearance holds up.

Here is the angle. The Library contains, on the story's canonical numbers, every possible book of 410 pages over its alphabet — which means that somewhere in it is every true statement, every proof, every refutation, every accurate prediction of the future, in the librarians' language. In one sense the librarians therefore possess all knowledge: it is all present, on shelves, in a script they can read. In another sense they possess almost nothing, because a book being true is no help unless you can find it and verify it, and the search space makes both effectively impossible. Notice that this is a kind of inverted image of the logical omniscience problem: the formal epistemologist worries about an agent who knows everything it could possibly deduce from what it knows, while Borges gives us agents for whom everything is deducible — indeed, already written down — and who still know nearly nothing. My tentative thought is that the story is best read as a study of the gap between implicit and explicit knowledge, and of what happens to agents and institutions when that gap is unbridgeable in practice. But I'm not sure the reading survives the story's own details, and I'd like you to test it rather than assume it.

In particular, please address:

1. Does the story support or complicate the common move of dissolving the logical omniscience problem by distinguishing implicit from explicit knowledge — saying the ideal agent merely implicitly knows the consequences of what it knows, so the formalism is harmless? The librarians seem to have maximal implicit knowledge and minimal explicit knowledge; does Borges show that the distinction is safe, or that the gap between the two is where the actual intellectual life of a bounded agent lives?
2. The librarians' coping institutions and practices — the inquisitors who purge supposedly useless and lying volumes, the sectarians who travel by the book of an ordeal, the sect who destroy books on the theory that the purges themselves were insufficient, the legends of a total Book and of a librarian who has read it, the seekers after personal vindications and apologetic prophecies — can these be read as rational strategies for resource-bounded agents in an environment where verification costs are astronomical? Do any of them correspond to strategies actually discussed in the literature on bounded rationality or on heuristics under costly search? Where do they instead read as the pathology that an omniscient idealization renders invisible?
3. The narrator is a subtle reasoner who, late in life, advances the conjecture that the Library is either infinite or, if finite, periodically repeating, and finds in this a personal consolation. Is his inference an example of a bounded agent doing respectable epistemology with the tiny fragment of the Library available to him — or of the epistemic vice the formalism idealizes away? Is his hope rational in any sense available to epistemic logic?
4. The story also plays with introspective omniscience: no librarian can know what they do not know, since the absence of a book can never be established, and a book you have read might be contradicted by one you never will. Does Borges dramatize the failure of negative introspection (axiom 5) more vividly than the failure of consequence-closure, and if so, should my framing put the introspection axioms at the center instead?
5. Finally, be an honest critic. Where does the story resist this whole apparatus — where is it about mortality, theology, or politics in ways that mapping it onto axioms would flatten? What does Borges give the debate that the formal literature cannot — and is there anything the formal literature gets right that the story, for all its brilliance, gets subtly wrong about bounded knowers?
