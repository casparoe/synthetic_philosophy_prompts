I'm a novelist — five books, mostly tragedies — and my editor has thrown down a gauntlet about the one I'm drafting now. Her claim is that what I keep calling an impossible choice is never truly impossible, only costly; that in every situation I engineer, one option is all-things-considered right, and the "dilemma" is my protagonist's reluctance dressed in grander clothes. For the book to work, I believe she has to be wrong about at least some of these choices: a tragedy only breaks the reader open if the choice genuinely breaks the agent — if whatever she does, she has done wrong, and not merely something regrettable, something with a price. Before I commit my postmistress to her ending, I want to know whether my own case-by-case verdicts actually add up to a coherent notion of a genuine dilemma, or whether I've been writing sheer atmosphere. Here are the cases, with my gut verdicts on whether each is a genuine dilemma, in my sense: the agent has no available option that leaves her having done no wrong, such that some guilt, or its moral equivalent, would be fitting afterward rather than neurotic. First, the mother on a rail platform whom a guard forces to choose which of her two children is taken, on pain of both being taken: this one I'm sure of — genuine; whichever child she points to, she has wronged that child, her lifelong guilt is fitting, and I'd resist any therapist who told her otherwise — though I confess I also think she did the only thing anyone could reasonably ask of her, and I don't know how both of those can be true. Second, a wartime doctor with one dose of a scarce antibiotic and two dying patients, clinically indistinguishable in need: not genuine; if she flips a coin, she has done no wrong, only suffered. Third, a woman who promised a dying friend to burn his letters, which now contain evidence that would spare a stranger a false conviction: not a genuine dilemma — she should hand them over — and yet I can't shake the sense that she owes the friend something afterward, an apology he can no longer hear. Fourth, the driver who ignored warnings about his brakes and now, careening toward the street, must swerve toward one pedestrian or strike two: my editor says his present choice is demoted to the last installment of a debt already incurred by his negligence, and therefore not a dilemma at all — I half believe her and half don't, because the swerve still feels like wronging the one he turns toward, not merely reaping. Fifth, a resistance courier faces execution unless my postmistress names him, but naming him collapses a network and costs other lives: one option wrongs through speech and the other through silence, and I find myself insisting, against my editor, that the speaking wrongs more — though if pressed I cannot say why that asymmetry is anything but sentimentality. Sixth, a woman who can save a stranger's life only by renouncing her vocation, her life's whole work, forever: I say this is not a moral dilemma at all, merely prudence — and yet I notice that the residue she carries afterward looks uncannily like guilt, which annoys me.

What I'd like from you is a principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely — what exactly makes a choice a genuine dilemma rather than merely a hard one — with each clause pegged to the case that motivates it, since I need to be able to test it against scenes as I write them. Then tell me what the principle predicts about the one case I cannot settle, which is the ending of the book: a widow whose teenage son was driving in a fatal hit-and-run that the police have misattributed to an innocent neighbor; her silence lets the neighbor be charged, her speech destroys her son — is that a genuine dilemma in my sense, and is the guilt I intend her to carry for the rest of the novel fitting or pathological? Finally, be honest about which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate: I would rather overturn a verdict than bolt an epicycle onto the theory, but I need to know which verdicts are load-bearing for my whole conception of tragedy, and whether giving them up leaves me with anything my editor couldn't have told me for free. Please also state how confident you are in the principle as a whole and single out the one step in it you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the floor is thin before I build the last chapter on it.
