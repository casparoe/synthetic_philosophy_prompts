I've gone pretty deep down the rabbit hole of the Lucas-Penrose argument — the idea that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind can't be a machine — and what really strikes me is how utterly stalled the whole thing is. Lucas put the argument out back around 1960, Penrose revived it in The Emperor's New Mind and then doubled down with a souped-up version in Shadows of the Mind, and at this point every move on both sides is a greatest hits album: the mechanists say the argument only goes through if the human somehow *knows* the machine is consistent, and we can't know that — we're fallible, we might even be inconsistent ourselves; Penrose says we can just *see* that we're not the kind of system that would assert false arithmetic, or at least that we're not whatever specific machine you propose; the mechanists reply that this confident self-assessment is exactly what an unsound machine would output; and then Penrose shrugs and says fine, mathematical insight must come from non-computable physics in the brain, at which point everyone starts arguing about quantum effects in microtubules instead of Gödel.

Here's my working theory: the disagreement was never really about Gödel at all. It's that Penrose has a strong pre-theoretical conviction that mathematical understanding is a faculty you can just recognize from the inside — that "seeing" that the Gödel sentence is true is a genuine cognitive act, not a computation — while the mechanists hold the opposite bedrock conviction that introspection tells you basically nothing about your own implementation. Both convictions are unargued, so each side just concedes whatever Gödel lemma it needs to concede and walks away feeling vindicated.

Is that right? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — a hidden empirical question about whether the brain is computable, a dispute about what "seeing" a mathematical truth amounts to, two camps answering different questions under the label "understanding", or genuinely bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Use what the two sides actually say to make the case, not just a taxonomy. And given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here: a sharpened version of the argument whose premises the mechanists would actually accept, a better theory of mathematical intuition, results from physics, or an honest case that this one is structurally unresolvable and we should stop expecting movement?
