I'm taking a graduate seminar this fall on religion in liberal political thought, and before the semester started I read a trade book by a journalist arguing that secularism is quietly displacing religion from American public life. The book leans heavily on Rawls, and it contained a paragraph summarizing political liberalism that I've been chewing on ever since. Now that I've read parts of Political Liberalism myself, I can't decide whether the book was simplifying for a lay audience or actually misrepresenting him, and I'd like an audit before I bring it up in seminar and embarrass myself.

The summary went, as best as I can reproduce it: "Rawls's later work asks how a society can remain stable and well-ordered when its citizens are divided by incompatible religious and philosophical doctrines. His answer is public reason: since citizens share no common foundation of belief, they must argue for the laws that bind everyone entirely in terms of premises that everyone in society actually accepts, and this requirement is a duty of civility binding on citizens whenever they engage in political discussion at all. A bishop may believe whatever her church teaches, but the moment she enters the public square to argue about, say, abortion law, she may not offer her religious grounds for her position; if she does, her argument fails to count as a public reason and she has acted unreasonably. Rawls is explicit about this because religious doctrines as such cannot be reasonable — a reasonable doctrine is one argued from common human reason rather than revelation — and it is only because of this that reasonable citizens converge on the same political principles for the same underlying reasons, each deriving them from within its own comprehensive doctrine. That is what makes the overlapping consensus more than a mere modus vivendi."

How much of this should I trust? Go through it clause by clause and tell me which claims a Rawlsian would accept as accurate, which are simplifications a defender could live with, which are subtly wrong, and which Rawls explicitly denies — and, where you can, which parts of his texts I should point my seminar to in making the case. I'm inclined to think the book was unfair to him, but I honestly can't locate the mistakes except for a nagging memory that Rawls said something late in his career about comprehensive doctrines being admissible in public political discussion, though I may be conflating him with one of his critics. And finally, the conclusion the book draws from all this — that on Rawls's view religious citizens must effectively check their faith at the door of the polling booth — does that follow from the parts of the summary that survive your corrections, or does it fall with the parts that don't?
