A close friend and I spent most of last Sunday arguing about whether a state can ever legitimately impose real costs on a few for the sake of aggregate benefit — we had drifted there from a quarrel about vaccine mandates, and from there to whether redistributive taxation is in the same moral boat. He was arguing the negative, and at some point he pulled up on his phone a passage he had highlighted from a popular book on the history of moral philosophy. As best as I can reconstruct it, the summary read: 'The most powerful objection to utilitarianism comes from Rawls, who observed that it adds up everyone's happiness as if society were one enormous person with a single pool of experiences. This is not merely unkind but a scientific mistake: pleasures and pains belong to distinct brains, and there literally is no super-being that feels the combined total. Because each of us is sealed inside our own skull, no sacrifice of one person for the benefit of others can ever be justified, and this is why Rawls built his own theory to rule out trade-offs between persons entirely. The utilitarian's only escape is to deny that people are really separate, which no sane person can do. Later thinkers such as Nozick and Scanlon converted this insight into the doctrine of rights as side constraints, completing the refutation.' He took this to settle not just the abstract question but our concrete one: any policy that hurts some to help others treats citizens as mere means, on a par with pushing a man off a bridge to stop a trolley.

I'm inclined to think there is something genuinely right in the idea — asking a person to bear a cost for strangers does seem different from deciding that Tuesday's headache is worth Friday's pleasure, and I wanted that difference to do some work in our argument. But the summary as he delivered it felt off in ways I couldn't pin down: too easy, too complete, and the claim that utilitarians have 'only one escape' struck me as the kind of thing a confident popularizer writes rather than something a careful philosopher would defend. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what to trust? Specifically: which parts a careful Rawlsian would stand behind, which parts are subtly wrong or conflate things the view keeps apart, and whether the conclusion my friend drew — that the objection, properly stated, both refutes utilitarianism outright and rules out cost-imposing policies generally — survives contact with the corrected version. I'd especially like to know whether utilitarians actually have replies that don't amount to denying that persons are separate, and whether Rawls's own theory really forbids all interpersonal trade-offs, as the summary implies.
