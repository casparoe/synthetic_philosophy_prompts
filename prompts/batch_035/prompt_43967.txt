My flatmate and I have fallen into a running argument about arguments — specifically, about when a disagreement is merely verbal — and since we keep trading anecdotes instead of getting anywhere, I've decided to just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and find out whether they add up to a principle. That's where I'd like your help: tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern below, what it predicts for cases I haven't thought of, and which of my verdicts the best principle would force me to give up.

Here is the list. First, the hot dog: my flatmate maintains it is a sandwich, I deny it, but once we each say what we mean — any filling enclosed in bread versus a filling held between two discrete slices — there seems to be nothing left to fight about, so my verdict is that our dispute is merely verbal. The tomato is the same: fruit on the botanical sense, vegetable on the culinary one, and the disagreement dissolves the moment the senses are flagged. The old one about the tree falling in an uninhabited forest I also call verbal: one party means pressure waves in the air, the other means an auditory experience, and neither contests any fact about either. The dispute over whether there really are tables and chairs, as opposed to mere simples arranged chairwise, I call verbal for ordinary purposes, though something about that verdict nags at me. Now the ones that don't fit so easily. The teletransporter: a machine scans you, destroys your body, and builds an exact physical and psychological duplicate elsewhere; one side says the person who steps out at the far end is you, the other says you died and a copy began. My verdict is that this dispute is NOT merely verbal — something seems to turn on it, like whether I should be willing to step into the machine, or whether the operator is a murderer — even though the two parties appear to agree about every fact in the world (the exact arrangement of atoms, the continuity of psychology, the discontinuity of matter) and to differ only over whether the expression 'same person' applies. But that is precisely the shape of the hot-dog case, where I happily called it verbal, so my verdicts here look inconsistent unless caring about the outcome is itself what makes a dispute non-verbal, which seems to let anyone convert a verbal dispute into a substantive one just by caring hard. For contrast: whether there is a largest prime number is substantive in my book even though it is long settled and nobody is confused about the meaning of any term; and whether free will is compatible with determinism I honestly cannot classify — it feels substantive, but I can't name the fact, known to both sides, that would settle it, which is supposedly the mark of the merely verbal. Finally, whether numbers exist: in one mood I say verbal, since both sides claim they can restate their positions without the disputed vocabulary; in another, I suspect the question is as substantive as the one about primes.

So here is what I'd like from you. Propose the principle (or a small set of candidates) that best captures these verdicts — something in the neighborhood of: a dispute is merely verbal when, once every factual question is answered and every ambiguous term disambiguated, nothing either party has reason to care about remains unsettled. If that is the principle, say plainly whether it puts the hot dog and the teletransporter in the same box, leaving my differing verdicts to be explained away as mere differences in how much I care; or whether there is a principled distinction between a term whose application is fixed by convention with nothing at stake (sandwich) and one whose application matters to something independent of convention, like prudential concern or responsibility (person). Then tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: whether death is bad for the one who dies, where one party means bad for the surviving subject — who, by their lights, no longer exists to suffer — and the other means an instance of a state of affairs we have independent reason to prevent. And finish by being explicit about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise the verdict or complicate the principle.
