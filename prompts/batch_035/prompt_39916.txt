I'm drafting a review chapter on the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, and it has struck me that the field mostly argues past itself because no one states the adequacy conditions up front — or, worse, everyone's list is reverse-engineered from the account they already favor. So before I survey the theories, I want the specification written independently of any of them.

Write the desiderata a theory of biological function must meet. At minimum, the list should address: the distinction between function and mere effect (why pumping blood is the heart's function and making thumping sounds is not), and whether that distinction can be drawn non-circularly; the possibility of genuine malfunction that does not collapse into statistical abnormality or disease; the ascription of function to evolutionarily novel traits, or to traits that performed a causal contribution before selection ever acted on it — the case where a structure is useful first and selected later; parts whose contribution is redundant or distributed across a system, which pressures causal-role analyses toward counting almost anything as a function; vestigial organs, where selection history and current contribution come apart; whether the account must be serviceable for teleosemantics, or whether that burden is an add-on from one camp that the rest need not accept; and whether the account must extend beyond evolved organisms — to engineered artifacts generally, and especially to synthetic organisms and artificially selected laboratory strains, where I suspect the etiological picture is on its weakest ground but is reluctant to say so.

For each desideratum, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and indicate whether it is a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp — noting which camp insists on it and which would happily drop it. Then do two things I care most about. First, tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, since if so the list itself is a result: the function debate would be a forced choice, not a search for the one true account. Second, identify the single desideratum that does the most exclusionary work — the one whose acceptance knocks out the most candidate theories.

Finally, address pluralism directly: if someone proposes that etiological and causal-role accounts are simply different concepts suited to different purposes, is that a resolution, or is it a refusal to answer the question? In answering, consider one concrete test case — the practice of genome annotation, where biologists assign functions to genes daily and would be baffled by the suggestion that they are equivocating between two concepts. Does a theory of function written by philosophers owe these biologists anything, and if so, does that change the specification?
