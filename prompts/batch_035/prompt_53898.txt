I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on causation in medieval philosophy, where we have spent half the term on the dispute between the Ash'arite occasionalists of al-Ghazālī's tradition and the Avicennian philosophers they attacked. For our final project, two of us — one arguing the occasionalist side, one the necessitarian side — must produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another round-table debate. I would like you to draft the joint statement we should both be able to sign.

The positions, as we've fixed them for the project: the occasionalist holds that fire has no power to burn cotton; what we call the burning is God's creating the burning directly, and it is regular only because God maintains a custom of conjoining contact with fire and the burning of the cotton — a custom that could be suspended in the case of a miracle. The necessitarian holds that fire, by its nature and given the absence of impediments, necessitates the burning; talk of divine custom is at most a description of how a thing's nature regularly manifests. In seminar we have been arguing past each other on three points: whether the sentence "the fire burned the cotton" is literally true or true only in loose speech; whether the regularity of nature is contingent; and what a miracle could even be if causes necessitate their effects.

Please produce the following, and keep the document formal enough that either party could submit it under their own name:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about what is in any case observed (that contact-type events and burning-type events are constantly conjoined in our experience), and about what the dispute is *not* about (e.g., neither party thinks experience alone could distinguish the two views, and neither is a mere regularity-theorist about what the regularity consists in).

2. A disagreements section, with each residual disagreement phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. Here I especially want an exact formulation of the disagreement over "necessitation": the necessitarian means something stronger than constant conjunction given the natures of the things involved, while the occasionalist reserves "necessity" for what holds in every metaphysically possible situation, and denies that any created thing's nature carries such necessity. Please make explicit whether some of our disagreement over the word "cause" is merely verbal, and what survives as substantive once the terminology is regimented.

3. A cruxes section. Work with the two thought experiments our seminar constructed, and add a third if you see a better one:

(a) The Repeated World: God creates, annihilates, and then re-creates an exactly similar situation — dry cotton touching flame, no wind, no impediment, everything intrinsic to the situation the same. Occasionalist prediction: nothing about the earlier instance settles what God will conjoin with what; identity of prior situation does not constrain the subsequent divine act, and at most the custom makes repetition to be expected. Necessitarian prediction: the burning recurs, guaranteed by the natures involved — though each side should flag how it formulates this guarantee, given that both parties agree God sustains the whole situation in being.

(b) The Patchwork World: a world just like ours except that, once every few trillion fire-cotton contacts, nothing follows the contact. Both parties agree that, up to any time we could actually observe, there is no experiential difference. Does the bare conceivability of such a world settle whether the regularity is contingent? Each side should record its prediction about whether the patchwork world is metaphysically possible, and what that verdict would show if granted by the other side.

For each crux, write down in advance what each party would count as an argument bearing on it — bearing in mind that both concede that empirical observation alone cannot decide the matter — or state honestly that no argument both parties would accept as probative exists, and that the collaboration must end by locating the remaining dispute.

4. A concluding section: if you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say precisely which part and why; if you judge the remainder to be substantive, say exactly what it is — whether it is at bottom a disagreement about modality, about divine freedom, or about something else — in wording each party has already accepted above.

Please do not try to adjudicate who is right; the point of the exercise is a document both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than settled.
