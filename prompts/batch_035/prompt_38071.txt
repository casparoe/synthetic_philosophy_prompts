Academic journals do this thing where they send your paper to two or maybe three anonymous reviewers, and if the reviews come back split, the editor usually just grabs a third reviewer to break the tie. If both reviewers say reject, it's dead; if both say accept, it's basically through (modulo the editor's whims). Reviewers also almost never see each others' reports before writing their own, and in a lot of fields the editor isn't really a subject expert on your specific topic -- they're just managing the process.

I'm trying to figure out what this setup actually presupposes about disagreement between epistemic peers, because it looks like it has commitments baked in that nobody ever argues for. Like, the tiebreaker mechanism seems to assume that a majority of peers beats any single peer's verdict, which sounds kind of conciliationist -- but it's majoritarian rather than split-the-difference, and conciliationism in the philosophy literature is usually about credences, not votes. And the whole "keep the reports independent" thing seems to assume each reviewer is a separate epistemic draw on the paper's quality, but that seems off, since reviewers in a field share training, share the same refereed orthodoxies, read the same reviewers' work, etc. Their verdicts aren't independent evidence the way three witnesses to an accident are -- they're more like correlated measurements. Does the practice quietly presuppose they're independent anyway, and does that matter for whether the majority is actually informative?

There's also the weirdness that nobody expects the reviewers themselves to update. If reviewer A thought the paper was great and then sees that reviewer B, whom A regards as just as smart, trashed it, nothing in the process asks A to revise anything -- the disagreement just gets fed into the counting machine. A steadfast theorist might say that's fine, each reviewer should stick to their guns and let the vote settle it; a conciliationist might say the process is epistemically broken precisely because it never makes anyone split the difference.

So basically: reconstruct the implicit epistemology of peer review as an institutional response to peer disagreement. Which parts of the practice need a conciliationist view, which parts fit better with steadfastness, and which parts don't actually presuppose either because they're just pragmatic workflow stuff that would survive under any theory? And then tell me what the process would look like if it were redesigned to be genuinely conciliationist (or genuinely steadfast) -- like, would mandatory reviewer discussion before verdicts, or editors who overrule unanimous reviewers based on their own conviction, actually be better or worse at tracking paper quality? I'm curious where the current system is philosophically underdetermined vs. where it's really committed to something specific, and I'd rather you be honest about that distinction than force everything into one tidy theory.
