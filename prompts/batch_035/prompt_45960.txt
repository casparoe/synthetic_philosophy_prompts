At a development-policy seminar last week, a speaker shut down a discussion of famine early-warning systems with the line that democracies don't have famines — the claim associated with Amartya Sen's work on the Bengal famine of 1943 — and used it to argue that constitutional reform, not food logistics or relief infrastructure, is the only famine-prevention policy that really matters. I'm sure the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, but I couldn't sort them out on the spot. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single gloss: as a near-tautology (if a functioning democracy is defined to include the accountability that would preempt famine, the claim risks becoming true by definition); as a bare historical generalization (which the Irish famine of the 1840s — a parliamentary regime with a comparatively free press, where the starving largely lacked the vote — would test one way, and the Great Leap Forward another); and as a causal claim about which feature does the work: competitive elections, an opposition with an incentive to make the government look bad, or a press free enough to report deaths the government would rather hide. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, which edge cases it absorbs and which it has to dismiss, and whether it would actually support the speaker's inference to constitutional reform over early-warning systems. Present it as a list I could bring to the next seminar.
