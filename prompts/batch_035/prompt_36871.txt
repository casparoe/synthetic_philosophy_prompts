I'm a high school senior taking a semester-long elective called "Big Questions in Philosophy," and for my final paper I've chosen a topic my teacher flagged as genuinely hard: origin essentialism, the claim that you could not have come into existence from a different sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from. I gather the idea traces back to Saul Kripke's suggestion in Naming and Necessity that a person's origins are essential to them, so that the person who would have been conceived had a different sperm won the race — even a sperm from the same parents — would have been someone else entirely, not you. I find myself pulled in both directions. On the one hand, it seems strange to say I could have been someone else entirely, born on the other side of the world; the intuitive pull is that those would just be different people whose lives I read about like fiction. On the other hand, if a different sperm had fertilized the egg, "I" would never have existed to miss anything, so what exactly is lost by admitting that possibility?

Here are two wrinkles from my own research that I want you to address rather than ignore. First, the "same gametes, different shuffle" problem: even if we grant that my particular sperm and egg are essential to me, meiosis involves recombination, so the very same sperm and egg, packaged differently, could have produced a genetically different person — which threatens to make origin essentialism unstable unless it descends to some finer-grained level. Second, human chimerism: there are actual people who began as two fertilized eggs that merged into a single embryo, so one person traces back to two complete gamete pairs — a case where it's not even clear which single origin the essentialist can point to. Please justify the conclusion rather than merely asserting it, and say what would have to be true for you to change your mind.
