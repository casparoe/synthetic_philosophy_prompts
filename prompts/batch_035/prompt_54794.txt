I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I'm asking you to help me broker a truce between two people close to me who have been arguing for about a year now.

The first is my former teammate, Dana, a committed effective altruist. She deliberately took a job at an ad-tech company because it paid substantially more than the alternatives, donates a fixed percentage of her income to charities chosen on the basis of cost-effectiveness estimates, and is weighing a much larger lifetime pledge. She talks about her career the way we talk about system design: as an instrument, with explicit tradeoffs.

The second is my sister, a labor organizer. Her view, roughly, is that effective altruism functions as a pressure valve: it lets talented people inside harmful industries feel fine about staying there; it redirects the exact people who could be building political power into doing charity math instead; and its insistence on measured outcomes makes it structurally blind to the biggest levers, because institutional change doesn't ship with a cost-effectiveness dashboard. She has called Dana's career choice the conscience line-item on a balance sheet.

What keeps me from just picking a side is that each of them, in calmer moments, concedes real ground. Dana admits the ad industry is net harmful and that her estimates are noisy. My sister admits some of Dana's charities do genuine good and that she has no counterfactual estimate for what her organizing actually changes.

I've read a little about adversarial collaborations, and that's the deliverable I want: not a debate transcript, and not your verdict on who's right, but the joint document both of them could actually sign. Please draft it with these sections:

1. **Agreed claims.** Numbered, each phrased so both Dana and my sister would sign it as stated. Include the concessions each makes in their weaker moments — Dana on her industry's harms and the noise in the estimates, my sister on the real benefits of the top charities and the absence of a counterfactual story for organizing. Do not achieve signatures by watering anything down into vacuity.

2. **Disagreements table.** Each row states one remaining disagreement in wording that the side holding that view would accept as a fair statement of its own position. Cover at least: (a) earning to give — career as instrument versus talent capture; (b) the measurability problem — whether privileging measurable outcomes is a self-correcting approximation or a structural blind spot, the way my team only ever fixes what shows up in the dashboards; (c) fungibility — whether Dana's donations and her continued presence at the company are substitutes for institutional change, complements to it, or something that actively delays it.

3. **Cruxes.** The subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each crux, name a concrete case, empirical finding, or thought experiment both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance — so that if the evidence ever came in, they will have already agreed on what it counts for.

4. **Verbal disputes.** If any part of their disagreement is really about words — say, different meanings of doing good, or of what counts as an institution — identify which part and why.

End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} naming the one crux from section 3 that both would agree is most load-bearing — the one whose resolution would move the most total conviction between them.
