I'm one of three volunteer readers for the fiction slush pile at a mid-sized literary magazine — roughly ninety submissions a month, and between that and our day jobs we realistically get fifteen or twenty minutes with most pieces, five minutes when the queue backs up before an issue closes. Our verdicts constantly diverge and the arguments are circular: one reader says quality can't be argued because de gustibus non est disputandum, another says that's just an excuse for letting personal taste masquerade as judgment, and our editor keeps gesturing at Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" — the idea that even though beauty has no demonstrable rules, some verdicts are simply wrong, and the standard is whatever properly qualified critics would converge on after enough delicacy, practice, and comparison. Great, but the essay gives me nothing I can actually run during a Tuesday night reading session.

What I want is a decision procedure extracted from Hume that our team can follow: the questions to ask of a piece, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, where it terminates in an acceptance, and — this part matters — where it stops and hands the call to a human because what's left is a judgment call that no procedure should pretend to settle. His ideal critic has those five qualifications (delicacy, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense, roughly), and I want the procedure to translate each into something checkable, not a vibe. I'm aware some parts won't survive translation — like the test of time, which seems useless prospectively since nothing submitted to us has stood the test of anything yet, and like the passage where he basically lets young readers off the hook for preferring love poetry, which suggests some disagreements are blameless and the standard just doesn't reach them. Be upfront about those limits rather than papering over them.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases from our queue: a technically polished but formulaically sentimental story that two of us adored and one dismissed; a clumsy first effort with a genuinely striking central image; and a deliberately ugly, misanthropic piece that our most practiced reader insists is the best thing we've gotten all year. Include at least one case where the procedure gives the answer I won't like (I suspect the sentimental one is mine to lose), and tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — where Hume's essay itself is indeterminate and any checklist built from it is judgment wearing a costume.
