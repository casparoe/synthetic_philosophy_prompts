I have spent the past year moderating a dispute between two colleagues in our department's philosophy of language workshop, and I have concluded that the only honest output of the exercise is the document they should have written and never did. One colleague is a committed epistemicist about vagueness: she holds that a vague predicate, applied to a fixed context and comparison class, divides the world cleanly into things that satisfy it and things that do not, and that our inability to locate the cut-off between, say, the last red patch and the first not-red patch in a sorites series is genuine ignorance — explained, roughly, by the limits of our discrimination and our knowledge of our own meanings, but not by any indeterminacy in language or world. The other is a committed supervaluationist: he holds that a borderline case makes a sentence neither true nor false simpliciter, that truth for vague discourse is truth on every admissible precification, and that the epistemicist's sharp cut-offs are artifacts of forcing a determinate semantics onto an irreducibly indeterminate practice. They argue past each other weekly, and several of us suspect that at least one strand of the fight is verbal — perhaps over the word "boundary" itself, or over what it means to call a sentence "not true."

Draft the joint statement these two should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on vagueness and the sorites paradox. Structure it in five parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — claims about what the sorites paradox demands of any solution; about the reality of penumbral connections, such as the tie between a patch's being red and its not being a wholesale counterfeit; about what would be lost if we simply banned vague predicates from language; and about which features of ordinary usage any theory must accommodate rather than legislate away.

2. A disagreements table stating each residual disagreement twice: once in wording the epistemicist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, once in wording the supervaluationist would accept as a fair statement of his. In particular, the disagreement over sharp cut-off points must be stated without begging it — neither "there really is a last red patch" nor "there really is no such patch," but formulations each side would recognize as their own.

3. The cruxes. My candidates: whether higher-order vagueness embarrasses one side more than the other (each accuses the other of this); whether the epistemicist's claim that we are ignorant of cut-offs admits any explanation that does not itself reintroduce the vagueness it was meant to dispel; whether the supervaluationist's definiteness operator can be iterated indefinitely without collapse; and whether the sorites major premise — that one grain, or one patch, cannot make the difference — is false, or true but unassertible, or something else entirely. But re-derive the crux list yourself; add or drop as the argument requires, and justify the additions.

4. For each crux, a test, argument, or concrete case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. One test must be ours, and I want to see how it fares: we ran a forced-choice task in which subjects sorted a slowly graduated series of patches from red to orange one at a time, with no "borderline" option and no revisiting earlier judgments. Every subject exhibited a sharp switch point — but the switch points scattered across a band of roughly two dozen patches, and each subject, shown their own switch afterward, insisted they could have gone either way on the patches near it. My epistemicist colleague predicts the scatter would collapse under richer context or repeated exposure; my supervaluationist colleague predicts persistent scatter, and expects the subjects' first-order reports to be correct about the band while mistaken about the switch points. Check whether I have stated their predictions fairly, and construct analogous tests for the remaining cruxes, including at least one drawn from the practical setting where courts must draw lines through vague statutory terms.

5. A closing section in which each side records, in its own accepted wording, what would move it to abandon its view — and, where you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part, which words are doing the equivocating work, and what remains genuinely substantive once the verbal residue is cleared away.

Finally, append a drafting note, kept separate from the statement itself, in which you say where the joint statement is most likely to fail: which agreed formulations are probably not something both camps would actually sign, which disagreement was classified as verbal on the weakest grounds, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about each of those judgments.
