I'm a PhD student in history, and last semester I got badly stuck — weeks at a time where I wrote basically nothing. So a few months ago I started tracking my daily wordcount and set a 500-words-a-day target. Honestly it fixed the stuckness, I write most days now, and the sheer momentum has been great for my mood. But I've noticed something odd in the last chapter draft I sent around. Reading it back, big chunks read like padding: sentences that restate the same point in slightly different words, long quotes I could have paraphrased in a line. And my revision time has ballooned, because I'm now spending whole sessions cutting stuff I apparently added just to get through the day. Its like the writing exists to hit the number, not to say anything.

My advisor's take is that I should drop the count entirely and just write when I have something to say. That scares me, because "write when I have something to say" is exactly the mode I was in when I got stuck for weeks. A friend in a more quantitative field suggested a middle path: keep the daily target, but add a second metric for quality — e.g., only count paragraphs that survive into the next revision, so pure padding earns nothing. That sounds clever but I have a sneaking suspicion it just moves the problem somewhere less visible.

So I have two questions, and they're really about the general pattern, not just my thesis. One: is there a principled way to think about when a number stops measuring a thing and quietly becomes the thing? It felt like an honest measurement tool at first, and somewhere along the way it turned into the goal, and I'd like to know where that line actually is. Two: does stacking a second metric on top of a first one fix anything, or does it just give me two numbers to game instead of one — and is there any real difference between those outcomes? Something tells me there's a mechanism here that shows up everywhere once you see it, but I can't put my finger on it, and I'd rather understand it than keep patching my system ad hoc.
