I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about Dutch book arguments by checking my gut on concrete cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern. Here are my verdicts:

1. Someone gives 0.7 to heads and 0.7 to tails for the same coin at the same moment, and will buy bets on either side at odds matching those numbers. A bookie takes them to the cleaers. Verdict: irrational, and the Dutch book is exactly what shows it.

2. Someone whose credences are perfectly coherent but who assigns 0.5 to literally everything and never updates on any evidence. Can't be booked, ever. Verdict: not irrational in the exploitable sense, but obviously doing terribly. So coherence can't be the whole of rationality — fine, the argument was only ever supposed to show incoherence is irrational.

3. Diachronic case: someone whose credences today aren't what you'd get by conditionalizing yesterday's credences on today's evidence. The standard story says a bookie can take money off them across time, so they're irrational. My gut says hold on. Tomorrow-me isn't betting against today-me; the bookie is just exploiting the fact that I changed my mind, and changing your mind over time seems fine, even required.

4. But now take someone who updates by some clearly awful rule — they always become certain that the last thing they heard is true, no matter the source. Gut: irrational. But if I just said the diachronic book doesn't bite, I've lost my tool for getting this verdict, and I still have it. Hmm.

5. Someone with cyclic preferences: they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and will pay a small amount to swap each way around the loop, so you can money pump them forever. Gut: irrational. But is this just the diachronic problem again, now with preferences instead of credences? Or is something different going on, since each individual trade is fine at the moment it's made?

6. Someone who refuses to bet at all, on principle — thinks gambling is beneath them, so no bookie can touch them — but their credences are a total mess. Gut: still irrational to hold the mess. But then it wasn't vulnerability to exploitation that made the mess irrational, and I have to wonder what the bookie was ever doing in case 1 either.

My tentative principle: Dutch bookability is a symptom of irrationality only when the incoherence lives in dispositions you'd act on at a single time; cross-time books show nothing, because your later self isn't conspiring with the bookie against your earlier self. Test this against my six cases, repair it or replace it, and be honest about which of my verdicts the fixed version still can't save. Also tell me what the best version says about a case I haven't made up my mind on: an agent who is perfectly coherent about everything except one proposition, where they just keep saying "no bet" whenever that proposition comes up — no odds in either direction, ever — so no book can be made against them at any moment, but their credence in that one thing never gets pinned down. Does my principle call that fine?
