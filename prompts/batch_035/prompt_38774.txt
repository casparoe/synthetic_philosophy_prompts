I write novels for a living — midlist literary stuff, the kind that gets one polite review in the back pages — and my new book is built on a metaphor I spent four years leaning way too hard on: DNA as code. My narrator is a bioinformatician who starts half-believing her own debugging metaphors about her genome, and the whole novel is basically me interrogating whether "code" is a genuine likeness or a category error that sounds nice. Anyway, my cousin teaches AP Bio in Ohio, she read an advance copy, and now she wants me to come do a 40-minute session with her juniors on whether DNA is really a code. These kids have had Mendel and they know their A-T-C-G, but no information theory, no philosophy, nothing. I can write a scene where someone explains something; I have never actually explained anything to anyone in real life, and I have forty minutes.

Don't explain the issue to me — I know it cold, it's in the book. Plan the session. What has to be established first before the question is even a question for them (my instinct is something about the difference between saying the cell *reads* DNA and saying DNA *means* something, but you tell me), which single concrete example should carry the whole forty minutes and what it needs to have and not have, the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form on the spot — I'm guessing "a code needs a coder" is one, and maybe some vibe that the DNA knows what it's doing — and exactly where in the sequence each one should get headed off rather than left to fester til the Q&A. I also want one question I can close with that would actually show me whether they understood the distinction or just heard a cool metaphor they'll forget by Friday. And tell me what to flatly refuse to get into with seventeen-year-olds, and how to say so at the front without sounding like a novelist dodging the hard parts. Last thing: if there's a moment where I'm likely to overreach because the metaphor is pretty — and I will, it's an occupational disease — flag it ahead of time so I can catch myself.
