This one's from my birthday dinner last month, where my friends Maya and Dev spent about an hour going at it about whether the ship of Theseus is really the same ship after every plank has been swapped. Maya went first: she said it's a merely verbal question. Once you know where every plank came from and what the ship did, you know all the facts in the world; "same ship" is just a label our linguistic conventions apply, and someone with different conventions isn't wrong, they're just talking differently — so the classic debate is two people fighting over a word. Dev said that can't be the whole story, and brought up the teleporter: a machine scans you, destroys the original, and assembles an exact copy on Mars. Whether the person walking out is *you* determines whether you should dread stepping in, so a question with stakes like that must have an answer that isn't just about words. Maya answered that he was inferring a fact from a caring — it matters to me whether a novel is "good," but that doesn't mean there's a goodness-fact in the world beyond the plot and the prose and the readers, and identity is like that: the planks and the history are the facts, and "same" is our filing system on top of them.

Dev came back that prudential concern isn't like liking a novel — if survival is conventional, then a linguistic reform could make teleportation count as survival, and it's bizarre that whether I die could be up to how we decide to talk. Maya then pulled something she called the internal/external move: inside our everyday person-framework, survival claims are settled, more or less trivially, by how we use words; the only substantive question left is which framework to adopt, and that's a practical question about usefulness — which, she said, hands Dev his reason to fear the machine without positing any deep metaphysical fact of the matter. Dev's last word was that this makes the difference between dying and continuing a choice of file labels, like inches versus centimeters, and that can't be right: on Maya's logic, someone who counts zero people in a packed lecture hall is speaking a different language rather than making a mistake, but surely that person is just wrong. Maya said wrong by our lights, and that's all "wrong" can mean for a question like this.

They eventually noticed I'd gone quiet and asked me to referee, and honestly I couldn't — Dev's stakes point felt right to me but Maya's calmness about it also felt right and I can't see how both can be. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And is there something both of them are missing that would dissolve part of this? Please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts and point out the one you'd be least sure of, since I want to bring this back to them at the next dinner.
