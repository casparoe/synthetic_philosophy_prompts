hey, so I'm in a philosophy of action course this semester (we just did joint action and shared intention, Bratman's stuff on interlocking intentions, Searle on we-intentions, Gilbert on plural subjects, etc.) and my study partner has an argument that's been bugging me all week. please respond as an expert in philosophy of action / collective intentionality, think carefully and maybe brainstorm a bit before locking in an answer, because I want a real diagnosis and not a hand-wave.

her argument is supposed to show that two people can never literally share an intention:

1. For us to walk together (or do anything jointly), we need a shared intention: I intend that we walk together, you intend that we walk together, and these intentions are meant to interlock.
2. For me to genuinely intend that we walk together, I can't be intending it while expecting it to fail — so I must at least believe that you intend it too.
3. Likewise, you must believe that I intend it.
4. But your intending it only counts as genuine if you believe I intend it, and my intending it only counts if I believe you intend it — and for my belief about your intention to be rational I need to believe you believe I intend it, and for that I need to believe you believe I believe you intend it, and so on up the whole ladder.
5. No finite mind can hold an infinite hierarchy of nested beliefs, and obviously nobody runs through anything like that before a casual walk.
6. So shared intentions never exist. At best there are two individual intentions that happen to line up.
7. And if there's no shared intention, there's no joint action in any serious sense: walking together is just two people walking in parallel whose paths happen to correlate, like cars pulling away from the same green light.

She's delighted with this because she also thinks it shows that things like duets, protests, and even two people carrying a table are all, strictly speaking, coincidences plus coordination conventions. That's the part that smells wrong to me but I can't say why.

I sort of want to keep (1) and (7) if I can — shared intention talk does real work for us, and it seems like joint action isn't just parallel solos. But honestly I'd take a diagnosis over my preferences. So: which step or inference should I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what do I pay for rejecting it? if there's a slide or equivocation somewhere (e.g. between what an intention requires and what believing in the other's intention requires, or between "believes" and "explicitly believes every level"), point at the exact spot. and then tell me what the standard escape routes cost — I know Bratman, Searle and Gilbert each have one, I just don't see clearly anymore which premise each of them is denying.
