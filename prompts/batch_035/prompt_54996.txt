I teach a sophomore-level political philosophy course (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and we're coming off a week on Arendt's Eichmann book. The problem: students arrive already knowing the label "banality of evil," and the label does all the work for them — they nod along and file Eichmann under Movie Villain Who Happened To Be Kinda Dull, which is pretty much the opposite of the reading she was trying to dislodge. I want an in-class simulation where they run into the machinery that produces thoughtlessness instead of being told about it.

Here's the shape I have in mind — fill in the details: a fictional bureaucracy, cal it something aggressively dull like the Office of Population Reallocation in a made-up republic, where the class splits into small desks (Scheduling, Rations, Transport, Records, Liaison) and proccesses index-card "forms" over several rounds. Every individual card must be genuinely banal: choosing between two bus timetables, reallocating a ration shipment, checking a ledger entry for errors. No card should say anything obviously sinister. The atrocity should only exist in the aggregation — maybe a "Transfer Ledger" posted at the front that's public but boring, which updates a running count, and which I fully expect nobody to actually read. Build in career incentives too: performance ratings, promotions to "Desk Chief," a leaderboard. Eichmann's motives were at least as much about advancement and out-doing rivals as about anything else, and I want them to feel that pull. Somewhere in there I'd like a question mechanic that is procedurally real but functionally toothless — a student can, once per game, file a Query Form, but answering queries earns points for the Liaison desk, so the system literally rewards swallowing doubts.

Then the debrief. It should map specific moments in the game onto specific claims: the thoughtlessness point (nobody looked at the ledger, nobody asked what "expedited processing" meant, and no rule ever prevented them — the absence is the finding), the careerism point, the euphemism point (the game's vocabulary should have done the anesthetizing, and the debrief should show them their own forms as evidence), and the gap between feeling uneasy and having anything to actually say, which I take to be Arendt's core: what failed in Eichmann wasn't conscience but the capacity to think from another's standpoint. Also drag in the honest complication — later scholarship, Stangneth's Eichmann Before Jerusalem, argues he was a convinced ideologue who performed dullness for the courtroom. So have the debrief end on whether the game can even adjudicate that: whether the system made him thoughtless, versus whether he was a true believer hiding it, is distinguishable from the inside at all. That's honestly the question I care most about and I'd like the students to leave with it unresolved.

Also please give me: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I figure at least one student will recognize the shape of it early and start moralizing from round two, plus the usual guy who thinks playing the eager little functionary is a comedy bit — how do I handle both without killing the game), a materials list I can prep with a printer and zero budget, and a frank paragraph on what the simulation cannot reproduce. I'm guessing it's real fear, the fact that real participants couldn't just stand up and leave at the 75-minute mark, and that in the actual apparatus the alternative to going along was never neutral — but tell me what else, because the students will ask.
