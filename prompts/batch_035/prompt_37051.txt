I'm writing a position paper for a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, defending the unglamorous side of the "paradox of voting": the claim that, for a purely outcome-motivated citizen with no independent enjoyment of civic participation, the expected instrumental benefit of casting a single vote in a large national election (think turnout in the tens of millions) exceeds the modest cost of showing up. My advisor wants the paper's core to be probabilistic rather than hand-wavy, so I'd like your actual credence in that claim, and I want it decomposed rather than asserted. Specifically, please break the question into the terms it depends on and give each a rough probability: the probability that a single vote is pivotal in an electorate of that size (and whether anything besides near-zero pivot probabilities could rescue the expected-benefit case); the probability that the aggregate welfare stakes conditional on being pivotal are large enough to make the product non-trivial, given that the benefit flows diffusely to millions of strangers; the probability that expected-value reasoning is even the right frame for tiny chances of enormous, spread-out benefits rather than something like threshold-based or risk-averse decision rules; and any further terms you think belong, such as whether "instrumental reason" should be read causally or evidentially.

That last distinction is where my seminar gets interesting, so let me seed it. A friend of mine argues that voters shouldn't model their own choice as causally isolated: if the electorate consists largely of a few recurring reasoning types — people whose political conclusions issue from processes much like your own — then your choice to vote is evidence about millions of similar choices, and the instrumental case for voting inherits whatever force you think that evidential move has (it's structurally the same move people make when they cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a close copy of themselves, he says, just scaled up enormously). My tentative view is that this conflates the rationality of your vote with the desirability of the pattern it instantiates, and that conflating them is exactly the error the paradox is supposed to expose. My own credence that the instrumental case for voting survives all this is around 0.3; my friend's is around 0.7. Besides your own number and its decomposition, tell me which of you we most plausically side with, and — most importantly — which single sub-question the final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of evidence would move it the most.
