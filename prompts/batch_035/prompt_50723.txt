I picked up a popular history of artificial intelligence at an airport a while back, and one chapter has stayed with me. It's about John von Neumann's last book, The Computer and the Brain, which he left unfinished when he died in 1957 and which came out the next year from notes for lectures he was too ill to deliver. I have no academic background in any of this — I work in insurance — but the chapter summarized the book roughly as follows, and I've been turning it over ever since:

"Von Neumann, the mathematician who built the first electronic computers, spent his final months comparing the brain to the machines he knew so well, and concluded that the brain was a digital computer too — just a badly built one by hardware standards. Its components are about a million times slower than the electronics of the day, its signals are all-or-nothing like a switch, and its parts are so unreliable that the brain has to keep multiple backup neurons on standby doing the same job, the way engineers build redundant systems. The brain's only advantages are the sheer number of its parts and that it runs them in parallel. Von Neumann also found its arithmetic precision pitiful — each element carries only a couple of decimal digits' worth of information. From this he drew the natural conclusion: once engineers could pack as many components into a machine as the brain has neurons, computers would match and then overtake it, since electronics is faster and more precise. In effect, he reduced thought to computation and regarded the mind as a problem solved in principle."

Something about the confident tone at the end bothered me, though I can't say exactly why. I'd like you to go through this summary carefully. Please tell me, claim by claim, what a careful reader of von Neumann's actual book would accept as correct, what is a loose but defensible simplification, and what misrepresents him. A few things I'd especially like sorted out: Did he really classify the brain as digital, or was his view of its basic mode of operation more complicated than that? Was his explanation of how a brain built from unreliable parts stays reliable really the backup-component picture, or something else? And did he in fact make that extrapolation about computers surpassing the brain, or is the ending of the paragraph the author's gloss dressed up as von Neumann's prediction? Finally, if the last sentence doesn't follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, please show me where the slide from reporting to editorializing happens.
