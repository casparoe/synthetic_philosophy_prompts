I've been using Beeminder on and off for a couple years now -- you pledge real money on a goal and the app takes it if you fall off the graph. StickK does the same thing, my gym has a deal where you pay upfront and forfeit it if you stop showing up, my friend's dad is in recovery and at parties he hands someone his car keys at the door and tells them not to give them back no matter what he says later, and there are savings accounts designed so the money is annoyingly hard to withdraw. So there's basically a whole economy of these commitment devices now, it's the default advice for procrastiantion and addiction everywhere you look. My friends and I were arguing about them after our book club (the book wasn't even about this, it came up over drinks) and nobody could say clearly what they're for, philosophically.

Here's what I keep chewing on: what view of the person do these practices presuppose? When Beeminder charges me $90 for skipping a workout, what theory of me is that charge built on -- that "I" am really two agents with different preferences fighting over one body, and the app just takes sides with the earlier one? Does the whole industry only make sense if you buy that preferences flip *systematically* over time, that discounting thing where Tuesday-me is predictably at war with Saturday-me, or would any old inconsistency between what I resolve and what I do give the devices the same work to do? And is the practice even consistent with itself? We admire the car-keys move and celebrate Odysseus for being tied to the mast, but we find it creepy when someone signs a contract binding future-them to never quit a job or never leave a relationship, and we get squeamish about people locking in big life decisions while depressed. Maybe the difference is just about which future selves we trust, but that's not obviously principled. Also, the car-keys thing isn't even enforceable -- the dad could just demand them back and nobody could legally stop him -- so it works on shame and social pressure, while Beeminder works purely on automatic enforcement. Do those two versions even presuppose the same thing about self-control?

Reconstruct for me what these practices are implicitly committed to: what picture of the will and of weakness of will, whether the earlier self is being treated as more authoritative than the later one and on what possible grounds, and which rival views of akrasia would make the entire industry pointless or perverse. Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- my hunch is that several quite different theories of why we fail to do what we resolve would all license the same apps, and I want to know which design features actually pin down one theory and which are theory-neutral. And if we decided future-you has exactly as good a claim to being the real you as past-you does, what specifically would have to change in how these things are built or marketed?
