I've been in and around the rationalist blogsphere for well over a decade now — since the Sequences days, through the diaspora of the 2010s — and one thing that community taught me is to distrust cached labels. Which is exactly my problem with the literature on joint action and collective intentionality. Everyone nods along to we did it together, but the moment you press on concrete cases, people who sound identical in the abstract come apart completely: some are happy to say a mob is a genuine agent, some aren't; some think joint action requires mutual openness, some think secret free-riding still counts; some want to reduce everything we do together to coordinated individual action, some treat that reduction as the map deleting the territory. I want to sort my readers by where they actually land, not by which philosopher's name they like, and the only reliable way I know to do that is through cases — the way the personal-identity quizzes sort people via teleporter and fission cases rather than by asking whether they're a Parfitian.

So here is what I'm asking you to produce: a which theory of joint action do you actually hold quiz for my blog, roughly eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three plain-language answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The leaves of the key should include at least: the deflationary or summative view on which joint action is just correlated individual behavior; a Bratman-style view on which shared intention is built from interlocking individual intentions that mesh; a Gilbert-style view on which the parties incur obligations to each other via joint commitment; the irreducibly collective camp, covering Searle-style we-intentions and Tuomela's we-mode; and something in the neighborhood of List and Pettit on group agents, where a group itself has attitudes its members need not have. The cases must include ones that force these apart, for instance: two strangers who each independently slow down to let the other merge, neither knowing the other is doing it deliberately; a driving instructor and a learner whose intentions mesh only because the learner is coerced; a duet in which one singer secretly plans to bail at the last second; a flash mob of strangers coordinated only through an app; a riot, where the question of whether the crowd is doing anything together gets uncomfortable; two people moving a piano where one is secretly free-riding; and some large distributed case like a Wikipedia article or a market price, where coordination happens without anyone intending the collective outcome. No question may ask about labels or use technical vocabulary — every option should be something a thoughtful reader with no philosophy background could pick honestly.

Three quality constraints, since this is the part I actually care about. First, construct the scoring key from the logical space rather than from a list of philosophers: lay out which combinations of answers the questions make available, locate the named positions in that space, and flag any combination nobody plausibly occupies, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, be honest about where the quiz fails: I expect, for example, that obligation-style views and mesh-style views will blur on cases involving asymmetric contribution, and that irreducible-collective views and group-agent views may not be separable by any case a quiz can realistically pose — say so explicitly rather than inventing fake discriminating power. Third, tell me what to do with inconsistent answer patterns, since I guarantee some readers will endorse the coercive mesh case while rejecting the secret free-rider case, and I would rather my key diagnose them as holding an unstable position than silently shoehorn them into a leaf. The deliverable is the quiz, the key, and a short note on the tree's known blind spots.
