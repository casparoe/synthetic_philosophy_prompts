I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice theory for philosophy and economics students — sixteen of them — and I have one 75-minute session left in a unit on Newcomb-style problems and the limits of equilibrium reasoning. For that session I'd like you to design an in-class activity built around Douglas Hofstadter's Luring Lottery, the thought experiment he ran as a puzzle in his Scientific American column in the early 1980s (it's retold in Metamagical Themas). In case any details are fuzzy: the setup is that anyone may submit as many entries as they like, one entry is drawn uniformly at random, and the winner receives a fixed prize divided by the total number of entries received. Submitting an additional entry always raises your own probability of being drawn, while diluting the prize for everyone; the individually "smart" reasoning cascades into a collective disaster, and Hofstadter's readers famously swamped the contest with entries, some submitted as astronomical exponentials, so the expected payout collapsed to nothing. His proposed cure was "superrationality" — the idea that if all participants are rational and know they all face the same problem, they can each conclude that whatever they do, all the others will reason their way to doing too, and so choose the action that is best when universally chosen: submit exactly one entry.

Design a runnable classroom version. I can't use real money, so propose a payoff scheme — extra credit points, or a modest prize like a gift card funded by the department, whichever you think produces the right incentives — and give me the full logistics: materials, how entries are kept secret (my classroom has a lot of side chatter), the timeline for a 75-minute session, and how the draw is performed. I want the activity staged in two rounds: a first round run in silence with no communication, then a facilitated open discussion in which students may say anything but make no binding commitments, followed by a second anonymous round. The pedagogical point I want them to feel is the gap between dominance reasoning ("my extra slip weakly improves my odds, whatever the others do") and the superrational conclusion ("if I can see the one-entry answer, so can everyone, so act as though the group has one will") — and then the disquieting question of whether the discussion round, which should push them toward the cooperative answer, is teaching them anything about rationality or just about enforceable norms among people who'll see each other again Thursday. If you can work in a brief third stage inspired by the companion Platonia dilemma — where winning requires that at most one person acts, and the superrational solution involves everyone randomizing with equal probability — I'd welcome it, but keep it short.

Please include a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the formal distinctions: conditional vs. unconditional reasoning, the role of symmetry and common knowledge, and the difference between a one-shot game among anonymous strangers and a repeated game with a face-to-face community. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess is someone submits a googol as a joke — how should I handle that within the rules?) and be honest about which features of the real dilemma a classroom cannot reproduce. Finally, a request about how you present the ideas: separate cleanly what is settled from what is genuinely contested. The game-theoretic structure itself isn't in dispute, but superrationality is not the consensus view among decision theorists — most would classify it as a controversial move in the same family as one-boxing and evidential reasoning, and I don't want my students leaving with the impression that Hofstadter simply solved the problem. Say plainly where the experts still disagree and what turns on it.
