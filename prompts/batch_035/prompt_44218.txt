I'm in a book club that mixes novels with a bit of philosophy, and our next pairing is Brave New World with Moritz Schlick. Most people know the standard critiques of Huxley's world — the citizens are happy, but it's a shallow, engineered, consumerist happiness, so the dystopia is really about contentment traded for depth. But I've been reading about Schlick's essay on the meaning of life (the one from around 1930), and I want to try a different angle. From what I understand, Schlick held that meaning in life comes from something like youthfulness and play: a life is meaningful when it's directed at goals still unattained, because the value is in the striving and the open future, whereas a life whose goals are all achieved or guaranteed is like a game that's already been won — the play is over, and there's nothing left to matter. If that's roughly his picture, then Brave New World suddenly looks like a thought experiment built to test it: the citizens' desires are conditioned to exactly match what the state supplies, so in a sense every game is won before it starts. Their happiness doesn't feel like the shallow-contentment critique, it feels like Schlick's nightmare of meaninglessness — satisfaction with nothing left to strive for — even though nobody in the world is unhappy.

So here's what I'd like from you: read Brave New World through Schlick's theory of meaning. Where does the Schlickian lens genuinely illuminate the book — does it explain, better than the usual hedonic critique does, what's wrong with the citizens' lives, and which characters does it illuminate most (Bernard wanting more but too vain to strive for it, Helmholtz's restless hunger for real work, Mustapha Mond who gave up goals for power, John whose whole life is striving toward the unattainable)? And where does the book resist the reading — is John's life, on Schlick's terms, more meaningful than Mond's, and if so does that verdict sit right, given how John's story actually ends? I'm also curious whether Schlick's idea (again, as I understand it — correct me if I've got it wrong, but don't lean on exact quotations since I can't check them) can handle suicide at all, since I recall he thought a meaningful life isn't really threatened by death. If the honest answer is that Huxley dramatizes something Schlick's theory can't quite absorb — some way striving for the unattainable can curdle into misery rather than meaning — say that plainly, because that's exactly the kind of thing my club loves arguing about. No need for quotes from either text; we all know the plot well enough. Just a reading that takes both the novel and the theory seriously and tells me where each one bends.
