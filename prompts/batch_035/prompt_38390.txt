I teach an upper-level ethics seminar of 16 students, and I have one 75-minute session to work with. The session's target is the question whether maximal moral goodness is desirable as a way of being a person — whether someone who spent all their character-building budget on virtue, with nothing left over, would be someone we would want to be, or befriend, or be. I want them to run into this question themselves, through a game with real choices and a payoff scheme, before any of it is named in the debrief.

Design the activity with the following structure.

Part 1 — The draft. Students divide into four teams of four. Each team constructs a person by allocating 100 character points across a menu of traits I will have printed on cards in advance: roughly half moral traits (compassion, scrupulous honesty, a powerful sense of justice, patience with the difficult, willingness to sacrifice comfort for others) and half non-moral excellences (wit, culinary skill, obsession with early polyphony, dry humor about oneself, curiosity about tide pools, being a good dancer). The constraint: at least 80 of the 100 points must go to the moral traits. So every constructed person is, by stipulation, an extremely good person; the only live question is what the remaining 20 points go to. Each team also writes a three-sentence biography of their person — a day in their life, what they do on a Saturday.

Part 2 — The interviews. This is where the force of the idea should appear in what happens, not in what I say. Each team's character is put through a rotation of live scenarios, played by the other teams, where a character trait is being chosen for, not a moral verdict being rendered. The scenario cards (write me the full set, eight to ten of them) should all be ordinary and non-moral in their content: who do you call at 1 a.m. when your engagement falls apart and you need someone to sit with you; who do you want on your trivia team; who do you trust to tell you, unprompted, that your haircut is bad; who would you rather be seated next to at a wedding; whose house would you drive four hours to visit. In each round, students vote by secret ballot for the character they'd actually pick, and the votes are tallied publicly. The point I want them to hit — without my prompting it — is discovering that the character who scores highest on the moral traits, and whose biography reads as an unimpeachably admirable life, is nearly unpickable in the scenarios, and that the characters they fight over are the ones whose points went to wit and tide pools. Some students will protest that they'd of course pick the saint; let the game be arranged so that the ballot is secret and the scenarios are ones where picking the saint costs the picker something concrete in the moment.

Part 3 — The stakes and the twist. Give the game a payoff structure with two separate scoreboards, not one. Scoreboard A, the adoration score, counts ballot picks across the interview rounds. Scoreboard B, the impact score, is computed by me from a sealed sheet each team files at the end of Part 1, describing what their character does with their evenings and money — hours volunteered, donations given, causes served. The twist: have the teams, before any scores are revealed, bet their adoration points on which character they believe will win the impact score. The uncomfortable discovery I'm angling for is a dissociation they can see numerically — that the character most worth admiring from a distance is not the character most present in their own lives — and, worse, that when asked to bet, most of them will hedge.

Then write the debrief. It should map specific game moments onto the conceptual structure: the difference between admiring a life and wanting it nearby; the distinction between ranking people and ranking lives; the question of whether the unpickable character is a poorly built person or a person built by an incoherent demand; and whether the students' ballots measure something morally suspect in them, or something defensible about the shape of human relationships. Do not let me simply announce the conclusion — give me a sequence of questions, starting from the tallies, that gets them to articulate it.

Finally, three practical notes. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the ways a team can game the biography sheet to look maximally impactful, the ways the interviews collapse into comedy or into flattery of the obviously saintly character, and what to do about each. Second, tell me honestly which part of the idea the game cannot reproduce — what has to be supplied by me in the debrief because no in-class mechanic can generate it. Third, keep the whole thing runnable in 75 minutes with printed cards, ballots, and two flip charts, and no money or material prizes — the payoff scheme should run entirely on points and public tallies. The register should be serious but not solemn: these are students who will enjoy voting for a tide-pool obsessive over a saint, and the debrief has to convert that enjoyment into an argument they didn't see coming.
