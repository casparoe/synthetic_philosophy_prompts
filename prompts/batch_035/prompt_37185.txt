Please explain a conceptual issue about how historical analogies mislead, in a form suitable for a seminar on reasoning from history. The usual critique of analogies such as Munich 1938, Vietnam, or the "Thucydides trap" targets the diagnostic step: the claim that the present situation resembles the cited historical case. But I want an account of how an analogy can fail at a second, prescriptive step that survives even when the diagnosis is granted. Because history runs each episode only once, the "lesson" encodes not "this was the best available response" but "this was the response actually tried, together with the consequences we happened to observe." Please explain this diagnostic/prescriptive gap in detail: how the single-run structure of history contaminates the prescriptive content of an analogy; why "never appease," read off Munich 1938, overgeneralizes from an untried counterfactual — the concessions not made, whose consequences nobody observed; why lessons drawn from loud, observed outcomes tend to crowd out lessons from quiet episodes in which the alleged trap never sprang; and, in general terms, what an analogizer would have to establish for the prescriptive step to be legitimate rather than merely persuasive. Please answer in American English.
