I'm an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and action, and this term each of us has to design and lead one 90-minute activity session in place of the usual reading discussion. I've drawn the session on Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that, which we've just covered in The Concept of Mind along with his charge that treating intelligent action as the application of theoretical knowledge generates a regress, and his broader claim that the intellectualist picture rests on a category mistake. What I want to avoid is a discussion session in disguise: the point of the format is that the class should run into the issue in what they do before anyone names it, so I don't want to open with any framing and I don't want the words knowing-how or knowing-that to appear until the debrief.

Here is the shape I have in mind, and I'd like you to build out the full design. The activity should have two phases that pull in opposite directions. In the first phase, teams get a physical skill task — I'm imagining three-ball juggling, though I'm open to alternatives that need less floor space — together with an exhaustively explicit written method: every hand position, throw, and timing written out as instructions, plus time to study and even memorize the sheet before being scored on the task. The design should arrange things so that the class discovers, first, that possessing the complete propositional specification doesn't add up to the ability, and, second, that they can't even begin to execute the instructions without further skills they already have — I want the debrief to be able to point at a specific moment in the room as Ryle's regress made visible. In the second phase, participants face a task where being told the way, stated entirely in words, is sufficient on the spot — I'm imagining something pencil-and-paper where a rule or key, once given, lets them succeed immediately — so that the class ends the activity genuinely unsure whether the difference between the two tasks lies in the two kinds of knowledge or merely in the kinds of subject matter. I need rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring or payoff scheme that motivates real effort without real money (a team leaderboard is fine).

For the debrief, please map concrete moments from both phases onto the distinction and onto the intellectualist reanalysis — the move that says the juggler knows lots of propositions and simply lacks a few more, and that knowing how to juggle is itself knowledge of a proposition about a way of juggling — and show how what actually happened in the room could be used to press or to blunt that move. Also give me an honest section on failure modes: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this (at least one student in the class can already juggle; some participants will insist the instruction sheet helped; the timing will be tight). Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: I take Ryle's deeper point to be about the logical grammar of mental-conduct verbs, a categorial claim that no staged exercise can demonstrate in the way it demonstrates a coordination failure or a bias — so tell me what the facilitator must supply by hand, and whether the two-phase structure risks leaving people with something subtly false, e.g. that the distinction just is the psychology distinction between declarative and procedural skill, and how the debrief should guard against that collapse.
