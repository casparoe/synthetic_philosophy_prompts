I write novels for a living, which means I have spent twenty-five years watching meaning happen to texts I thought I controlled. Readers write to me about things I did not put in my books, and I cannot honestly tell them they are wrong; I have reread my own early work and found themes I am certain I never intended but can no longer prove I didn't. So I approached a recent fascination of mine — how courts read statutes — expecting the professional humility of people who also live with texts. Instead I found a fight between two schools, and I want someone to explain to me what theory of meaning each side's practice actually presupposes, because neither quite matches what I know from the inside.

The first school, the textualists, refuse to consult legislative history on the ground that a legislature has no intentions — it is hundreds of people who voted for the same words for hundreds of irreconcilable reasons — so the meaning of a statute must be fixed by the words a competent reader of the language would have taken them to bear at the moment of enactment. The second, the purposivists, read a statute as an instrument with a job to do, and are willing to let a reasonably attributable purpose bend the text when the words admit of more than one reading. Here is what strikes me about the textualists: they have killed the author — and then resurrected him as the reader, and not even a living one, but the reader of record at the date of publication, frozen forever with the dictionary of that year in hand. They arrived at an audience-centered theory of meaning by way of distrusting authors, and I suspect that route bought them more metaphysics of meaning than they bargained for.

The practice is also mixed in ways that puzzle me. The same textualist judges accept the absurdity doctrine, under which a statute will not be read to command an outcome no one sensible could have wanted, however plain the words; they accept scrivener's-error corrections, where an obvious drafting slip is repaired because everyone is confident what was meant; and the same legal system that treats statutes this way is frankly intentionalist about contracts, where interpretation is officially a search for what the parties intended and extrinsic evidence is routinely admitted — precisely the material a textualist would refuse if a legislature had produced the document.

So my questions. What theory of meaning does each half of this practice presuppose — the textualist half, the purposivist half, and the statutory-versus-contract split within a single system? Does textualism strictly need its official rationale about legislatures having no mind, or does it secretly require something much stronger: a stable, shared public language at each moment of enactment, something like ordinary meaning as a fact about a whole population, and is that a more demanding picture of meaning than the intentions it replaced? Where is the practice underdetermined — which rival theories of meaning could each part of it accommodate equally well, so that the judges' rhetoric outruns what their decisions actually commit them to? And what would judicial interpretation have to look like if the courts took my novelist's experience seriously — if meaning were allowed to drift with the living language, as it does for every reader of every novel, or if intention were openly treated as partly a retrospective construction, as it is for every author who rereads himself? I do not want a tour of the debate's famous names or a history of the doctrine; I want the implicit semantics of the practice reconstructed, and a considered judgment about whether it coheres.
