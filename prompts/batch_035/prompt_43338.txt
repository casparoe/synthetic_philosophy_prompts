Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent 35 years as a physiologist, mostly on thermoregulation and renal stuff, retired two years ago, and have since been reading a lot of philosophy of medicine and realising I have opinions I can't defend as well as I thought. I've drafted the notes below as a potential essay (blog post really, but a long one) and would really appreciate comments — especially on whether the core argument actually works, and whether I'm being unfair to the other side.

The essay's basic claim: normativism about disease is a cop-out. The idea that health and disease are partly constituted by our values strikes me as a relapse into vitalism by other means. My alternative, roughly following Boorse though I'd want to extend him: a disease is a failure of a subsystem of the organism to perform the function it was selected for, relative to a reference class of organisms of the same sex and age. This looks value-free to me, like anything else in biology — nobody thinks saying a bird's wing is "for" flight smuggles in values, so why would saying a heart that can't pump is diseased?

Where I want to go beyond the standard version is the stuff that has always bothered me as a bench scientist, and I'd like to know if it sinks the whole approach:

1. Fever. A fever is not a malfunction — it's one of the most well-characterised adaptive responses we have, and there's decent evidence that suppressing it can worsen outcomes in some infections. Same with diarrhoea clearing pathogens, or sickness behaviour making you rest. So strict naturalism seems to imply that antipyretics treat a *function*, which is absurd. My tentative fix is distinguishing the defense from the condition triggering it — the infection is the dysfunction, fever is the organism working fine — but then doesn't that mean most of what patients actually want treated (the symptoms, the suffering) isn't the disease at all? Which is uncomfortably close to conceding the normativists' point that what brings people to the doctor is the disvalue, not the dysfunction.

2. Aging. Nearly universal cellular decline, so my statistical reference-class clause makes it hard to call age-related decline "abnormal." And natural selection arguably doesn't "want" us to fall apart after reproduction, it just doesn't care — is failing at something selection never designed you to do a "dysfunction"? That one keeps me up at night a bit, honestly.

3. Heterozygote advantage. A sickle-cell carrier in a malarial region is running a system that's doing exactly what selection shaped it to do. Functionally perfect, statistically normal, and still the basis of a serious disease in one's homozygous kids. I think my framework handles this but I genuinely don't know how.

So my questions for you: does the argument for value-freeness even get off the ground given (1)? Am I wrong that Boorse-style theories have no trouble with fever and aging, or is this a known objection I havn't found yet? Is my wing/flight analogy doing more work than it should? And do you think the essay is more interesting as a defence of naturalism or as a confession that the harder cases pull toward the other side — I keep flip-flopping on which essay I'm actually writing, and if the second one is better I'd rather know now before I polish this one.
