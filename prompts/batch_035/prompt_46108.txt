I teach a second-year course on historical methods, and next week I have 16 students for a 90-minute seminar on archives and power — the idea that what we can know about the past is fixed partly by what got recorded and kept, and that the silences in the sources aren't accidents. I want a game where they feel this instead of me just saying it. Rough idea: half the class plays the records office of an archive in a fictional 18th-century colonial parish, drowning in paper and told they can only afford to keep maybe a tenth of it; the other half then plays historians two hundred years later who have to answer specific questions using only what survived. Design it properly for me: what the document slips should say and how the pile should already be skewed before anyone chooses anything (mostly official and property stuff, very little from anyone who couldn't write or rarely dealt with the state), and which questions the future historians get — I'd like at least one that's answerable from boring records the archivists will be tempted to bin, one that's unanswerable no matter what they kept, and one that only looks answerable, because no source of that kind got generated in the first place. Also give me the rules, the timing, how to hand off between the groups, what the future historians actually recieve (just the surviving slips? a catalog too?), and a debrief that maps what happened onto the different points where silence can enter: when records are made, when they're kept or destroyed, and when historians go looking for them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume the archivists keep the one juicy personal diary and chuck the tax receipts — and be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
