I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a couple years back I profiled a behavioral economist — I'll call her Dana — who's turned into a real friend. Whenever we grab coffee she runs this little game she's clearly been playing her whole career: I describe some behavior I've come across reporting, and she issues a ruling on whether it "reveals a preference," meaning whether she'd let it into the person's utility function if she were modeling them. She's steeped in the whole revealed-preference tradition running back to Samuelson, but she thinks the naive reading — whatever you choose, you must have preferred, full stop — is lazy, and she's got no patience for the old Chicago line that tastes just are what they are. She also insists she means utility in the choice-representation sense, not wellbeing, though honestly she wavers on that. I've started keeping notes on her rulings because they're weirdly consistent and I can't reverse-engineer the rule.

Her track record so far. There's a guy who knowingly pays $4.50 for the exact same latte he could get for $3 two doors down — she ruled that's a real preference, convenience is worth something to him, done. Someone who's auto-renewed a streaming subscription for four years without once thinking about it — no preference revealed, that's inattention, not choice. My own doomscrolling till 2am, which I disavow every single morning and then do again — no, and she was pretty firm about that one too. And a woman who paid triple for medicine at the only pharmacy open during a flood and says she'd do it again in a heartbeat — yes, obvious preference. Last week I was telling her about two people for a piece I'm working on and she stopped me and said she needs to "sit with" them, which she's never done before, so naturally I'm dying of curiosity about what her rulings will be.

The two cases. First, a methodical gambler: studies the odds, sets loss limits, plans his casino trips like business travel, fully informed about the expected value, and yet he told me the wanting doesn't feel like "really him" and he'd take a pill tomorrow to make it disappear. Second, a woman who picked her city on basically a coin flip — some whim about a photo she saw, zero deliberation — and now, years later, endorses it completely, says it's the best decision she ever made. So: predict Dana's ruling on each, reconstruct the rule (or rules) she's actually running. My amateur guess is that there's a "only considered choices count" rule and a "only choices you'd stand behind count" rule that both fit everything she's said so far, and they come apart on exactly these two people, but you tell me. Give me your best guess with reasons, tell me which of the two cases does more work in discriminating between the candidate rules, and design me a further case that would split whichever rules are still tied after her new rulings come in. I'm not just after the verdicts — I want the machinery, since I half suspect I'm going to end up writing about her decision procedure and I'd rather not embarass myself.
