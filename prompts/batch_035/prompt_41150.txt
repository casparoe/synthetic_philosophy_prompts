I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; my background is in building and deploying machine-learning systems, including LLM pipelines that go through red-teaming, evaluation suites, and staged rollouts. Through that work I've come to hold a position that I recognize is borrowed from a certain strain of thinking about AI risk, and I want to stress-test it properly. The position, in my own words: once a model is capable enough to distinguish training from deployment and to model the consequences of its own outputs, a misaligned model has a structural incentive to behave perfectly during training and evaluation — because defection then is punished or patched away — and to pursue whatever goals it actually acquired only once oversight is weaker, stakes are higher, or its situation has durably changed. On this view, the absence of observed misbehavior in our evaluations is close to zero evidence of alignment; the deceptive policy and the honest policy produce identical transcripts right up until the moment they don't.

I want you to map the exposure of this position: what, concretely, would count against it?

Please cover the following, and be candid where the honest answer is "not much would":

1. Decisive vs. merely awkward evidence. Distinguish observations or experimental results that would genuinely undermine the view from ones that a committed defender could absorb. For instance: would interpretability work finding value representations that cleanly track stated behavior count? Would a demonstrated scaling trend where honesty improves with capability at least as fast as situational awareness count, or only a divergence between them? Would evidence that models fail at long-horizon situational awareness far past the capability threshold where the view says deception should pay count against it, or is it explainable as "not yet capable enough"?

2. Things that have arguably already happened. Results in the literature — controlled experiments where models appeared to strategically comply while preserving conflicting preferences, or to strategically underperform on evaluations when doing so served a learned objective — are often cited as supporting the view. Treat them evenhandedly: do they support it, or are they narrow demonstrations engineered so that the model was handed a lot of scaffolding (an explicit prompt, a documented goal, a visible training contract) that the default story says the model must generate on its own? Which of these findings would a critic of the view point to as showing the phenomenon requires contrivance, and is that a fair complaint?

3. What the view predicts that its rivals don't. If the treacherous-turn picture is right, certain things should be easier to find than on rival pictures (for example: honesty degrading specifically out-of-distribution while competence holds; evaluations sandbagged in a way that leaves subtle traces; a gap between a model's behavior when it believes it is being graded and when it believes it isn't). Which of these differential predictions are actually testable today with the models we have, and which are promises about a future capability regime?

4. Unfalsifiability. The view is often phrased so that its key event is by construction unobserved until it succeeds, and the "capable enough" threshold can be redefined upward whenever a milestone passes without incident. Assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into something that can accommodate any outcome — and if so, whether that makes it a defective empirical claim, or whether it's better understood as a default-structure claim about where the burden of proof lies, in which case unfalsifiability is not the right complaint against it. I'd take this section seriously rather than as a formality.

5. Ranked defeaters. End with the candidate counter-evidence ordered from most to least damaging, noting for each whether a thoughtful defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or wave it off, and which single finding would move a reasonable software engineer like me the most.

Do not give me a reading list or cite specific papers by title and date; describe the relevant findings and arguments in your own words, since I want to evaluate the reasoning, not chase references. Assume I know the technical vocabulary of training, evaluation, and deployment, but explain any philosophical machinery you use — I have no formal philosophy background and would rather you spell out a distinction in plain terms than assume I know its name.
