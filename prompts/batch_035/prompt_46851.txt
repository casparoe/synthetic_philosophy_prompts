I'm presenting a paper to our department's work-in-progress seminar in two weeks, and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. The paper argues that weakness of will is best understood not as a failure of judgment but as the breakdown of an intrapersonal bargain. My sketch: the experimental literature on intertemporal choice suggests that both humans and other animals discount future rewards hyperbolically rather than exponentially, which makes preference reversals structurally guaranteed — I can prefer "one dessert on Friday" to "two desserts next week" tonight, while preferring the two next week when I consider the pair on Wednesday. Following George Ainslie (Picoeconomics; Breakdown of Will), I argue that stable long-range agency is achieved by bundling individual choices into personal rules, so that each choice is treated as a precedent for one's future selves, and the choice situation starts to resemble a repeated prisoner's dilemma played with oneself. Willpower, on this view, is nothing but the reputation one has with oneself. Akrasia — the smoker who stubs out a cigarette's worth of resolve for the tenth time — is then not a mysterious counterexample in the philosophy of action but the rational defection of a player who no longer believes the cooperative equilibrium is sustainable. I use this to argue that both the Socratic strategy of denying that akrasia is possible and Davidson's attempt, in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?", to reconcile acting against one's better judgment with intentional action are answering a question that dissolves once the discounting structure is on the table.

The audience is specific and mostly hostile in different directions. The seminar is dominated by philosophers of action and ethicists: one senior Davidsonian who will say the psychology is window dressing on a problem Davidson already solved; two Humeans about practical reason who think "acting against one's own motivation" is a category error and that my view concedes too much to the anti-Humean by allowing any notion of defeat; an ancient philosopher who takes the Protagoras at its word and will defend the Socratic denial; and, awkwardly, a visiting behavioral economist who actually works on intertemporal choice and will know exactly which discounting results I'm leaning on and which ones I'm glossing over.

Please give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it (the economist's "your preference-reversal data come mostly from small-stakes monetary choices; show me the external validity to addiction" is a different objection from the Davidsonian's), each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front — I suspect the Humeans can be placated rather than defeated — and where I should hold. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And if you think the paper's core equation — akrasia with hyperbolic preference reversal — is itself confused, or that my use of the experimental literature is shakier than I'm admitting, say so plainly rather than building me a prep sheet for a position that can't survive the seminar.
