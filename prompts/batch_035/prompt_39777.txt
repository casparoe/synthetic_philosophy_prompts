I am giving a departmental colloquium next month in which I defend Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility — roughly, that to be truly responsible for what one does in the way our blame practices presuppose, one would have to be responsible for the way one is in relevant mental respects, that to be responsible for the way one is in this way one would have to have intentionally made oneself that way, and that this leads to a regress one cannot escape without at some point having made oneself the way one is without in any way being responsible for being that way — which is impossible. But I am not merely re-running the argument. My paper extends it to epistemic desert: a great deal of the blame we actually assign is for what people failed to notice or failed to infer — negligence, culpable ignorance, should-have-known cases — and I argue this presupposes responsibility for one's doxastic character, for the habits of attention and standards of relevance that shaped what one saw and what one passed over. That regress runs exactly parallel to Strawson's, and I claim the argument is, if anything, stronger there, because attention is passive in a way that choice is not: even if one could somehow choose one's character, one cannot choose what strikes one as salient before the choice is made. I then take a step most people who accept the Basic Argument decline: I argue that this does have consequences for practice, and that our blaming practices should be revised into something forward-looking and communicative rather than retained intact on pragmatic grounds.

The audience is a mid-sized department with no other free-will skeptics in it. The specific composition: two Frankfurt-style hierarchical compatibilists who will press identification, wholeheartedness, and the idea that taking responsibility for a desire can break a regress even if it cannot establish ultimate self-creation; one philosopher of action who works on reasons-responsiveness and guidance control and will want to know whyresponsiveness to reasons isn't enough for the kind of blame worth wanting; a senior member who is essentially a reactive-attitudes Strawsonian, no relation to Galen, and believes the entire debate is idle because the reactive emotions neither need nor admit of justification; a libertarian, roughly agent-causalist, who has sat through many versions of this talk from many people; an epistemologist who knows the doxastic-voluntarism literature and will attack my epistemic extension where it is most vulnerable — I suspect on whether negligence-based blame really does presuppose responsibility for character, or only for specific avoidable omissions; and several graduate students who will want to know why I bothered writing any of this if no one is responsible for anything.
