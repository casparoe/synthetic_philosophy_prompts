I understand the broad strokes of Dennett's heterophenomenology, but I keep getting stuck on one specific step. The experimenter records a subject's introspective reports verbatim and then, suspending judgment about whether the reported experiences really occurred, constructs the subject's heterophenomenological world — the world as the subject takes it to be, complete with afterimages, itches, and streams of thought — treating it roughly the way a literary critic treats the world of a novel. Two questions about this. First, what work is the fictional-world construction actually doing? The reports have already been recorded as data; the intermediate step of building a make-believe world out of them looks redundant between transcription and explanation. Second, Dennett contrasts his method with autophenomenology, the would-be first-person science of one's own experience, which he argues no one can really practice, since a subject investigating their own mind must rely on the same ordinary probes anyone else would use — so where, in the resulting picture, could a sincere report's authority over the subject's own experience come from at all? Critics charge that this makes heterophenomenology behaviorism in a literary costume: a method that never lets a sincere avowal count in favor of the experience being as reported has settled by fiat the very question — whether introspection is ever authoritative — that it claims to hold neutral. Please explain the machinery carefully enough that I can see exactly how Dennett answers that charge, without taking sides on whether the answer ultimately works.
