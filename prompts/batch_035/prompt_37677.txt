I'm a novelist, and my first memoir contains a composite character (two neighbours merged into one, both long dead), roughly a dozen scenes of reconstructed dialogue, and a chronology I compressed so that two years read as one. I've been invited to defend the book on a conference panel about the nonfiction writer's contract with the reader, and the other three panellists — a magazine fact-checker, a literary editor, and a memoirist who refuses to write any scene she cannot claim to remember word for word — hold that deliberate invention of any kind voids the genre. My defence rests on a sorites-style argument: everyone accepts some reconstruction, since memory itself confabulates, selection is shaping, and even the strictest memoirist invents dialogue's exact wording; there is no principled stopping point between acceptable compression and disqualifying fabrication, so the honest line is a disclosure convention rather than a purity rule. Prepare my objection sheet: the questions these three will actually ask, phrased in their own vocabularies (verifiability, reader trust, testimony to the dead), ordered by how much damage each would do, with a short reply to each and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — in particular, whether my vagueness argument would licence the same liberties in a memoir about a living person who could be hurt by them.
