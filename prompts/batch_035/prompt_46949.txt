I'm running a two-hour discussion section for second-year philosophy students on whether the "better than" relation is transitive. They'll have just covered the standard axioms of rational preference, so they'll arrive treating transitivity as settled — a quasi-mathematical constraint on any coherent ranking, not a substantive claim open to counterexample. My plan is to build the session around spectrum arguments: chains of outcomes in which each step differs only negligibly from the last, yet the endpoints differ enormously, so that denying transitivity starts to look like the only honest response. Please plan the session rather than explain the material to me. Specifically: what has to be in place before the first spectrum case can do any work (I suspect students must first accept that "I can't tell the difference" does not entail "I'm indifferent" — but tell me if something else must come earlier); which single case should carry the whole discussion, and whether it should involve a continuum of options or only finitely many discrete steps — I can see arguments both ways, and the choice seems to matter for which escape routes stay open; the misconceptions students will predictably form (my guesses: that this is just the sorites paradox about vagueness in disguise; that any failure of transitivity would license money-pumps and so cannot be real; that the phenomenon is an artifact of imperceptible differences rather than something that could afflict "better than" itself) and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and one question I could pose at the end that would reveal whether they've distinguished the claim "transitivity can fail" from the much weaker claim "our judgments about particular comparisons are unreliable." Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I'm inclined to set aside the question of whether "better than" is even a two-place relation, but I'd take your word over my instinct.
