I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I've been trying to work out my own views on when someone has a reason to do something, by going case by case and seeing what my gut says. I'd like your help extracting the principle, if there is one. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. My colleague hates doing code reviews, always has. But he does care about shipping software that works, and reviews demonstrably catch bugs. Verdict: he has a real reason to do reviews, and I can point to exactly where it comes from — something he already cares about.

2. Me and cardio. I've tried to care about longevity; I don't, and years of trying hasn't changed that. When someone tells me I have a reason to exercise anyway, I waver. Lean: yes, there's some reason there even though nothing in me moves toward it. But it feels thin.

3. My friend's gambling. He's honest, self-aware, and reports — and I believe him — that the only thing he wants anymore is gambling. He's tried to care about his finances and his marriage and can't make himself. Verdict: he absolutely has a reason to stop, maybe the strongest reason in his life, despite there being nothing in him that points at quitting.

4. My cousin joined something between a startup and a cult. Every desire he now has — his whole motivational economy — was installed by them: the recruitment materials, the daily rituals, the community that structured his entire social life. Everything in him says stay. Verdict: he does NOT have a reason to stay. Or at least, his wanting to stay carries no weight as a reason. Those desires are defective somehow.

5. Someone tells me I have a reason to learn Rust because it's "objectively good for engineers to know." Verdict: no reason. Not a small reason — none. Unless it hooks into something I actually care about, there's nothing there.

6. My boss says "you have a reason to finish this feature by Friday." Verdict: he's making a command, not stating a reason — unless he can show me how it connects to something I care about, like the paycheck or not shafting my teammates.

The trouble is 3 and 5 look structurally similar — in both cases the person lacks any motivation toward the action — yet my verdicts are opposite. And 1 and 4 pull against each other from the other side: in 1, a desire grounds a reason; in 4, a full motivational set seems to ground nothing. I can imagine a patch: a reason must connect to motivations that would survive some kind of cleanup — not the motivations you happen to have, but the ones that would persist under better conditions, whatever "better" means. That might save 3 (the gambler's caring about his family would survive, or ought to), 4 (the cult desires were implanted, so they don't survive), and 5 (nothing of mine points at Rust under any cleanup). But then 2 gets awkward: if the cleanup is allowed to add new motivations like "cares about staying alive," it proves too much — it would give me a reason to do almost anything a well-cleaned-up version of me would want.

So: is there a principle that captures all six verdicts, or close? If the best principle forces me to drop or revise some verdict, tell me which and why. Then apply it to two cases I haven't listed: (a) a person with severe depression whose motivations have flattened to almost nothing — do they have reasons to act on projects they no longer care about but used to? and (b) me promising to review a friend's pull request, then a month later no longer caring at all — did the reason expire with the caring?

Please give the reasoning in prose first, then finish with a JSON object with exactly these fields: "principle" (your best statement of it, in one or two sentences), "case_verdicts" (an array of objects, one per my six cases, each with "case": a short label, "outcome": "preserved" | "overturned" | "revised", and "why": one sentence), "new_case_predictions" (an array of two objects for cases (a) and (b), each with "case", "predicted_verdict", "reasoning"), "hardest_conflict" (which pair of my verdicts was hardest to reconcile, and how your principle resolves it or concedes it can't), and "honest_limits" (what the principle cannot settle, stated plainly).
