Write the joint statement that a committed cliodynamicist (broadly in the tradition of Peter Turchin's structural-demographic theory and the Secular Cycles work) and a skeptical narrative historian (who holds that quantitative macrohistory produces curve-fitting dressed up as science) could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties accept — about what Turchin-style crisis indices actually measure, about the empirical regularities in the data (e.g., the recurring association of elite overproduction, declining real wages, and state fiscal stress with waves of instability) that the skeptic concedes are not artifacts, and about the limits of what the models claim; (2) a table of the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording — e.g., whether a recurring lag-lead structure in demographic and elite variables constitutes a mechanism or merely a correlation, and whether "prediction" retrodicted from pre-existing data counts as a test; (3) the cruxes, such as whether out-of-sample forecasting of instability indices is feasible over decadal timescales, whether the Secular Cycles pattern survives when coders without knowledge of the hypothesis assemble the cases, and how much disconfirming historical detail a cycle may tolerate before it becomes unfalsifiable; and (4) for each crux, the kind of evidence or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that evidence written down in advance. If part of their dispute strikes you as merely verbal — for instance, over whether describing the English 1640s and the French 1780s as instances of "the same" process is a substantive claim or a bookkeeping choice — say which part and why, and confine it to the agreed section rather than letting it inflate the disagreement table.
