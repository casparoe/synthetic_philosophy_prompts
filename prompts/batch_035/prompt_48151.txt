I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the early LessWrong days, which means I have sat through more re-enactments of the Lucas–Penrose argument than I can count: someone cites Lucas's 1961 paper, someone else brings up the mistakes Penrose later acknowledged in print, someone links Boolos's one-syllable exposition of the second incompleteness theorem, the thread runs to four hundred comments, and everyone leaves holding exactly the views they arrived with. One thing the format never produces is a case that goes anywhere unexpected, so I want one commissioned to order.

Here is the specification. The standard argument runs the incompleteness theorem in one direction only: for any consistent machine M, there is a Gödel sentence G(M) that the human mathematician can allegedly see to be true but that M cannot prove. I want the reverse-direction thought experiment: a case in which the human's putative Gödelian advantage turns against her, and something Gödel-like comes out in the machine's favor — and, crucially, it should come out because of the very fallibility and formal underdetermination that mechanists invoke when they dodge the second incompleteness theorem.

My suspicion is that the honest way to build it is an asymmetry of self-knowledge. The machine's source code is open, so it knows exactly which formal system it is; the mechanist's usual reply — that the human is *some* machine, perhaps an unsound or even inconsistent one, and that there is no telling which — leaves the human without any definite system whose Gödel sentence she could inspect in the first place. Use that lever if it works, or find another; but the Gödel machinery must be handled honestly throughout: specify which formal system belongs to which agent, which sentence is in play at each step, and which theorem — first incompleteness, second incompleteness, or Löb — does the work there. No hand-waving about anyone directly seeing truth, and no stipulating the verdicts; they must fall out of the theorems plus the setup. The Löbian obstacle discussions have taught me that these limits bite AIs reasoning about themselves and their successors, so the case has to treat both sides with the same formal care, not quietly rig one of them.

Constraints: the scenario itself should be a single paragraph; it may be semi-formal — two agents exchanging proof attempts and conditional claims — rather than kitchen-sink realism, but no omniscient predictors, no idealized superintelligences, and no human stipulated to be sound. After the scenario: (a) give the verdict a committed Lucasian would deliver, with the reasoning; (b) give the mechanist's verdict, with the reasoning; (c) identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the case collapses back into the standard Lucas arrangement once the human is granted exact self-knowledge; and (d) state the strongest Lucasian countermove, the one most likely to stop the case from counting at all.

End with a final line of the form: Verdict: [one short phrase] naming which side the case, as actually built, favors — Lucasian, mechanist, or genuinely symmetric. No hedging, and the phrase must match what your walkthrough showed, not what the folklore says.
