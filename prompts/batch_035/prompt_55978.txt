Last night my closest friend and I spent nearly five hours arguing about whether the possible inconsistency of our most basic mathematical axioms — those of ZFC, or even of Peano arithmetic — should be treated as a live intellectual problem or as idle skepticism dressed up in rigor. He defended a fallibilist position organized around a distinction he insisted on calling "practical consistency": a theory is consistent-for-us just in case no contradiction can be derived from it by any proof we could ever write down or mechanically check, and since any actual inconsistency in our foundational theories would almost certainly require proofs so long that the lifetime of the universe could not contain them, he concluded that the question of "real" consistency beyond that threshold is empty — either there is no further fact there, or there is one that could never matter to us. I resisted. It seemed to me that his view quietly assumes that the value of a proof lies solely in its being checkable, whereas I wanted to insist that the discovery of an inconsistency, however astronomically long the witnessing derivation, would retroactively alter the epistemic standing of everything already proved by shorter means: mathematical trust, on my view, is unlike a bridge, which stands until it falls, and more like testimony whose reliability, once impugned at any distance, is impugned everywhere. He replied that this makes my position irrational in an instructive way — that it treats mathematical confidence as non-monotonic in a direction no engineering practice would accept, since no civil engineer refuses to use a standing bridge because some possible bridge, somewhere, might collapse. We then argued about whether a paraconsistent compromise could give each of us what we want: keep classical mathematics in ordinary use, but quarantine any contradiction that surfaces the way software engineers quarantine bugs in a running system, neither fixing the world nor abandoning it. Neither of us could decide whether that compromise is a genuine position or a refusal to take the question seriously, and we parted without resolution.

Write an essay on whether the possible inconsistency of our basic axioms is a genuine intellectual problem or a pragmatically empty one. Weigh the case for reducing consistency to "practical consistency" against the case for treating consistency as a substantive question whose answer matters even if no contradiction is ever humanly accessible. Address my "retroactive impugnment" claim directly: does it survive his bridge analogy, or does it, as he charged, demand a kind of global distrust that no rational practice could coherently adopt? Assess the paraconsistent quarantine strategy as well — is it a coherent middle route, or does it merely change the subject from truth to bookkeeping? Do not end on a non-committal note: reach and defend a verdict, even if the verdict is conditional, and make clear what would count against it.
