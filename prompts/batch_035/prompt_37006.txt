My mom died three years ago from Huntington's disease, which we only found out about fairly late, and my brother and I have been going back and forth ever since about whether to get the predictive genetic test. We each had a 50/50 shot at inheriting it, and the test will basically tell us definitively whether we have the gene. I keep leaning toward not taking it. My brother thinks that's irrational, and his argument goes like this: the test is free, it's accurate, and if you don't want to act on the result you can just... not act on it. You hear the answer, shrug, and live exactly the life you would have lived anyway. On his view there's literally no scenario in which having a fact in your head leaves you worse off than not having it, since information is just an input and you remain in control of what you do with it.

The thing is, I don't trust that argument at all, but I can't fully pin down why. I'm the kind of person who can't unread a spoiler for a movie I was excited about — once I know something, I can't seem to stop myself from reweighting everything else around it. If I got a positive result, there's no treatment that would change the outcome, so on paper it changes nothing, and yet I can't seriously imagine my inner life being the same. Meanwhile my sister-in-law, who took a decision theory course, told me they apparently proved in class that a rational agent can never be made worse off by receiving extra information for free — something about how you can always fall back on your old plan — and that this is supposed to be a theorem, not an opinion. So now I have my brother's intuition dressed up as a formal result, and my own strong feeling that it's hiding something.

There's also a wrinkle that makes me doubt myself: my partner and I have been talking about having kids. If I take the test and it's positive, that genuinely changes what I'd do — we'd look at IVF with genetic screening so we wouldn't pass it on. In that context the information seems obviously valuable, and my brother's argument feels obviously right. Same piece of information, opposite verdict depending on what I'm planning to do with my life. So what's actually going on here? Is he right that knowing can never hurt you, and my resistance is just a psychological quirk dressed up as a principle? Or is there a real sense in which information can leave you worse off, and if so, does the difference between my two situations track something principled about when knowing is worth it? I'd love a clear way to think about this that doesn't just tell me to weigh the pros and cons by feel.
