I'm a high school junior and I run the philosophy club at my school, which meets weekly. For about two months now we've been stuck on the omnipotence paradox — the one that asks whether an omnipotent being could create a stone so heavy that it could not lift it. I've noticed something about the way our debates go, and I'd like a diagnosis of the situation rather than another round of the arguments themselves, since we have those memorized by now.

Here is roughly where we always end up. One faction says the answer is obviously yes: omnipotence means being able to do anything, and "make a stone you can't lift" is a thing, so either God makes it (and then can't lift it, so there's something God can't do) or God can't make it (so there's already something God can't do). Either way there's a limitation, so omnipotence is incoherent. The other faction says the question is malformed. On their view, "a stone an omnipotent being cannot lift" is like "a square circle" or "a married bachelor" — not a description of a difficult task but of no task at all, since lifting any stone is trivially easy for an omnipotent being, so "unliftable-by-the-omnipotent" doesn't describe anything. A third, smaller faction holds a middle position: omnipotence means the ability to do anything logically possible, and the paradox just shows that "logically possible" is the right way to define the term, or alternatively that a being could bind itself voluntarily — that creating such a stone would be an act of self-limitation, like signing away your own rights, which counts as an exercise of power rather than a lack of it. The problem is that when this third view comes up, the first faction accuses it of changing the subject ("that's not what omnipotence meant when we started"), and the second faction accuses it of conceding too much.

What I want to know is this: what kind of disagreement is this, really? My working hypothesis, though I'm not confident in it, is that it's at least partly verbal — that our two main factions are defining "omnipotence" differently (maximal power over tasks, versus power over everything coherent) and then talking past each other, with each side's "gotcha" landing only on the other side's definition. But I have doubts about that hypothesis, because it seems like there might be a real substantive question hiding underneath the definitional one: whether the laws of logic themselves are supposed to constrain an omnipotent being, or whether they are more like rules the being plays by (or even rules it wrote). I've heard that Descartes seems to have held something like the second view — that not even mathematics and logic are off-limits for God — while most later philosophers treated logic as fixed for everyone, God included, but I've only encountered this secondhand and I don't want to lean on it if I've got it wrong.

So please do three things for me. First, diagnose the standoff: is it a verbal dispute about how to define "omnipotence," a genuine metaphysical dispute about whether logic constrains power, or two different questions (maybe a semantic one and a theological one) being fought over under a single heading? Second, tell me whether the middle faction's self-limitation move actually dissolves the paradox or just relocates it — and how you would tell the difference between those two outcomes. Third, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: some distinction we haven't drawn, some way of reformulating the question, or an honest argument that this puzzle is irresolvable in principle and why. I should say that I'm more interested in the structure of the disagreement than in any particular answer, and that I'd rather be told my hypothesis is wrong, if it is, than be agreed with. Our next meeting is on Thursday, and I'd like to walk in with something better than "let's agree to disagree" again.
