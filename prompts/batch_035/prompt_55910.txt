At a workshop on the limits of computation, a senior participant dismissed a paper on analogue hypercomputation with the remark that the Church-Turing thesis is a definition, not an empirical hypothesis, so there was nothing there to test or refute. I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and while I suspected the dismissal was too quick, I could not say precisely why. The trouble, I think, is that the slogan that the thesis is "just a definition" compresses several distinct claims, differing along several axes: whether the thesis is analytic or revisable; whether it concerns formal calculability by an idealized human clerk or computation by physical systems; and whether, if it is not a mere convention, it is established by conceptual analysis, by induction over the convergence of independent formalisms, by appeal to the laws of physics, or by proof from substantive extra assumptions about what counts as a mechanism. Lay out, as a list I can use in a response paper, the different precise theses that remark could be asserting, and for each say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial or contentious, and whether it is actually false as stated. Then tell me which, if any, of those readings would genuinely license the move the discussant made with it: dismissing as confused, rather than merely mistaken, the question whether some physically realizable device could compute a non-recursive function.
