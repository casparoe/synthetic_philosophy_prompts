I'm a high school junior, and I serve as the student representative on my district's Instructional Materials Review Committee — the body that hears formal challenges to books taught in our classrooms. A case has come to us that I'm supposed to write a recommendation on, and it has turned into a genuine philosophical fight between two of the adult members, so I'd like your help drafting the memo I'll circulate. The case: our eleventh-grade English teacher wants to keep teaching Mark Twain's Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, and a group of parents has formally challenged it because of the racial slur that appears throughout the novel. The teacher's defense rests almost entirely on what Twain meant — he was, the teacher argues, a satirist exposing the moral blindness of his characters and of the slaveholding society around them, and to strike the book because of one word is to confuse the words on the page with what the book is actually saying. One of the parents, who teaches English at a nearby college, answered that this is exactly the mistake: whatever a dead author intended in the 1880s does not travel with the book into a classroom today, where the meaning of the novel is, in part, what happens when a class that includes Black students reads that word aloud or hears it read to them. She invoked the idea, which I think comes from Roland Barthes, of the death of the author — and my AP Literature teacher once mentioned something called the intentional fallacy, though I confess I only half understood it at the time.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on interpretation and the meaning of texts, and who also has practical experience with institutional decision-making, because the memo has to do two jobs at once. Please think carefully before writing — maybe sketch the strongest version of each side's position first — and then draft the written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (keep the book, remove it, or keep it under specified conditions, such as how it's framed, whether it's read aloud, and what accompanies it), with reasoning the committee members could actually respond to. Both sides deserve a fair hearing: the teacher's appeal to intent shouldn't be dismissed as naive, and the parent's appeal to the reader's situation shouldn't be dismissed as an end run around the text. One thing I'd especially like your help with is a wrinkle I've noticed but can't resolve: it's not obvious to me that the philosophy each side invokes actually entails the policy conclusion they want. Even if Twain's intentions don't fix the novel's meaning, that doesn't automatically mean the book must go; and even if his intentions did fix the meaning, that might not settle how a teacher should handle the word in a classroom. If the philosophical dispute underdetermines the decision, the memo should say so honestly and show what considerations should decide it instead. Please keep it to something the committee can read in ten minutes before the meeting, and treat me as a smart seventeen-year-old — you can use the technical terms, but explain them when they first appear.
