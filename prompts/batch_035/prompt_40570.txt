I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and every few months a manuscript or lecture crosses my desk that leans on Gödel's incompleteness theorems for a claim about something other than formal arithmetic. Over the years I've developed gut verdicts about when this is legitimate and when it's a red flag, and I'd like to know whether they add up to a principle, or whether I'm just tracking prestige. Here are the verdicts. A proof that some question about the physics of a toy quantum system is undecidable, reported as a genuine mathematical result about a physical model: legitimate, and good science writing. An argument that Gödel shows the human mind cannot be a machine: overreach, I feel, though it comes from serious people, so I hedge in print. A pop-philosophy book claiming Gödel proved that all truth is relative or that every belief system is self-undermining: an immediate red flag, no exceptions. An economist writing that no social theory can fully account for itself, "just as arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency": I wince but let it pass when the rest of the argument is good — and I can't say why that pass doesn't also cover the pop-philosophy book. A historian using incompleteness as one strand in explaining the collapse of Hilbert's program and the fading of a certain kind of foundational ambition: entirely legitimate, exactly what a history should do. And a reviewer saying the theorems counsel "epistemic humility" about science: I allow the loose version and would never print the claim that Gödel *proves* scientific theory can never be complete, though I'm not sure the distance between those two is a real distinction rather than a stylistic one. Please give me your analysis as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely; (2) the clause of it that each of my two hardest cases — the economist's metaphor and the humility-versus-proof line — motivates; (3) which of my verdicts the principle forces me to overturn, if any; (4) its verdict on a case I haven't listed, such as a theologian arguing from incompleteness to the incompleteness of any human theistic system, or a cryptographer leaning on undecidability for a security claim; and (5) what argument or consideration would move you to a different principle, or where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
