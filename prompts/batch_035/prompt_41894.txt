I've been teaching philosophy of mind for about twenty years now, and every year I teach the zombie argument and every year the class splits down the middle and stays there. You know the dialectic: Chalmers says we can conceive of a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of me with no experience at all, that whatever is conceivable is (at least prima facie) possible, so physicalism is false. The physicalists respond that zombies are only "negatively" conceivable -- we can't rule them out a priori, but that proves nothing, since water being H2O was also a posteriori necessary. Kripke's pain / C-fiber story gets invoked, Mary's room gets invoked, someone brings up the phenomenal concept strategy (the idea that special concepts, not special properties, explain the explanatory gap), and Chalmers's replies to it, and then the seminar ends exactly where it started.

Here's what I want from you, and it's NOT a rerun of the arguments. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has been completely static for decades. Please act as a senior philosopher of mind who's watched this go on and think carefully -- maybe start with some quick brainstorming before you commit, then lay out your actual answer step by step. Some candidate diagnoses to take seriously (though dont feel bound to them): Is it a verbal dispute, where the two sides mean different things by "conceivable" or by "physical"? Is it a genuine but in-principle-undecidable metaphysical disagreement? Is it really a clash of starting intuitions -- one camp finding the conceivability of zombies compelling, the other just not -- that no argument can touch because the arguments only restate the intuitions? Is the hidden question whether epistemic gaps can be brute facts or must always be explained?

My own hunch, for what its worth, is that the whole thing pivots on whether you think an a priori entailment from the physical to the phenomenal is required for physicalism, and that neither side ever argues FOR their answer to that, they just assume it. But I might be wrong about that. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, use what the sides actually say to support your diagnosis, and tell me honestly what would even count as progress here -- a new distinction, a formal result, some empirical finding about the concepts -- or whether we should just accept that this one is stuck for good.
