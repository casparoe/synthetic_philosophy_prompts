Everyone treats the 2016 Brexit referendum as a case study in populism or direct democracy or lying buses, but I want to look at it through the boundary problem in democratic theory — the question of who counts as "the people" before you can even say the people have decided. Map the episode properly first: the franchise for the referendum was basically the Westminster one, so British, Irish and Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK could vote, plus Brits living abroad for under 15 years, plus Gibraltar — but most EU citizens living in the UK couldn't, and neither could 16 and 17 year olds (even though the Scottish independence referendum two years earlier had let them vote). So an Australian on a working holiday could vote on whether a Polish nurse's future in Britain, while the nurse couldn't. With that mapping in place, run the main answers to the boundary problem over the actual franchise: the all-subjected principle, the all-affected or stakeholder principle, and whatever other candidate you think is strongest. For each, say which bits of the real franchise it vindicates, which bits it condemns, and which bits it can't even see — I'd genuinely like to know if any principle can explain why Irish citizens were in but Romanian ones weren't, other than as pure historical residue.

Then the harder direction, which is what I actually care about. The boundary problem is supposed to be circular: you can't democratically decide who the demos is, because the body doing the deciding already presupposes a boundary. So what does the referendum do to that circularity? It didn't create a demos, it assumed one — and then the result immediately re-opened the question, since Scotland voted Remain and straightaway started asking whether it's part of this demos at all, and the 3 million EU citizens and 1 million odd Brits abroad found out they'd been affected-but-not-counted all along. Does Brexit confirm the boundary problem is a genuine instability at the heart of democratic legitimacy, or does it show that in practice democracies just muddle through on inherited franchises and it never really bites? Be honest about where the theory is silent and don't force an answer — "the demos is whatever the franchise happens to be" is a real position, but I want to know if it's a defensible one or just resignation dressed up.
