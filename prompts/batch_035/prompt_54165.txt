okay so — I'm a journalist (science + ideas beat, twenty years in, mostly physics and biology stories with the occasional philosophy book review) and I've gotten myself into something. A big public astrobiology festival has asked me to give the closing address. This year's theme is "should we say hello?", and I've decided — half because I believe it, half because the organizers wanted something spicier than the annual Drake-equation retread — to argue that the dark forest hypothesis deserves more respect than it gets. To be clear about my thesis: not that it's true as a picture of the galaxy, but that it's the sharpest formulation we have of a genuine decision problem — whether deliberately beaming messages (METI, as opposed to just listening) is the kind of choice any one institution gets to make on behalf of the whole species, given that the downside is catastrophic, one-shot, and irreversible. The dark forest is bad cosmology but good decision theory, basically. That's the line I want to hold.

The room: about 150 people. A third working astrobiologists and SETI folks, including a couple who've been in the METI-moratorium fights for decades and have very strong feelings. There'll be the standard guy who thinks the Fermi paradox is just solved by the Great Filter being behind us and can't understand why we're still talking about it. A radio astronomer or two who think the whole debate is moot because our TV leakage is detectable to anyone with a big enough array, so "hiding" was never an option and worrying about deliberate signals is rearranging deck chairs. And a large contingent of members of the public who are there because they adored Liu Cixin's novels and will be disappointed if I distance myself too much from them. I have a reputation for not being a sci-fi guy, which I'd like to keep, so my replies can't read as fan service.

Please take on the role of a senior philosopher of science who also knows the game-theory literature cold and has sat through enough conference Q&As to know how they actually unfold. Before writing anything, brainstorm the likely lines of attack — really think about what this specific crowd objects to, not what a general audience would. Then write my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually face, ordered by how much damage each could do to my thesis, each phrased the way a SETI researcher would actually phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Include the one question I'm praying nobody asks.

A few threads I want woven in somewhere, because I keep going back and forth on them:

1. The dark forest treats "a civilization" as a single rational agent. My whole journalistic angle is about coordination problems, so I can't decide whether this assumption kills the hypothesis or, if real collectives can't even coordinate enough for silence to be a stable strategy, whether that somehow cuts in favor of the danger being real. Flag it whichever way and tell me what to do.

2. Earth has been advertising life for hundreds of millions of years — an oxygen atmosphere is a biosignature visible across interstellar distances to anyone with a spectrograph. This seems to undercut both "we should hide" and "if they were out there they'd have struck already," and I honestly don't know how to handle it. I suspect it's the question I'm hoping nobody asks, but you tell me if there's a worse one.

3. The "it's unfalsifiable" charge. My reflex is to say the same is true of nearly every Fermi solution — they're all just-so stories until we find something — but I have a feeling that reply is weaker than it sounds, and I'd rather know why now than on stage.

Keep it practical — I have twenty minutes of Q&A and a train to catch. No citations or reading lists; I want arguments I can actually say out loud from a lectern. And please be honest about the soft spots rather than making me feel prepared when I'm not. That's the whole point of this.
