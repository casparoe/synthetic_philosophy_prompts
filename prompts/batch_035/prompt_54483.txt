I'm the lead information designer at a state public-health agency. Nearly every week someone asks my team for a chart for a public report or a poster destined for a clinic waiting room: vaccination uptake by county, opioid deaths over a decade, smoking prevalence by age group. The charts we inherited from my predecessor are a mess of scaled-up people icons — one giant figure standing in for a million people — 3-D bars, and symbols whose size varies by whim. A few years ago I took a course in the history of design and became fascinated by Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE project, the picture statistics his Vienna team developed in the 1920s and 30s: a fixed vocabulary of pictograms, quantities shown by repeating identical symbols at a fixed unit value rather than by scaling a single figure, and strict consistency across charts, all in service of Neurath's conviction that a disciplined visual language could be read and compared by ordinary people — including across language barriers — in a way that words, which divide, cannot. His team had a rulebook; I have a folder of inconsistent charts and a communications office with opinions.

Please turn Neurath's principles into a procedure my designers can actually run on each incoming chart request. The constraints are real: it has to be executable in the half hour between a request landing and the kickoff meeting, by junior designers with no statistics background, with sign-off from the epidemiologist who owns the data. The procedure should spell out the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; when a pictogram series is the right tool versus a plain bar or line chart; how to set the unit value when the numbers are awkward (a county with 41% uptake versus one with 92%, say); what to do when the required repetition of symbols becomes visually unusable; and — this matters most — where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person, because the question is one of judgment about audience or emphasis that the principles don't settle. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases we actually face: (1) vaccination uptake across our fifteen counties, ranging from 92% down to 41%; (2) a decade-long opioid death series with yearly counts running from 30 to 700; and (3) a request from the communications office to make our infant-mortality numbers look better than the neighboring county's by choosing a flattering baseline year. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the requester won't like, and be candid about what the procedure cannot do at all — in particular, whether it can settle anything once the disagreement is political rather than perceptual.
