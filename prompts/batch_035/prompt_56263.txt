After a three-hour argument with a friend I'm left genuinely unsure what our disagreement was even about, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The subject was the color phi phenomenon: two stationary dots flash in quick succession at different positions, and observers report a single dot moving between them; when the two dots differ in color, many observers report seeing the first color move and change into the second partway along the path — before the second dot has even appeared. Dennett, in Consciousness Explained, frames this as a dilemma between two "revisions": a Stalinesque brain that withholds experience until the second flash arrives and then delivers an edited show containing the whole trajectory, versus an Orwellian brain that delivers experience in real time and then promptly falsifies the record in memory. His claim, as I understand it, is that no possible observation could distinguish the two, and that the felt need to distinguish them is a residue of the "Cartesian theater" — the assumption that there's a privileged place and time where experience "really" happens.

My friend, a neuroscience grad student, thinks this is evasion. Neural events happen at definite times; representations of red and of green have definite onsets; so there is, in principle, a fact of the matter about which story the brain implemented, and a sufficiently fine-grained recording plus a forced-choice report given mid-trial could settle it. I kept replying that anything he could timestamp is just more content in the stream — another draft, not the moment of experience itself — but I noticed I couldn't say what would make me wrong, which made me suspect I'm defending a verbal maneuver rather than a position.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden empirical question about postdictive windows and their boundaries, which better experiments could close? A verbal dispute about the word "experience," with the two of us reserving it for different things (functional integration versus something phenomenological)? Or two different questions under one name — one the neuroscience can answer and one that is ill-posed as posed? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a sharper experimental design, a conceptual distinction, a reformulation of the question — that would count as real progress, or should we expect this one to stay stuck, and why?
