I am a junior on my high school's debate team, and this year's practice topic concerns whether religion is, on balance, a force for good. Nearly every round, someone on the other side reaches for the same move: religion, they say, is a mind-virus — Dawkins and Dennett made the comparison famous — a thing that spreads because it is good at spreading, not because it is good for the people who carry it. The conclusion they draw is the one you would draw about any pathogen: it should be treated, contained, and ideally eradicated, and the fact that it is widespread proves nothing in its favor except the effectiveness of its transmission. A teammate of mine counters with an analogy of his own: religion is less like a virus and more like a domesticated crop or a symbiotic gut bacterium, something humans have cultivated because it does things for us, whatever else it does to us. I have a suspicion that both sides are using their analogies as arguments rather than illustrations, but I am not sophisticated enough in either biology or philosophy of religion to untangle it myself, and I would rather not repeat a move in a round that I cannot defend in cross-examination.

Could you audit the virus analogy for me, the way you might audit any argument that leans on a comparison? Specifically, I would like an explicit mapping: in the analogy, what plays the role of transmission, mutation, virulence, and the host's immune system, and what, if anything, plays the role of the epidemiologist? Then I would like to know where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for how it is being used in rounds. A few places where I suspect trouble, though I may be wrong about all of them: first, calling something a virus seems to import the claim that it is bad for the host, but biologists tell me there are viruses that have been domesticated by their hosts — that parts of the placenta depend on genetic material that came from ancient viral infections — so is "virus" even a verdict about harm? Second, a trait can spread because it benefits the group rather than the individual carrying it, so if religion is adaptive for communities while being costly for particular believers, does the analogy leave room for that, or does it quietly force a choice between virus and symbiont that the biology itself does not force? Third, my opponents move from "it spreads for its own sake" to "its claims are false," which sounds to me like a fallacy, but I would like to understand exactly why the origin of a belief cannot by itself settle its truth, and whether the analogy ever actually addresses truth or only spread. Fourth, and this is my own addition, so please be honest about whether it is any good: if the virus picture were right, it would seem to predict that what we would want is something like a vaccine — an attenuated version that delivers whatever benefits there are without the costly parts — and I have read that secular congregations, like the Sunday Assembly and various humanist communities with Sunday meetings, hymns, and coffee hours, have been described by their founders in roughly those terms. Does that prediction coming true count in favor of the analogy, or does the fact that these congregations have struggled show that the analogy was carrying weight it could not carry?

Finally, my teammate's rival picture: give it the same treatment. If religion is a symbiont or a cultivated crop, what maps onto what, what does that comparison smuggle in — for instance, that whatever is functional is thereby legitimate, or that there is a gardener with standing to cultivate — and where does it break? At the end, I want a verdict I can actually use: whether either analogy, once its disanalogies are on the table, can still support the conclusion it was brought in to support — eradication in the one case, preservation in the other — or whether both sides in my rounds have been letting their metaphors do their arguing for them. If there is a better analogy available for the phenomenon, one that is fairer to what we know about how religions actually change over time, I would like to hear that too.
