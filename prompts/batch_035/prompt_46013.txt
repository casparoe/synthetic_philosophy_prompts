I've been keeping a list of my actual gut reactions to cases about future people and wronging someone who doesn't exist yet, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them or whether my intuitions are just a mess. So, case one: a teenager decides to have a child she's in no way ready to raise, the kid has a rough childhood but a life that's still clearly worth living -- I feel like she did something wrong, and wronged the kid somehow, even though if she'd waited it would be a different child, so this exact child can't coherently complain that they'd have been better off. Case two: society spends down its resources over two centuries, so the people alive in the depleted future have worse lives than they would have -- but again, different people would exist under a sustainable policy, and those future folks still have lives worth living -- and I feel like that's a massive wrong too. So far so good for me, but here's where it gets tangled. Case three: a clinic tells a couple that if the woman conceives within the next month while she's on this medication, the child will very likely be born with a serious but manageable condition, and waiting a month means a different, unaffected child; the couple go ahead anyway, and I actually feel less angry about this than about a completely comparable case where a couple conceives knowing the child will have the same condition but where delaying wouldn't change the child's identity -- like a genetic condition the father carries where it's the same kid either way. That asymmetry feels like it shouldn't survive scrutiny, but it's stubbornly there. And case four, the one that keeps bugging me: the deaf couple who deliberately select, using a deaf sperm donor, for a child who will be deaf, on the grounds that they want a child who shares their culture and language, and the child grows up happy and deeply embedded in that community. My gut reaction is that this is somewhere between permissible and borderline -- much more defensible than the medication case -- and I suspect that's because I'm letting the parents' cultural attachment do work I wouldn't let a hearing couple's mere preference do.

So what I'm asking is: is there a principle that gets all these verdicts? The obvious candidate I keep half-formulating is that you wrong someone when you make their life go worse than some threshold it was within your power to give them, identity-shifts be damned -- but I can see that's either incoherent or too strong when I try to state it precisely (what threshold, and worse relative to what, when there's no same person to compare against?). And I have a nagging worry that some of my verdicts are contaminated by thinking a child with the condition is worse off full stop, which is not a thought I endorse on reflection. Please tell me which principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the pattern, what each one says about a case I haven't listed (make one up -- ideally something involving present people too, like whether the same logic applies to choosing to bring a pet into a neglectful home), and straight up which of my verdicts has to go if everything else is to be saved. I'd rather lose one intuition than patch things with some ad hoc hybrid, but if a hybrid is genuinely needed I'd like to see what it looks like and what it costs.
