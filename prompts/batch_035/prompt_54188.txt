I'm a philosophy PhD student doing a placement with our national transport safety board — specifically the team that classifies blame after incidents involving autonomous or semi-autonomous systems (for now mostly advanced driver assistance, some port robots, and we're getting the first wave of warehouse-to-truck logistics systems). Our investigators are engineers, they hate philosophy, and every time a system does something nobody exactly intended we get a fight over whether to file it under operator error, engineering defect, or the increasingly common "emergent behaviour, no responsible party" box — which our legal people say we can't keep using because it basically means nobody did it. So what I need is a decision procedure they can actually run in an afternoon during an investigation: a question tree that takes them from "here's the incident trace" to either a defensible attribution (operator, deployer, manufacturer, certifier...) or a documented finding that this one is a genuine responsibility gap, plus what to record either way.

Ground it in whatever the literature on responsibility gaps actually argues — control conditions, knowledge/foreseeability conditions, tracing through collective action, all that — but translated into questions an engineer can answer from the logs. It has to handle the hard cases: the operator who had nominal control but ten seconds of reaction time, the training-data choice that made the behavior unknowable to the engineers but foreseeable to the deployer, the emergent interaction between two components each built by a different company. Mark the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgment call has to happen, and be upfront if the honest answer is that "responsibility gap" sometimes just means "our attribution concepts don't fit here" rather than "no one is responsible." Then run it on three hypotheticals: a delivery robot that optimizes a route in a way that traps a wheelchair user in an elevator lobby, an ADAS system that disengages two seconds before a crash after the driver had learned to stop paying attention, and a case where two route-planning systems from different vendors deadlocked in a way neither vendor could reproduce afterwards. If the procedure gives a conclusion the safety board won't like in one of them, good, keep it — I want the fights now, not in the report.
