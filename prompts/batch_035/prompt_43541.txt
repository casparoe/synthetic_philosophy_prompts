My flatmate is doing a masters in philosophy of language and she's decided promises are impossible. Not just breakable — normatively empty, like pretend money. She cornered me over the washing up and I've been stuck since. Here's her argument, reconstructed as fairly as I can manage:

(1) When I promise to pick you up from the airport, the utterance itself creates an obligation — before I spoke you had no claim on me, after I spoke you do. That's just what promising is; the words don't describe anything, they do something. (Austin performatives, fine, I accept that part.)

(2) Creating an obligation means imposing a requirement on someone who wasn't under it before.

(3) You can't impose a requirement on a person without their consent. Otherwise anyone could conjour duties out of thin air just by talking — I could walk up to a stranger and say "I hereby oblige you to wash my car."

(4) When I promise, the person the requirement lands on is me — or rather my future self, who will actually have to drag herself out of bed at 5am.

(5) My present utterance is not my future self's consent. She said nothing. She may deeply resent the whole arrangement.

(6) So either the promise creates no obligation (and promising is a collective fiction), or it creates one without consent and (3) turns out false anyway.

Then she says it gets worse. (7) Everything normative that comes out of a mouth is in the same boat: contracts, oaths of office, "I do" at a wedding, "I consent" at the hospital. Each is just a noise made by a present person purporting to bind a person who exists later. (8) So no speech act ever creates an obligation — signing a lease, swearing an oath, consenting to surgery, it's all normatively inert. Speech is just breath with manners.

I refuse to accept (8). I've promised to drive her to the train station tomorrow and I fully intend to feel bound. But I went through (1) to (6) with her and every single step looked fine. Which step is the weak one? And if I reject it, or reinterpret it, what am I paying — what else goes with it? I'd most like to keep (3), the consent principle, because it seems to be doing real work elsewhere, but I can't see how to keep it and not loose the promise.

One more thing that's bugging me: she claims (3) is untouchable anyway, because the consent principle is just what makes utterances like "I consent" meaningful in the first place — so the principle and the performative are supposed to prop each other up, and attacking either brings down the house. Is she right about that, or is that mutual support actually where the whole thing is weakest?
