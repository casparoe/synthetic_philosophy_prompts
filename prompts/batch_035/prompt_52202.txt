I'm weighing how much weight to put on the situationist critique in a paper on whether empirical psychology can touch ethical theory, and before I write it I want a number to push against. Please give me your credence that situationism, in its strongest defensible form, succeeds against virtue ethics: roughly, that ordinary people's behaviour is insufficiently consistent across situations for trait-attributions like "honest" or "compassionate" to do real explanatory and predictive work, and that this undermines virtue ethics as an approach rather than just a naive picture of traits. Decompose the credence into terms you think belong — I'd expect at least: (a) the probability that the classic findings (obedience and mood effects on helping, the Hartshorne-and-May-style work reportedly finding cross-situational correlations in character around .2, if I remember those figures right) reflect real, large situational effects rather than weak effects amplified by selective reporting; (b) the probability that such effects, even if real, threaten virtue ethics itself and not merely the assumption that most people already have stable traits; and (c) the probability that virtue ethics was ever committed to empirical claims about how most people behave, as opposed to an account of what good character is and why it's worth cultivating. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, show how you combine them, and tell me which term the total is most sensitive to. Then, as a separate exercise: suppose a large, well-powered, pre-registered study found that adult helping behaviour is consistent across situations at around r = 0.5 — tell me which of your terms that would move, roughly how far, and whether it would change the headline number. Throughout, please mark which of your sub-claims are settled among people who actually work on this and which are genuinely contested between, say, philosophers sympathetic to empiricism about character and those who think the whole debate targets a strawman — I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus.
