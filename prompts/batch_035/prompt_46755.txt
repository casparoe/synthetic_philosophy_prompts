I teach a graduate seminar on democratic theory and I'm putting together the take-home final. My problem is that almost every question I've ever seen on the boundary problem lets students get away with reciting the all-affected interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, listing a counterexample for each, and stopping there, which anyone can do straight from the lecture notes. I want a question that forces them to actually adjudicate. The idea is to build it around a fictional scenario I can hand out with the exam, something like this: the Republic of Costa Serrana is a mid-sized state whose demos currently consists of all adult citizens. Its territory includes Isla Cardonal, an island annexed sixty years ago whose inhabitants pay taxes and are subject to its courts but were never offered citizenship; it hosts roughly 400,000 guest workers on rolling five-year permits, some of whom have now lived there for two decades; a lithium mine on the Costa Serranan side of the border is leaching waste into a river that 2 million people in the neighboring Republic of Alvea depend on; there is a national debate about whether the declining birthrate means today's voters are obliged to give weight to the interests of the not-yet-born; and about 300,000 citizens live permanently abroad but still vote by mail. Feel free to tweak the details if you think it sharpens the cruxes, but keep the basic structure: each group should be designed to pull a different principle in a different direction, so that no single principle cleanly covers all the seperate cases.

The question should be answerable in about 90 minutes and worth 25 marks, and it has to require three things of a strong answer: diagnosing which group is a hard case for which principle and why; saying what to do when the principles issue conflicting verdicts (the mine's downstream victims are clearly affected but not subjected, the islanders are arguably both, the guest workers are subjected in some respects and not others); and confronting the view that the boundary problem cannot be solved by any principle at all, because the very people who would apply the principle are themselves picked out by it — the regress or bootstrapping worry. A student who just taxonomizes principles without ever taking a stance should not be able to scrape an A.

So please give me three things: the exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of around 700 words that actually takes positions instead of surveying the options, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (totaling 25) plus, for each point, one sentence on how students typically fumble it. Please write all three parts as continuous prose, no bullet points or headings or tables, since I paste the whole final into a single prose document and formatting just breaks everything.
