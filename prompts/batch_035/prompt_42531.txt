I'm a high school senior taking an ethics elective, and we recently spent two weeks on Peter Singer's essay Famine, Affluence, and Morality (1972). The argument my classmates keep repeating is the shallow pond: if I pass a pond and see a small child drowning, and I can wade in and pull her out at the cost of ruining an expensive pair of shoes, I plainly must do it; distance, the number of other able bystanders, and whether the child is "my" responsibility make no difference to that; Singer then says we can't see why any of those factors would start mattering just because the endangered child is far away and helped through a charity. The conclusion drawn in class is that our duty to give to distant strangers is exactly as stringent as the duty to wade in — roughly, give until giving more would cost something of comparable moral importance.

Our teacher then pushed back with two objections, and I can't work out how much damage they actually do. First, the coordination objection: at the pond there is one rescuer and one child, whereas famine relief is a collective task shared by millions of potential givers. She told us this line of thought is associated with the philosopher Liam Murphy, and that on such a view my duty may be capped at my fair share of what would be needed if everyone did their part, so it's unfair to demand that conscientious people shoulder the slack of non-givers. Second, the mediation objection: at the pond I see the child, I see the water, and I know my own action is what saves her; with a charity, the causal chain runs through an organization, exchange rates, logistics, and foreign governments, so the effect of my marginal dollar is genuinely uncertain in a way that muddy shoes are not.

I've also noticed the analogy doing extra work beyond what Singer himself claims: our school's effective altruism club uses the pond to argue not merely that we must give, but that we must give only to whichever charity is currently provably most cost-effective — as if the pond case licensed a whole decision procedure about how to give, not just how much.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Specifically: (1) a correspondence table mapping the elements of the pond case — the child, the water, the ruined shoes, the other passersby who do nothing, the immediacy of the rescue — onto the famine case; (2) the disanalogies sorted into those that genuinely threaten the demanding conclusion and those that merely change the flavor of the situation — for instance, does it matter that at the pond I cannot delegate the rescue, whereas with famine relief I can only act through intermediaries, or that at the pond there is one victim while with famine relief my money is spread across many? (3) a verdict on whether the demanding conclusion survives once the coordination and mediation objections are granted, or whether the pond was carrying the conclusion all along; and (4) if the pond fails, a better analogy for our duties to distant strangers, with a note on which conclusions that better analogy can and cannot support.
