At a graduate workshop on population ethics next month I will be defending a view I call decaying asymmetry: suffering counts against a life more than happiness counts for it, but the weight of the asymmetry is not constant — it decays as the life's overall welfare level rises. Adding a given pain to a life near the suffering floor matters a great deal; adding the very same pain to a flourishing life matters much less, proportionally, than either a fixed asymmetry or classical utilitarianism would imply. At the limit of very happy lives the view collapses into ordinary symmetrical utilitarianism; near the floor it approaches strong negative utilitarianism. My argument runs: (1) the intuition that pushes people toward suffering-focused ethics — the priority of relief over benefit — is at its strongest precisely where lives are worst and fades as lives improve, so it supports a graded, not a lexical, asymmetry; (2) a fixed asymmetry generates the absurd verdicts opponents use to caricature negative utilitarianism, such as near-indifference to creating something close to paradise because it contains a pinprick of suffering; and (3) indexing the asymmetry to welfare level is the simplest repair, and I have been illustrating it with a weight that shrinks roughly exponentially as the life's total welfare rises.

The audience is about fifteen people, and I need to know what each part of it will throw at me. Four or five are committed classical utilitarians who work on population ethics and know the repugnant conclusion literature cold; they will press me on where exactly my view stands on the great population axioms and whether the decay function just reimports everything they already accept, plus the uncomfortable question of whether, near the floor, I inherit the standard reductio of negative utilitarianism — that a world of very bad lives relieved by small comforts could rank below no world at all. Three are welfare economists from the policy school who think in QALYs and will object the moment my view makes welfare non-additive across persons or state-dependent within a life; one of them will ask whether the decay function is even coherent as a measurement device rather than a metaphor. One Kantian will argue that any graded aggregation, however cleverly weighted, misses the point that persons are not containers. Two effective-altruism-adjacent students came sympathetic to suffering-focused views and will be disillusioned if I concede too much. The rest are neutral.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way each sub-audience would phrase it, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is soft, and a separate note on what I should concede up front to buy credibility. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — the calibration question, whether there could ever be evidence fixing the shape of the decay function rather than merely tolerating any shape we like — and tell me honestly: does my "collapses into symmetrical utilitarianism at high welfare" move, which I think is my best card against the classical utilitarians, cost me the support of the suffering-focused students, and if so, is that a trade I should make?
