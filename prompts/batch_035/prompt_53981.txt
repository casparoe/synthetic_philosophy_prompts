I'm an undergraduate in a political philosophy seminar, and last week's debate on epistocracy versus democracy has been nagging at me ever since. The proposal on the table was a moderate one: not rule by experts, but a competence requirement for voting — an exam covering basic political knowledge (which branch does what, who your representatives are, roughly what's in a budget), with everyone who passes receiving an equal vote, the way everyone who passes a driving test gets a license. Our TA defended it with the driving analogy: we accept licensing for driving because bad driving harms other people, and voting steers an entire polity, so testing before voting is the same safety logic at a larger scale. A classmate shot back with the literacy-test analogy: formally race-neutral exams were the textbook instrument of disenfranchisement in the American South, so an "epistocracy" that starts as a competence test ends up as a tool for whoever writes and grades the test. Neither side budged, and I came away feeling that both of them were doing all their arguing inside an analogy rather than about the proposal itself.

Please audit both analogies for me. For each, set out the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the license examiner, the road test, the other drivers, the harm of a collision, and on the other side what plays the role of the targeted group, the registrar with discretionary power, and the grandfather clauses — and then say where the mapping breaks. I'd like the disanalogies sorted into those that matter for the conclusion the analogy was used to support and those that don't: for instance, if the driving analogy fails because voting is partly an expression of equal civic standing rather than a purely instrumental activity, does that actually block the exam, or only the rhetoric around it? And if the literacy-test analogy fails because our hypothetical exam is administered uniformly to everyone and scores mechanically, does that blunt it, or was the historical force of literacy tests precisely that a formally neutral test can still be written and graded so as to fail a chosen group, in which case the analogy reasserts itself at one remove? If one side (or both) has a better analogy available, offer it and say what it can carry that the original couldn't. To give you a second variant to test against: our professor sketched Mill's nineteenth-century proposal for giving extra votes to the more educated, so you can check whether your conclusions hold for a graded scheme and not just a pass/fail threshold.

Finish with a line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, which of the two analogies — the TA's or my classmate's — can actually carry the conclusion it was deployed for in our seminar, or whether neither can.
