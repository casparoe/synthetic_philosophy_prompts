Novelist here, working on a book where Adam Smith — yes, that one, the Wealth of Nations guy — ends up in the present day (the how doesn't matter, accident at a physics lab, it's mostly a fish-out-of-water story that gets more serious as it goes). He lands with a computer science postdoc who's weirdly obsessed with the 18th century, so he gets a crash course in modernity, and at one point she takes him along while a scandal unfolds: a professor's decade-old forum posts get dug up, a screenshot goes viral, and within about three days he's fired, disinvited from a conference, and his book deal is gone. A student group is also campaiging to get a controversial speaker disinvited from campus. My postdoc character keeps asking Smith what he makes of all this, and I need to write his answers without turning him into a sock puppet for my own opinions.

So — what would Smith actually say? I know he's not just the free-market guy; the Theory of Moral Sentiments is all about how we judge conduct by putting ourselves in other people's shoes, plus that impartial spectator business. It seem like there's interesting material in several directions and I want to know which ones are actually Smith and which I'd be making up:

1. As I understand it, his story about resentment is that the wronged person's desire for payback only becomes legitimate punishment when bystanders can go along with it. Modern pile-ons are basically the spectator machinery running at hyperspeed — millions of spectators forming a judgment in hours. Would he see that as sympathy working exactly as designed, or does something break when the spectators vastly outnumber the actual wronged parties? My instinct is the second, but I don't know what in his system would say so.

2. I half remember him arguing somewhere that a bystander will sympathize with the victim's resentment but only up to a point, and that punishment beyond what spectators will endorse is itself an offense. Does that map onto anything in the modern cases, where often nobody who was actually hurt by the old posts is even leading the charge — it's strangers doing the resenting on someone's behalf?

3. The justice/beneficence distinction — I gather he thought justice was the only virtue you could enforce with coercion, and that everything above the minimum (generosity, warmth, civic-mindedness) can only be met with disapproval, never force. Firing someone, pulling a book deal, disinviting a speaker — are those coercive punishments in his sense, or just the natural consequence of other people withdrawing their approval? Where exactly does he put that line, and does it hold up when the withdrawal of approval is total, ie you can't eat anymore?

4. The impartial spectator is supposed to live inside you, right, an internalized judge you consult even when nobody's watching. What happens to the shamed person's internal spectator when the entire external world is screaming at them — does Smith's system even have a story for that, or did he never imagine spectators that numerous and that loud?

I don't need a verdict on whether cancel culture is good or bad. I need Smith's likely reactions with the actual reasoning he'd use, including the places where his own commitments would pull him in two directions at once — my editor wants a scene where he argues with himself. And flag where you're extrapolating beyond anything he wrote, because I don't want to put words in his mouth that a Smith scholar would laugh at. For plot purposes you can assume he's skimmed a couple of modern pop-sociology books by this point but no modern philosophy.
