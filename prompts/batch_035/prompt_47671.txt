I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'd like a careful conceptual explanation of something I keep bumping into. Please bear with the framing; it's the only vocabulary I have.

As I understand it, standard epistemic logic models an agent's knowledge as closed under logical consequence: if the agent knows p, and knows that p implies q, then the model says the agent knows q — and, by extension, knows every theorem derivable from what it knows. Real agents plainly aren't like this: I "know" arithmetic, in some sense, yet I can't produce the proof of a hard conjecture that follows from the axioms I accept. This gap is what I gather philosophers call the problem of logical omniscience.

I don't want a history-of-the-field survey. I want you to work through a specific angle, because it maps onto something I deal with at work. In computing, there's a fundamental difference between a system in which a fact is derivable and a system that can find or recognize the derivation at acceptable cost: a property can hold of all well-typed programs, while constructing the proof of that fact for a given program can be arbitrarily expensive. That distinction — derivability in principle versus proof search in practice — seems to be exactly where the philosophical problem lives, so please organize the explanation around it. Specifically:

1. Is it fair to say the standard knowledge operator behaves more like a provability operator than a description of any actual epistemic state? If so, is the "problem" less that epistemic logic is wrong and more that it was answering a different question — one about what follows from an agent's knowledge rather than what the agent can access?

2. How do the main proposed repairs map onto distinctions I'd recognize from computing? For instance: treating knowledge as resource-bounded proof search; separating implicit knowledge (everything entailed by what you know) from explicit, currently-available knowledge; or modeling agents with a limited vocabulary or awareness, so that whole classes of propositions can't so much as be entertained by them. For each such repair, I'd like to know what it gives up — I assume things like closure under known implication — and why that tradeoff is considered acceptable or unacceptable.

3. Is there anything like a settled view on whether this is a bug in epistemic logic or merely a misreading of it? That is, can the idealized notion be defended as a legitimate idealization, the way ideal fluids or frictionless planes are in physics — useful in the limit, with the departures from reality being the explanandum rather than a defect — or does the idealization do real damage when these logics are put to work, say in formal treatments of games or of knowledge in distributed systems?

Where it helps, ground things in computational analogies: type systems, proof length versus proof existence, the gap between a problem being decidable in principle and being tractable in practice. I'd rather have a genuine conceptual working-through, including honest remarks about where the analogy between knowing and computing breaks down, than a list of positions.
