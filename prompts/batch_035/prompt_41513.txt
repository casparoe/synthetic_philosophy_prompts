So heres my situation. I'm a novelist, and my third book (draft done, out next year hopefully) follows two brothers who each text while driving on the same stretch of highway, on the same night. One clips a mailbox and drives home shaky. The other kills a kid who was crossing the road. Same negligence, same act, wildly different outcomes. The whole conceit of the book is that my narration judges them identically — I never let the second brother's guilt curdle into "worse person" territory — while the town around them does exactly that, and the reader is meant to sit in the gap between those two judgments and feel uncomfortable. Basically I'm smuggling the moral luck problem (Nagel and Williams territory) into the craft itself, never explained on the page.

Next month I'm giving a craft talk to my writing group defending this choice: five literary novelists, one of whom also writes crime fiction and is very much of the "actions have consequences or the plot feels rigged" school, and one with a philosophy background who'll smell a thesis-novel from a mile off. It's a friendly room, but they will come for me on this, and honestly I have doubts myself about whether it works.

Act as a philosopher who also works as a story editor — someone equally at home in the moral luck literature and at a workshop table. Before writing anything, actually brainstorm the craft-side objections from these specific people, not generic ones. Then give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a novelist would put it — in their vocabulary of pacing, payoff, catharsis, character arcs, reader trust, not "consequentialism" — with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what I have to hold the line on no matter what. And include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, plus my answer if it comes anyway.

Two things I insist on: be decisive. Don't hand me "you could argue either way" hedging — tell me what to actually say, where my position genuinely breaks, and whether I should restructure the ending or defend it as is. And be honest about the soft spots even if they'd sink the conceit. American English, and keep it conversational, like a smart friend prepping me over coffee — not an academic paper.
