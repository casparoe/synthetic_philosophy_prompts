I'm the editor of my high school newspaper, and every week I face the same problem: deciding whether to run a story on the evidence we have by press deadline, which is sometimes one anonymous source or a single first-hand account with no corroboration. For a philosophy project attached to this work, I would like the debate between W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" and William James's "The Will to Believe" converted into an actual decision procedure I can apply before each issue. It should capture more than Clifford's slogan: in particular the part of his argument where the harm of believing on insufficient evidence lies in the habit it trains — one careless believer makes credulity easier for everyone downstream. And from James it should take the exact restrictions he places on the right to believe: that the option before you must be live, forced, and momentous, and that the right only applies where evidence genuinely cannot settle the matter in the time available. The procedure should also address a question I can't resolve myself: whether publishing a claim with a hedge — 'according to one source' — counts as escaping the Clifford problem or is just a disguised way of inducing belief in readers while pretending not to have it myself.

Please give me the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops and hands the decision back to me as a matter of values rather than facts; and then worked applications to two cases: first, an anonymous email claiming a coach pressured a teacher to raise an athlete's grade, and second, a named student's sole-source account of an assistant principal's conduct. Then run it on a third case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — for instance, a well-sourced rumor that the school board will cut an arts program at a meeting scheduled before the following issue, where the procedure tells me either to sit on something urgent or to run it with less certainty than I'd want. Be explicit about which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure, say how confident you are in each part of the procedure, and single out the point you are least sure of. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a short phrase, the single step of the procedure you judge most likely to misfire.
