I'm a senior engineer at a mid-sized municipal water utility — roughly 400,000 customers, a legacy of lead service lines, an elected board, and a public health advisory panel that must sign off on any decision that changes water quality. Over the last few budget cycles I've come to suspect that our hardest internal debates are secretly arguments about the doing/allowing distinction, though nobody at the table ever calls it that. When we switch coagulants and a byproduct risk shifts from one group of customers to another, we did something. When we defer lead service line replacement for five years to fund a plant upgrade, we arguably allowed something. When we decline to install backup power at a pumping station, we're in the strange territory of double prevention: nobody dies because of what we did, but people die in a prolonged blackout because of what we didn't install. Our lawyers care about liability and regulatory exposure, but I care about the moral bookkeeping, and I want it done in a principled way before the next emergency rather than improvised during one.

Please write a decision procedure my staff can actually run on a candidate decision: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a clear verdict, and where it explicitly hands the question over to the advisory panel as a values question that engineering can't settle by itself. It should be grounded in what the doing/allowing literature actually says — the killing/letting die cases and their close cousins, the bare-difference argument that the distinction is morally inert, the attempts to save the distinction by pointing at how a harm is produced rather than whether it is produced, and the challenge from those who hold that failing to prevent harm and causing it are morally on a par — but translated into language a staffer with no philosophy background can apply in an afternoon memo, and honest about which parts of the underlying theory are load-bearing in the translation and which you deliberately dropped as unworkable at our scale.

Then run the procedure on three cases we genuinely face in some form: (1) a coagulant switch that would lower a chlorination byproduct linked to bladder cancer for the system as a whole while slightly raising the risk of gastrointestinal illness for the subset of customers on an older branch with different pipe chemistry; (2) a proposed five-year deferral of lead service line replacement in order to fund a treatment plant upgrade that averts a different and larger risk; and (3) declining to install backup generators at three pumping stations because the same money, spent on pipe replacement, would save more lives in expectation. At least one of these should come out with a verdict the board will hate — I want to see which one, why, and a defense of the procedure's honesty in that case rather than a fudge that quietly reweights the steps until the comfortable answer falls out. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire: the recurring situations in which the doing/allowing framing does more harm than good at a public hearing, and the points at which the distinction our lawyers care about and the one the philosophers care about come apart.
