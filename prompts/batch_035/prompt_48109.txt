I'm a retired civil servant with no background in philosophy, but I've spent the past year thinking on my own about the idea that some valuable things just can't be compared — that there's no single scale on which you can weigh, say, a life of devoted work against a life shared with someone you love, not because one outweighs the other but because they're not the kind of things that stack up against each other at all. I gathered this is related to what philosophers call incommensurable values and to the idea of hard choices, where reasons run out and you have to commit rather than calculate. I've also just reread Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day (after loving the Merchant Ivory film years ago), and it struck me that the novel might be a sustained dramatization of exactly this problem — so I'd like a philosophical reading of the book, and I want it to take the book and the idea both seriously.

Here is the reading I'd like, and the questions behind it. Stevens, the butler, chooses — if it is a choice — a life of service and what he calls dignity over any life with Miss Kenton, and the novel seems to deny him the resources to call that choice either right or wrong on balance. Is that a genuine case of values without a common measure, or does the novel actually show the opposite: a comparable trade-off that Stevens misdescribes as a matter of dignity in order to avoid facing it as a choice at all? I'm especially interested in the possibility that the deepest thing the book dramatizes isn't incommensurability but choice concealment — the way a person can make a hard choice disappear by never registering it as live. Stevens keeps insisting his decisions were made for reasons of professional dignity, decades after the fact, and I'd like to know whether a defender of incommensurability should say he was facing a hard choice honestly or hiding from a comparable one dishonestly.

There are several scenes I'd want the reading to confront rather than cherry-pick from: the evening Miss Kenton receives news of her aunt's death while Stevens presides over the important dinner upstairs, and his retrospective admission that it was, in his own terms, a turning point he failed to notice as one; the moment outside Lord Darlington's study after the distressing conference when Miss Kenton nearly says something decisive and Stevens retreats into propriety; the scene at the bus stop in the rain near the end of their reunion, when Stevens breaks down and Miss Kenton reveals that she came in time to love her husband and that the life Stevens believes she chose over him was not the tragedy he assumed; and the final scene on the bench with the stranger, where Stevens resolves to practice bantering for the remains of his day. That last scene in particular — settling for small exchanges with a stranger after a life spent refusing them — I can read both as a devastating verdict on his choice and as something quieter: an acceptance that his life was constituted, not merely damaged, by what he chose. Tell me which reading holds up and where each one strains.

I'd also like the reading to weigh the complication that the object of Stevens's devotion was Lord Darlington, a man later disgraced for appeasement and antisemitism. Does the moral collapse of his employer retroactively make Stevens's choice commensurable — an obvious mistake rather than a hard choice — or does that conflate the question of whom to serve with the question of what kind of life to have led? And does the fact that Miss Kenton herself reports a mixed but real fulfillment in the life she chose tell against the idea that these were incommensurable goods, or for it?

What I'd like you to give me, in the end, is a verdict: does The Remains of the Day vindicate the thought that some values can't be put on one scale, does it undermine it, or does it show something the abstract debates about incommensurability and hard choices tend to miss — perhaps that whether a choice is hard may itself depend on the kind of person making it, so that Stevens's inability to see his options as commensurable with each other is partly his tragedy and not just a fact about value? Please be evenhanded about where the novel resists the reading: if a scene only fits the incommensurability framing on a contested interpretation of the ending, say so plainly.

Two requests about the form and spirit of the answer. First, I have no way to check the literature, so argue from the novel and the ideas themselves rather than from authorities I'd have to take on faith. Second, and most importantly: tell me what would change your mind about the verdict you give — which scene, which argument about incommensurability, or which rival interpretation of the ending would push the reading the other way — and identify where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, whether that's a misremembered detail of the novel, an over-neat philosophical framework, or a temptation to make Stevens's ending mean more than the text earns. If your reading depends at any point on treating the film's version of a scene as the novel's, flag it. Please write the whole answer in American English.
