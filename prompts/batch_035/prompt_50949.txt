I became a naturalized citizen this spring, and I am now preparing to vote in my first national election. In the months since the ceremony, I have noticed something strange about the cluster of norms that surrounds the act of voting in my adopted country, and I would like some help figuring out what philosophical commitments it rests on. On one hand, everyone from my civics instructor to the volunteers registering voters outside the supermarket treats voting itself as a duty: low turnout is described as a civic disgrace, people who stay home are said to have forfeited their standing to complain about the government, and the whole apparatus of get-out-the-vote campaigns, reminder text messages, and stickers seems designed to celebrate the act of participating rather than any particular way of participating. Yet I gather that a single vote has an astronomically small chance of changing any outcome, so it is hard to see what the duty could be a duty *to*, if what matters is the result. On the other hand, when the subject turns from whether to vote to how to vote, the message becomes strikingly consequentialist: in a closely contested race I am told that voting for a third-party candidate is throwing my vote away, or even a tacit gift to the greater evil, and that I must support the lesser evil however tepidly. But friends who live in states where the outcome is a foregone conclusion tell me the same people would urge them, without embarrassment, to vote their conscience there. The same normative system, in other words, seems to treat my vote as weightless in its effects yet mandatory as an act, and as binding in its content only where its effects are non-negligible. I am told some countries even make voting legally compulsory, which would sharpen the puzzle further.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory here. Does the duty-to-vote side of the practice presuppose a non-consequentialist account of the vote — something like a civic or Kantian duty whose point is independent of outcomes — or can a consequentialist save it with appeals to expressive effects, the value of high turnout as such, or the generalization of the duty to everyone voting together? Does the lesser-evil norm presuppose the opposite, namely that the vote's significance lies entirely in its causal contribution to the result, and is the combination of the two norms coherent, or does the practice rest on an unresolved tension between viewing the vote as an expressive act and viewing it as a marginal causal intervention on an election? I would also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — whether, for instance, several rival theories of voting's ethics could each license the whole package, and if so which. And finally, if we replaced whatever view the practice presupposes with a straightforwardly expressive one, on which the vote is a statement of one's values rather than a lever on the outcome, what exactly would have to change? Would exhorting people to vote in safe districts, shaming abstainers, or even compulsory voting survive the replacement, or would the norms we currently bundle together come apart?
