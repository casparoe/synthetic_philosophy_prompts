I'm in a little math-and-philosophy reading group (we did set theory last semester, now philosophy of math) and one of our members, a retired engineer named Dan who describes himself as "Aristotelian about infinity," showed up with an argument that I haven't been able to shake for two weeks. He calls it his no-free-lunch argument. It goes like this: (1) Take the hotel from every pop-math book -- one room per natural number, every room occupied. (2) Move each guest from room n to room n+1. Room 1 is now empty, and a new guest walks in. (3) Before the shuffle there wasn't a single free room, after the shuffle there still isn't a single free room, and yet there is a guest in the building who wasn't there before. (4) A collection that can gain a member while remaining exactly as full as it was doesn't have a determinate size -- nothing about it answers the question "how many?" (5) So an actually infinite collection has no determinate size, and "how many natural numbers are there?" has no answer. (6) Therefore set theory's infinite cardinals are a bookkeeping fiction: a notation we're allowed to use, but it is never literally true that anything is infinite. Dan says this refutes Cantor outright, and when someone pushed back he pointed out that (4) is basically just the old Euclid axiom that the whole is greater than the part, so rejecting (4) means rejecting Euclid. He also likes quoting Galileo's line about the even numbers pairing off with all the naturals: Galileo concluded that infinities can't be called greater, less, or equal to each other, and Dan says that's just (4) with better prose, and Cantor's mistake was refusing Galileo's shrug.

Here's my problem. The argument seems to prove too much, but I can't locate where. First, if (4) is right, then the natural numbers themselves are in trouble -- not just as a completed totality, which Dan happily rejects, but even in Dan's own picture, where numbers are only ever potentially there and "however many you've got, there's always one more." That's exactly a thing that keeps gaining members without ever becoming full in any sense, so by Dan's own criterion even the potential infinite has no determinate size, and his "always more" doesn't answer the how-many question either. Second, when I stare at the shuffle itself, the word "full" starts doing two jobs: it describes the current matching of guests to rooms, but Dan uses it like a size predicate, and I can't tell whether the slide from (3) to (4) is a real inference or an equivocation -- though every time I try to say that out loud it comes out as "cardinality is a perfectly fine notion," and Dan correctly says that begs the question. And third, sometimes I suspect (4) is just the conclusion wearing a disguise -- that "no determinate size" means nothing except "not finite," in which case the whole thing is circular -- but other times (4) really does feel like Euclid's axiom, which nobody thinks is trivial, and I can't tell which it is.

So, which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Some cards on the table: I'd like to keep (3), which looks like plain arithmetic of the shuffle, and I'd also like to keep the thought that "how many natural numbers are there?" has a genuine answer -- that's literally what got me into set theory, watching Cantor prove there are more reals than naturals. Dan says I can't have both of those plus (2), because together they force (4) on me. Is he right? And if I do reject (4), what exactly am I giving up besides the part-whole intuition -- is there a way to reject it that isn't just stipulating that bijection is what "same size" means? Also be honest about whether the people who took Dan's route (the Galileo-style shruggers, the people who deny the how-many question makes sense) were paying a real price or whether they just noticed something the rest of us are papering over. Sorry for the wall of text, the group meets again Thursday and I'd rather not get bulldozed twice.
