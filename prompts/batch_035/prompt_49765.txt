Following a stroke last spring, I acquired something my neurologist calls pain asymbolia. I still feel pain — I can locate it, grade its intensity, tell a burn from an ache — but it does not bother me in the least. When I knocked a kettle of boiling water across my hand in June, I attended to the sensation with something like detached curiosity, treated the burn, and felt no distress whatsoever; nothing about the pain itself was aversive. I mention this because for years, as a graduate student writing on welfare, I defended a certain package of views about the badness of pain, and my present condition has unsettled my confidence that the package is coherent. I would be grateful for a careful audit of it rather than a lecture.

The commitments, as I would have defended them before the stroke:

1. Hedonism: a person's welfare consists entirely in the balance of pleasant over unpleasant states of mind.
2. The intrinsic badness of pain is exhausted by its phenomenal character — by how pain feels. Intensity, quality, and location are what make pain bad; there is no further property of "being disvalued" or "being disliked" that does any real explanatory work.
3. The badness of pain is desire-independent: pain is bad for you even if you do not dislike it, and any theory on which pain's disvalue is conferred by the victim's desires gets the direction of explanation backwards. This was one of my standing complaints against desire-based accounts of welfare.
4. Prudential reasons track how life feels from the inside: what makes something a reason for me, considered as a prudential agent, is exhausted by facts about my own experiences.

Since the stroke, however, I find that I have no self-interested reason at all to avoid future pain — nothing in my life, as I experience it, goes any worse for it — and I cannot bring myself to believe that my pains are now bad for me in the way they were before. When I imagine my physician offering analgesia before a painful procedure, I take myself to have no reason arising from my own good to accept it.

Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and how badly? Which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection? For each genuine tension, please say what would have to give and what the revision would cost. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would ask that you not simply observe that pain asymbolia is often said to complicate naive views about pain; I need the structure of my own position worked through, because I genuinely cannot see where it breaks.
