I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the odd survival of hereditary monarchy inside some of the world's oldest democracies — the United Kingdom, Sweden, Norway, Japan. The pattern I want to think through is this: these polities vest legal authority in an office filled by hereditary succession, while their political cultures (and, in places, their constitutional texts) insist that legitimate authority derives from the people. Ministers govern in the monarch's name; courts render judgments in the monarch's name; soldiers and legislators swear allegiance to the monarch rather than to the constitution or the electorate; royal assent remains, in form, a condition of legislation even though refusing it is now unthinkable; and the office retains reserve powers that everyone agrees must never be exercised but that no one seems willing to abolish. What theory of political legitimacy, if any, does retaining these forms presuppose? Is the practice only consistent with a broadly symbolic or continuity-conception, on which the Crown stands for something deliberately non-electoral — history, unity, a state above partisan contest — or can consent-based and authorization-based theories of legitimacy accommodate hereditary forms after all? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, which features (the oaths, the hereditary exclusion, the immunities, the reserve powers) could be licensed by several rival theories of legitimacy and which fit awkwardly with any story on which authority flows from the people. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the oaths, the legal powers, the succession rules, the immunity from ordinary law — if a democracy decided to bring the office into line with the source-of-authority story it already tells about itself.
