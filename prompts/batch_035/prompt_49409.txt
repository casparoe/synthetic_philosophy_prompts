I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about insects by going on gut reactions to cases, and the results are kind of a mess, so here's the list.

Swatting a mosquito that's about to bite me: obviously fine, I wouldn't lose a second of sleep. Spraying the ant colony that's gotten into my pantry: fine, though I notice I'd rather use something that kills fast than something slow. Driving on the highway in summer knowing my windshield is going to get plastered with bugs: doesnt bother me at all, genuinely zero. Mowing the lawn, which must shred untold numbers of insects and eggs and whatever else: fine. Eating honey: fine. Switching some of my protein to farmed cricket flour if it were cheaper than beef: fine, actually feels like a small moral improvement. Gassing the wasp nest above my door that could sting my kid: fine, no hesitation. Watching a video of a kid burning ants with a magnifying glass just to watch them die: not fine — and it feels like a moral reaction, not just an aesthetic one, like there's something bad happening *to the ants*. A friend pulling the wings off a fly to see what happens: not fine, and I'd probably say something to him about it. Boiling a live lobster: I used to be totally fine with it and now I'm honestly not sure.

And heres the thing that bugs me (sorry). I've read enough about the scale argument to know its shape: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is tiny, there are so many of them — quintillions at a time, I've seen different numbers but it's a lot — that if you multiply through, the expected number of sentient beings affected by the stuff I do without thinking, like the windshield and the lawnmower, is astronomical. And my gut says that has to be a reductio: the windshield just isn't a tragedy, no matter what the multiplication says. But the same gut says the kid with the magnifying glass is doing something wrong, and not only because he's developing bad character — it seems like what he's doing to the ants is bad for them somehow, even if each ant barely matters. So I apparently believe each insect counts for almost nothing, that huge numbers of them count for something, and that one kid torturing a handful of them counts for something. I can't get these to sit together.

Is there a principle, or maybe a couple of candidates, that captures all of this? I suspect it can't be straight expected numbers, because that washes out the difference between the windshield and the magnifying glass, which feels like the biggest difference on the whole list. Maybe it's something about intention, or about the probability of sentience crossing a threshold when it's concentrated and deliberate rather than diffuse and accidental, or maybe the honest answer is that my windshield verdict is just numbness and I have to bite that bullet. Tell me what fits, which of my verdicts the best candidate has to overturn and why, and what it says about two cases I haven't made up my mind on: whether it's worth paying noticeably more for an insecticide that kills faster, and whether a charity working on reducing wild insect suffering generally (habitat stuff, less painful control methods, whatever they actually do) is worth a donation or whether the math that makes it look good is the same math I refuse to accept for the windshield.
