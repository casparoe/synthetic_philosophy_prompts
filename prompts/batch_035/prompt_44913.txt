I'm a retired condensed matter physicist who got hooked on philosophy of mind a couple years after retiring, and I've become obsesed with the panpsychist combination problem. Help me turn a half-baked idea into a two-page proposal for a three-month visiting fellowship at a philosophy institute.

In my old field, macroscopic properties aren't sums of microscopic ones — they arise by coarse-graining, and the coarse-graining throws information away. Nobody thinks a glass of water fails to have a temperature just because individual molecules don't. My hunch: the combination problem feels insoluble only because everyone, critics and proponents alike, implicitly models macro-consciousness as something that has to preserve or total up all the micro-experiences, like gluing snapshots together, instead of an effective property produced by lossy compression of them. The question I'd work on: can that analogy be made precise enough to actually do work — can we say what a coarse-grained subject of experience is such that the standard worries (how do many micro-subjects become one, why is there a boundary at my skull, why am I not a crowd) lose their grip — or does the analogy break somewhere a physicist wouldn't notice?

Write the proposal with the question stated precisely, why the existing responses to the combination problem leave it open, what I'd actually do month by month (no experiments, it's conceptual work, though I could build toy models), and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing. Don't bother with citations, I'll add those.
