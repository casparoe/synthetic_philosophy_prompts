I have no academic background — I run a small business and read history for pleasure — and over the years I've worked myself into a view about why some regions become prosperous and others don't. The view, in short: material explanations (resources, geography, technology, who conquered whom) are necessary but not sufficient; the missing ingredient is values, and values operate as a genuine cause rather than as a reflection of material conditions. I gather a case something like this was once made about Protestantism and capitalism, but nothing I believe turns on whether that particular story was right, and my version isn't about any religion. It has three specific commitments I've worked out on my own. First, transmission is micro: the causal work is done by what parents praise and shame in their children — appointments kept or broken, debts honored or dodged, manual work admired or sneered at — not by what intellectuals or churches write. Second, the lags are long: a shift in what a community teaches its children can take three or four generations to show up in economic behavior, which means decade-scale evidence for or against the view is nearly blind. Third, the claim is testable in principle through what I think of as clean splits: groups that share the same institutions, markets, and technologies but differ in what they inherited — immigrant enclaves within one city, neighboring regions divided by an old frontier. Where values differ and nearly everything else is held fixed, any economic gap that opens over generations is the culture's signature.

What I want from you is an honest map of this view's exposure. What would count against it — observations, comparisons, patterns in the historical record — ranked by decisiveness, from fatal through serious through merely awkward? What does the view actually predict that its rivals don't, so that it risks something concrete instead of accommodating whatever happened? And press hardest on the question I suspect you'll see immediately: the long-lag clause seems to shield the view from any short-run evidence, and I worry I've built myself something that cannot lose. Is that so? If the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that disqualifies it as a causal claim about history — or whether lagged, hard-to-observe causes of this kind are a legitimate feature of historical explanation, so that the insulation is a cost worth paying. For my part, what would move me most is a case where values demonstrably changed and nothing economic followed for several generations afterward; tell me whether that is the right thing to look for or whether I'm staring at the wrong evidence.
