I'm a high school junior, and at our last philosophy club meeting we spent the afternoon on the old question of whether an omnipotent being could create a stone so heavy that the being itself could not lift it. Two members argued about it for over an hour, and the meeting ended before they reached anything. I took notes and am reproducing their exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) Maya opened with the standard trap: either the being can create such a stone or it cannot. If it can, then there is a stone it cannot lift, so there is something it cannot do. If it cannot, then there is something it cannot make. Either way, something is beyond its power, so omnipotence as usually understood is internally contradictory.

(2) Daniel replied that the trick stacks the deck. A stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift is like a square circle: it doesn't describe a difficult task, it describes nothing at all, since for that being no such stone is a possible thing. Failing to create a non-object is not a shortage of power, any more than his own inability to draw a four-sided triangle is a weakness in his drawing.

(3) Maya called that a quiet amendment of the claim. If omnipotence means only "can do whatever is logically possible," then something outside the being — logic itself — draws the boundary of its power, and calling it omnipotent is closer to advertising than description. She also pressed on the classification itself: what makes "a stone too heavy for its maker to lift" logically impossible rather than merely unprecedented? Calling it impossible seems to presuppose that the being is already bound by logic, which is exactly what was supposed to be settled.

(4) Daniel answered that not every "cannot" reports a limitation. A limitation should be a respect in which power could have been greater, but "could have made two plus three equal seven" is not a respect in which power could have been greater, because it isn't anything at all. Limits set by logic are not walls the being runs into; they are conditions of there being tasks to perform in the first place.

(5) Maya granted the point provisionally but said it proved too much. On Daniel's account, the original question was never about stones at all — it was a test of whether "omnipotent" does any real work beyond "extremely powerful," and his answer revealed that the work is done by the qualifier "logically," which she doubted anyone means when they use the word unqualified. She then raised the version where the being binds itself: if omnipotence includes the power to limit oneself — to create the stone and to bind oneself not to lift it — the paradox seems to return as "can it also unbind itself?", and Daniel's distinction between power over objects and power over one's own future power looked to her like moving the debate into bookkeeping rather than capacity. Daniel insisted the two ledgers are genuinely different and that self-binding is coherent precisely because unbinding never leaves the being's control, but by then the room was closing and we stopped.

Neither of them convinced the other, and several of us left unsure who had the better of it. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right at each stage — not merely who wins overall — and tell me whether there is something both of them are missing that would change the score. I'd also appreciate your view on Maya's closing suggestion that the whole dispute reduces to how much work the word "logically" is doing.
