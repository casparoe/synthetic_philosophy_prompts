At next month's departmental colloquium I will be defending a restrictionist response to Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I would like your help preparing for the question period. My thesis, stated plainly: the semantic anti-realist is entitled to a knowability principle — every truth is such that it could, in principle, come to be known — but only if the principle is restricted to truths that do not themselves concern the state of knowledge of actual inquirers. So restricted, the derivation no longer runs: Fitch takes an unknown truth p, conjoins it with the claim that p is not known, observes that this conjunction could not be known without contradiction (knowing it would imply knowing p, contradicting the second conjunct), and then applies the knowability principle to the conjunction to conclude that all truths are already known. Since the conjunction is precisely the kind of proposition my restriction excludes, the anti-realist keeps a principle that is still substantive — it still rules out truths that are knowable by no one in any world — without inheriting the absurd conclusion.

The audience is a department of about twenty. Two-thirds are mainstream analytic epistemologists who work on testimony, epistemic value, and contextualism, and who will hear "anti-realism" as a foreign vocabulary; two are modal logicians who will read the handout's formalism line by line; one senior metaphysian regards anti-realism of any stripe as a confusion about language and has said so at colloquia for thirty years. Nobody works on Dummett or intuitionistic logic, which cuts both ways: I will not have to fight the intuitionistic background, but I also cannot assume anyone sees why the knowability principle was ever attractive.

Please draft a Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise — in the order they are likely to arrive, phrased the way these people would phrase them — each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft. Indicate which objection does the most damage, what I should concede up front rather than fight, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Pay particular attention to the logicians: I expect they will probe whether the restriction can be given a principled characterization rather than one reverse-engineered to block the proof, and I would like to know how much of that fight I can honestly win.
