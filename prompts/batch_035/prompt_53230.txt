For the past two years, since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been reading systematically in the literature on distributive justice, and my philosophy discussion group has asked each member to circulate a short position statement for the others to challenge. I have drafted mine and would like it audited before I send it around. Here is the set of commitments as I currently hold them:

(1) The currency of distributive justice should be something publicly measurable and interpersonally comparable. For that reason I am sympathetic to resourcist views — something like Rawls's primary goods or Dworkin's equality of resources — rather than equality of welfare, since subjective wellbeing cannot be measured to my satisfaction, and I distrust theories resting on quantities that cannot in principle be measured.

(2) I nonetheless accept the force of Sen's critique of resourcism: resources are merely means, and people differ systematically in their capacity to convert resources into actual functionings — a wheelchair user and I require very different bundles to achieve the same mobility. Purely resourcist equality therefore strikes me as blind in an important way.

(3) At the same time, I am uncomfortable with capability-based views as I have encountered them, because the space of functionings is plural and incommensurable: there is no capability scalar, no fact of the matter that one bundle of capabilities is exactly twice another. A theory of justice built on such a quantity seems to me to give up the rigor that made the resourcist approach attractive in the first place.

(4) In my final decade of work I served on a panel that allocated clinical and research funding using quality-adjusted life years, and I defended that practice then and would defend it again: QALYs are cardinal, aggregable, and decision-usable in a way that philosophical currencies rarely are.

(5) I hold that the just society must be neutral among its citizens' conceptions of the good life; the state has no business ranking ways of living as more or less worthwhile.

(6) For public policy purposes I accept aggregation across persons: it is sometimes right to allocate a scarce resource where it produces the greatest total benefit, even when that means the worst-off do not receive it.

I suspect some of these are in genuine tension and others only appear to be, but I do not trust myself to tell which. Please go through them pair by pair, identify the real conflicts, rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave, and for each genuine conflict say what would have to give and what surrendering it would cost. I would particularly like your view on whether my insistence on measurability in (1) — which I have been treating as a scientific habit of mind rather than a moral principle — is doing more damage than good, and whether my attachment to QALYs in (4) reflects a considered position or simply nostalgia for the instruments I used for forty years. I have no settled view about which commitment I would abandon, and I would rather see the full map of the tensions before forming one.
