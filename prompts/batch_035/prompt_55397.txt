Here's what the student wrote: "The inefficacy objection says that in large-scale cases of collective harm, an individual's contribution makes no difference to the outcome. Climate change is produced by billions of emitters, so one person's flights are neither necessary nor sufficient for any particular harm — no specific flood traces back to my marginal ton of CO2. Similarly for meat: eating one burger doesn't cause more animals to be raised, since producers respond to aggregate demand rather than to individual purchases. And in voting, a single vote matters only in an exact tie, which almost never happens. So the objection concludes that individuals have no obligation to cut emissions, stop eating meat, or vote. The strongest reply is the expected utility reply, associated with Parfit and defended more recently by Singer: even if each contribution has only a tiny probability of being the decisive one, the harm at stake is enormous, so the expected disvalue of the contribution is substantial. Parfit made this point in Reasons and Persons with his discussion of imperceptible harms, where he argued that since no individual's act makes any perceptible difference to anyone's welfare, each contribution to a collectively harmful outcome is permissible — the wrongness lies only in the collective outcome as a whole, which is why we should blame the group rather than any of its members. The expected utility reply works best for voting, where there's a clean threshold: either your vote breaks a tie or it doesn't, and empirical estimates put the probability of a single vote being decisive in a large election at roughly one in ten million, which given the stakes yields a substantial expected value. The same structure applies to climate: there's some threshold concentration of greenhouse gases at which, say, the Greenland ice sheet tips irreversibly, so my flight has a small chance of being the one that pushes us over — exactly analogous to a tied election. Since the expected harm of my flight is therefore nonzero, and carbon offsets let me cancel that expected harm for a few dollars, the utilitarian should conclude that keeping flying and offsetting dominates abstaining on her own view. So the inefficacy objection fails for all three cases, though partly because of the offsetting point rather than the threshold point."
