Alright, so I've basically converted to luck egalitarianism -- the view that inequalities are unjust when and only when they trace back to brute luck, and that the outcomes of genuinely voluntary gambles are your own problem. Roughly Dworkin's equality of resources territory. I'm not asking for the standard tour of objections, I already know the big ones (Anderson's harshness objection, the imprudent sick person left to die, etc.). What I actually want is a map of what would count against the view, and I want it ranked: from "this would sink it" through "committed luck egalitarians themselves would concede this hurts" to "merely awkward, people will paper over it."

A few specific things I keep chewing on, tell me where each lands on that ranking:

1. The brute luck / option luck line. The usual move is to run hypothetical insurance markets: ask whether a prudent person behind some veil of ignorance would have insured against this or that (lack of talent, handicap, unemployment). But people have pointed out that the two main uses of the device pull in opposite directions -- the talent version compensates you for something you couldn't have insured against at all (you don't know your talents until you have them and the ambition that comes with them), while the handicap version sometimes denies compensation because you didn't prudently buy cover you could have bought. If there's no principled way to run both without the ambition-dependence leaking back into what was supposed to be brute luck, does the view collapse into either "nobody's responsible for anything, so equalize everything" or into justifying market outcomes wholesale? Is that fatal or is it just where you say the cut is normative rather than metaphysical?

2. Determinism. If choices ultimately trace back to genes and upbringing and the causal history of the universe, then all luck is brute luck, and luck egalitarianism seems to collapse into straight-up telic egalitarianism (leveling down and all -- I do NOT want to end up there). Luck egalitarians usually bite the bullet and say responsibility is compatible with determinism, fine, but then they need determinism-plus-compatibilism to be true, or at least need *something* to ground the responsibility half of the view that isn't itself luck. How much of the view is hostage to that?

3. Cultivated tastes. If I deliberately cultivate expensive tastes -- plovers' eggs, fancy wine, the whole cliché -- my welfare tanks but my resources don't. Welfareist versions of the view have to decide whether deliberately acquired tastes are option luck (tough luck) or just preferences that justice should be neutral between. It feels like whichever way you go, a hard case gets mangled. Where does that sit in the exposure map?

Also two bigger-picture things: what does luck egalitarianism actually predict that its rivals (rawlsian, prioritarian, sufficientarian, telic egalitarian) don't, so that anything could count as evidence for or against it at all? And has it been qualified into unfalsifiability by now, with all the epicycles about hypothetical insurance and reasonable prudence and degrees of responsibility? If it has, be honest about whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what political philosophy is like. FWIW I'd find a clean collapse argument most persuasive -- showing it either becomes telic egalitarianism in disguise or else can't refuse the harshness stuff.
