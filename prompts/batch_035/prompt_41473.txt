I teach an evening "philosophy for everyone" class at a community center — mostly retired folks plus a few curious commuters, no background in philosophy but they can handle real arguments if I don't drown them in jargon. Next session is on natural kinds: whether the world comes pre-sorted into real categories that science discovers, versus categories we project onto it. I've got 90 minutes and a whiteboard. Plan the session for me — don't just explain the topic to me.

What I need: the order the pieces have to go in, what has to be in place before the next bit will make any sense to them (I keep thinking you can't do much with the carving-nature-at-its-joints idea until they've already seen a case where the carving is genuinely ambiguous, but you tell me). One carrying example that has to do alot of the work — I've always used water/H2O but I've started to suspect it secretly rigs the discussion, since it makes it look like every kind is going to turn out to have a neat hidden essence waiting to be found. So tell me whether to keep it, replace it (jade? species? some psychiatric category?), or use two and what each one pays for. The misconceptions I should expect — my guesses are that science's current categories just are the natural kinds, that if kinds are real there must be exactly one correct way to classify everything, and the reflex-cynic-in-the-back-row one that it's all just language and there's no fact of the matter — and where in the sequence each should get headed off rather than left to fester until Q&A. And a closing question or exercise that would show me whether they actually understood the issue rather than nodded along politely.

One more thing, and it matters to me: be explicit about which parts of this are settled among philosophers of science and which are live fights. I know just enough to know that things like whether species are individuals rather than kinds, or whether there's one true taxonomy or several equally legitimate ones, are actual contested positions, and I don't want to walk out having taught some Kripke-and-Putnam-style essentialism as if it were the consensus answer when people have been picking fights over it for decades. Mark it for me in the plan itself — which moves I can make as this-is-just-how-it-is and which I have to present as here's-where-people-split, and where the smart money actually sits on the splits if you had to say.

Also, should I bring in the social kinds question at all (race, money, that sort of thing)? This class has opinions about everything and only 90 minutes. Tell me if it's worth the derail or better as a not-tonight that I name and refuse, and say so explicitly.
