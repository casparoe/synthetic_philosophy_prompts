My housemate insists that all pleasures have one thing in common, and I have been testing her with cases to find out what she takes that thing to be. Her verdicts so far: the taste of a perfectly ripe peach — pleasure. The flood of relief when a biopsy comes back clear — pleasure. Bittersweet nostalgia while an old song plays — pleasure, and not despite the sting but partly because of it. Schadenfreude at a rival's pratfall, instantly regretted — still a pleasure. The jolt of gladness on learning that a fellowship you wanted starts next year — pleasure. Deep absorption in a chess game she never wanted to play, called satisfying only in retrospect — not a pleasure. A rock climber who endorses the climb while it happens but reports no particular feeling during it — pleasure. Enjoying a sip of cheap wine she believes to be expensive — pleasure. Next week we reach the hard cases: the pain a masochist endorses and wants during a consensual scene; a medication-induced flatness the patient occurrently accepts — thinking, in the moment, that the state is fine — while feeling nothing and wanting nothing; and the warm glow of believing you helped someone, when it turns out your help made no difference. Predict her verdicts on the three new cases. Set out the rules that could be generating her track record — I can see at least a shared-feel view, a concurrent-endorsement view, and a desire-satisfaction view fitting it, and perhaps others — and say where those rules come apart on the new cases. Give your best guess with your reasons, name the further case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules, and say which of the three verdicts you are least confident about. Along the way, mark where the underlying question — what, if anything, all pleasures share — is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on it, so that I do not mistake one school's answer for the settled view.
