My grandfather died three weeks ago, at ninety. He was a metaphysician by trade and an endurantist down to his bones — objects are wholly present at every moment they exist, none of this being spread out through time with temporal parts dangling off you like a worm. He had a forty-year running argument about this with his oldest friend and colleague, a diehard perdurantist, and the two of them loved each other enormously and never conceded a single point. Grandpa was very clear, whenever the subject came up at holidays, that he did not want perdurantist consolation at his funeral — he specifically did not want anyone saying he's "still there" in the earlier slices of the block universe, that nothing is ever really lost because all our temporal parts are laid out eternally along our worldlines. He thought that was the great seduction of perdurantism, that it smuggles in immortality for free, and he thought it was false, and he thought being comforted by something false was a coward's metaphysics. He left a note asking that the eulogy not do this to him, and honestly everyone in the family knows about the note.

So here's the problem: I'm giving the eulogy. The room will be roughly half philosophers — including his perdurantist old friend, front row, who I know will be listening for exactly this — and half family who just want to hear something warm about Grandpa and will not know what a temporal part is. And the hard part is that endurantism, at least combined with the way he thought about it, makes the usual comforting moves hard: I can't say a "part of him lives on" (he'd say there's no part to do the living), I can't lean on the block-universe consolation he explicitly banned, and if I'm honest the plain endurantist story is just that he existed, wholly, at every moment of a long life, and now doesn't, which is true but is not going to get anyone through the reception.

Write me the eulogy. It should comfort the family, land for the philosophers without becoming a lecture, and not say anything Grandpa would have called false — and if you can find room for one affectionate nod to the perdurantist in the front row, something that honors the argument rather than settling it, that would mean a lot. Five minutes or so. He was also a gardener and suspicious of most of Heidegger, if that helps.
