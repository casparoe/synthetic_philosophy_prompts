I've been rereading Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia for a book club, and the chapter section on animals ("Constraints and Animals") is the one everyone skips but me, apparently. There he floats this infamous half-serious proposal that we might owe utilitarian treatment to animals but Kantian treatment to people -- i.e., animals count and can be traded off against aggregate welfare, while humans are protected by inviolable side constraints. What's striking is that he raises the view as a live option, seems genuinely pulled by it, and then basically drops the whole subject and gets on with defending the minimal state. The guy died in 2002, right before basically everything interesting in animal ethics went mainstream, and I keep wondering how he'd react if you could sit him down in 2025.

So here's what I want you to do: play out that meeting. Show him cultivated meat getting regulatory approvals in Singapore and the US, the wave of corporate cage-free pledges and stuff like California's ballot initiative banning extreme confinement -- which, note, is the *state* restricting what farmers can do to animals, very much not minimal-state behavior. Would he see that as straightforward rights-violating overreach (and of whom -- the farmers? animals can't be rights-holders on his picture, or can they?), or would the utilitarianism-for-animals half of his view actually license it, since the side constraints don't apply to what's done to animals in the first place? And then hit him with the really post-Nozick stuff: the wild animal suffering people arguing we should consider intervening in nature to reduce predation and disease, maybe even gene-editing animals to suffer less. It seems like his framework cuts weirdly there -- if animals really are under pure utilitarian bookkeeping, then a consistent Nozickian should be *more* open to intervention in nature than your average animal rights person, who grounds everything in species-typical flourishing and leaving wild animals alone.

I also want you to take a stance on whether the 1974 Nozick would answer these differently from the later, softer Nozick -- the one who by Philosophical Explanations and The Examined Life had loosened up a lot of his earlier positions. Don't just say "he'd find it interesting," he found everything interesting, that's not a prediction. Give me your best reconstruction of the actual arguments he'd deploy, flag where you're extrapolating vs. where you're on solid textual ground, and tell me where you think his framework just runs out of steam on these questions. My book club meets in two weeks and I want to walk in with a genuinely defensible take and not just vibes.
