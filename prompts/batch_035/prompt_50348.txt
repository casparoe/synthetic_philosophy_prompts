I lead the policy-pilot team at a mid-sized city government; we run eight to twelve pilots a year, on everything from participatory budgeting to changes in waste collection, and every one requires an eventual recommendation to scale, continue, or stop. I've come to suspect we do this without any principled account of what makes a pilot a genuine experiment rather than theater — or of who the experiment is supposed to inform. A colleague with a philosophy background keeps telling me that John Dewey spent decades arguing that democracy and inquiry share a single structure: problems arise when the indirect consequences of some activity escape the institutions that produce them; publics form around those consequences; and democratic life consists in organizing inquiry so that the people affected can recognize their own problems and test responses to them, with the results feeding back into the situation rather than ending in a verdict handed down from outside. I would like this operationalized for my team.

Please write a procedure we could actually run on a proposed pilot, from the moment a department brings us an idea to the final scale-or-stop decision. It should tell us, in order: what makes something a problem worth experimenting on rather than one to decide directly; how to identify the relevant public, including what to do when the people affected are diffuse, future, or hard to reach, and how to tell when the public we convene is not the public that bears the consequences; what counts as a genuine test, including how to resist the standing temptation to design pilots that cannot fail; how to handle ambiguous results, and results that conflict with what the affected public itself wants; and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to elected officials because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a proposal to pilot four-day intervals for street-bin collection, with strong internal sponsorship and easy measurement; (2) a proposed participatory budgeting exercise in a district where turnout is expected to be low and skewed toward the already organized; and (3) a pilot of an enforcement policy the affected residents oppose but whose measured effects look good. In at least one case the procedure should deliver an answer my team will not like; say so and explain whether that is a defect of the procedure or a cost of taking Dewey seriously.

Constraints: the procedure must be executable by policy officers with no philosophical training in a workshop of two or three hours per pilot; elected sign-off is required for anything touching budgets or enforcement; and we can almost never sustain a pilot beyond eighteen months. Please say explicitly what the procedure cannot do within those constraints.
