Write a dialogue between two philosophers preparing a joint graduate seminar on indicative conditionals, with Lewis's triviality results as the running battleground. One, a truth-conditionalist, holds that indicative conditionals are genuine propositions with truth conditions, along the lines of a variably strict possible-worlds semantics; she concedes that the probability of a conditional can come apart from the corresponding conditional probability. The other, a probabilist in the tradition of Adams, holds that indicative conditionals have no truth values at all: an assertion of one expresses a high conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent, and there is nothing more to its assertoric content.

Stage the dialogue as a working session in which the two test the seminar's central moves on each other. Have the probabilist walk through a triviality argument on a concrete partition, such as the six faces of a fair die: if the chance that the die lands six given that it lands even is 1/3, and if that chance is the probability of some proposition true at only some of the even outcomes, then the same constraint forces impossible values for further conditionals on that partition — so no proposition can have the conditional probability as its probability in general. The truth-conditionalist should reply by biting the bullet: the equation holds only approximately and only for simple, unembedded conditionals, and its failure is the price of embeddability — negated conditionals, conditionals embedded inside conditionals, and conditionals conjoined with other sentences all appear to have ordinary truth-apt contents, which the no-truth-value view must rescue through special assertibility rules or paraphrase. The probabilist should counter with cases where the truth-conditional reading diverges from intuitive conditional credence (long-shot antecedents, conditionals under negation, iterated conditionals) and with the observation that a conditional bet — one that is called off if the antecedent fails — tracks the conditional probability rather than the probability of any proposition.

Both characters should be equally competent, each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. End not with a winner but with each candidly stating which concession on their own side they regard as least bad.
