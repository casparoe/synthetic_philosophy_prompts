I am a high-school junior enrolled in an elective on metaphysics, and we have just finished a unit on Leibniz's principles. While revising for the midterm, I came across a popular article (I no longer remember where, possibly a philosophy blog linked from a subreddit) that summarized the identity of indiscernibles roughly as follows: "Leibniz held that if two objects agree in every qualitative respect — same shape, same mass, same color, even the same history — then they are not two objects at all but one and the same. He believed this as a kind of empirical generalization: in all his experience, nature never contains perfect duplicates, so he concluded that it never could. In the twentieth century Max Black devised a counterexample: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres of chemically pure iron, two miles apart, with nothing else in existence. Because the spheres share all their properties, intrinsic and relational, the principle says they must be one sphere; since there are plainly two, the principle is refuted, and philosophers today agree that the identity of indiscernibles is false. Its converse — that if two things are one and the same, they share all their properties — was never in question, which only shows how deceptive the original principle was."

Something about this summary sits oddly with me. Our teacher warned us that popular accounts of Black's example tend to exaggerate what it establishes, but I did not follow her explanation at the time, and I cannot now pin down where the article goes wrong; I am inclined to think it is broadly on the right side, since the description of Black's universe matches our textbook. I would therefore like an audit of this summary, and I would like it in a strictly fixed format: a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no text outside the list. The five points should be: (1) what in the summary a Leibniz scholar would accept as accurate; (2) the first significant misrepresentation, covering the article's account of why Leibniz believed the principle; (3) the second significant overstatement, concerning what Black's example actually establishes; (4) whether the article's closing sentence about the uncontroversial converse is fair, or whether it runs two distinct principles together in a way a defender of Leibniz would protest; and (5) an explicit statement of what would change your verdict — which consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would lead you to judge the summary fair — together with an honest note on where your own reasoning in the preceding points is most likely to fail. Please hold yourself to the two-sentence limit per point even where the material tempts you to write more, and if the limit itself forces an oversimplification anywhere, say so within point five rather than breaking the format.
