I'm giving the farewell speech at the retirement dinner of my predecessor as editor-in-chief of a printed reference encyclopaedia — a woman who ran the edition for thirty-two years and who hands the office over to me next month. The room will be mixed: half her editorial staff, who will catch every nuance, and half family and longtime contributors, who know her simply as exacting and fair. Her intellectual signature, as everyone on staff knows, was the line she insisted on keeping in the preface of every volume over the marketing department's objections: a statement to the effect that errors inevitably remain in the work, and that responsibility for them is hers alone. She has explained to me more than once that this was not boilerplate humility. As she approved each entry she believed it, and she would stand behind that belief entry by entry; yet she also believed, with equal sincerity, that somewhere in the finished volume there had to be at least one error — and she held that both beliefs were rational, since any other posture would have been either credulous or paralysing. The staff refer to this among ourselves as "her paradox"; a philosopher would apparently call it the preface paradox, though she never traded in labels.

Please write the speech. The philosophical point has to do real work rather than serve as decoration: a speech that concludes that everything she published was in the end true would quietly undo her, and a speech that shrugs that she made mistakes like anyone else would misrepresent her, since her view was that believing each entry while doubting the whole was not a tension she needed to resolve but the only honest stance available to an editor of her temperament. The speech should let the staff feel that their craft has been taken seriously, let the family hear a speech about her rather than about a puzzle, and avoid both lecturing the non-philosophers and condescending to the editors. If it can be managed without excess sentiment, it should close by turning the paradox towards me and the incoming team — she is famously unsentimental and would be embarrassed by grandeur. Roughly five minutes at speaking pace, please.
