Before we take up any particular proposal in my graduate module on machine minds, I want the class to have the specification in hand. Write the desiderata that any behavioral criterion for attributing a mind — intelligence, understanding, whatever the criterion is meant to track — has to meet in order to count as adequate. Fix the purpose yourself, but let it be practical: a criterion that could actually be used to decide whether an unfamiliar system deserves the attribution — a language-less animal, a conversational AI, an alien artifact recovered from a probe, a patient with locked-in syndrome — on the basis of observation and interaction alone, without privileged access to the system's internals.

Among the requirements I want considered, each with the motivating case behind it and a note on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp:

- Species- and substrate-neutrality. The criterion must not quietly bake in that the candidate is human-like, carbon-based, or embodied — with the case of an octopus or an ape that is intelligent but fails every conversation-based test.
- The other-minds point. The criterion should make our everyday attributions to other humans non-arbitrary, or explain why it is entitled to be more demanding of non-humans than of the people applying it.
- Resistance to rote. It must not be passable by a system that is, in principle, just a very large table of pre-stored responses, however long the interaction. If no purely behavioral criterion can rule this out, say so plainly rather than hiding the cost.
- Falsifiability. The criterion must be capable of returning a negative verdict — a test that every candidate passes, or that no candidate could ever pass, is not doing any work. Include the case of conversational systems that succeed by gaming shallow judge expectations (unusual personas, deflection, low-bandwidth channels) rather than by understanding.
- The modality problem. Whether the criterion can demand a linguistic channel at all, given candidates with intelligence but no output modality suited to conversation.
- Symmetry with human baselines. The criterion must not require more of a candidate than ordinary humans deliver on an off day, nor accept less.
- Handling of gradation. Whether verdicts must come in degrees, or a binary criterion can be defended.

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — I have my eye on the tension between restricting ourselves to behavior and interaction, and excluding the giant lookup table — and state which requirement you would drop first and what dropping it costs. That joint-satisfiability verdict is the part I care about most. Then, in a short closing paragraph only, say how the imitation game, the intentional stance, and a clinical-style checklist of capacities each fare against the list; keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
