The exam question was:

"Explain the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, then present what you take to be the strongest objection to it, and defend a verdict on which side you find more convincing."

And here is the student's answer, reproduced as written:

"The Epicurean argument runs like this. Death is the total absence of experience, since once you're dead there's no subject left to have any experiences at all. And all badness that can accrue to a person must be bad for them experientially -- pain, fear, frustration are all states of a subject. So when death is present, we are not, and when we are, death is not. Death therefore cannot be bad for the person who dies. Epicurus also argued that the time before our birth, when we equally did not exist, is not regarded by anyone as a misfortune, so neither should the time after our death be -- our prenatal and postmortem nonexistence are perfectly symmetrical, and our intuitions treating them differently are just bias.

The strongest objection is the deprivation account, associated with Nagel. On this view, death is bad for you because it deprives you of the goods you would otherwise have had -- if I die at 40 instead of 85, I lose out on decades of valuable life, and that loss is a bad thing for me even though I'm never around to feel it. Nagel concedes that the Epicureans are right that harm must be experiential, but argues that a person's good and bad fortune can depend on things that happen outside their experience anyway; his example of someone left in a permanent vegetative state after brain damage is meant to show that unexperienced losses can still be harms, since the person is harmed by being cut off from the life they could have led. Since there's no one around to be the subject of the harm once death occurs, Nagel concludes the harm must be located in the period while the person is alive, and that this happens at the moment of death for the person who dies.

I find the deprivation account more convincing. The Epicurean reply would be that you can only be deprived of something you want, and a dead person has no unfulfilled desires, but this just means we should look at the desires the person had while alive, and those were typically for continued goods. Overall I side with the objectors."
