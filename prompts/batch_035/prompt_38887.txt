I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a set theory elective, and our problem-set study group has fractured over something one member — call him Daniel — brought in last week. Daniel is sympathetic to finitism and thinks he has found the crack in Cantor's argument, not by denying the diagonal exists but by getting it to testify against itself. His argument runs as follows. (1) Cantor's diagonal procedure shows that for any list of real numbers, one can construct a real differing from the nth entry in its nth digit, so no list contains every real. (2) Consider the list of all reals that are definable — reals uniquely pinned down by some finite string of English — ordered alphabetically by their shortest description. (3) Apply the diagonal procedure to this list; call the resulting real D, which differs from the nth definable real in its nth digit. (4) D is itself definable, since we have just given a finite recipe for it: the real whose nth digit differs from the nth digit of the nth definable real. (5) So D both is and is not on the list of all definable reals. (6) Therefore the definable reals cannot be enumerated — they are, in Cantor's own sense, uncountable — which contradicts the plain fact that finite strings over a finite alphabet form a countable collection, and every definable real has such a string. (7) Since the method of (1), applied to a perfectly well-defined collection, generates a contradiction, the diagonal method is unsound, and the uncountability of the reals was never proved but merely staged with theatrical machinery. Daniel says the same rot runs through the whole menagerie: if the diagonal is broken, then Hilbert's hotel is a sideshow built on an error, and the finitists were owed an apology a century ago.

I have agreed to write the group's official response before we meet on Thursday, and the more I stare at the argument the less I trust my instincts. Each step looks like something I would accept in another context: I believe in the diagonal argument, I believe descriptions are countable, and I cannot find the false step hiding anywhere. My tutor, when I brought it to office hours, only smiled and said the argument has a cousin in the old puzzle about the least natural number not definable in fewer than twenty words, then sent me away to think about what the two have in common. I would rather keep both (1) and the countability of descriptions and give up something in the middle, but I cannot yet say which middle step is the culprit or what made it look so innocent.

Please walk me through this carefully: identify the exact premise or inference that fails, explain what tacit assumption allowed it to pass as obvious, and price the alternatives — in particular, whether the repair forces us to say something strange, such as that a collection can be countable from the outside even though no enumeration of it can be written down in the very language we used to define the collection, and whether that strangeness is a genuine cost or mere unfamiliarity. I would also like to know whether Daniel can regroup afterward: if his escape route is closed, is there a nearby argument against actual infinities he could still run, or does the failure here generalize to the whole family of tricks he wants to indict? Please write your answer as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just paragraphs, the way one would write a long letter.
