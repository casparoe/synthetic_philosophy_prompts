I chair the quarterly portfolio review at a mid-sized software company: every three months, a panel of eight engineering and product managers has to decide which in-flight projects continue, which get more time, and which we kill. The official doctrine, pushed hard by our CFO, is that these decisions must be forward-looking only: money already spent is gone, so the only question for each project is whether the next pound spent there beats the next pound spent anywhere else. Sunk costs are, on this view, simply irrelevant, and any appeal to them — 'we've come too far to stop now', 'we can't waste what we've put in' — is flagged as the sunk cost fallacy and struck from the discussion. But in practice the panel keeps running into cases that make us doubt the doctrine is the whole story. Killing a project one month from delivery because the forward-looking numbers have dipped seems to discard something real; and everyone senses that 'how much has already been spent' sometimes carries genuine information about whether the remaining spend is worth it — though nobody on the panel can say crisply when, or whether that's just the fallacy talking in a nicer accent.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the panel can actually run in the meeting: roughly twenty minutes per project, no finance analyst in the room, managers with no training in decision theory, and a written rationale for each decision that the CFO's office will audit afterwards. The procedure should be grounded in what decision theory and the empirical literature on escalation of commitment actually support, translated into questions a manager can answer without the theory behind them. The hard part, as I see it, is separating the legitimate from the illegitimate uses of the past: past investment as *evidence* about future payoffs (learning curves, what completion unlocks, what the history reveals about the team's estimates) versus past investment as a supposed *claim* on the future ('we owe it success'). The procedure needs to make that distinction operational, and it also has to handle the things the pure doctrine ignores: option value of being nearly done, the reputational and morale costs of killing things, the fact that the counterfactual 'spend the money elsewhere' is often fictional because the alternative isn't a project but nothing. Please mark the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgement call has to take over, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire — say, the systematic biases it can't detect from inside the room.

Then run it on three cases so the panel can see it in action: (1) a data-migration project, eleven months in, budget 60% spent, where the forward-looking case for finishing is genuinely close but completion is two weeks away; (2) a customer-facing feature, four months in, whose forward-looking case is clearly negative, but where the team that built it is one we will need for the next project and morale is fragile; and (3) a case where the procedure's honest answer is 'kill it' even though the panel will hate saying so and will be tempted to bend the procedure — pick the details of that one yourself. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: the questions it cannot answer even in principle, and where a panel using it in good faith could still get the wrong answer. Please write the whole thing in British English.
