Two fellow PhD students in my cognitive science program have been arguing for weeks about the extended mind thesis, and as the bystander who got asked to referee, I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened.

Some background. After a concussion left Dana with a much shakier short-term memory, she rebuilt her academic life around her phone: every reading annotation, lab meeting note, and appointment lives in it, and alarms prompt her through the day. At a lab meeting she said the phone is now literally part of her mind, not a tool her mind uses.

Marco objected that constant use doesn't make something part of you: she consults the app the way a pilot consults instruments or a scholar visits a library, and no one ever counted visiting a library as remembering. Dana replied by parity: were the same information stored in her skull and retrieved with the same speed and fluency, no one would hesitate to call it memory, and she can't see why the boundary of the skin should mark a difference that matters. Marco answered that her retrieval isn't fluent in the relevant way: she has to decide to check, she sometimes doubts her own notes, and when she lost the phone for a weekend she didn't lose memories, she lost access to records, whereas a stroke would have taken the memories themselves. Dana's last move was that the checking has become entirely habitual — she endorses the notes unhesitatingly, relies on them the way she once relied on recall, and felt the loss as a kind of amnesia — and that proponents of the thesis have proposed roughly these criteria (reliability, automatic trust, easy access), which she satisfies. Marco closed with a bloat objection: on that standard his glasses, his bicycle, and perhaps the department administrator's filing system all become parts of minds, which suggests the criteria aren't individuating cognition at all, just licensing a rhetorical reclassification of ordinary tool use.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who's right at each step and why, where each side concedes too much or too little, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
