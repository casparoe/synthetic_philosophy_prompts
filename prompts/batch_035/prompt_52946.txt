I'm a science and ideas journalist, and my next longread is about researchers who claim to find cognition in unexpected substrates: octopus arms that act semi-autonomously with most of their neurons outside the animal's brain, slime moulds solving shortest-path problems without any neurons at all, engineered neuromorphic chips, and now talk of intelligence in fungal networks. Almost every one of these stories quietly assumes some version of functionalism -- the idea that what makes a state mental is the causal role it plays, not the stuff it is made of, so that the same mental kind can be realised in wildly different physical systems. Before I write a word, I want the conceptual groundwork laid properly, and I'd like your help with the specification rather than the summary.

Please write out the desiderata: the full list of things that any adequate account of multiple realisability must deliver if it is to license these cross-substrate claims in my feature. By that I mean -- which cases must it get right (for instance, must it count the octopus's decentralised nervous system and a human brain as realising some of the same kinds, or does it owe me a principled way to say they don't?); which distinctions it must respect without collapsing them (causal role versus mere functional analogy, realising a mind versus simulating one, a system's being organised so that it can realise a mind versus its actually doing so, near-decomposability versus genuine integration); and which empirical results from neuroscience, evolutionary biology, and ethology it cannot contradict (the independent evolution of complex nervous systems several times over, the radical diversity of neural architectures at comparable behavioural competence). For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration behind it -- the reason a theory failing it would fail for my purposes, which is adjudicating claims about thinking in arms, moulds, chips, and mycelia.

Then do two further things. First, tell me which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are really the prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands on all comers -- I suspect functionalists and their critics smuggle in at least one criterion each that the other side should be allowed to refuse, and I'd like you to name names as it were. Second, flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why; if the list itself can only be met by lowering the bar on what counts as a realisation, I want to know that before I publish, because it would change the shape of the article entirely.

Finally, very briefly, assess how role functionalism, machine-state functionalism, and a teleofunctional approach each fare against your own list -- no more than a short paragraph each, since the criteria are where the thinking is and I'll develop the scorecard myself.

Please write the whole thing in British English, and at a level of formality suitable for me to work from directly when drafting.
