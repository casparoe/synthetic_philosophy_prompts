So the standard line on sunk costs is: the money/effort is gone, only future costs and benefits count, so letting past investment affect your current choice is straight-up irrational. Fine. But I've noticed there are at least two ways people try to rescue sunk-cost-ish behavior, and they get constantly mushed together, and I want an argument for the interesting one.

The boring rescue: staying the course because quitting looks bad, reputation, commitment devices, blah blah. That's just future consequences smuggled back in — you're not really honoring the sunk cost, you're paying for a signal. Skip it, I dont care about that one.

The interesting rescue has two flavors I can see, and I want you to build the strongest case you can for one of them (your pick, but say which):

(a) the epistemic flavor: your past decision to invest in the project is itself evidence about the project's value — evidence you now have trouble reconstructing, because you've forgotten why you were optimistic, or because your current misery is a noisy signal. On this view "I've put so much in already" is shorthand for "past-me, who wasn't exhausted and demoralized, judged this worth a lot, and that judgment still carries weight."

(b) the intertemporal bargaining flavor: your current choice isn't a one-off, it's a precedent for your future selves. If I quit this project the moment it gets hard, my future selves can't trust that I'll stick with anything, which changes what projects are worth starting in the first place. So honoring sunk costs is like not defecting in an iterated game across time-slices, even when defecting looks optimal this round.

Whichever flavor you pick, I need you to actually deal with the obvious objection, which my brother keeps hammering: whatever evidential or precedential weight the sunk cost has, a rational agent should just condition on all the evidence — including how bad things look right now — and once you've done that, the past investment per se can't tip the scales unless it carries real information, and if it carries real information you should be able to say what it is. So either your rescue collapses into ordinary reasoning about the future, or it's spooky.

And be honest at the end about where the argument gets thin — like, does the epistemic flavor survive the case where past-you was demonstrably self-deceived, or where you literally wrote down at the time "I'm doing this because of FOMO"? Does the bargaining flavor survive the fact that you could just... decide to be a quitter forever and start only projects worth quitting?
