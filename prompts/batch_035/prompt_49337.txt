My book club is doing The Remains of the Day next month, and completely by accident I've been reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? at the same time, which has turned out to be a wild combination. Tolstoy's whole theory is that art is the transmission of feeling from artist to audience -- the artist feels something, and by gesture, words, rhythm, whatever, "infects" the viewer with that same feeling. And he says the main condition for real art is sincerity: the artist has to have actually felt the thing. If he's just imitating the surface of other art, or producing effects for money and status, it's counterfeit. He was brutal about this -- used the theory to trash Shakespeare and even most of his own earlier novels as counterfeit or elite art for the bored upper classes. He also wanted good art to be clear and universal, comprehensible to a peasant, transmitting feelings that unite people rather than feelings of pride or vanity that divide them.

So now I can't stop reading Ishiguro through it. Stevens is a machine of insincerity: his whole narration is a performance of professional dignity built to hide his feelings from himself, the banter he can't do, Miss Kenton he explains away as staffing arrangements, his father's death handled mid-conference. He narrates his own life as counterfeit art. And yet the novel made me cry. So the question I want an answer to is basically: whose sincerity matters for Tolstoy -- the narrator's or the author's? The book seems to be sincere Ishiguro deploying an insincere Stevens to transmit a feeling (grief, regret, self-deception) extremely effectively, which is maybe the one thing Tolstoy's theory doesn't have room for, since he never really talks about art that stages its own insincerity. Does it count as infection when the feeling transmitted is the failure to feel?

There's also the clarity problem. Tolstoy hated obscurity and elite art, and Remains of the Day is the great restrained literary novel -- everything crucial happens in what's withheld. Is that counterfeit by his lights, or is the withholding itself the gesture that transmits the feeling? And the ending: the pier, the rain, the chat with the stranger, Stevens crying on a bench and then deciding he'll work on his bantering. Is that scene Tolstovian art or exactly the kind of thing he'd call an effect calculated for connoisseurs? I honestly can't tell.

So what I want is: a reading of the novel that takes Tolstoy's test seriously, figure out whether the book passes, fails, or breaks the test, and say what the novel shows about the theory that the theory itself can't see. Which scenes should I point the group to? And is there a version of this where Tolstoy is right that the book is counterfeit and I just liked it anyway?
