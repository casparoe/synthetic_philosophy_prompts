I retired three years ago after a career in pharmacology, most of it spent on preclinical model systems, and I have since thrown myself into philosophy with more enthusiasm than training. The topic that has grabbed me is the thought experiment — what Daniel Dennett called intuition pumps — because to a laboratory scientist it looks uncannily like a model system: a deliberately stripped-down preparation in which some phenomenon is isolated from its usual clutter so that we can observe what it does on its own. In pharmacology we spent decades learning that this is a double-edged strategy. An in vitro assay is clean, cheap, and reproducible, and precisely for that reason it can be a beautiful artifact that predicts nothing about the organism; the simplification that makes the model run is often the removal of the very features that made the phenomenon real. So when I read Searle's Chinese room, or Mary the color scientist, or the teletransporter, I keep asking the same operational question I would have asked about a new assay: what is this preparation actually a model of, and what would I need to check before trusting its output?

What I would like from you is a prioritization, not a survey. There seem to be several open questions floating around this method, and I want to know which ones matter most, in what order, and — critically — why, because I am old enough to be stingy with my remaining attention. The candidates I can identify are roughly: (1) whether the intuitive verdicts these scenarios elicit count as evidence at all, or merely as psychological data about the reader; (2) whether one can principledly separate a genuine intuitive verdict from the framing effects of incidental scenario detail — the equivalent of asking whether my assay is measuring the drug or the solvent; (3) whether cross-cultural and individual variation in intuitions is a fatal confound, a mere calibration problem, or actually irrelevant; and (4) whether there is any prospect of a design discipline for thought experiments, as there eventually was for instruments — norms for when a pump should be trusted, when it should be run with its parameters perturbed to check robustness, and when it should be discarded. On that last point I will confess my own working hypothesis, which I have not seen stated anywhere and which may simply be wrong: that the most underrated open question is what I would call the reversibility check. In my field, a crucial test of an in vitro finding was always to ask what the model would have shown had some innocuous-seeming background condition been flipped; my suspicion is that many famous pumps have verdicts fixed entirely by an unstated boundary condition that flips trivially, and that a systematic method for detecting this would do more for the method's credibility than any amount of theorizing about intuitions in general.

So please rank these questions — and add any I have missed, or demote any that I have overweighted — by which are most worth a semi-trained but analytically minded outsider's attention, saying for each what is upstream of what, which are tractable by someone who cannot run experiments on experimental philosophy, and what considerations would move a question up or down your list. I care far more about the reasoning behind the ordering than about the ordering itself, and I would rather hear that one of my four questions is a dead end than receive a diplomatic hedge. Please keep the whole thing under 600 words.
