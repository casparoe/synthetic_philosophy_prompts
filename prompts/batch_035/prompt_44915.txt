I'm a graduate student drafting my philosophical profile on pleasure for a reading group on well-being, and before I circulate it I want a consistency audit. The set of commitments is this: (1) I'm a hedonist about well-being — pleasure and the absence of pain are the only things good for a person in themselves. (2) I accept that the heterogeneity problem is real: the pleasure of a hot bath, of a solved proof and of an evening with close friends seem to share no single felt quality, so I've concluded that pleasure isn't a feeling at all but an attitude of being pleased by something — which can attach to wildly different experiences precisely because it isn't one of them. (3) I take pain asymbolia, the condition in which patients report feeling pain but are unperturbed by it, to confirm this: the disvalue of pain lies in the aversive attitude, not the raw sensation. (4) I nonetheless think bodily pleasures like the hot bath are the paradigm case, and any account of pleasure that demotes them to a gerrymandered afterthought is wrong. (5) I think pleasures can occur unfelt and unnoticed — a background enjoyment of a long walk that you only register afterwards — and that these still count towards well-being. (6) I'm convinced hedonism, so understood, remains genuinely distinct from desire- or preference-satisfaction theories, and I don't want it collapsing into one. Which of these commitments are in real tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? I want you to take a definite position on which commitment, if any, has to go, and give your reasons for it rather than laying out the escape routes and leaving me to choose; and please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning.
