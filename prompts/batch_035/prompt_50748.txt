I'm drafting a piece for a student-run philosophy journal on whether moral compromise between opposed value systems can be modelled on economic gains from trade, and I'd be grateful for comments on the notes below. I'm particularly interested in two things: whether the central analogy does any real philosophical work or is merely decoration, and whether my distinction between two kinds of compromise is genuinely a distinction — or whether, on inspection, the "good" kind collapses into the "bad" kind. Comments on where the argument is weakest, or where a critic would press hardest, would also be welcome.

The idea, provisionally titled 'The Compromise Frontier', is as follows. Picture two co-legislators who must set policy together: one a prioritarian utilitarian who cares only about aggregate welfare weighted towards the worst-off, and one a Kantian deontologist who regards certain constraints (never punishing the innocent, no deception in public office) as absolute. The common view of their situation is that any policy they can agree on is a dilution: each should bargain for a point on a single continuum as close to their own ideal as possible, and every interior point is worse than at least one party's ideal. Call this mixing compromise. But if we instead think of the space of possible policies as high-dimensional — not a single left–right axis but thousands of mutually independent levers — and think of each moral system as imposing a preference ordering over that space, then the two orderings will typically disagree non-uniformly: some levers matter enormously to the utilitarian and very little to the deontologist, and vice versa. This opens the possibility of what I want to call frontier-seeking compromise: trades in which each party concedes heavily on the levers it values little and demands its way on the levers it values most, in the hope of landing on the joint Pareto frontier — policies neither party would choose alone, but which both prefer to any feasible unilateral outcome. My claim is that some actual political compromises are plausibly of this second kind (the utilitarian trades away agricultural subsidies in exchange for a categorical torture prohibition), and that these are not dilutions at all but the moral analogue of gains from trade, made possible by what one might call comparative moral advantage.

The obvious disanalogy, which I try to defuse rather than hide, is that in commercial trade preferences are merely different, whereas in moral disagreement each party takes the other to be wrong — there is no neutral currency in which to denominate the exchange. My response appeals to moral risk: if each legislator is even modestly uncertain that their own system is the correct one, then the expected value of a policy is a weighted mixture across systems, and the mixture itself generates the cross-cutting indifference contours that trade requires. Uncertainty, in other words, does the work that differing tastes do in economics. My worry is that this makes the whole thing question-begging: a confident deontologist will say that only mixing compromise is ever on offer, and that my frontier is a mirage produced by pretending that error is a taste. I'd love to know whether you think this response succeeds, and more generally whether the gains-from-trade framing illuminates anything that a straightforward appeal to moral uncertainty would not already deliver.
