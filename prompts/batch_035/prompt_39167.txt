I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar called The Ethics of Punishment, and our final assignment is a roughly 8,000-word research paper. The professor has explicitly warned us against writing a generic survey of retributivism versus consequentialism — she wants each of us to identify a genuinely open question in the debate over punishment versus rehabilitation and defend why that question deserves the words. My instinct is to write about the contrast between the Norwegian model, with prisons like Halden built around the idea that imprisonment itself is the punishment and life inside should otherwise approximate ordinary life, and the American system of mass incarceration, where degradation, gang structures, and post-release disenfranchisement often seem to be doing expressive work that the sentence length alone doesn't capture. But I've realized I don't actually know which of the questions lurking in that contrast are the live ones, and I have about ten weeks to choose.

Here are the candidate questions I've been circling, though I suspect I'm missing better ones. First, there's the confound problem: people cite Scandinavian recidivism figures as if they settled something about penal philosophy, but Norway's outcomes may owe as much to its welfare state, sentencing severity, and baseline crime patterns as to its penal ideology — so what exactly, if anything, does the Halden comparison prove? Second, there's what I think of as the translation problem: whether Norway's guiding idea that deprivation of liberty is the only punishment, with everything else aimed at reintegration, can survive being transplanted into a jurisdiction with vastly different demographics, gang economies, and rates of violent crime — or whether importing the aesthetics of rehabilitation without the surrounding social contract just yields a softer warehouse. Third, there's a worry from the historical treatment model: if you demote desert and make rehabilitation the core justification, don't you risk reviving the logic of indeterminate sentencing, where people are held not for what they did but for what a professional board believes about who they'll become? Fourth, whether retributive proportionality and rehabilitative aims are genuinely in tension or only contingently so — whether a system could satisfy a thoroughgoing retributivist while running Halden-style regimes.

What I'd like from you is a defended prioritization rather than a survey. Pick the three or four questions in this space — from my list or beyond it — that are most worth a ten-week philosophy paper, and rank them using explicit criteria: which matter most for actual penal reform if answered, which are tractable for someone doing conceptual rather than empirical work (I can't run recidivism studies), which are neglected in the sense that the obvious positions have already been staked out and refuted, and which are upstream of the others in the sense that a lot of adjacent work quietly depends on how they resolve. I'm equally interested in the negative half of the ranking: which parts of the Norway-versus-America comparison are, in your view, overrated or don't actually turn on any contested philosophical claim at all. And since the professor grades partly on how we'd revise under new evidence, tell me what a shift in the empirical picture — say, a credible study showing rehabilitation-centered prisons failing somewhere unexpected — would do to your ordering.
