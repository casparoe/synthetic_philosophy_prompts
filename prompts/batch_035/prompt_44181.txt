My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about a decision our allotment committee keeps deferring: whether to phase out a copper-based treatment we have used for years and now suspect is quietly accumulating in the soil, in favour of a biological control that nobody has trialled on soil like ours. She took the line that the precautionary principle, whatever its rhetoric, has nothing to say when every option carries uncertain harms: switching is itself the precautionary move, so the principle seems to demand caution about the precaution, and it ends up silent. I kept noticing that what actually moved me was not which harm was more probable but which error could be undone later: the slow buildup looked bad but stoppable, whereas a badly chosen introduced organism, if it misbehaved, might not be. Please describe the most important implications of the precautionary principle for how a committee like ours should reason about choices of this shape, where a familiar intervention and a novel one each carry uncertain harm, and the two possible errors differ chiefly in how reversible they are. Present the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two in length, with no introduction or conclusion, and please write in British English.
