Last night my friend and I concluded a two-year argument about a single judgment call, and we ended in an odd place: we now agree on every first-order fact, and have discovered that we disagree only about what to do with our own disagreement.

The occasion was mundane. Working separately from the same materials, we had reached opposite conclusions about a moderately technical question, each of us with a full explanation of why the evidence supports our side and the other side has misread it. We then each learned how the other had reasoned, and we each concede the other is an epistemic peer — comparable ability, comparable background, no known bias, no reason to think either of us got unlucky with the evidence. On learning this, I did what seems to me obvious: I split the difference and moved my confidence halfway toward his. He did not move an inch. His ground was that he can still see, in the materials themselves, exactly how they support his conclusion, and the bare fact that I cannot see it is no reason for him to doubt what he can see. When I asked whether he then thinks my immobility is also rational — since, symmetrically, I can see how the materials support mine — he said yes, and seemed unbothered that this commits him to the possibility of two people reasoning impeccably from the same evidence to flatly conflicting conclusions. That is where we stalled.

Since then the argument has become a general one about higher-order evidence, and it has sharpened around a case we invented. Suppose I review my own reasoning, find nothing amiss, but then receive a reliable report that people who feel exactly this confident, in exactly this kind of case, are mistaken most of the time. My friend says the report gives me no reason to alter the belief itself — at most a reason to stop acting on it. I say that is epistemic akrasia: believing what you simultaneously believe you ought not to believe, and no more defensible than the ordinary sort. A second, quieter issue runs underneath: if I must weigh evidence about the reliability of my weighing, must I then weigh evidence about the reliability of that weighing, and so on without end? He claims any policy of deference drowns in this regress; I suspect the iterations converge to something stable, but neither of us can show it.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on the original question. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that we are not really disagreeing about evidence at any order, but about what rationality is for: whether epistemic norms are policies for managing an unreliable self over time — in which case deferring to a peer is like a trader trusting the market price against his own reading — or whether they are rules for tracking truth from where one actually stands, in which case my friend's steadfastness is not arrogance but the only method by which anyone has ever found anything out. Is that the right way to cut it? Is the dispute substantive, or are we using one word, 'rational,' for two different things — one evaluating a person's whole epistemic conduct and one evaluating a belief from inside it? And is the regress question a genuine formal matter doing hidden work in the debate, or a red herring we could dissolve? Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here: a case that cleanly splits the two positions, a worked-out model of iterated higher-order updating, or nothing — and, if nothing, say plainly whether you think the question is ill-posed or merely hard.
