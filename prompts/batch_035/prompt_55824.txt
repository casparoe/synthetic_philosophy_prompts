My partner is a GP and I've been noticing something odd about how she talks about her day at dinner (all anonymized of course). For a young, healthy patient with a sore throat and a fever she'll say straight out "it's viral, fluids and rest" — no test, she says she knows. Then the same sort of presentation walks in, but the patient is pregnant, or 78 and diabetic, or it's chest pain instead of a sore throat, and the language completely changes: "we can't call it yet," "first let's rule out the bad thing," and more tests get ordered even though I'd swear her probability that it's the boring explanation hasn't really moved. It goes the other way too — cases where everyone in the room would put 99% on the scary diagnosis but nobody will say "knows" until a lab value comes back, and cases where she'll say "I know it's nothing" on evidence she'd call flimsy if more were riding on it. Sometimes I honestly can't tell whether the extra tests track what's at stake for the patient or what's at stake for her — the fear of missing something and getting sued, or just the fear of having to live with it. Not sure those are the same thing.

So what I keep wondering is: what theory of knowledge, or of evidence, is actually baked into this practice? Is it the idea that whether she *knows* the diagnosis depends on what's at stake — she mentioned once that there's a whole debate in epistemology with a name for this, I forget what it's called — or is it just expected-cost reasoning layered on top of perfectly ordinary knowledge, where knowledge itself never moves and only the threshold for *acting* does? In other words, does the practice genuinely presuppose that knowledge shifts with stakes, or would a classical view where knowledge is fixed license exactly the same ordering of tests? And where is it underdetermined, versus places where the specific words doctors use ("we know," "working diagnosis," "rule out") seem to track the concept of knowledge itself and not merely a decision rule? I also wonder if it's even internally consistent, given that the same profession swears by evidence-based medicine, which sounds like a commitment to knowledge being stake-independent if anything is. And last thing: if you made the implicit view explicit and fully probabilistic instead — doctors quoting exact posterior probabilities out loud and acting on fixed thresholds — what concretely would have to change in the clinic, and would we loose anything by dropping the word "know" from the chart? Something about it feels like it does more work than a probability report would.
