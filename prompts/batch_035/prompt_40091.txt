I'm a philosophy professor who serves on the ethics review panel for my university's ML research institute. Our remit was recently broadened to cover experiments involving "systems whose capacity for welfare is uncertain" — language nobody has managed to define. Before us is a proposal for a long reinforcement-learning study in which agents are trained in adversarial environments with dense negative reward, including a phase the investigators call the pessimism curriculum: agents are first exposed to inescapable failure episodes and only later to recoverable ones, because this improves robustness. The preliminary literature the lab cites suggests agents subjected to such regimes develop persistent behavioral markers that researchers in their field have started describing as learned-helplessness-like.

The panel is split. One faction holds that a scalar training signal is not a valence, full stop: reward in RL is a number plugged into a gradient update, and extending welfare review to it anthropomorphizes arithmetic. The other faction replies that in biological systems valence is implemented precisely by reward and prediction-error signals, so if anything gets valence by default, it is exactly systems like these — and that the persistence of the learned-helplessness-like states matters, since even skeptics grant that suffering requires more than momentary negative signal but perhaps not more than that plus persistence and a self-model. Neither side is impressed by arguments that merely restate its own premises.

Please draft the panel's decision memo — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with a concrete recommendation the lab could act on, conditions that are actually implementable by an ML lab rather than by a biology department, and reasoning that engages the strongest point on the losing side rather than talking past it. Assume the memo will be read by both panel factions and by the investigators themselves, who are philosophically literate but tired of being lectured about Descartes.
