A lecturer in a philosophy of physics course I'm auditing explained the epistemic view of the quantum state by analogy with classical statistical mechanics. Her point was that in statistical mechanics a distribution over phase space describes our ignorance about which microstate the system is really in; the distribution is a bookkeeping device, and nobody thinks it is a physical thing spread over the world. She said the quantum state is like this: a probability distribution over deeper states of affairs that we simply don't know, so that collapse on measurement is nothing but Bayesian conditioning on new data. On this basis she concluded that the wavefunction need not be taken as real, that the measurement problem dissolves into a puzzle about updating our records, and that quantum mechanics, properly understood, makes claims only about our knowledge, never about what exists. My physicist friend grumbles that this is precisely the picture that results like Bell's theorem and the Pusey-Barrett-Rudolph theorem are supposed to constrain, and that even interference already strains it. Could you audit the analogy? Please give me the correspondence table (quantum state ↔ phase-space distribution, underlying real states ↔ microstates, collapse ↔ Bayesian update, and whatever else maps), then say where the mapping genuinely holds, where it breaks, and which of the breaks matter for her conclusion and which are incidental to it. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: can the wavefunction still be demoted to a state of knowledge, or was the classical-statistical-mechanics picture carrying the conclusion the whole way?
