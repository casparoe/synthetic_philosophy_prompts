My logic reading group has spent over a year on future contingents and we're totally stuck. Quick map of where everyone's dug in: one guy holds the Aristotelian line that 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' is neither true nor false today; another is an Ockhamist — it's true or false right now, and the threat to bivalence is an illusion created by confusing truth with knowledge; a third is a supervaluationist, so the sentence is true only if it comes out true on every history through today, which makes ordinary future contingents neither true nor false, yet somehow 'either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't' still comes out true; and a fourth wants the thin red line — a distinguished actual future among all the branches.

Here's my working theory, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: we stopped moving because there's nothing substantive left to move about. The supervaluationist and the Aristotelian seem to agree on every actual sentence and differ only on bookkeeping, basically what to do with classical tautologies. The Ockhamist might just be using 'true' to do a different job, tracking a property of the utterance-plus-history pair rather than the utterance alone. And the thin red line person is arguably doing branching-worlds metaphysics, not semantics, so that's a different arguement entirely. If that's right, we're runing three or four conversations under one heading and no clever case we invent will ever decide anything between them.

So: is that diagnosis right, or do the positions genuinely collide somewhere all four would recognize as the same question — maybe over the past, or retrocausation, or what a prediction actually asserts? And given whatever diagnosis you settle on, what would count as progress here — a new distinction, a class of cases, splitting the question into pieces?
