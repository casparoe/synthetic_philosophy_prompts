I'm putting together a handout for an intro ethics section on when influence crosses the line from persuasion into manipulation. Write a "where do you actually draw the line?" quiz: 8 concrete scenarios, each with 3 answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

Cases I want in (use all of these, you can add one or two of your own if you need them to split leaves): (1) a salesperson who mirrors your posture and speech rhythm the whole conversation; (2) a charity ad featuring one named child instead of statistics about thousands; (3) a friend who builds up your confidence all evening right before asking to borrow money; (4) a supermarket that puts milk at the back of the store so you pass everything else; (5) a partner who exagerates how upset they'll be if you go out with your friends; (6) a surgeon who tells you the survival rate is "90%" rather than that mortality is 10%; (7) an app where signing up is one tap but cancelling is a maze of screens and retention calls; (8) a waiter who sincerely compliments your taste in wine before recommending a pricier bottle.

Leaves: at least a deception-and-pressure view (it's manipulation only if it involves lying or overt threats), a bypass view (it's manipulation when it works around your deliberation, even if every statement is true and the method is in plain sight), a hiddenness view (the line is whether the target could easily notice what's being done to them), and a deflationary leaf (no principled line, just more or less of it). The scoring key should be honest about who ends up deflationary.

Also do two diagnostic things: flag at least one pair of leaves that this set of cases can't really separate, and describe at least one answer pattern that doesn't map cleanly onto any leaf — say what you'd tell someone who lands there, rather than forcing them into the nearest box.

No question should just ask "is this manipulation?" — the options should each describe what bothers (or doesn't bother) the reader about the case, so people get sorted by their reasons rather than by which labels they like. American English please, and keep the whole thing — questions and scoring key together — under 900 words.
