I'm a retired physicist — spent about thirty five years on superconductors and phase transitions, mostly low-temp stuff — and now that I've got time on my hands I've been reading philosophy of mind and feeling like a tourist. Just worked my way through Gödel, Escher, Bach, and the dialogue with the Anteater and poor Aunt Hillary has been living in my head rent-free for weeks. The idea that the colony could be the thing that's conscious while every individual ant is dumb as a doorknob obviously rhymes with things from my own turf: no single electron in a superconductor 'decides' anything, but the condensate as a whole behaves like one macroscopic object with it's own degrees of freedom. But I keep going back and forth on whether that analogy actually helps the Aunt Hillary side or quietly sinks it, because with a superconductor we can say pretty exactly what the collective entity IS — an order parameter, a broken symmetry — and nobody thinks the condensate feels anything.

So here's what I want: write a dialogue between two characters arguing about whether an ant colony could literally have a mind. One (call her Hilary, after the ant) says yes, and she should make the strongest version of the case, not just vibes about emergence. The other — maybe a skeptical neuroscientist type — should be equally sharp. I'd especially like them to use my superconductor analogy both ways: Hilary leans on it, and then the skeptic points out where it breaks, e.g. in my field the collective entity is individuated by crisp physical criteria like a symmetry-breaking transition, so what's the equivalent criterion for saying there's ONE colony there rather than two colonies casually sharing workers? Somewhere in there, have them chew on a thought experiment I've been toying with: suppose you slowly replace the ants one at a time with little solar-powered beads that do the exact same pheromone-following job — does the colony-mind fade out gradually, drop off a cliff at some critical fraction, or was it never there in the first place? Don't let either of them win too easily, and keep them responding to each others actual points instead of giving speeches past each other.

One more thing: I'm new to the philosophy literature, so keep the jargon to a minimum — write it so a smart physics retiree can follow every exchange.
