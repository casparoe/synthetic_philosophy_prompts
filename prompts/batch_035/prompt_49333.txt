Two friends of mine got into it at dinner last week over a health app one of them uses, and I promised I'd get an outside opinion, so here I am. The app collects pretty detailed data on users' sleep, diet and moods, and the company just announced that it will share "fully anonymised, aggregated" data with a research institute that wants to study links between sleep patterns and reported anxiety. The anonymisation seems legit as far as anyone can tell: no names, coarse location buckets, nothing at the individual level ever leaves the company's servers.

They went back and forth for a while and I mostly stayed quiet and ate my risotto. As best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dan said there's simply no privacy issue here. Privacy is about protecting identifiable people from exposure — your diary getting read, your movements getting tracked, your medical file getting leaked. Aggregate statistics contain no person, so nobody's privacy is touched. People who object to stuff like this, he said, are really just voicing a vague discomfort with data collection in general, and that's not a principled objection, it's a vibe.

(2) Maya said that can't be right, because privacy isn't only about hiding things. It's about whether information flows in ways that fit the context in which you handed it over. You give an app intimate details so that it can show you your own trends — that's the deal. Shipping the data to a third party, even anonymised, changes what the information is *for*, and that's a violation in itself, whether or not anyone could ever be identified from it.

(3) Dan said that makes the concept of privacy hopelessly vague: if any unexpected use of information is a privacy violation, then a friend recommending a curry house because she knows I like spicy food becomes a privacy violation, which is absurd. And anyway, he said, if the real worry is what the researchers — or whoever ends up with the data down the line — might *do* with it (re-identify people, target ads at the anxious, whatever), then the wrongness lives in those downstream uses, and we should regulate those directly rather than pretending the transfer itself is the harm.

(4) Maya said you often can't regulate uses you couldn't foresee, which is exactly why control at the point of transfer matters. And she added a seperate point: even perfectly anonymous aggregates *do* something — they train the institute's models, they could help an insurer argue for higher premiums across a whole region, they change how a group of people gets treated. Being processed as a data point rather than dealt with as a person is itself a loss, she said, even if nobody can point at your name.

(5) Dan replied that if "being treated as a data point" counts as a privacy loss, then the census is a privacy violation too — and Maya definately doesn't want to abolish the census. So her view needs a principled line between the census and the app, and he didn't think she had one.

I honestly couldn't referee this and they eventually asked me to. So: please go through the five moves, say who's right about what at each step (and flag where either of them is stretching), then give me a verdict on who's more right overall. And if you think they're both missing something about what privacy actually is or why it's worth caring about, I'd like to hear that too.

Two practical things: write your answer in British English, and keep the jargon to a minimum — I've never read a philosophy paper in my life, so plain language please (coining your own terms to keep things straight is fine, just define them when you do).
