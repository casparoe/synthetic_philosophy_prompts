I teach an undergraduate seminar on historical method, and one recurring problem in it is that students arrive with a demarcation reflex trained almost entirely on how weird a conclusion sounds: Atlantis is fringe, the Henrician Reformation is mainstream, and everything in between gets sorted by vibes. I want to displace that with a handout that sorts claims about the past by the epistemic practice of the people making them — what they treat as evidence, how they handle refutation, where they publish, what risks they take with their own thesis — rather than by how tame or strange the content is. The handout should be a decision tree: the student enters at the top holding a description of a claim together with its community of proponents, answers a sequence of yes/no questions about features of the case, and lands on a leaf naming the position.

I want to fix the test cases in advance, so that the tree is forced to earn its keep rather than being built to fit whatever positions you find convenient to enumerate. The cases are: Fomenko's New Chronology, which holds that the conventional dating of antiquity and the Middle Ages is inflated by roughly a millennium of duplicated "phantom" centuries and sustained by misdated or fabricated sources; the ancient-aliens family of claims about prehistoric monuments and artifacts; the Atlantis tradition that treats Plato's Timaeus and Critias as garbled testimony to a real Bronze Age civilization, in the style of Ignatius Donnelly's nineteenth-century scholarship; the so-called Silurian hypothesis, the 2018 thought experiment in which Adam Frank and Gavin Schmidt asked, as a methodological exercise in astrobiology and Anthropocene studies, whether the geological record would even preserve detectable traces of an industrial civilization millions of years old; and at least one case of sincere, competent, discipline-internal revisionism that most of the field nonetheless considers almost certainly wrong — the recurring proposals to shorten Egyptian chronology by a few centuries are a candidate, but choose your own if you know a better one.

Two constraints should shape the tree. First, every branch node must be a question a student can answer from a plain description of the claim and its proponents, without already knowing which leaf it feeds — so no node may ask whether the view counts as pseudohistory, fringe, or anything similarly self-answering. Second, the tree must do two specific pieces of work: it must place the Silurian hypothesis and the ancient-aliens claims — which agree in content that the deep past may conceal an advanced civilization whose traces we misread — at leaves far apart, and it must place the discipline-internal wrong-but-serious revisionist somewhere distinct from the conspiracy-style construct historian, even though both reject the mainstream account. Where you cannot separate two leaves cleanly — for instance, if cautious heterodox re-dating and careful methodological speculation come out adjacent only because of how tame or wild the conclusion is, which is exactly the feature the tree is supposed to abstract away from — flag that explicitly rather than papering over it with an extra node. Please also name the unoccupied combinations your branch questions permit (say, a school that accuses the entire evidentiary record of coordinated forgery yet operates through the discipline's own journals and accepts specified disconfirmation risks), and say for each whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, career-fatal, or merely unexplored. Aim for seven or eight leaves, keep each branch label to a single question, and keep the whole thing printable on one page.
