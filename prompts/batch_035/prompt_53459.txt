I'm putting together materials for a seminar on environmental ethics, and I want a thought experiment that isolates a question we keep circling: whether standard conservation metrics and the aggregate welfare of wild animals can come apart, and in which direction. Construct two hypothetical ecosystems — call them Preserve A and Preserve B — that are identical on every measure a conservation biologist would actually use (species richness, total biomass, trophic structure, long-run stability, presence of rare or endangered taxa) yet that differ in the *sign* of aggregate wild animal welfare: one plausibly net positive, the other plausibly net negative, under the assumptions specified below. A visitor to either preserve should see what looks like healthy, flourishing nature in both cases; neither may be a blighted wasteland, a diseased herd, or anything else whose badness is visible on its face.

The constraints: first, use only animals a layperson knows well — rabbits, mice, deer, songbirds, foxes, and the like — no species whose life histories a reader would have to look up. Second, the divergence must not depend on invertebrate sentience or on any unresolved question about which creatures feel pain; stipulate that all vertebrates involved are sentient and leave everything else out of the picture. Third, and most important, the welfare verdicts must be *derived* from the scenario's stated life-history details, not stipulated by fiat: you may not simply declare that the animals in Preserve B suffer more. My own suspicion is that the work will be done by the demographic mix — the balance between long-lived animals in stable populations and short-lived animals in boom-and-bust ones, given that in most such populations the overwhelming majority of individuals die before maturity — but you should work this out rather than assume it.

After the scenario, do three further things. Walk through the verdicts: what a total utilitarian would say about destroying each preserve versus leaving both alone, whether an average utilitarian would rank the options differently, and whether the two rankings can be made to conflict in your case or whether the case collapses into agreement (say honestly which of these your construction achieves, and if there is no conflict, whether one is achievable under the constraints). Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks that destroying a flourishing ecosystem for welfare reasons misses the point entirely — something in the spirit of the land ethic, where the biotic community itself rather than the experiences of its members is the object of moral concern — and say whether your case could be redesigned to pressure that view too, or whether that view is simply immune to welfare arguments. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature: don't present any particular view about the badness of death, the moral weight of an animal that dies young, or the relationship between total and average welfare as though it were consensus when people in the field disagree. Please write your answer in British English.
