I have accumulated a set of my own judgments about what grounds what, in the metaphysician's sense of a non-causal determination relation in virtue of which one fact obtains, and I would like to know whether some single picture of grounding and fundamentality underlies them, or whether I must give some of them up. The ball's being red, I hold, obtains in virtue of its being scarlet. The truth of a disjunction, in virtue of its true disjunct. The existence of the singleton of Socrates, in virtue of Socrates himself — and not conversely, whatever the reductionist says about building the concrete out of the abstract. The illegality of moving a rook diagonally, in virtue of the rules of chess together with the conventions and expectations that sustain them. The fact that a certain French word means dog, in virtue of the linguistic practices of its speakers. But the wrongness of torturing a cat I judge NOT to be grounded in any natural fact — not in the suffering, not in anything else — even while I accept that there is no possible world that is exactly alike in natural respects but differs morally. So some facts that are metaphysically necessitated by others are grounded in them, and some are not, and I cannot say what marks the difference. A further datum: when a colleague says the match lit because it was struck, I want to say this is explanation without grounding; yet with the gas's temperature in virtue of the mean kinetic energy of its molecules, I go back and forth. Please tell me whether there is a principled account of grounding that captures all of these verdicts. If not, say which of my verdicts you would overturn and why, and state the resulting account precisely enough that it delivers a verdict on a case I have not listed — say, the negative existential that there are no unicorns, and what, if anything, grounds it.
