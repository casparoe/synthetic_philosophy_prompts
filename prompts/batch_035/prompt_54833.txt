Something I've been chewing on and can't resolve myself: the entire practice of clinical anesthesia has to guarantee you're unconscious, but the anesthesiologist can't check for experience directly — you can't report while you're under. So the field runs on proxies: whether you move when the knife goes in, whether heart rate and blood pressure spike, and, more recently, processed EEG monitors that squash your brainwaves into a single number from 0 to 100, where they aim for a band that statistically means "very unlikely to be conscious." And the outcome the profession officially defines itself as preventing is "awareness with recall" — the patient who wakes up during surgery and remembers it afterward. Which creates this awkward wrinkle: part of the standard drug cocktail (the amnesic component, e.g. midazolam) is there specifically to block memory formation, so in principle you could have a patient who was experincing the surgery but will recall nothing, and the field mostly treats that as a tolerable near-miss rather than the emergency that awareness-with-recall is.

So here's my question: what theory of consciousness does this whole apparatus actually presuppose? Is clinical anesthesia implicitly committed to something like global workspace theory or an access-consciousness picture — where consciousness just is whatever tends to produce reports and memory encoding, so no-report plus no-consolidation equals no-harm-done? Or could a defender of phenomenal consciousness as something over and above access run the exact same practice, just grudgingly, treating all the proxies as an epistemic crutch while insisting the amnesic-but-awake patient was genuinely harmed even though no instrument could ever show it? Relatedly, does the practice rule out any views outright — would someone who thinks integrated information or recurrent processing is the real story of consciousness have to run the operating room differently, or would they converge on the same monitors and the same target numbers anyway?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory baked into the practice, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it's underdetermined. And then say concretely what would have to change — in the monitors, the drug protocols, the definition of an adverse event — if we collectively decided that phenomenal experience without any downstream access is the thing we're obligated to prevent. Bonus points if you can work in the meta-problem angle: the profession's measures are downstream of patient reports in a way that seems to make the "hard" question of whether anyone was really experiencing anything structurally unaskable from inside the practice.
