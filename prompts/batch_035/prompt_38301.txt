I am a novelist, twelve years and four published books into a career, and the recurring professional decision that I handle worst is not any of the decisions about writing itself but the one about whether to keep going: whether a stalled manuscript deserves another year of my working life or whether it should be shelved. I have noticed that my reasoning in these moments is a tangle of sunk-cost rationalizations and legitimate forward-looking considerations, and I cannot reliably tell them apart from the inside. I would like a decision procedure I can actually run, grounded in what the philosophical literature on the sunk cost fallacy actually establishes, and honest about where it stops being philosophy and becomes judgment.

The principle, as I understand it, is that costs already incurred are rationally irrelevant to whether I should incur further costs on the same project. But I do not want a procedure that simply intones that principle at me, because I know the principle is contested, and the contest matters for my cases. The procedure must take seriously the arguments that honoring sunk costs is not always irrational: that a policy chosen once over a whole writing life ("I finish what I contract for," or "I don't abandon books at 80,000 words") can dominate act-by-act reasoning even though every individual act looks like capitulation to sunk costs; that my past self is a kind of counterparty whose reliance I ought to weigh; that the years already spent are themselves evidence — my past self knew things about this project that I now only half remember; that finishing a broken book teaches craft that shelving never does; that my agent sold the trilogy on the strength of a partial and my publisher has paid an advance in reliance on delivery. The procedure should not pre-judge which of these survive scrutiny; it should tell me where each one enters the analysis and what it would have to be true for it to count.

Constraints on the procedure. This decision arises perhaps twice a decade; each wrong answer costs me a year or more, and my agent and editor are parties to whatever I decide. There is no time pressure once the question is asked, but there is enormous emotional pressure, which is the reason I need the procedure at all. So: questions in a fixed order, each phrased so that I can answer it from my desk in an evening; a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; explicit stop-points where the procedure's output is "shelve it" and, separately, where the procedure hands the decision back to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Be candid about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; I would rather know where the seams are than be flattered by false rigor.

Then run the procedure on three cases drawn from my actual situation. First: the third volume of a contracted trilogy, one hundred thousand words written of a planned hundred and ten thousand, but my own most recent reread confirms what a trusted reader gently suggested — the central premise collapses in the final act, and repairing it means rewriting from the opening of the second act onward. Second: a first novel abandoned four years ago at sixty thousand words, which I still love, but which two readers I trust have each quietly failed to finish, and which I have never been able to diagnose. Third: a half-finished literary novel under no contract, with no advance and no external expectation, that I have told myself I will return to "between projects" for a decade. Include at least one case where the procedure's verdict is one I will dislike — I confess I want it to license finishing the trilogy, and I suspect it will not — and say plainly what the procedure cannot do for me in each case.

Finally, I want to be able to see where the philosophy lives. Mark the step or steps at which the verdict would flip if strict sunk-cost irrelevance were true, versus some honor-the-sunk-costs view being true, so that I can tell which of my answers are doing the philosophical work and which are merely psychology. Please write in American English, and keep the register formal; this will sit in a file I read at my lowest moments, and it should sound like a document that deserves to be obeyed.
