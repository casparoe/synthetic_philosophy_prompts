I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the quest for the historical Jesus — fourteen students, one three-hour session. They will already have read standard treatments of the criteria of authenticity (dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, linguistic Semitisms), so I do not want a discussion of the criteria; I want an activity in which students apply them and run into the method's limits before anyone names those limits. Here is the design I have in mind, which I would like you to work out in full.

You should design a small invented corpus — roughly twenty sayings and short pericopes that you compose yourself, precisely so that nobody in the room can lean on memory or on received scholarly verdicts — with controlled features baked in. Some items should be attested in multiple documents, where I hand out the "sources" as separate sheets with partially overlapping content and partially shared dependencies, so that students have to discover for themselves that multiple attestation collapses once the sources turn out not to be independent. Some should contain Aramaic-flavored constructions; some should be awkward for each of several possible pictures of Jesus; some should be authentic-seeming in every formal respect while embedding a view of Jesus that the criteria themselves give us no way to arbitrate.

Split the class into four small "seminars," each secretly assigned a different working profile of who Jesus was — an apocalyptic prophet, a wandering Cynic-style sage, a messianic revolutionary, a Pharisaic-minded reformer within Judaism. Each group votes on the authenticity of every item using the criteria alone, with its profile as background judgment, and records its verdicts in some format more interesting than a show of hands (colored ballots, a public ledger, something with a trace we can compare across groups afterward). The payoff I want them to feel is that the same criteria, applied to the same corpus, reconstruct four recognizably different figures — and, more unsettling, that each group will find its own profile subtly confirmed by the criteria it privileges.

Please supply: the full instructions participants receive, the four profile cards, the structure of the source sheets and their dependencies (including where you have deliberately made "independent" attestation spurious), a session timeline, the voting and recording mechanism, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the standard objections — the circularity lurking in dissimilarity, the interdependence of criteria, the slide from "plausible within my picture" to "authentic." Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students recognizing the profiles early, or treating the corpus as too fictional to take seriously, or a group deciding all criteria are worthless and voting randomly), and include a frank closing note on which feature of the real historical-Jesus debate this activity cannot reproduce — above all the actual source-critical questions about the Synoptics, Q, and John, which no invented corpus can stand in for — and how I should supply that feature in the debrief without it becoming a lecture.
