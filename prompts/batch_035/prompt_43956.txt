Explain how counterpart theory and transworld identity differ in their treatment of claims like "Humphrey might have won the election." Which view does a better job of making such de re modal claims true, and why?

and here's the student's answer, copied verbatim:

Transworld identity says the very same person exists in multiple possible worlds, so when we say Humphrey might have won, we point to a world where that very individual, Humphrey himself, wins. Counterpart theory, defended by David Lewis, denies this: nothing exists in more than one world. Instead, what makes "Humphrey might have won" true is that in some other world there is someone who closely resembles Humphrey — a counterpart — and that person wins. Kripke's notion of rigid designation supports counterpart theory nicely: since "Humphrey" names the same man in every world where he exists, we can see that the counterpart relation holds in virtue of this sameness of reference. Also, the counterpart relation is an equivalence relation, so it partitions individuals into tidy classes, which keeps the ontology neat. One might worry, following Plantinga's complaint that Lewis's view tells us what happens to Humphrey's counterparts but not to Humphrey himself, but that objection is just a point about English idiom, not about the metaphysics.
