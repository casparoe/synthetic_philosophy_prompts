Most introductory science textbooks open with a short historical chapter, and most science museums arrange their halls chronologically, and both do the same thing: a parade from antiquity to the present in which each figure is credited roughly in proportion to how closely they anticipated what we now believe — Aristarchus gets a paragraph for guessing heliocentrism, Newton gets a page — while whole traditions that lead nowhere are quietly omitted. Historians call this way of writing "Whig history." Setting aside the moral question of whether it's bad history, I want to know what the practice presupposes. What view of science, and of truth, has to be in place for this to be the natural way to tell the story — that belief accumulates toward a fixed target we now occupy, and that earlier thinkers were working on the same project as us, so they can be scored on how far they got? Or does the practice not need anything that strong, and would several different views of science license it equally well? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that the same textbooks elsewhere insist that discarded theories were rational in their own context — can the same chapter both praise precursors for anticipating us and absolve the others for not? And finally, tell me what the opening chapter or the museum hall would look like if built on the opposite view, that the questions earlier thinkers were asking were partly not ours at all: what gets added, what gets cut, and whether any historical chapter survives at the front of the book.
