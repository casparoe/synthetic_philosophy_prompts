I am a retired analytical chemist. For most of my career I worked in a university conservation-science unit, where my job was the instrumental analysis of paintings — X-ray fluorescence, infrared reflectography, cross-sections of paint layers — largely in the service of authentication disputes. Now that I have time on my hands I have begun reading aesthetics, and there is a cluster of practices surrounding forgery that I cannot make sense of using the habits of mind my training gave me. I would like your help in reconstructing the implicit theory of value that these practices presuppose.

Consider the following. When a major auction house discovers, decades after a sale, that a painting it sold as a Vermeer is in fact a twentieth-century forgery, it refunds the buyer — even though the object hanging on the buyer's wall has not changed by a single molecule, and even though the buyer may have derived decades of genuine aesthetic pleasure from it in the meantime. Museums do something analogous with labels: a canvas reattributed from a master to his pupil loses most of its monetary and insurance value and is often relegated to storage, though nothing visually detectable in the work has altered. And yet the very same institutions will cheerfully mount a blockbuster exhibition of Fakes and Forgeries, display the objects they have thereby condemned, and charge admission — clearly expecting the public to enjoy looking at them.

From my side of the lab bench this looks incoherent. My instruments measured physical composition, and my working assumption was always that the value of a picture must supervene on properties of that kind — properties visible in the work or at least detectable in it. A relabeling alters none of them. So my questions are these. What theory of value does this cluster of practices presuppose: is it purely epistemic, the thought that a forgery involves a deception whose exposure somehow retroactively devalues the aesthetic experience it supported, or does the practice require the original to be aesthetically better in itself, along the lines philosophers have urged when they claim that any difference between an original and even a perfect copy must count aesthetically? Please distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, identify where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined — could several quite different accounts of value license the same refunds, relabelings, and exhibitions? — and tell me what would have to change in institutional practice if the physical-aesthetic supervenience view I find natural were adopted instead. Take on the role of a professional philosopher working in aesthetics and the philosophy of art, think through the problem carefully step by step, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to a reconstruction. And do feel free to correct my assumption that the supervenience claim is straightforwardly true.
