I came to Stoicism on my own a few years ago, after a period where everything seemed to go wrong at once — a layoff, a parent's slow decline, a friendship that fell apart for reasons I never fully understood. The idea that finally gave me some peace was the dichotomy of control: that everything splits into what is up to me (my judgments, my decisions, my efforts) and what is not (other people, outcomes, the past, my body), and that my distress came precisely from pouring effort and expectation into the second pile. Since then I've kept a daily journal where I sort the day's troubles into those two columns, and I've become genuinely convinced that this division is the deepest piece of practical wisdom I've ever encountered — that almost all human unhappiness is a misplacement problem, and that someone who fully internalized the dichotomy would be close to unshakable.

But I want to stress-test this rather than just enjoy it, and I've accumulated some worries I can't resolve on my own. First, the line doesn't look as clean as the slogan suggests: some modern writers I've come across propose a "trichotomy," adding a middle category of things we partly influence — my health, my reputation, whether my marriage survives — and I can't tell if that's a genuine improvement or an admission that the original dichotomy can't handle most of real life, which seems to happen mostly in that gray zone. Second, the "safe" side of the line may not be safe: my judgments and impulses are themselves shaped by habit, upbringing, depression, addiction — things I didn't choose — so if nothing on the outside is fully up to me, maybe nothing on the inside is either, and the whole edifice rests on a notion of control nobody actually has. Third, and this bothers me most, I've read that psychology research on perceived control runs in the opposite direction — that people who believe they can influence external outcomes tend to do better, be healthier, and cope more effectively, which would make the dichotomy's prescription quietly corrosive rather than liberating. And there's a political version of the worry: it sounds like the doctrine could be preached to abused spouses or oppressed workers as a way of telling them to stop complaining.

So here is my request. Take my position at its strongest — that the dichotomy carves the world at its real joint, and that internalizing it is sufficient for tranquility — and tell me what would count against it. Sort the possible defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which of the worries I listed (or ones I haven't thought of) would a committed Stoic have to concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as a misreading? I'm especially interested in what the position actually risks — is there any fact about human psychology, or any real-life situation, where following the dichotomy would predictably make things worse, such that the view pays for itself with predictions rather than slogans? And finally, judge whether the refined versions — the trichotomy, the idea that externals have "some" value as preferred indifferents — are honest repairs or a slow retreat into a view that can no longer lose.
