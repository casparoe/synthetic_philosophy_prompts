I am a novelist: twenty years of writing and revising third-person-limited narration, managing what my characters know and don't know, and a standing professional interest in what dramatists call the recognition scene — the moment a character is seen, truly, by another, and the story pivots on it. I have no training in philosophy. I have tried to read Hegel and failed; the sentences about the master and the slave, the struggle for recognition, seem to be about the one thing I think about every working day, and I would like them translated into the only technical vocabulary I actually command.

That vocabulary is this: point of view and its permissions (who is allowed to know what, and when); free indirect discourse (the narrator slipping into a character's private language while remaining grammatically in the third person); the distinction between what a character wants and what a character needs, which is how my trade talks about the difference between a stated goal and the deeper satisfaction the story withholds until it is earned; agency, meaning a character's capacity to act under pressure rather than merely to be acted upon; and offstage versus onstage — whether a character's inner life is ever rendered directly or only inferred by others through behavior.

Please take Hegel's master–slave dialectic — the life-and-death struggle, the slave's terror of death, the master's dependence on the slave's recognition, the slave's transformation through work, the claim that the master ends up less free than the servant — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, entirely in these terms. I want the struggle rendered as a question about point of view: is a character ever real to himself in his own narration, or only when he appears in someone else's? I want the master rendered as a character who can never be seen — who has purchased immunity from every other POV in the book, and who therefore cannot get the one thing the story of his inner life requires. I want the slave's labor rendered as the craft process itself: the character who is worked on by the work, and who, in free indirect discourse, gradually acquires a voice the master never gets. Map each stage of the dialectic onto a stage of a novel's construction, and tell me which moves of the dialectic become, on this translation, ordinary craft wisdom — things I already believe about which characters can carry a book and which cannot.

Then the residue report, which is the part I most want. Which parts of Hegel translate cleanly? Which had to be distorted to fit — in particular, does the dialectic's famous necessity, the claim that this outcome follows from the structure of recognition itself rather than from anyone's character or choices, survive in my vocabulary at all, where nothing is necessary except what the author makes necessary? Is there anything in the philosophical position that has no counterpart in craft terms — and if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something a novelist ought to have, or because Hegel's necessity is less substantive than it looks on the page? Please be honest about the temptation I suspect you will face: a novelist can always invent a character who defies the structure, so it is open to me to conclude that Hegel is writing a very ambitious book review of a book that cannot exist. Tell me whether that conclusion is right, or whether it is the one place my vocabulary cannot follow.
