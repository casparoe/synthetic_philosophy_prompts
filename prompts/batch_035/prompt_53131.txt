I write historical fiction, mostly set in the nineteenth century, and at a recent workshop a critic with a history background twice dismissed passages in which my characters speak of their country as if it were a person — a homeland to be loved, defended, or betrayed. Both times she closed the discussion with the same remark: that a nation is an imagined community. It was played much like a trump card, and it was clearly meant to establish that in depicting heartfelt national feeling among rural characters I had committed an anachronism: that such feeling, in such a setting, could not have been real.

I have since found the phrase everywhere. Reviewers deploy it; so, apparently, does half of my profession. I gather it originates with a book on nationalism by Benedict Anderson, and that it travels in company with a companion slogan, to the effect that traditions are invented. What I cannot work out is what the sentence actually claims, since the work it is made to do varies wildly: sometimes it seems to mean only that no one ever meets more than a handful of their compatriots; at other times it appears to imply that national feeling is somehow fraudulent, or manufactured and therefore inauthentic.

Please lay out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise theses that "a nation is an imagined community" could be expressing, from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, say whether it is true or defensible, and, crucially, whether it would actually support the use my critic put it to: ruling out sincere patriotic attachment in ordinary people before the age of mass literacy and mass media. Where relevant, do the same for the claim that traditions are invented, since I cannot tell whether the two slogans make the same point or merely sound as if they do. I would be particularly grateful if you flagged which readings trade on a slide between something being imagined and something being imaginary, or between being constructed and being fake — slides that matter enormously to a novelist, whose whole stock-in-trade is making readers feel genuine emotions about people who never existed. I intend to use your list both when revising the novel and the next time the phrase is played against me at a workshop.

Please answer in British English.
