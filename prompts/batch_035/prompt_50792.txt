Been reading up on Augustine on lying and I keep wondering what he'd say about stuff that didnt exist back then. My understanding of the view: lying is always wrong, full stop — no lying to save someone's life, no lying to spare feelings, not even joke-lies. The only outs seem to be shutting up, being vague on purpose, and letting people draw their own conclusions. Which sounds insane untill you actually read his reasoning, and then it's at least not naive.

Anyway, here are the scenarios I want his predicted verdicts on. Please ground them in his real logic — his definition of lying as asserting something against what you believe, his distinction between concealing truth and telling a falsehood, his answer to the person-hiding-from-pursuers cases — not just "lying bad."

1. Deepfakes. No one asserts a sentence at all. Someone produces a fake video of a politician saying something he never said and it spreads on its own. Is this lying in Augustine's sense, or does his definition just not reach it? What would he say the producer's sin is, exactly, if the producer never speaks?

2. Bluffing in poker. Everyone at the table knows deception is part of the game, so nobody is deceived about the rules of what they're getting into. Does that matter to him? He seemed to think lies were wrong partly because of what they do to the deceived person's mind, and here the "deceived" person consented in advance to the whole setup.

3. Placebo prescriptions. Doctor gives a sugar pill and says it should help with the nausea, which is false as stated but causally helps because the patient believes it. His kind of nightmare case, right? Or does the fact that it actually helps change anything?

4. Lying to an AI. You tell a chatbot you're a safety researcher running an eval so it'll answer a question it otherwise refuses. Augustine's account of lying seems to involve aiming a false assertion at a rational mind that deserves the truth. Is a language model a rational mind in any sense he'd recognize, and does it matter for his verdict whether the thing you're deceiving can form beliefs? Curious what he'd do with a target that might not have a mind at all.

5. Surprise parties. You tell your mom you're busy Saturday so the surprise works. Kind lie, but the deceived person endorses the whole thing once it's revealed — most people actively want to be surprised this way. Does retroactive endorsement do any work in his framework, or would he say that's just the self corrupting itself with two lies instead of one?

Also: does any of these actually stress his position, or does the strictness handle them more comfortably than I expect? I suspect the AI one and the deepfake one are the genuinely novel ones for him, and I'd like to know if you agree and where you think his framework has to be extended vs where it just applies as-is. And if you think he'd bite some bullet so hard that modern readers would write him off entirely, say which bullets.
