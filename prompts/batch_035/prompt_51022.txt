At a recent session of my philosophy of mind workshop, devoted to whether large language models can literally have beliefs, a colleague waved off my skepticism with Dennett's slogan that beliefs are real patterns: if a system's internal states support robust prediction under the intentional stance, then it has beliefs, full stop. A second colleague objected that on that reading the word "real" is doing no work, since by the same standard the average consumer or a currency's purchasing power would count as real; she added that Dennett himself compares the reality of beliefs to that of centers of gravity, which will strike readers as either very strong realism or very weak realism depending on how one takes centers of gravity. I left the session unable to say what the slogan actually commits us to. Could you disentangle the distinct precise theses "real patterns" might be expressing — from the near-trivial claim that there are exploitable predictive regularities, through the claim that something can be real without being fundamental, to the radical claim that what is real is relative to a stance — and, for each, say whether it is plausible and whether it actually secures beliefs for large language models? Please also tell me whether any reading vindicates the second colleague's thought that reality requires more than predictive utility. I would like the readings set out as a numbered menu, ordered from weakest to strongest, that I can circulate before our next meeting.
