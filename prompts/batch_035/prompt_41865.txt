I'm a lay member of a regional health authority's public consultation panel — I joined after answering a newspaper ad, and most of what we handle is mundane, like visiting-hours rules. But our current item is anything but: we've been asked for a formal written recommendation on whether to publicly fund pre-implantation genetic testing for IVF patients who carry genes for a serious, progressively disabling childhood condition. The clinical case in favor is straightforward — the program would prevent a predictable amount of severe suffering in young lives. Our nine-member panel is split roughly three ways. One group wants it funded with no strings attached. A second group, which includes two parents of children who have the condition, objects to the framing itself: saying the program "prevents suffering," they argue, amounts to the state saying that children with the condition ought not to exist, and that the money would be better spent on care and support for families who already exist. The third group (which includes me) is unsure and keeps going back and forth. I've been assigned to draft the memo we'll circulate before the vote in three weeks, and I want it to be honest about the difficulty rather than a cover for a decision we've already made.

The reason I'm unsure is something I've been turning over in my own head for weeks, without any training in philosophy to help. If a couple uses the testing, the child eventually born is a different child from the one who would have been born otherwise — that's just how the biology works. So it seems that no actual person ends up worse off with the condition than they otherwise would have been, and no child is actually rescued from it; the before-and-after comparison simply doesn't line up person by person. And yet I can't shake the feeling that the total amount of suffering in the world really does differ between the two futures, and that this difference is precisely what the funding decision is about. There's also a second asymmetry I keep bumping into and can't resolve: almost nobody thinks parents or the state are obligated to bring additional happy children into existence, yet nearly everyone thinks it's wrong to knowingly bring a child into the world whose life will be dominated by pain. If creating a burdened life is bad but creating a happy life isn't required, what exactly is the funding decision tracking — and does our panel's choice even have a victim or a beneficiary in the ordinary sense?

Please draft the memo: a clear recommendation (fund outright, reject, or fund with conditions — such as pairing the program with expanded support services), the reasoning behind it, a serious and respectful engagement with the parents' objection, and an honest treatment of the two puzzles I described, written so that every panel member can follow it without any philosophical background. My co-panelists will smell evasion if the memo just changes the subject when the hard part comes up, so please don't.
