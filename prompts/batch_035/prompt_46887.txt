I am putting together a diagnostic quiz for a reading group, and I would like your help writing it. Let me give you the background first, because it constrains the job.

My younger brother is awaiting sentencing for a serious assault committed during an untreated psychotic episode. Since his arrest I have joined a small reading group on free will and moral responsibility — partly for distraction, partly because the questions have stopped being academic for me. Our next session is on P. F. Strawson's 1962 essay Freedom and Resentment, and I have been asked to lead it. I want to open the session with a sorting quiz titled, roughly, "which view of moral responsibility do you actually hold?" The honest truth is that I am also the quiz's target audience: before my brother's arrest I had a settled view of blame, and now I find that my reactions to cases point in different directions, and I want an instrument that sorts by what people actually say about cases rather than by which label they find flattering.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology, agency, and the free will debate, with experience writing diagnostic instruments for seminars. Think carefully before writing: start by sketching the logical space — the independent questions about responsibility that concrete cases can probe — and only then locate the named positions within that space. Build the quiz from the cases, not from the labels.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options. Every question must be answerable by someone with no philosophy training, and must not give away which position an answer supports. No question may simply ask the reader to self-ascribe a label. The following cases must appear, since my group has argued about all of them:

1. A sudden-onset case: a person with an undetected brain tumor causes grave harm through an unquestionable physical pathology.
2. A gradual-deprivation case: a person shaped by horrific circumstances with no pathology at all, where bad luck seeps in over decades rather than striking all at once.
3. A covert-manipulation case: a person whose deliberation is entirely normal, but whose values were secretly engineered by a third party.
4. A moral-luck pair: two equally careless drivers, only one of whom kills a child.
5. A case close to my family's actual situation: whether someone can still appropriately be the object of resentment or a sanction if, through treatment, the person who acted now no longer exists in the relevant psychological sense.
6. At least one first-person question about the reader's own reactive attitudes — for example, whether they can feel genuine resentment toward someone they sincerely believe is not blameworthy — to separate readers who treat reactive attitudes as data about desert from readers who treat them as non-evidential feelings, or as feelings to be explained away.

The scoring key must map answer patterns onto these leaves: the retributive basic-desert view; hard incompatibilism or free-will skepticism; the Strawsonian quality-of-will view, on which blame tracks ill will and indifference rather than metaphysical freedom; reasons-responsiveness compatibilism; and revisionist or instrumentalist views on which blame is justifiable only by its effects on behavior and relationships, including an explicitly illusionist variant if the answer patterns support it.

Three further requirements. First, before the scoring key, identify the unoccupied regions of the logical space — combinations of answers no live position accommodates — and diagnose each briefly: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored? Second, flag honestly any pair of leaves the quiz cannot separate (for instance, if you find that no case cleanly divides quality-of-will views from reasons-responsiveness views, say so rather than pretending one of the questions does the work). Third, expect mixed profiles: someone like me will answer the tumor case like a skeptic and the manipulation case like a compatibilist, and the scoring key should handle this with a short interpretive paragraph about what the tension amounts to, rather than forcing a single leaf. In my own case, please do not resolve the tension for me by fiat.

Keep the cases short and plainly written, and close with one candid paragraph on the limits of the instrument — what this quiz genuinely cannot sort out, even at its best.
