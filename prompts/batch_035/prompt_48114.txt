I'm preparing notes for a philosophy-of-art reading group on what, if anything, we can get from other people's say-so about aesthetic matters, and I've been cataloguing my own gut reactions in the hope of finding the pattern behind them, so I'd be grateful for help turning the catalogue into something principled. My oldest friend, whose judgement has tracked mine for twenty years, tells me a new film is a masterpiece; I believe her straightaway and would even say I know it is, and that feels entirely fine. When I've read the same assessment in a long review by a critic I rate, I believe it just as readily, though I somehow feel more entitled to act on the belief (booking tickets) than to voice it as my own. When 40,000 strangers have rated the film 8.7 out of 10, I'll happily let that settle whether I see it, but I'd feel a fraud telling someone at a party that it's a masterpiece on that basis — even though the aggregate is, let's be honest, at least as reliable as any single critic. A sommelier friend tells me a wine is superb; I believe him, I buy it, I serve it, but when a guest praises the wine and I find myself nodding along, I feel I'm agreeing to something I haven't earned, despite believing it. A friend who has read the novel tells me the ending is devastating; I believe her, though plainly I haven't been devastated, and that case seems uninteresting rather than puzzling. And when a poet I trust tells me my own poem is one of the best things I've written, I find I can't take it in at all — his word settles nothing for me in a way the critic's word does.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these — something about the difference between taking a verdict on trust and acquiring whatever it is one has when one has seen, tasted or read for oneself — or will the best account have to be a patchwork? I'd like you to state the principle, or your two or three best candidates, precisely enough that I can apply them myself; tell me which of my verdicts each clause is there to save; and be honest about which of my reactions no version of the principle can accommodate without becoming not worth wanting. I would particularly like you to look hard at the pair I can't make sit together: I claim outright knowledge from one trusted friend about a film, yet refuse the standing to say something much weaker from forty thousand strangers, and refuse it again about a wine I'm actively serving to people. Then tell me what your preferred principle predicts for cases I haven't decided: whether a prize juror who has read only the reviewers' assessments of the shortlisted books can justifiably rank them; whether a gallery caption assuring me that the painting in front of me is the finest in the room gives me something worth having; and whether I can trust my friend's word that a haircut suits me in the way I trust her word about a film.

Since I'll be defending whatever I settle on to the group, please say how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the structure is load-bearing than receive a smooth verdict. If the honest conclusion is that my reactions are a mixture of a genuine epistemic asymmetry and mere social squeamishness, say so and tell me which verdicts belong to which category.
