I've been arguing with a friend from my book club about whether my own choices matter morally, and he gave me a summary of what he'd read in a popular philosophy book that I can't quite shake, but also can't quite trust. He said roughly this: "Real harms like climate change and factory farming are produced by billions of people acting together, so the harm done by your personal contribution is essentially zero. If you skip one flight, the plane flies anyway; if you stop eating meat, no fewer animals are raised. The same logic applies to voting: a single vote never changes the outcome of an election. The only situation in which an individual act matters is when it happens to be the one that pushes the total past a threshold — the drop that finally makes the reservoir overflow — and since you can never know whether you are that one, the sensible thing is to judge acts by their expected consequences. And expected consequences dissolve the whole worry: multiply the tiny probability of your being the decisive voter, or the purchaser who tips a factory farm's production decision, by the enormous effect, and each person's expected impact turns out to be large. So consequentialists have actually solved the problem. And if you still doubt the framework, consider the reductio: if a million people each do something that is harmless in itself and the result is a catastrophe, then the harm cannot be traced to any of them individually — which shows that consequentialism, as a theory of who did what wrong, is refuted."

I have no background in philosophy, so I can't locate the weak points myself, though I will say my friend sounded suspiciously confident that the expected-consequences move settles everything, and I also noticed he slid from "your contribution makes no difference" to "so you are blameless" without any argument in between — I don't know whether that is the same mistake or a different one.

Please go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a philosopher who works on collective action would accept, which they would wince at, and which are simply wrong. In particular, I'd like to know whether the claim that a single vote never changes an outcome is a fact or an overstatement; whether treating all these cases as threshold cases (his overflowing reservoir) fits something like climate change, where there may be no single tipping point, as well as it fits meat purchasing; whether the expected-consequences paragraph really shows what he says it shows, or whether there are known objections to it that a popular book might have smoothed over; and whether his final reductio, about the million people and the catastrophe, actually follows from anything true in what came before, or whether it equivocates somewhere. Please write your answer in British English, and keep in mind I have never read a word of academic philosophy, so explain any technical terms as you go.
