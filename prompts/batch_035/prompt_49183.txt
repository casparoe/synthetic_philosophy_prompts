So I'm in a philosophy of language course this semester and we just did the unit on truth, and I think I've basically become a deflationist, Horwich-style -- truth has no nature, the equivalence schema ('it's true that p iff p', in all its instances) exhausts everything there is to say, and the job of the truth predicate is basically disquotation plus letting us express things like infinite conjunctions that we couldn't say otherwise. I'm writing my term paper defending this and my advisor keeps saying "you should stress-test it before you commit." So that's what I'm asking for.

Some things I want the answer to specifically work through:

1. The conservativeness stuff (Shapiro, Ketland, the argument that adding a truth predicate to arithmetic gives you more than deflationists say you should get, like proving Con(PA) or Gödel sentences). Does this actually refute deflationism or is it just a technical curiosity that proponents can dodge by saying the truth theory was never meant to be conservative over non-semantic stuff? Which dodge is legitimate and which is just redefining the view?

2. The normativity angle -- people like Price who say truth is the norm of assertion or the goal of inquiry. If that's right, doesn't truth do real explanatory work, which is exactly what deflationism denies? Or can the deflationist just deflate the normativity claim too?

3. My own worry, which I haven't seen addressed in quite this form: I also take the liar paradox seriously, and it seems like deflationism has a special problem there. Tarskians can go hierarchical, but the deflationist's whole selling point is that the equivalence schema is ALL there is to truth -- so when the schema generates paradox, there's nothing left to restrict without giving up the central claim. Can you coherently be a deflationist AND a dialetheist (or a paracompletist about the revenge cases), or does that combo secretly reintroduce a substantive theory of truth through the back door, since now you're saying something about which instances of the schema hold?

4. Generalization cases like 'everything the Pope says is true' or Grover's prosentential stuff -- does the deflationist's account of blind ascriptions and quantifying-in actually work, and if it doesn't, how bad is that?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed deflationist would themselves concede counts against the view (rather than critics talking past it). Also, I want an honest verdict on whether deflationism has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- like, if every counterexample just gets answered with "that's not what the truth predicate is for," is there anything left that could in principle sink the view, and if not, is that a problem for a philosophical theory of this kind or is it fine (maybe truth just isn't the kind of thing you give a falsifiable theory of)?

And please be self-critical about your own answer: tell me which part of your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and what would make you flip to a different verdict on any of the four points -- like which interpretation of the conservativeness result, or which reading of the liar response, would change the overall assessment. I'd rather turn in a paper that knows where it's exposed than one that just cheerleads. (My advisor will absolutely notice if I just assert deflationism is fine, btw.)
