I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on a feature about social learning and market frenzies. A colleague lent me a popular economics book whose chapter on crowd behavior, as closely as I can paraphrase it, makes the following claims:

"Information cascades explain why crowds are so often wrong. The theory, developed by economists in the early 1990s, shows that when people make decisions in sequence and can observe each other's choices, each new decision maker ignores their own private information and simply copies what the earlier movers did. This behavior is individually irrational but collectively rational, which is why it counts as rational herding. Once a cascade starts it is self-perpetuating and cannot be stopped by new information, which is why fads, bank runs, and asset bubbles all follow the same explosive dynamics, and why the wisdom of crowds breaks down completely wherever decisions are made sequentially rather than simultaneously."

Before I put anything resembling this into print, I want to be certain I am not propagating a garbled account of the underlying theory. So please take on the role of an expert in microeconomic theory and behavioral economics — someone who knows the canonical cascade and herding models inside out, has taught them to graduate students, and has a professional interest in how they get mangled in popularization. Begin by thinking carefully and brainstorming, before writing anything final: set out for yourself what the canonical sequential-decision models actually assume about agents, signals, and observability, what exactly they establish, and what the standard caveats and extensions (on fragility, on information aggregation, on when herding does and does not occur) actually say, so that your assessment is grounded rather than impressionistic. Then go through my paraphrase claim by claim and tell me, for each: (1) whether a theorist working in this area would accept it as stated; (2) if not, whether it is a harmless simplification I could keep with a qualifier, or a genuine misstatement that would embarrass me in front of informed readers; and (3) if it is a misstatement, what the defensible version of the claim is. I am myself uncertain about three points in particular: whether the models really have everyone abandoning their private information rather than merely giving it less weight once public evidence accumulates; whether "individually irrational but collectively rational" has its polarity right; and whether the claim that cascades cannot be stopped by new information is in fact the opposite of what the fragility results show. To close, tell me which sentences I could keep essentially as written in a magazine piece, which need hedging, and which I should cut and replace — and be blunt about whether the closing contrast between sequential and simultaneous decision-making, which I find rhetorically appealing, actually tracks the theory or merely sounds good.
