I'm doing a biosecurity policy PhD and sit as the student rep on our institute's dual-use publication review board. We've got a case next month and I keep going back and forth on it, so I want to sanity check what our own track record actually commits us to. The precedents, roughly in order:

(1) We approved full publication of a gene-drive design for mosquito populations — all three safety reviewers were comfortable, even though nothing that detailed existed in the open literature yet.

(2) We rejected a synthesis protocol for a nasty bioregulator peptide — one reviewer wanted it published (detection methods), two didn't, and it would've dropped the synthesis barrier by a lot.

(3) We approved a paper analyzing vulnerabilities in the biosafety procedures of a widely used containment lab design, over one member's objection that it was basically a sabotage roadmap — the majority noted the same weaknesses were already discussed in industry white papers.

(4) We rejected a computational model showing how a vaccine candidate could be engineered around. One reviewer in favor, two against.

The new case: a package of code plus a wetlab recipe that makes it significantly easier to predict and produce transmissibility-enhancing mutations in an avian influenza strain. The internal vote is 2-1 in favor of publication, the objector being our most senior virologist, who thinks the marginal capability uplift is just too big. Wrinkle: one co-author has told us informally she'll stick it on a preprint server regardless of what we decide.

Predict our verdict, or better, tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves. I can see at least two rules that fit all four precedents — something like straight majority expected-benefit voting, versus a "unanimity required for actionable artifacts, majority is fine for analyses" rule — and maybe a redundancy-based one too, though I suspect precedent (1) kills that. Which is the closest analog, what does each choice commit us to as a principle, and what kind of case would actually tell the surviving rules apart? Also curious how the preprint threat should factor in, given the board exists in the first place to defuse the unilateralist's curse in the Bostrom-and-colleagues sense — if it'll come out anyway, does rejection buy us anything, or does caving to the threat just teach everyone they can route around us?
