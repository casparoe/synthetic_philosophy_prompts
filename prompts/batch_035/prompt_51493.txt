I'm a master's student in philosophy, and last week our seminar on philosophy of language worked through Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following — the skeptical argument that no finite set of past uses fixes what rule someone was following, the famous plus/quus example, and Kripke's "skeptical solution" on which meaning-attributions are grounded not in facts about individuals but in communal standards of assertion and correction. Afterwards, two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, spent over an hour arguing about it in the department café, and I ended up as a baffled bystander. They've since asked me for my take, and I'd rather not embarrass myself.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this. (1) Dana endorsed the skeptical conclusion: nothing in a person's past behavior, or any candidate fact about them, determines that they meant plus rather than quus — the same finite history is compatible with either. She then extended it to something we'd all been talking about anyway: a language model trained on addition examples, which performs flawlessly on inputs like the training data but gives weird outputs on far-out inputs it never saw. She claimed that nothing in the model's past fixes what function it "learned," so there's simply no fact of the matter about whether it's making mistakes out there or answering a different question correctly. (2) Marcus said this conflates epistemic underdetermination with the absence of a fact: two models trained on the same data but with different architectures or inductive biases diverge on novel inputs, which shows something over and above the data — the weights, the training trajectory — settles what happens, so a fortiori something settles what the model "grasps." (3) Dana answered that the weights are just a compressed description of what the model will do; if the model outputs something bizarre on a novel case, Marcus can't distinguish "it grasped plus and erred" from "it grasped quus and was right," because correctness-conditions are exactly what's in dispute, and appeal to actual or counterfactual behavior presupposes them. (4) Marcus said the circularity charge misses its target: his appeal is to present-tense structure, not to future manifestations, so he's not presupposing the correctness-conditions he's meant to ground. (5) Dana replied that structure doesn't wear its semantics on its sleeve — for any configuration of weights you point to, it remains open why that configuration counts as meaning plus rather than merely being a reliable cause of plus-like outputs, so the skeptical question just recurses one level down. (6) Marcus, visibly retreating, conceded that structure alone doesn't constitute meaning, but insisted Dana's "no fact" conclusion was an overreach: he'd now settle for the view that the relevant facts essentially involve communal standards — that what makes it right to attribute plus rather than quus is our practice of holding speakers (and, controversially, maybe systems) answerable to correction, so meaning-attributions earn their keep by their role in that practice even without being grounded in a fact about an individual taken alone. (7) Dana pounced: so in the model's case, the weights appeal from move (2) was idle, and claims like "the model understands addition" come out neither true nor false but merely useful projections of our practice onto a thing that cannot so much as notice correction. (8) Marcus objected that this made his position look more radical than it is — our practice of attributing meanings is itself a perfectly ordinary natural pattern, so he's not a non-factualist, or at least not a global one.

I'd like you to adjudicate this as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of language and philosophy of mind would. Please think carefully and take your time; before rating anything, it would help if you briefly mapped each of the eight moves onto the familiar positions in this literature (the skeptical paradox, simple dispositionalism, appeals to structure or "categorical" facts, and the skeptical solution), while flagging places where Dana's extension to the language model genuinely changes the argument — for instance, whether normativity plays a different role for a system that cannot respond to correction, and whether that difference strengthens Marcus or merely lets him dodge Dana's strongest point. Then go through the exchange move by move: for each step, say whether the speaker is right, wrong, or right-about-a-narrower-claim-than-they-claim, and justify the verdict. Finally, tell me who comes out more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular whether the dispute from move (6) onward is substantive or has quietly become partly verbal, as Dana hinted at the end.
