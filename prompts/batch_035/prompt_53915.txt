I am a PhD student in moral psychology, currently embedded one day a week with a hospital's clinical ethics consultation service. A recurring problem in our consults has no settled method, and I want to see whether it can be given one.

The problem is this: when a patient's stated choice about treatment looks suspicious to the team — a refusal of dialysis after a bleak prognosis was delivered in a particularly fatalistic register, a stated "I would rather die than live like this" about a disability the patient has had for a month, a demand for an intervention the whole team considers futile — the consultation has to judge whether we are looking at a value the patient actually holds or at an artifact of how things have been presented to her: order effects in how the prognosis was framed, anchoring on the first number she was given, status quo bias, or the well-documented adaptation by which people's stated desires shift to match their new circumstances. Morally these come apart. A settled preference, most of us think, deserves the full authority we give to autonomous choice; a bias deserves at most respectful acknowledgment and an attempt at better information. The trouble is that from the outside the two present identically: both are simply what the patient says she wants.

I have been toying with a scaffold that I would like the procedure built around, and I want to see whether it can bear the weight. Call it the invariance probe: re-present the identical facts under differently ordered, differently valenced, but strictly equivalent descriptions, and observe whether the stated choice survives. Pair it with what I call the counterpart probe: ask the patient what she would want for a patient in every relevant respect identical to her, but a stranger to her. My working hypothesis is that a preference which survives both probes has a much better claim to authority than one that flips under either, and that this tracks something real about the patient rather than merely producing agreement with whatever the team already thinks wise. But I am not certain of this, and I especially do not want the procedure to smuggle the team's view of what is reasonable in under the guise of testing for bias.

With that in mind, please write a decision procedure the consult service can actually run within the one or two conversations we typically get with a patient, under time pressure, with the stakes being decisions about life-sustaining treatment. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask, in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where it ends with a recommendation to treat the stated choice as authoritative; where it licenses one further attempt at informed reconsideration rather than either acceptance or override; and where it must stop and hand the judgment to a human being because what remains is a question of values rather than of facts.

Then run it on three cases. First: an eighty-one-year-old refusing dialysis after a resident delivered the prognosis in a way that emphasized the worst outcomes and mentioned none of the survivable ones. Second: a recently paralyzed patient stating he would refuse the ventilator weaning trial, where we suspect that adaptation to his new condition is doing most of the work. Third: a patient demanding a treatment the entire team considers futile, whose demand does not waver under either probe. I want at least one worked case in which the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot do: which patients it will fail, and how the invariance probe itself can misfire — I can already imagine it treating a hard-won, deliberate change of mind as a bias, since people who have genuinely thought something over will sometimes answer differently to differently framed questions, and that is not obviously a defect in them.

Two final requests. First, do not have the procedure lean on any named published framework; I want it to stand on its own reasoning. Second, close with an honest assessment: state how confident you are in each of the procedure's main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of. If that point is the validity of the invariance probe itself, say so.
