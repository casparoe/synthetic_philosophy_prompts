You are a professional political philosopher with deep expertise in Hannah Arendt's writings on truth, lying, and politics. I am building material for a graduate seminar, and I want you to construct a thought experiment to order. Please think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorm of two or three candidate scenarios before committing to one.

The target: a case in which a consequentialist has every reason to applaud a political lie, while Arendt's framework condemns it — and, crucially, where the standard consequentialist safety valves are unavailable. The usual escape routes are that lies eventually get discovered, corrode trust, or produce bad epistemic habits downstream. Your scenario must close all of those exits: the lie succeeds completely, is never exposed, and leaves the population better off by any ordinary measure. The only ground left for condemning it has to come from Arendt's own ideas about what lying does to the shared world, not from any hidden bad outcome.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Realistic and contemporary: a public health emergency, an evacuation, a financial panic, a diplomatic crisis — no science fiction, no omniscient deceivers, no trolley-style abstractions.
2. The lie must concern facts of the common world — what is happening, what happened — not the leader's private conduct or character, so that reading it as mere hypocrisy is off the table.
3. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.
4. The lie must be an assertion of something the leader believes false, not a mere withholding or an act of state secrecy, since Arendt distinguishes lying from discretion and confidentiality, and your case should not blur that line.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification:

(a) Walk through the consequentialist's verdict, explaining why the favorable outcome is not merely incidental but robust across plausible versions of the case.
(b) Give Arendt's verdict, being explicit about which elements of her account are doing the work — the difference between the liar and the truth-teller's relation to the world, the way organized lying treats facts as malleable, the effect on citizens' capacity to share a common reality — as opposed to any appeal to consequences.
(c) Identify the feature of the case that is essential, and say what happens to Arendt's verdict if you remove it: for instance, if the leader had merely concealed rather than asserted, or lied about intentions and promises rather than about facts.
(d) Explain how a smart defender of beneficent deception would attack the case — what exactly they would deny about the harmlessness stipulation, and whether Arendt has a reply that does not quietly smuggle consequences back in.

Treat the design constraints as the point of the exercise: a scenario that merely stipulates that the lie corrodes the shared world, rather than letting that emerge from the structure of the case, fails the assignment.
