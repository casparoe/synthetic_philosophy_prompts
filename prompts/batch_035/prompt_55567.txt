You are a decision theorist with a strong background in the philosophy of rational choice. Please think carefully about the situation below before advising me: I'd like you to first diagnose what my two friends are actually disagreeing about, then assess their positions on the merits, and only then advise me on the decision itself.

I'm 34 and work as a staff engineer at a large, stable company. My savings are about $180k, and my salary comfortably covers my life. A friend from university is founding a company and has offered me a founding-team role: a pay cut to roughly 60% of my current salary, plus equity that is realistically worth nothing in the median case and a great deal in the tail. I have no dependents. I can't decide, and the two people I trust most disagree in a way I find more interesting than the decision itself.

My first friend, who trades for a living, says I should think of my whole financial life as a bankroll and my career moves as bets on it. On his picture, the right objective is not the arithmetic expectation of my wealth but its growth rate — what compounds. A bet that risks too large a fraction of the bankroll is bad even with positive expected value, because a large loss sets compounding back more than an equal-sized gain advances it, and ruin ends the game entirely. On his numbers, the startup offer sits right at the edge of what a pot that took me a decade to build can prudently risk. He also concedes he would recommend shading the position down somewhat from what his formula says, because my estimate of the odds is crude.

My second friend, a philosopher, says the first has smuggled in a picture of me as a casino patron playing the same game forever. Career choices do not arrive as long series of divisible, repeatable wagers at stable odds; this specific offer is close to a one-shot, the equity cannot be partially cashed out mid-flight, and my bankroll is not really a bankroll — savings, human capital, and option value do not combine into a single quantity that gets multiplied by itself year after year. Where the game is not repeated, he says, the compounding argument loses its grip, and I should simply ask which outcome I prefer in the ordinary expected-utility way, weighted by how much I would regret the downside.

Please do the following, in order:

1. State precisely what the disagreement between them amounts to. My suspicion is that it is not about the arithmetic of my offer at all, but about what my life is, decision-theoretically — a long run of comparable gambles or a sequence of mostly unique choices — and I'd like you to confirm or correct that.
2. Assess each friend's position on its merits: where each is strongest, where each overreaches, and whether either is making an empirical assumption dressed up as a mathematical one.
3. Say whether there is a principled middle position between them, what it implies about the offer, and whether there is anything both of them have left out that a careful decision theorist would flag — for instance, whether the option value of turning the offer down, or of taking it and later exiting, changes the structure of the problem.
4. Give me your bottom-line recommendation about the offer itself.

Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
