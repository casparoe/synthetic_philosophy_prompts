got home an hour ago from a dinner that turned into a two hour arguement about the extended mind. my friend R is fully on board with the clark and chalmers stuff, otto's notebook and so on, but what he kept coming back to was this picture: the mind is like a spider's web. the spider sits in the middle, sure, but the thing that actually feeds the spider is the whole web -- silk lines, anchor points, the vibrations that travel in. nobody says the web is "inside" the spider, but nobody studying how the spider eats stops at the spider's body either. R says my phone, my notes app, my saved places are exactly the silk: cognition spun out of my head, and the head is just where the spider happens to sit. and he wasn't just being cute, he was using it for a real conclusion: his mom has early dementia and basically lives through her phone now (reminders, photos captioned with who everyone is, a shared calendar that R himself maintains), and he says people who tell him "her mind is shrinking" are drawing the boundary in the wrong place -- her mind includes the phone and arguably includes him updating the calendar, so taking the phone away would be like cutting strands out of the web.

I pushed back that a spider builds its web out of its own body, which my phone obviously isn't, and he said fine, hermit crab then, shell instead of web, same job done. I want to know whether either version survives scrutiny. give me the correspondence table -- what plays the spider, the silk, the prey, the anchor points, and who or what is the crab and the shell in the second version -- then where each analogy breaks, and crucially which breaks actually matter for the conclusion he's drawing about his mom versus which ones are just noise. does "her mind includes the phone (and maybe me)" come out of this intact, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? and if there's a better analogy in the same neighborhood that does what he wants it to do, give me that too, because we're apparently doing this again over christmas and I want to be ready.
