I'm putting together some material for an applied ethics reading group on moral status, and I keep running into something I can't quite sort out, so I want help reconstructing the implicit theory behind it. Here is the practice I have in mind. In the European Union's directive on the use of animals in scientific research (Directive 2010/63/EU), any procedure on a vertebrate requires ethics review, licensing, and attention to the animal's welfare, and the directive also singles out one invertebrate group — cephalopods (octopuses, squid, cuttlefish) — for the same protections, explicitly citing scientific evidence of their ability to experience pain and distress; insects, by contrast, can be used in any procedure without any oversight at all. More recently, the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 recognized all vertebrates, plus decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters) and cephalopods, as sentient beings, while leaving bees and other insects outside the category. So across jurisdictions there's a fairly stable pattern: vertebrates are protected wholesale, a couple of invertebrate groups with complex nervous systems have been admitted recently, and everything else — regardless of its behavior — is unprotected.

What I'd like you to work out is what theory of moral status this pattern actually presupposes. The obvious reading is that it embodies a commitment to something like brain complexity being morally relevant: the line falls where a centralized brain with a certain architecture shows up, which suggests the law is treating morphological features of nervous systems as a proxy for (or even a constitutive part of) the capacity to suffer. But I want you to be careful here, because several things don't fit tidily. The line is binary, not graded — a mouse gets full protection while a bee with arguably comparable behavioral flexibility gets none — which looks like a commitment to a threshold view of moral status rather than a continuum. Vertebrates are protected across the board, though, regardless of brain size, so the law doesn't seem to care that a cow's brain is vastly larger than a crow's; is brain size itself doing no work once the architectural threshold is met? And the recent extensions were justified by evidence of sentience rather than by any claim about brain size or complexity as such, which raises the question of whether the practice is genuinely tracking a property of the animals or just tracking what researchers have happened to study.

So the deliverable I want is this: reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) that makes the practice coherent, then be explicit about which parts of the practice strictly presuppose it and which parts merely sit comfortably with it — i.e., could a purely sentience-based view (no claim about neuroanatomy at all) license the same regulatory line, or does the line only make sense if the law is in fact committed to something about nervous-system structure? Where you find the practice philosophically underdetermined — several rival theories of moral status that would all license roughly this same line — say so rather than manufacturing a single answer. And finally, tell me what would have to change in the regulatory pattern under each of the leading alternatives: if moral status depended only on sentience and not on brain organization, should we expect insects to come inside the line? If status admitted of degrees rather than thresholds, would the all-or-nothing licensing system survive? I'd like the answer to be one a member of the reading group could follow without prior background in philosophy of mind, but with the philosophical distinctions genuinely doing the work, not just gestures at "science says."
