For the final project in my eleventh-grade elective on justice and society, we each have to design a thought experiment rather than summarize a theory, and I've chosen meritocracy and desert as my topic but am struggling to build the case to my teacher's specifications. What the assignment demands is a scenario about two people entering the same profession in which careers are genuinely open to talents: no discrimination anywhere, identical schools and family resources, no inherited advantages on either side, and neither person ever wrongs the other or breaks a rule. Despite all of that, the person who tries hardest must end up materially worse off than the person who coasts — and, crucially, the difference in how hard they try has to be plausibly traceable to temperament they were born with, since my teacher says the interesting tension only appears if the effort gap itself might be a form of luck. In other words, I want a case where the meritocracy has done everything it promises and still fails to reward effort, so that the usual assumption — that rewarding effort and rewarding talent are different things — comes under pressure from inside the meritocratic machinery itself.

The scenario itself must be realistic (a school or a workplace, no science fiction, no trolley-style contrivances), only one paragraph long, and built around exactly two characters matched on everything except natural aptitude and effort, so those two variables are cleanly isolated. After the scenario, I have to show my work rather than assert conclusions: state what verdict an effort-based theory of desert, an outcome- or contribution-based theory of desert, a luck egalitarian, and a Rawlsian using the difference principle would each give for the distribution, with the reasoning behind each. My teacher was explicit that a case which simply announces who deserves what, instead of generating the verdicts from the theories, fails the assignment. Then I need to identify which single feature of the case is doing all the load-bearing work, explain what happens to each theory's verdict if that feature is removed, and finish with the strongest objection a defender of effort-based desert could raise against the case.

Please construct this for me in the form I have to submit: the one-paragraph scenario first, then the four-verdict walkthrough, then the analysis of the load-bearing feature, its removal, and the objection. Keep the whole thing under 700 words, and write it at the level of a high school student who understands the four positions but hasn't read the primary texts.
