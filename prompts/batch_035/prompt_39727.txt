My two friends have been arguing about the absent-minded driver for like a year and finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I've somehow been asked to draft the joint statement. Setup so we're on the same page: a driver leaves a bar and comes to two identical intersections, one after the other. At each he can exit or continue. He's absent-minded -- at an intersection he can't tell which one he's at, dosn't remember any earlier choice, and has no commitment device or way to mark the road. Exit at the first intersection pays 0, exit at the second pays 4, continuing past both gets him home for 1. If he could commit before setting out, the best plan is to exit with probability 1/3 (expected payoff 4/3). But evaluated from inside an intersection, where both of his "selves" are in identical positions, exiting with probability 1/2 is a best response to yourself (payoff 5/4), and always continuing gets you 1.

One friend says the rational driver implements the 1/3 plan: rationality is about what you'd endorse on reflection from the start, and accepting 5/4 is just letting each forgetful self veto the plan. The other says that's fantasy without commitment: each choice point is its own agent, 5/4 is all that's actually on the table, and 4/3 is a mirage that requires pretending you control your other self.

Write the document they could both sign: a numbered section of claims both accept (about the math, and about what does and doesn't change if the driver is told which intersection he's at, or if the payoff for getting home is raised above 4), then a disagreements table with each row worded so its own side would accept it, then the cruxes (one is probably whether rationality attaches to the whole plan or to each choice-point taken in isolation), and for each crux a concrete variant of the scenario both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If any part of their fight turns out to be purely verbal, say which part and why.
