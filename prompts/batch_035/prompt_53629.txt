I've been reading the decision-theory and bounded-rationality threads on the LessWrong-adjacent side of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and there's a move that gets made there over and over that I've never found fully convincing: the claim that the computational intractability of exact expected-value maximization is an engineering inconvenience rather than a philosophically interesting fact about rationality. The usual countermove is to say that bounded agents should approximate the ideal as closely as resources allow, and that intractability only affects how close we can get, not what the ideal is. I want to stress-test the other side with a properly built case rather than a hand-wave, and I'd like your help constructing it.

The specifications are as follows:

1. An ordinary agent — a hospital administrator, an office manager, a logistics coordinator, someone like that — faces a real decision whose exact expected-value calculation, given the evidence actually available to them, is computationally intractable: the underlying optimization problem should reduce in a natural way to a known NP-hard problem. Scheduling, rostering, and allocation problems are good hunting grounds; do not rig the case with an artificial cipher or a contrived gadget bolted on purely to make it hard.
2. Two computationally cheap heuristics, each with a respectable track record in the agent's own experience, must recommend opposite actions — and neither heuristic may be a strawman.
3. The scenario must be realistic — no perfect logicians, no exotic physics, no superintelligences, no predictors — and must fit in a single paragraph.
4. The verdicts must be generated by the theories rather than stipulated: an agent who tries to just compute the expected value as best they can should face a principled obstacle, not merely a deadline, and the case must not smuggle in its conclusion by asserting which choice is in fact better.

After the scenario, walk through what ideal-observer decision theory says the agent should do in principle, what each heuristic recommends and why, and then be explicit about which structural feature of the case is doing the philosophical work. Tell me what happens if that feature is removed — for instance, if the instance happens to be small enough to brute-force, or if the two heuristics happen to converge — and sketch how a defender of the "intractability is just an engineering problem" line would attack the case. I'm especially interested in whether the case supports anything beyond the modest conclusion that bounded agents need heuristics, for example whether it puts pressure on the ideal theory itself as a normative standard.

One epistemic ask: please keep visibly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. The complexity-theoretic facts and the reductions are, I take it, settled; but whether intractability has any normative force, whether satisficing and heuristic choice are rationally permissible or merely excusable, and what an ideal theorist is entitled to say about bounded agents are live disputes. Don't present a contested position as the consensus of the field — my whole purpose is to map where the real disagreements sit, and I would rather have an honest map than a tidy one.
