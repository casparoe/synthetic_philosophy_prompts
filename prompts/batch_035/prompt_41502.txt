A colleague in political theory sat in on my ethics seminar last week and afterwards gave the students what he called "the sixty-second version" of the lying/misleading debate, roughly: 'The standard view, going back to Kant, is that lying is asserting what you believe to be false — you put the falsehood out under your own name — while misleading works by implicature: you say something true and let the hearer draw the false conclusion themselves, so they're complicit in their own deception. That's why Kant says you can't lie to the murderer at the door but you may mislead him with a carefully worded truth. Grice actually undermines the whole distinction, because what's implicated is part of what's communicated, so a consistent Gricean should say misleading just is lying with extra steps. And the distinction collapses in practice anyway — someone who tells a creditor "the check's in the mail" when it isn't is exactly as culpable as a liar, so the supposed moral difference is just a lawyer's fantasy.'
