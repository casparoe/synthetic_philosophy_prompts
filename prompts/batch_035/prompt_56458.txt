I'm an applied cryptography engineer with no philosophy background, and I'm trying to find out whether a style of argument we use constantly has a name. When we say that nobody will find a preimage of a given SHA-256 hash, or that the digits of pi will never reveal a compressible pattern, or that the primes show no sign of the clustering that would make certain conjectures false, none of us believes these objects are random. They were fixed by deterministic processes long ago. The reasoning is a policy instead: treat the object as though each piece of it was settled by a separate, uncoordinated coin flip, unless someone can exhibit a specific connection between the pieces. It is also openly defeasible in practice — the policy was trusted for particular hash constructions until collisions were found in them, and the response was to move the trust to newer constructions, not to claim that the broken ones had been random after all. So my questions: does this policy have an established name, and what exactly does the named version claim? I want more than a label. I want to know how it differs from the three neighbors it keeps getting confused with in my head: assuming the object really is random, the probabilistic method in combinatorics, and a plain burden-of-proof default on which absence of evidence for a pattern is treated as evidence of absence. Is it, in the end, just an argument from ignorance with better manners, or does it have a genuine epistemic structure? And if there is no established name for it, coin one, define it so that those three neighbors fall outside it, and say something about the conditions under which it predictably misleads.
