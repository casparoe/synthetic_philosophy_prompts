Here is the puzzle as I have it. A man is driving home at night along a road with two identical intersections. He is so absent-minded that when he reaches an intersection he cannot remember whether he has already passed one, and the intersections look exactly alike, so he has no way of telling which one he is at. At each intersection he can either turn off or continue straight. If he turns off at the first intersection, he arrives home the long way, which I'll score as 1. If he continues past the first and turns off at the second, he arrives home the short way, worth 3. If he continues through both, he ends up in the wrong town entirely, worth 0. If he could remember where he was, he would obviously continue past the first and turn off at the second and collect his 3. But he can't, so he must act without knowing which intersection he is at.

Working through what he can do, I found that if he decides to continue with probability one in three at whatever intersection he finds himself at, and turns off otherwise, his expected score is four thirds, which beats turning off at every intersection for sure (1) and continuing at every intersection (0). As far as I can tell, that one-in-three mixture is the best he can manage. And here is a thing I noticed that I can't interpret: if I ask what continuing is worth from the point of view of standing at an intersection, given that he might be at either, the sums come out so that he is exactly indifferent between turning and continuing at that one-in-three mixture. I don't know whether that is a deep fact or an accident of the numbers I chose.

Now the disagreement. A friend of mine, who has read more about decision theory than I have, says I have this wrong. His view is that the driver standing at an intersection is really making one decision that happens to be instantiated twice: whatever he concludes now, he concluded the same way at the other intersection, because he is the same person reasoning the same way, and the same reasoning reaches the same conclusion. On that way of seeing it, he says, the driver should ask what whole pattern of behaviour he endorses rather than treating the two intersections as separate little gambling opportunities, which he accuses me of double-counting. He also says the puzzle is a cousin of Newcomb's problem, which I have only read about casually, and that people who take the one-boxing side there should side with him here. I can't fully reconstruct his reasoning, but I believe his answer comes out as: the driver should turn off at every intersection he comes to, take his 1, and my four thirds is an illusion. He seems to feel that the gap between the 3 the driver would get with a memory and the four thirds he gets without one is a price a clever enough reasoner shouldn't have to pay, and I don't know what to make of that.
