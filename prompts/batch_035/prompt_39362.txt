I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course, and this term we worked through the 2016 Journal of Consciousness Studies special issue on illusionism — Frankish's target article and the commentaries that followed. I came out of it more sympathetic to illusionism than I expected to be: the view that phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, is an introspective illusion, with the introspective mechanism misrepresenting perceptual states as having intrinsic, ineffable, qualitative properties that they do not in fact have. I've since found myself defending something like this in seminars, and I want to know, before I dig in further, what I've actually signed up for. What would count against it?

I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like you to distinguish what committed illusionists would themselves concede as counting against the view from what only their critics press. A few specific worries I want addressed rather than sidestepped:

First, the retreat problem. As I understand it, there is a strong version of the view, on which there simply are no phenomenal properties at all, and weaker versions, on which the offending properties are only the intrinsic, ineffable, "primitive" ones, with everything else about experience left intact. It looks to me as though the weak version could, under enough pressure, be qualified into something barely distinguishable from realism about experience. So one thing I want assessed is whether the view risks collapsing into a rival if each retreat is conceded, and at which point in the sequence of retreats, if any, a defender ought to say: no, this far and no further — and what they are then left standing on.

Second, the collapse-into-disanalogy problem. The standard defence likens the introspective error to other, supposedly well-understood illusory posits — witches, caloric, phlogiston, the moral facts of the error theorist. I want to know which disanalogies, if they hold, would genuinely count against the view, as opposed to merely annoying its defenders. The one that worries me most: in the historical cases, we can say what the erroneous belief was about, and what got it right and wrong, in entirely non-magical vocabulary; it is far from clear we can do that for the belief that one is undergoing an experience of redness. If the disanalogy cannot be repaired, is that fatal, or does illusionism have the resources to say that some illusions are simply not explicable in the ordinary way — and if it says that, what is left of the view's claim to take consciousness seriously scientifically?

Third, empirical exposure. I want to know whether the view actually predicts anything that a dualist or a realist about phenomenal properties does not, such that a result could come in against it. For example, would evidence that introspection is, in the relevant respects, highly accurate — that the mechanisms producing these judgements track real features of experience rather than misrepresenting them — count against it, or would an illusionist just reinterpret the "tracked features" as intentional and discriminative ones and carry on? Similarly, does the view predict anything about the developmental and comparative story — should creatures (or infants) lacking the relevant metacognitive machinery nonetheless show the full pattern of pain behaviour, and would either result here bear on the view, or is this an area where illusionism is silent?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether illusionism has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its qualifications, and, if it has, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind — a philosophical thesis rather than a scientific hypothesis — or whether demanding falsifiability-style exposure here mistakes what sort of claim is being made. If the honest answer is that nothing could count against it, please say so plainly; I would rather know now than in a seminar.
