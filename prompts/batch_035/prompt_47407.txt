I'm co-organizing the final session of a graduate seminar on concepts and categorization, and the last unit dealt with Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' across handwriting, typefaces, and the endless letterforms no one has ever seen before. For a closing reading I'd like a villanelle, and I'd like the form to carry the philosophy rather than just decorate it.

The seminar's central tension, which I want the poem to enact, is the dual constraint on any letterform: it must be recognizable as its letter (its role, its essence -- which we argued is defined largely by contrast with its neighbors, an 'a' being what it is by not being an 'e' or an 'o') and simultaneously as a citizen of its typeface or style (the "spirit" that migrates from glyph to glyph, so that a 'g' can be unmistakably of the same family as the 'a' beside it). So the two refrains of the villanelle should be, in effect, these two demands speaking -- one voice insisting on the letter's identity, the other pulling toward the style's unity -- each returning line giving that demand its clearest expression.

Here is the harder formal request: let the refrains return slightly varied each time. Each recurrence should be restyled or degraded a little, the way a letterform survives the pressure of a new typeface, so that the poem itself demonstrates recognition under variation. The penultimate refrain should be the most distorted yet still legible as itself, and the final couplet, where the two refrains finally meet, should be the moment a form is simultaneously fully itself and fully of its family -- the point where the two demands, which the poem has kept apart, turn out to be one constraint seen from two sides.

A few constraints: keep it a proper villanelle (nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, refrains in the conventional positions) so the experiment stays honest; plain text only, no typographic tricks or unicode gimmicks -- the interest should be in the words and the form, not the typesetting. Someone who knows the Letter Spirit idea should find the poem philosophically right, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem, so no name-dropping or jargon unless a line earns it.
