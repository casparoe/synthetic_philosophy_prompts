You're a political philosopher who works on global justice, and you're good at reverse-engineering the views that institutions act on. So think carefully about this one — honestly, maybe do a short brainstorm before you settle on your answer, because I've asked a few people and gotten hand-waving.

Here's the thing I keep noticing about foreign aid. Almost everything about the way rich countries actually do it looks like charity in the old-fashioned sense. It's fully discretionary — no donor has ever been obligated to give any fixed amount, and parliaments cut or cancel aid budgets whenever things get tight at home, without anyone claiming the recipients were wronged the way a creditor is wronged. The money comes branded (logos on the aid trucks, gift-from-the-people-of-X plaques), there's an expectation that reciepients be grateful, and donors attach conditions about policy or require the money be spent on goods from the donor country. There's no court a recipient can take a donor to. Yet the arguments people give FOR aid usually sound nothing like charity arguments — campaigners talk about historical wrongs, unfair global rules, colonial extraction, as if a debt were being paid down rather than alms handed out.

So my question: what does the actual practice presuppose about what rich countries owe poor ones? Is the practice committed to the view that this is beneficence — a duty of assistance that's real but optional in amount, timing, and target — and does it thereby quietly rule out the idea that poor countries hold determinate claim-rights against specific donors? Or is the practice underdetermined here, such that you could run a justice-based theory underneath the exact same behaviors (treating the branding and the gratitude-demands as just bad manners layered on top of a genuine debt)? Where does the practice genuinely commit, and where is it philosophically loose enough that several theories would license it?

I'm curious whether the changes are mostly surface stuff — drop the logos, stop expecting gratitude, maybe stop calling it "aid" at all — or whether something structural would have to go too, like conditionality, or donors unilaterally picking which sectors get funded.
