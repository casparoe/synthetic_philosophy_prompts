I've been trying to work out what moral worth actually tracks, going by my gut on cases, and my gut is not cooperating. Cafe owner charges everyone fairly and never shorts the tourists, but only because he's figured out that shorting people kills repeat business — right action, but I want to say zero moral worth. Guy finds a wallet stuffed with cash and returns it, but only after he notices the lobby security camera — again, nothing. Same guy, different week, no camera anywhere, returns it because he can't stop picturing the owner's face when she realizes her rent money is gone — full moral worth, feels obvious. Then it gets messy. A woman gives a big chunk of her income to famine relief, feels nothing for the recipients at all, honestly kind of resents doing it, but does it from the settled judgment that it's required of her — part of me says this is the MOST worth of anything on this list, part of me says it's less than the wallet guy. The natural helper: helps everyone easily, takes visible pleasure in it, never has to override a single impulse — my gut says tons of worth, though I gather Kant would supposedly say little or none, which makes me suspect my gut is just wrong somewhere. Someone who only helps because otherwise the guilt would eat them alive — somewhere in between? Worse than the sympathetic wallet returner, better than the camera guy, but I couldn't tell you why. And a cold, rigid sort of person who keeps a promise at real cost to himself, not because he thinks "I ought" in any explicit way, but because breaking promises just isn't who he is — identity, not duty.

A friend of mine has been pushing a framing on me and I can't decide if it helps. He says moral worth is basically a credit-attribution problem: the worth of the act is like the credit an architect gets for a beautiful cathedral, where you have to subtract out the thousand masons, the donors, the site conditions, the plain luck — moral worth is whatever residue of the goodness flows from the agent rather than from self-interest, external constraint, upbringing, or coincidence. On that picture the camera guy and the shopkeeper are clear (the goodness flows from the camera and the ledger, not from them), but I honestly can't tell whether the natural helper should come out high (the goodness flows from her, even if the flowing is easy) or low (her upbringing is doing the work, the way the masons did).

So: is there a principle that captures my verdicts, or close? Does my freind's credit picture actually do the job, or does it silently mishandle one of my cases (the guilt one feels like trouble for it)? Say plainly which of my verdicts has to go, and why. Then run whatever principle you land on against some cases I haven't thought through: a person raised so gently they never even register temptation — do they ever act with moral worth, or is it like grading a fish on swimming; a soldier who throws himself on a grenade before any thought at all, pure reflex, deliberation arriving a second too late to matter; and whether a committee or a group can do something with moral worth, or whether worth only ever lands on members. I'd also like to know what your principle predicts about the guilt-driven case and the identity-driven promise-keeper, since those are the two where I'd actually change my mind if the argument was good.
