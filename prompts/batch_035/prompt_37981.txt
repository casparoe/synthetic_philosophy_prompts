I'm a PhD student working on intergenerational justice, and my dissertation has drifted into population ethics — largely against my will, since it turns out you can't say much about the long-term future without taking a stance on the value of additional lives. I've been wading through Part Four of Parfit's Reasons and Persons with a reading group, and rather than adopting one of the catalogued theories, I've ended up with a bundle of verdicts and commitments that each seemed individually right at the time. Before I defend a chapter built on them, I want a consistency audit, because I have the strong suspicion that there's a problem somewhere in the set, but I genuinely cannot locate it. Here's the package:

(1) The procreation asymmetry: it is pro tanto wrong to create a life not worth living, where this is avoidable, and the wrongness is owed to the person created. But creating a happy life is not required — it's permitted, in the way rescuing a drowning stranger is not. (2) Whenever the same people exist under all the options on the table, the right act is whatever maximizes total welfare — no distributional constraints, no agent-relative permissions, plain aggregation. (3) A woman who conceives while on a medication she knows will permanently impair her child's cognition, when she could have waited a few months and conceived a healthy child, acts wrongly — even though the impaired child's life is worth living, and there is no one for whom her choice makes things worse. (4) The Repugnant Conclusion is false: a world of ten billion excellent lives is better than a world of arbitrarily many lives barely above the neutral level, however many there are. (5) The better-than relation over outcomes is complete and transitive — I need this on principle (indifference has to be earned, not posited) and in practice, since I do expected-value reasoning about climate policy, and an intransitive or gappy ranking is useless for that. (6) Impartiality extends to population: there is no morally relevant difference between my future children and strangers' future children, and any agent-relative permission I have does not apply to choices about who gets to exist.

What I want is not a survey of population ethics — I know the standard escape routes (critical levels, lexical thresholds, variable-value views) and I'm not asking which theory to sign up for. I want you to treat my six commitments as the object: tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damning. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, and what giving it costs elsewhere in the set. Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most.
