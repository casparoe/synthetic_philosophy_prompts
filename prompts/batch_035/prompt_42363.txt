At the metaphysics workshop I run, two of my colleagues — call them Elena and Paul — spent last Thursday's session locked in a dispute about Bradley's regress, and since I will have to moderate the follow-up session, I would like a careful adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded. The occasion was a paper claiming that the unity of the proposition is an unsolved problem: the proposition that Socrates is wise cannot just be the pair of Socrates and the property of wisdom, since the same items occur together in the false proposition that Socrates is not wise; something must distinguish the case in which the property is predicated of the man from the case in which it is merely collected with him. The paper then raised Bradley's regress: whatever is introduced to do the uniting — say, an instantiation relation tying Socrates to wisdom — will, if it is itself a constituent standing alongside the others, need a further tie to connect it to what it connects, and so on without end, so that no unified proposition is ever completed.

(1) Elena argued that the regress is fatal. The whole point of introducing the instantiation relation was to explain unity; if the relation itself must be bound to its relata by something further, the explanation never terminates, and the original problem — how a many becomes a one with a truth-value — is not solved but postponed at every level. She added that Paul's own analogy with syntax only strengthens her case: a sentence is unified by its grammatical structure, which is finite and antecedently given, whereas Paul is positing unity in the world by fiat.

(2) Paul replied that the regress is generated only by a demand he rejects: that whatever unifies must itself be unified with what it unifies by a further item. A relation, he said, does not need a relating tie in order to relate; relating is what a relation is. The regress is thus a pseudo-problem resting on a picture — the picture that unity must always be produced by an additional constituent — and once the picture is abandoned, the fact that Socrates instantiates wisdom simply is a unified whole, with predication built into the fact rather than added to it.

(3) Elena pressed: if the fact simply is unified, brutely, then the original puzzle about the pair versus the proposition has not been answered but stipulated away, and the explanatory demand has merely been relabelled. She noted an asymmetry she found telling: Paul's methodology licenses positing whatever brute natures one likes — by his lights, one could equally say it is just in the nature of phlogiston to dephlogisticate — whereas a principled account would say in advance which primitives are admissible.

(4) Paul answered that the comparison is unfair: posits earn their keep by the explanatory work they do, and the instantiation relation earns its keep by explaining the difference between Socrates's having wisdom and the mere collection of Socrates and wisdom — a real difference, marked in every language by the difference between a predicate and a list. Some explanations, he insisted, legitimately terminate in the nature of the thing explained, and not every "why" must be answered by citing a further entity; otherwise nothing could ever be grounded and every discipline would face its own Bradley's regress.

(5) Elena's last word was that Paul had quietly shifted the question: the issue was never whether every explanation must invoke a further entity, but whether the specific explanation on offer — unity achieved by a constituent — is coherent, and his own move concedes it is not, which is why he had to abandon it for brute nature.

Both are serious philosophers, and neither position is a strawman. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is sound, mistaken, or partly right, and justify the rating. Then address the two questions I most need answered for the follow-up session. First, is the regress Paul faces actually vicious, or is it a benign repetition of a demand that need not be met at every level — and does anything in the exchange turn on that distinction? Second, is there anything of substance both of them are missing — for instance, a principled criterion for when terminating an explanation in the nature of the explanandum is legitimate and when it is a dodge? Finally, tell me who comes out more right overall, and be willing to say that one of them lost the exchange even if her underlying view is the more defensible one.
