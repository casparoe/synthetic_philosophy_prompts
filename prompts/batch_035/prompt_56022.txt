I'm a sanctions officer at a local benefits office — I decide whether someone's payment gets docked for missing a job-search appointment, turning down a referral, that kind of thing. We're told we're applying conditionality "fairly" but nobody's ever explained what fairness means here, and the official guidance is just a stack of rules. My colleague Maria has a one-line rule she swears by: never sanction someone for something they couldn't have done otherwise — if the appointment clashed with something their childcare didn't cover, or the "voluntary" placement was basically unpaid work for a for-profit warehouse, the miss wasn't their fault, so no sanction, end of story. I like it, but it can't be the whole thing: some claimants clearly could have shown up and didn't, and even then docking them feels off in a way Maria's rule doesn't capture. And sometimes the point of a condition isn't fairness at all but nudging behavior, which is a whole different justification that cuts the other way.

Can you turn the deservingness debate — desert vs reciprocity vs deterrence, the "could have done otherwise" test and whether it even makes sense for someone who's been ground down by months of unemployment, whatever else the welfare-state literature says matters — into a decision procedure I can actually run in about ten minutes per case? I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure outputs a "no, don't sanction," and where I have to stop and hand it to my supervisor because the remaining question is one of values, not facts. Then run it on two cases I've actually had recently: (a) a single dad who missed two appointments because his kid was sick and told us afterwards but not before, and (b) a laid-off machinist in his late 50s who shows up to every appointment but refuses to apply for the warehouse jobs we refer him to, says the work is beneath him. Include at least one spot where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and be honest about where it'll misfire if we roll it out — I have maybe 15 minutes per claimant on a good day, a lot of them don't trust us enough to explain their situation, and anything that sanctions fewer people needs sign-off from above, so tell me which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
