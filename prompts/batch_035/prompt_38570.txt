I'm a graduate student in a distributive ethics seminar writing a term paper on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and I've noticed that virtually every exchange in this literature starts from a list of test cases and intuitions that the author simply asserts -- the transplant surgeon who could harvest one patient's organs to save five, the rescue squad that can save the greater number or the fewer, the state that taxes a few to benefit the many, a public project that imposes a small risk of death on each of a million people in exchange for a large aggregate benefit. Utilitarians say all of these come out the same way, by simple addition; their critics say some of them reveal that adding across persons is exactly what morality forbids. Before I line up with either side, I want the specification both sides are implicitly fighting over.

Please act as a professional moral philosopher and write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of how morality weighs gains and losses across different persons has to get right. I want the requirements listed with the case or consideration behind each -- which cases it must decide correctly (and what "correctly" could even mean here), which distinctions it cannot collapse (interpersonal versus intrapersonal aggregation, being used as a means versus merely ending up worse off, harming versus failing to aid), and which results from elsewhere in philosophy it cannot contradict (for instance, Parfit's reductionist view of personal identity, which seems to undercut the very datum of separateness, or the widespread sense that Taurek was wrong and numbers do count when you must choose whom to rescue). Then do two further things I care most about: mark which requirements you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp dressed up as adequacy conditions; and tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable -- I suspect the anti-aggregation intuitions conflict with the requirements any workable theory of permissible public policy or risk imposition has to meet, and I'd like that suspicion confirmed or debunked. A table is fine. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate requirements before committing to the final list, and be candid about which single requirement does the most to exclude the familiar contenders -- classical utilitarianism, prioritarianism, Scanlon-style contractualism -- though keep the scoring brief, since the criteria are where the real thinking is.
