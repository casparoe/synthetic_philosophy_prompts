I've drafted the core argument of a short paper on the Ship of Theseus and would be grateful for detailed comments. The draft follows; my specific questions are below it.

---

Every restoration of a ship is an argument about which ship, if any, survives it. The standard way of defusing the Ship of Theseus puzzle is to distinguish identity under a sortal from material continuity: the museum ship remains the same *ship*, the reassembled planks form the same *collection of matter*, and the puzzle arises only from sliding between these criteria. On this view, which has become something close to orthodoxy, the puzzle is verbal. I want to press a different line: the puzzle survives the sortal move, and what keeps it alive has gone largely unexamined. Identity judgments about artifacts are hostage not to the object's properties but to our evidence about its history.

Consider a case. In 1890, a maritime museum begins a rolling restoration of a famous clipper, replacing one plank per month and storing the originals in a warehouse across the harbor. By 1980 every plank has been replaced, and by the museum's lights the ship in the water is the original, continuously maintained and continuously afloat. Now suppose dendrochronological analysis in 2010 reveals that roughly forty percent of the timber installed between 1910 and 1930 was milled from wood scavenged during an earlier, poorly documented intervention in 1905 — wood the museum's ledger had classified as replacement stock. Nothing about the ship itself changes when this evidence arrives. The planks are where they were; the harbor is where it was. Yet several identity-relevant predicates flip at once: the fraction of original material, the claim that material continuity was never disturbed, even the accuracy of the restoration timeline the museum has published for a century. If identity judgments supervene on facts of this kind, then whether the ship is "mostly original" changed in 2010, while the ship did not.

I draw two conclusions. First, the sortal-based defusal is incomplete: our confidence that the museum ship is the original ship was never grounded solely in the sortal criterion but partly in a factual narrative about causal provenance, and that narrative is revisable in ways the criterion is not. Second, and more provocatively, identity talk about artifacts has the structure of historical inquiry rather than of metaphysics. It answers to evidence, it can be overturned by archive work, and it can change while the world does not. If this is right, the realist about artifact identity is in serious trouble, because her real object — the ship — plays no role in settling what may truly be said about it.

---

I'd appreciate comments on the following points in particular:

1. Does the retroactivity argument genuinely threaten the conventionalist defusal, or does it show only that our evidence about which historical narrative obtains can be wrong — a point about epistemology that no participant in the debate was denying? Is there a principled line between "identity verdicts change" and "our evidence about identity changes," and on which side of that line does my 2010 case actually fall?

2. Is the analogy between identity talk and historical inquiry defensible, or does it conflate the semantics of identity predicates with their conditions of warranted assertion? I suspect a critic will say that the sentence "the ship is mostly original" was either true or false in 1980 and that 2010 merely told us which — I have some sympathy for that reply but haven't answered it.

3. Where is the argument weakest as it stands, and what would the sharpest critic attack first?

4. Which element of the draft is most worth developing into a full paper, and what would that development require — more cases, a tighter argument, or engagement with the rival view that identity indeterminacy in such cases is semantic all the way down?

Please be candid; I would rather learn the argument is a relabeling of a familiar epistemological point now than after submission.
