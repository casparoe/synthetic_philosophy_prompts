I spent four decades as a physiologist, most of it on the autonomic nervous system, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind. In the laboratory I never once needed to ask what anything felt like; now, in retirement, I cannot settle the question of what my own feelings are doing while I talk about them. I have tried the method I trust from science: write down my honest verdicts on concrete cases, and see whether some principle captures them. I have the verdicts; the principle is eluding me, and I would like your help.

Here are my verdicts on what causes what:

1. A pinprick to the hand; the hand withdraws within a fraction of a second through a spinal reflex, before any cortical event could be involved. Verdict: the tissue damage and the nociceptor discharge caused the withdrawal. No feeling was needed, and none occurred in time.

2. The same subject, a moment later, says that it hurt. Verdict: I want to say the pain caused the utterance — it seems transparent that he complained because he hurt — even though I know the entire chain from nociceptor to speech can in principle be written out without mentioning anything felt.

3. A blindsight patient, asked about the affected field, denies seeing anything, yet points to the stimulus well above chance. Verdict: the visual information caused the pointing; and, strangely, I also want to say that his report of seeing nothing was caused by the absence of visual experience — though an absence of a byproduct is a peculiar cause, and I am not sure I should allow it.

4. A patient under ketamine reports that the pain is there but does not bother him. Verdict: something felt is doing work here. The sensory component and the affective component have come apart, and the affect, not the sensation, seems to cause the distress — or its absence, the calm.

5. A girl with congenital insensitivity to pain: she burns herself badly and never complains. Verdict: unproblematic. Where nothing is felt, nothing is reported; and the injuries are explained, if you like, by the absence of feeling, but nothing felt causes anything.

6. A chronic pain patient in whom I can find no tissue damage and no ongoing nociceptive traffic. Verdict: her pain is real and causes her reports, and I will not tell her the feeling is an idle byproduct, whatever my principle says.

I have tried to pull these together with a principle of my own, borrowed from the chart recorders of my working years: feelings are like the trace on the recorder. The pen's deflection is caused by the current; we explain the trace by citing the current; the trace itself causes nothing downstream. On this principle the feeling of pain is a byproduct: cortical activity causes both the feeling and the complaint; the complaint is explained by citing the feeling; but the feeling never causes anything, not even the complaint. This fits cases 1, 3, and 5. It fits case 2 only if I stop saying that the pain caused the complaint, and I find I am reluctant. It fits case 6 only by an austerity I am ashamed of. It fits case 4 barely, by saying the affect is itself a byproduct of a different sort — which looks like changing the subject.

So I ask you to do for me what I cannot do for myself. Test this principle against the cases; repair it, replace it, or abandon it. State the resulting principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why I should give those up rather than complicate the principle to save them. Tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — for instance, a patient who reports pain under complete pharmacological blockade of all nociceptive input, or a hypothetical perfect duplicate of me that felt nothing at all: would it say the same things, and with the same conviction?

And then address the worry that keeps me awake, since it seems to follow from my own principle. If the feeling causes nothing, then my judgment that I am in pain is not caused by my being in pain. But if the same neural causes produce both the pain and the judgment, and the pain has no hand in the matter, I cannot see how my judgment is even about the pain — how it reports it rather than merely accompanying it. My judgment about the pain would then be one more effect of causes that know nothing of it. I gather this difficulty has a name in the literature, something like the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and I would like to know whether any response to it survives my chart-recorder principle, or whether the principle itself has to go.
