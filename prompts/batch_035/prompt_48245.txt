I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last year I published a long feature on Douglas Hofstadter's strange loops, which sent me back into a corner of I Am a Strange Loop I'd half-forgotten: the passage where he imagines the Careenium, an elaborate snooker-like table whose rolling balls — the simmballs, themselves clusters of smaller units — fall into momentary patterns that get recognized, scored, and thereby steer the later motion of the very balls that compose them. That analogy did more for my understanding than any technical paper I read: it made it feel non-mysterious that a high-level pattern could genuinely push the substrate around, rather than just being a convenient way of talking about the substrate. Now, somewhat to my surprise, the philosophy of mind reading group at a nearby university — they apparently liked the feature — has invited me to present, and I've rashly accepted. My thesis, roughly: the symbol level is a real level of causal explanation, and the Careenium shows why believing otherwise is an artifact of taking too narrow a view of what causation in a complex system looks like.

The audience deserves description, since it will drive the questions. It's a dozen or so graduate students and faculty, almost uniformly committed physicalists — no dualists in the room, which ironically makes my job harder, since I can't be accused of smuggling in souls and instead have to defend the claim on their turf. Two of them, I'm told, are devoted to the exclusion worry: the argument that if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, then a higher-level cause is either identical to that physical cause — in which case "symbol-level causation" is a redescribing, not a causing — or it is surplus to requirements and does nothing, and so the picture of a symbol pushing simmballs around is either trivial or false. At least one other member is sympathetic to multilevel explanation in the sciences but regards the phrase downward causation as a metaphor that philosophers should have retired decades ago. My fear is that they will treat me, politely, as a science writer who has mistaken a vivid picture for an argument.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order this specific audience will actually raise them — not the objections a general audience would — each phrased the way a philosophy-of-mind graduate student would phrase it, so I can recognize it in the wild. They will not ask me about free will or quantum mechanics; they will ask about overdetermination, about realization, about whether the symbol level makes any predictions that plain physicalism doesn't, and about why a pattern needs to be a cause rather than a compressed description of causes. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold at all costs, and where my best available reply remains soft. Include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks — my suspicion is it has something to do with the fact that the recognizer and the scorekeeping in the Careenium sit outside the table, whereas in a brain nothing outside is doing the scoring, and I don't know how Hofstadter's analogy survives that disanalogy. Begin your response with the bottom line stated in the first sentence — which single objection I cannot afford to fumble and why — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my setup.
