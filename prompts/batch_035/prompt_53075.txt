I've been reading around the situationist critique of virtue ethics — Doris, Harman, the Milgram and Darley–Batson material — and I'd like a careful case analysis of a real episode run through that debate, in both directions: what the theory explains about the case, and what the case shows about the theory.

The episode is Hugh Thompson's intervention at My Lai. On March 16, 1968, a company of roughly a hundred American ground troops swept the hamlet of My Lai 4, in Son My village, South Vietnam, expecting a Viet Cong battalion that was not there, and killed several hundred unarmed civilians. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson Jr., flying a light observation helicopter with doorgunner Lawrence Colburn and crew chief Glenn Andreotta, marked wounded civilians with smoke for evacuation (they were found dead on the crew's return), repeatedly protested the killings over an open radio net that superiors were monitoring, landed to confront Lieutenant William Calley at an irrigation ditch full of bodies, and was told the matter was none of his business. He then landed his aircraft between a pursuing squad and a group of civilians hiding in a bunker and told his crew, in effect, that if the Americans fired on him or the villagers, they were to fire on the Americans. He confronted the squad's leader, coaxed the civilians out, and persuaded the pilots of two escorting gunships — who had taken no part in any confrontation — to evacuate them. Andreotta then waded into a ditch of roughly a hundred bodies and pulled out a still-living child. Thompson reported the killings up his chain of command; the reports went nowhere amid a coverup. The story surfaced in 1969 through the veteran Ronald Ridenhour and the journalist Seymour Hersh. Thompson testified against those responsible in 1970, was widely condemned as a traitor, received death threats, and struggled for years afterward; he stayed in the Army until 1983. Calley alone was convicted, and served a short house arrest. In 1998, Thompson, Colburn, and Andreotta (killed in action three weeks after the massacre) received the Soldier's Medal. Two further facts matter: several ground soldiers in the same situation refused direct orders to fire (without physically intervening), and some of the escorting gunship crews cooperated in the evacuation after the fact.
