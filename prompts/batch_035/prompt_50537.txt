I'm taking a seminar on Nietzsche this term, and a family assignment has just landed on me that I'd like your help with. My uncle — the person who originally got me into philosophy — is giving the toast at my older sister's wedding next month, and he has asked me to draft it, on the grounds that he wants it done properly and that this is what my coursework is for. He is a lifelong amateur Nietzschean: he has reread The Gay Science and Zarathustra for something like thirty years, he keeps a small notebook listing things he has decided to affirm, and when he lost his job two years ago he told the family, entirely calmly, that this too he would live again given the chance — and as far as any of us can tell, he means it. He wants the toast built around the thought experiment from section 341 of The Gay Science: the demon who comes to you in your loneliest loneliness and tells you that you must live this exact life over again, innumerable times, with nothing new in it — every pain and every joy in the same sequence, even this demon and this moment — and the question of whether you would collapse in despair or answer that you have never heard anything more divine. He wants to put that question to the couple, gently, as a wedding blessing: whether they would marry each other again, this very day with all its mishaps and dj disasters, endlessly. Please write the toast, roughly four to five minutes as spoken, in his voice: warm, a little grandiose, completely sincere.

The constraints are why I'm asking rather than writing it myself. The room will be mixed: perhaps ten graduate students in philosophy from the groom's side, and otherwise family members who mostly still assume my uncle is in a phase he entered around 1989. The toast has to land for both without lecturing either. Internal consistency matters most of all, and it's the technical part of the brief: since the uncle claims to affirm everything about his life, nothing in the toast may be something he would not will eternally — no seizing of an unrepeatable day, no laments about time flying, no once-in-a-lifetime framing, nothing that treats the wedding or the marriage as unrepeatably singular, and no complaint, however mild, that he wouldn't sign up for infinite repetitions of. If there is one moment of self-aware comedy available, it lives exactly in the gap between professing universal affirmation and being a human being at a wedding, and I'd rather it live there than anywhere else. The recurrence must also arrive as a blessing rather than a burden: the couple should not leave the toast feeling that a demon has pre-weighed every future quarrel they will ever have. Please also respect the distinction between the cosmological reading and the practical reading — the uncle holds the practical one, recurrence as a test for how to live rather than a thesis about physics, and he would never forgive me if the toast implied the metaphysics the way a lazy after-dinner speaker would. One last rule of his: no direct quotations at a wedding, which he considers a failure of style — everything must be transposed into his own words, though the graduate students should still be able to hear precisely which passages are being rearranged.

At the very end, add a short note of two or three sentences in my voice, addressed to my uncle, flagging the single line in the toast that most risks the room going quiet, so that he can decide whether to keep it.
