For thirty-two years I was an invertebrate neuroethologist, most of that time studying odor-guided navigation in flying insects, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind in earnest for the first time. It has been a humbling education. I have now agreed to chair the species-review subcommittee of my old university's animal care and use committee, and the committee has just been handed a problem my reading has not prepared me for: our national funder has decided that potentially sentient invertebrates should receive some form of welfare consideration, and someone has to decide, protocol by protocol, which animals that actually covers and what "consideration" amounts to.

The philosophical literature I have read argues about which animals are conscious at the level of grand theories -- higher-order theories, global workspace views, integrated information, biological naturalism, and the outright skeptics who think even the question is malformed. Each theory draws the line in a different place, or declines to draw one at all. But the committee does not face the theory question; it faces a caseload. What we need is a decision procedure a non-philosopher can actually run: an ordered set of questions about a species and a protocol, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer, clear stopping points, and a defined place where the question stops being empirical and becomes one of values that must go to a vote or to the attending veterinarian.

Please write that procedure, and ground it in whatever the philosophy actually supports. Specifically:

1. The questions should operationalize the distinctions that matter for protection decisions: nociception versus pain, the difference between mere sensory discrimination and something worth caring about morally, and the tension between behavioral markers (motivational trade-offs, protective grooming, analgesia self-administration) and neuroanatomical ones (centralized integration, recurrent processing), including what to do when the two kinds of evidence point in opposite directions.

2. The procedure must take a stand, or explicitly refuse one, on whether consciousness is a threshold or a graded quantity, and say what follows for proportionate protection -- whether a "somewhat sentient" animal gets somewhat less procedure.

3. It should say where it stops with "no special protections," where it mandates protections, and where it correctly hands the decision to a human judgment that cannot be eliminated.

4. Then run it on three protocols we actually anticipate: an octopus learning study in a physiology department, a bumblebee foraging experiment in ecology, and high-throughput work on nematode worms. One of these should come out in a way I will not like -- I have spent my career defending the sophistication of insects and I half-suspect the honest procedure will be more generous to bees than our budget can absorb, or stingier with the worms than our molecular biologists will accept. Do not soften it.

5. Finally, be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure cannot do at all.

Constraints: the committee meets monthly, we have perhaps twenty minutes per species, the members are a veterinarian, a statistician, a laboratory manager, and two scientists, none of whom will read any philosophy. The procedure should fit on something close to one page. No citations; if we need a source, I will chase it myself.
