I have a set of verdicts about hard choices, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures them or whether some of them have to go. I set them out honestly, including the ones I suspect conflict.

When I compare two holiday destinations that each appeal to me for quite different reasons, I feel no tug: either would do, I would happily flip a coin, and I feel nothing was lost whichever came up. When I chose between conservatoire training in the cello and graduate entry into medicine, by contrast, neither option seemed better than the other, but flipping a coin felt obscene — it seemed to matter that I commit myself to one, and that the commitment was mine to make. Yet I refuse to say that one option really was better and I merely could not tell which; the difficulty felt internal to the comparison, not a gap in my information. When I imagine a fire in a gallery with a stranger and a great painting inside, I would rescue the person every time, but I do not want to say the person is worth more in the ordinary sense: no number of paintings adds up to a life, and choosing the person leaves me with something like guilt rather than the satisfaction of having taken the better option. When it comes to friendship, I would not sell an afternoon with a close friend for any fee, and I would call someone who took a large enough fee not merely imprudent but corrupted; yet I have in fact accepted a better-paid job that costs me evenings I would otherwise spend with friends, and I do not think of myself as corrupted for it. And although I say no quantity of money compensates for a friend's betrayal, I did once forgive a betrayal for the sake of years of shared history, which looks rather like compensation. There is also the matter of small improvements: with the two holidays, adding a free upgrade to one of them settles the matter, and I am tempted to infer that the originals were never really on a par, merely close in value — but with the career choice, adding a modest signing bonus to one option would not have settled anything, and I do not know why the same test gives different answers. Finally, since committing to medicine, it has come to seem to me that that was the right choice; I cannot tell whether my commitment revealed a rightness that was there all along or partly created it.

Please begin by stating, in two or three sentences, the best single principle — or the best small set of principles — that fits these verdicts, then work through each case showing what the principle delivers and why. I would like you to be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and to offer me a considered recommendation on whether to keep the principle and revise the verdict or keep the verdict and complicate the principle; do not settle for reporting the tension and moving on. Please also explain how a defender of the simple view — that for any two options, one is better, worse, or equal in value — would attack my verdicts, and how far that attack succeeds. Then apply your principle to a case I have not mentioned: a council deciding between funding the restoration of its Victorian theatre and funding a mobile dialysis unit, where many people feel the two are somehow comparable but neither outweighs the other. And tell me what the principle implies about a question that has been nagging me: under what conditions, if any, is it legitimate to settle such a choice by coin flip, and what exactly, if anything, goes wrong when it is not?

Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and in British English.
