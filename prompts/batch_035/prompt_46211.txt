As a journalist covering science and ideas, I sit on the editorial standards committee of a general-science magazine. We commission long-form features, and a dilemma has arisen over a piece we have in the pipeline: a profile, several thousand words in, of a single Atta (leafcutter) ant colony on a research reserve, written from the standpoint that the colony itself is the individual organism with a mind of its own — the ants being, in the writer's framing, something like its neurons. The piece draws explicitly on the tradition of Hofstadter and Dennett's dialogue about the intelligent ant colony, treating the colony's fungus gardens, pheromone trails and caste regulation as its internal economy and nervous system. It closes by asking whether readers might owe something like moral consideration to colonies as such, given how pest control is usually reasoned about.

The dispute on the committee is not about quality — everyone accepts the writing is excellent and the science reporting accurate. It is about framing. One half of the committee wants to run the piece essentially as pitched, arguing that the 'colony as individual' view is a defensible position in evolutionary biology and philosophy of mind, and that imposing disclaimers would be patronising. The other half thinks that presenting a contested metaphysical claim in the declarative register of a profile risks misleading lay readers into taking it as settled fact, and that at minimum the framing must change. There is also a concrete sub-dispute: the writer wants the colony referred to by a name, and by 'who' rather than 'which' throughout, on the model of our style rules for elephants and ravens, and our fact-checkers are unsure whether those rules can stretch that far.

Please draft the committee's decision memo — run as pitched, run with conditions, or decline to run — with reasons the writer and the features desk could actually work with rather than feel managed by. The memo should take the intellectual stakes seriously: it is not enough to say 'this is opinion, label it'; the memo must grapple with the genuine question of what a magazine's declarative voice is doing when it adopts an intentional stance toward something that may or may not be a subject.

Please structure the memo under the following headings, in this order:

1. 'Summary of recommendation' (roughly 120 words, giving the bottom line up front);
2. 'The case for running the piece as pitched' (roughly 250 words);
3. 'The case for requiring changes' (roughly 250 words);
4. 'Decision and conditions' (roughly 350 words, including a clear position on the naming and pronoun question);
5. 'Guidance for future features' (roughly 150 words, stating a test the desk can apply when a piece's central claim is a contested metaphysical or interpretive thesis presented in factual register).

The losing side's strongest consideration should receive an honest treatment, not a token paragraph.
