I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, and I'm writing a chapter on material constitution in which I want to pressure-test the classic statue-and-clay puzzle with a case my advisor and I have been developing. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers on opposite sides of the coincidence debate, arguing about that case.

The background: a sculptor uses a robotic clay printer that deposits clay layer by layer directly in the shape of a statue. There is never a moment at which a shapeless lump of clay exists prior to the statue, and there is never a moment at which the clay exists in any form other than that of the statue. Once printing is complete, the sculptor names the piece and sells it to a museum. The museum's registrar must decide how many objects she is taking custody of: one, or two.

One character in the dialogue should defend coincidence: the statue and the piece of clay are numerically distinct objects that occupy exactly the same region and share all their microphysical properties. The other should hold that there is only one object there, and that the appearance of two is generated by confused counting practices or by treating two sortals as if they introduced two things. Neither character should be a strawman; both should be technically competent and should press their strongest arguments.

I'd like the dialogue to work through at least the following moves, with each character responding directly to the other's points rather than talking past them:

1. The modal argument for distinctness — the claim that the clay could survive being squashed into a ball while the statue could not, so they must differ in their persistence conditions and hence cannot be identical. The anti-coincidence character should challenge whether this argument still has a grip in the printed case, where no lump was ever independently present to be tracked, and should also raise the general worry that coincidence multiplies objects intolerably (why stop at two rather than statues-plus-clay-plus-aggregates-of-clay-molecules?).

2. The sortal-relative reply — the suggestion that "statue" and "piece of clay" are two ways of counting or two ways of answering the question "what is there?" rather than two answers, and that identity statements between them are neither true nor false but ill-formed. The coincidence character should press on whether this can be made to cohere with standard quantification: if the registrar asks how many objects are in the crate, is there no fact of the matter?

3. The twist in the case: partway through the statue's museum life, a corner chips off and the conservator replaces it with new clay of the same type, keeping the chip in a drawer. The characters should disagree about what, if anything, this does to their earlier positions — for instance, whether the coincidence theorist now faces a sorites-style question about when the original piece of clay ceased to exist, and whether the one-object theorist can explain why the museum seems entitled to say it still owns the very statue it bought while being unable to say the same about any particular piece of matter.

The dialogue should be around 900 to 1200 words, in a seminar-room register: informal enough to read as spoken exchange, but with the characters making precise, well-structured points. Please end with the two characters identifying, in one or two exchanges, exactly what they take the deepest remaining point of disagreement to be, rather than reaching an artificial resolution.
