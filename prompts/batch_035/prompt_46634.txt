I'm taking a survey course on early modern philosophy and writing a term paper on Leibniz's ethics. The unit in class treated the Theodicy almost entirely as an argument about God and the problem of evil, and barely touched his moral philosophy proper, so to fill the gap I picked up a popular book on the history of optimism -- the breezy kind written for a general audience -- and it characterizes his position roughly like this: "Leibniz proved that we live in the best of all possible worlds. Since God is all-powerful and all-good, He had to create the best world He could, which means everything that happens is for the best: every evil is really a disguised good, suffering exists only so that a greater good can follow from it, and whatever occurs is exactly what ought to occur. Consequently Leibniz's morality is essentially fatalism -- since all is for the best, resisting evil is pointless and complaining about injustice is a confusion, an error in the arithmetic of goods. His system leaves no room for genuine evil, no room for moral effort, and therefore no room for morality at all. Voltaire saw this clearly enough, and after the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 the position collapsed."

I suspect this runs together several things Leibniz worked hard to keep apart, but I don't know the primary texts well enough to say where. A few things I've picked up elsewhere suggest trouble with the book's picture: a classmate mentioned that Leibniz defined justice as "the charity of the wise man" and built a substantial natural-law theory around it, which sounds like more moral content than fatalism allows; and I remember our professor stressing that Leibniz's deepest worry was distinguishing God's choice of this world from Spinoza's kind of necessity, whereas this passage has God "having to" create it -- though I confess I can't say whether that's a real tension or whether Leibniz just bites that bullet with his "moral necessity" idea.

Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a Leibniz scholar would accept, what's an innocent simplification for a popular audience, and what's a genuine misrepresentation? I'm especially interested in whether the slide from "God chooses the best on the whole" to "every event is for the best" is something Leibniz ever licenses, and whether his actual moral philosophy -- the justice theory, what he says about permitting versus willing evil, and about our obligations to resist evil -- is really compatible with the fatalism the book attributes to him. And since this is going into a paper, please keep separate what is settled interpretation of Leibniz from what scholars genuinely disagree about: I don't want to present a contested reading as the consensus.
