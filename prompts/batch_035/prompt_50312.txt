next term i'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar that ends with the interpretations of probability, and i want to open with a question the textbooks skip: what is an interpretation of probability even *for*? Write me the desiderata list — what any adequate account of what probability statements mean has to deliver. I'd want at least: consistency with the Kolmogorov axioms, numbers we can somehow ascertain or estimate, an explanation of why observed frequencies track probabilities (not just a shrug that they usually do), assignments to genuinely single-case events like the decay of one particular atom, and something to say about the refrence class mess — the probability that *i* die before 70 seems to depend on which population you lump me into, and an interpretation that goes quiet at that feels incomplete. For each requirement, say what motivates it and whether it's a genuine demand or a camp preference smuggled in as an adequacy condition — i suspect "probabilities must be out there in the physical world" is the latter. Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most interpretations, and whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable — my hunch is that full ascertainment plus single-case objectivity can't both be had, but i've never seen anyone come out and say it straight. Don't bother scoring frequentism vs. subjectivism vs. propensity against the list, the students will do that themselves in class. it's for a handout so bullets are fine.
