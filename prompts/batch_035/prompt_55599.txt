I'm a psychometrician who spends her days on cross-cultural survey work — checking measurement invariance across translated questionnaires, hunting differential item functioning, worrying about acquiescence bias, reference-group effects, and the whole problem of what "the same construct" even means across populations. Restate Montaigne's argument in "Of Cannibals" in my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. The moves I want translated are: that we call barbarous whatever merely departs from our own customs, so the judgment tracks the observer's reference group rather than anything in the observed; that ritual cannibalism, re-described inside its own frame of honor and warfare, is less cruel than what Europeans were doing to living bodies in the religious wars; and that custom is the thing that blinds us, so hearing a description in the frame of another people is a method for correcting the bias. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all. I'm especially interested in his appeal to nature and reason as a standard that outranks any single group's customs — that looks to me like it has no counterpart in a framework where "valid" can only ever mean "measures the same thing, relative to an anchor group." Tell me whether that residue points to something my vocabulary is missing or to something Montaigne never actually made clear. Two cautions: don't let the translation smuggle in the conclusion that because there is no culture-free instrument there are no cross-cultural norms — if you want that conclusion, argue for it; and be explicit about where your reasoning is likeliest to fail, which residue claim you'd withdraw first, and what would change your overall verdict.
