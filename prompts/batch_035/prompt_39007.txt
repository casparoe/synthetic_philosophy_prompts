I've been reading about the problem of dirty hands in political ethics -- basically the Walzer-style idea that a decent political leader will sometimes face situations where every available option is morally wrong, and that the right thing is to choose the lesser evil knowingly, accept that you did wrong, and carry the guilt rather than tell yourself your hands stayed clean. Supposedly the paradigm cases are things like torturing a terrorist to find the bomb, or lying to the public to prevent a panic. My professor keeps insisting the concept is more subtle than "the ends justify the means," and I get that in the abstract, but I want to stress-test it on a real episode, so here's what I want to know.

Take Iran-Contra. As I understand the facts: in 1985-86, people in the Reagan administration secretly sold anti-tank and anti-aircraft missiles to Iran -- a hostile regime that was backing the groups holding American hostages in Lebanon -- partly in hopes of getting the hostages out, and then funneled the profits to the Nicaraguan Contras after Congress had cut off their funding with the Boland Amendment. When it broke in late 1986, Oliver North testified in uniform, more or less proudly, and became something of a folk hero to a chunk of the public; Reagan himself offered up his famously passive acknowledgment that mistakes had been made. So here's my ask: analyze this episode as a dirty hands case, and then tell me where the fit is bad. Map it carefully -- who is supposed to be the agent with dirty hands here (Reagan? Poindexter? North? some distributed "administration"?), what exactly is the "wrong" committed (dealing with hostage-takers and arguably incentivizing more hostage-taking, deceiving Congress, breaking a law the actors thought was itself a mistake?), what plays the role of the greater evil being prevented, and whether the necessity condition is even remotely satisfied, since they didn't merely choose the least bad option but manufactured the options themselves.

Then run it the other direction: does the episode reveal something Walzer's framework under-theorizes or never anticipated? The parts that bug me are (a) the collective/hierarchical structure -- no single person made the choice or could feel the guilt Walzer demands, and (b) the total absence of guilt; North felt vindicated, and his supporters felt vindicated for him, which looks less like a tragic necessity and more like the dirty hands vocabulary being borrowed after the fact as PR for ordinary lawbreaking. Where is the framework just silent here, and where does it actively misdescribe what happened? And if you think there's a better lens -- plain constitutional usurpation, or a collective-action version of the problem -- tell me what it explains that dirty hands can't. Be honest if the answer is that Iran-Contra just wasn't a hard case at all.
