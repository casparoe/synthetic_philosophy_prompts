I am a high school senior, and I would like your help thinking through a pattern in my own behavior that I cannot make sense of, and that the usual study-habit advice does not seem to touch. I would rather have careful reasoning than tips.

Here is the pattern. Every night at ten, I write in my planner that tomorrow at seven I will do my calculus problem set. When I write it, I mean it completely: the assignment is clear, the stakes are clear, and my intention is not in the slightest degree insincere. But when seven o'clock arrives, the problem set has somehow acquired a quality that it did not have the night before — it looks like a gray wall, while the alternative, an hour of videos, looks warm and available. I put the assignment off. By nine, watching the videos feels like nothing at all, and I am genuinely sorry. Then at ten I write the same plan again, with the same complete sincerity, and the cycle repeats.

What unsettles me is that I seem to have no false belief anywhere in the loop. My ten o'clock self, my seven o'clock self, and my nine o'clock self all agree on the facts: that an A in calculus matters more to me than an hour of videos, that I will regret trading the one for the other, that this exact trade has ended in regret every previous evening. It is not that any of us misjudges the value of the outcome. It is that the same two options appear to weigh differently depending on how far away I am standing from them, and the weight shifts in exactly the direction of whatever is nearest. So my first question is whether this even counts as irrationality, or whether it is something a perfectly rational person could do — and if it is a genuine failure of rationality, where exactly the failure is located, given that no false belief is involved.

My second question concerns who should win. A friend of mine says the seven o'clock self is the "real" one because it is the one that acts, so its preference is authoritative and my evening resolutions are just theater. But this seems arbitrary in the other direction: why should the self that happens to be closest to the choice get to overrule the selves who have had time to think? Conversely, I cannot see why my ten o'clock self should be allowed to sign contracts that it will never have to execute. Is there any principled way to decide which of these selves is speaking for me, or is the whole idea of one "me" with a single consistent ranking of options what is breaking here?

Finally, the practical question, which I want treated philosophically and not as productivity advice. People keep telling me to precommit: hand my phone to my mother until eight, install a blocker, sign up for a study room with a late-cancel penalty. I have noticed something odd about these devices, though. The version of me that installs the blocker at ten is not persuading the seven o'clock version of anything; it is arranging for that version to be simply unable to act on its own preference. It is treating my later self the way one treats an opponent — planning around it rather than reasoning with it. Does that kind of self-management actually make me more rational, or does it just make me powerless while leaving the underlying problem untouched? And if it works, what does its working imply about what I am — whether it even makes sense to say a single person made these plans and these choices?
