I lead a fortnightly philosophy discussion group at a community centre — mostly retirees, a nurse, a retired engineer, no formal background, but genuinely willing to work through things. Next month the theme is "the self," and I want to devote the first thirty-five minutes of one session to Avicenna's flying man before opening the floor. The version I'll present is the classic one: suppose a human being were created fully formed but suspended in a void, in total darkness, with the limbs separated so they don't touch, receiving no sensory input whatsoever and having no past experience of any kind. Avicenna's claim is that this person would still be aware of their own existence — would affirm it if somehow asked — so our self-awareness cannot be something that reaches us through the senses. I want the group to come away seeing why this counts as an argument rather than an evocative image, and what exactly it does and doesn't establish.

Please plan the session for me rather than give the explanation yourself. What has to be in place before I even present the case? My suspicion is that they need to see the background position it's aimed at — roughly, the thought that all our awareness is ultimately fed to us by the senses — or else the flying man will just read as a sad story about a lonely man in the dark, with no dialectical point. Should the thought experiment itself carry the whole session, or should I reach for a contemporary substitute — sensory-deprivation tanks, people born without sight or touch — and if the modern cases look friendlier but actually do worse as substitutes, at what point should I say so and why? And which misconceptions should I be braced for? I can anticipate at least four: that this is simply Descartes' cogito five hundred years early; that it's an empirical prediction about what such a person would actually report; that it proves the soul is immortal; and that it shows the self has nothing to do with the body. Where in the sequence should I head off each one, rather than letting it take root and trying to dig it out later?

Two further things. I've read that in Avicenna's later, more compressed presentations of the case, he doesn't describe a hypothetical stranger but addresses the reader directly — something like: make this happen to yourself, set aside everything you're aware of, and notice what remains. Is that second-person version worth building in from the start, or does it risk the group confusing the conclusion with a mood of detachment? And what should I explicitly refuse to cover at this level — I'm guessing Avicenna's full psychology of the rational soul, the debate over whether the argument is meant to establish immateriality or only independence from sense experience, and the medieval Latin reception, but correct me if something else belongs on that list, or if one of these is actually load-bearing. Finally, suggest one question I can ask at the end that would show me whether they've grasped the argument's actual conclusion rather than merely enjoyed the picture.
