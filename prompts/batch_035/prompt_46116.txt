A while back my brother-in-law, who teaches mathematics at a secondary school, told me about something a colleague of his did that has been occupying my mind ever since. On a Monday morning she announced to her class that there would be a test at some point that week, Monday through Friday, and that the day of the test would be a surprise: on the evening before the test, the students would not be able to work out which day it would fall on. The students, being a clever bunch, reasoned it out like this. The test cannot be on Friday, because if Thursday evening arrived with no test yet given, everyone would know it had to be Friday, and then it would not be a surprise. But with Friday eliminated, the test cannot be on Thursday either, since Wednesday evening everyone would know it must be Thursday, the only remaining day. And the same elimination runs backward all the way to Monday, so no surprise test is possible at all. The class concluded the announcement was empty, relaxed, and was then genuinely astonished when the test arrived on Wednesday morning. My brother-in-law swears the students were truly surprised, and that is the part I cannot square with anything.

I have been chewing on this on my own for months and have gotten nowhere tidy. My first thought was that the whole thing hinges on what the students are entitled to assume the teacher will keep her word about, so I tried shrinking the announcement to a single day: there will be a test tomorrow, and it will be a surprise. Here that just looks like a false announcement rather than a paradox, which makes me suspect the week-long version smuggles in something extra, though I cannot say what. My second thought was that the argument is somehow self-undermining: by concluding that no test can occur, the students stop expecting a test on any particular day, and that very expectation is what makes whichever day the teacher picks a surprise — the announcement makes itself true by appearing to make itself false. But I cannot tell whether that is an actual solution or just an elegant restatement of what bothers me. My third thought, which I like less, is that the backward elimination only works if the students treat the announcement as completely reliable at every step, and maybe they are not entitled to do that even once, let alone five times over.

Please write an essay on whether the students' backward reasoning is sound or where exactly it breaks, weighing the case for taking it seriously against the case for saying it proves too much, and reach a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter open. Cover the one-day version along the way, and say plainly whether you think the surprise, on the day it happened, was real or an illusion of hindsight. Do not cite or refer to any papers or named paradoxes from the literature; argue it out from the case as I have described it, as if we were working it through over a kitchen table. Present the essay as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion before or after the list.
