I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on my dissertation's third chapter, which defends consequentialism against the demandingness objection. Below is a condensed version of the central argument of a draft section. I would be grateful for comments of the kinds I list at the end. (Note: please don't supply reading lists or bibliographic references — I'm interested in the strength and accuracy of the reasoning, not pointers to literature.)

The argument, stated as it appears in the draft:

The demandingness objection says consequentialism is false, or unacceptable as a moral view, because it demands too much: if I must always do the best I can for others overall, my own projects, relationships, and life get no protected space. My thesis is that this objection, once we press on it, establishes far less than its users want. Here is why. Any objection to consequentialism's demandingness must specify a ceiling: a level of sacrifice beyond which morality does not demand more. Ceilings need grounds, and there are only two kinds available. Call a justification agent-protective if it appeals to what the agent is owed: her projects, her integrity, her life as her own. Call it beneficiary-limited if it appeals to what the beneficiaries of sacrifice may reasonably demand: the claim that there is a point at which the needy cannot demand more of you even though you could give more and they would benefit.

I argue, first, that the beneficiary-limited route fails on its own terms. The natural version is a fair-share view: I owe only my share of relieving need, no more. But the world's worst harms are mostly indivisible: someone's famine relief is saved-for or it isn't; the marginal donation either crosses a threshold or doesn't. If everyone gives exactly their fair share and people still die, then the fair-share view holds that morality permits letting people die for the sake of an appealing division of labor — which is not a rationale at all unless we think the division of labor itself has moral value, and no one who makes this argument actually thinks that. Fair shares track what's reasonable to expect when others are noncompliant, as in Murphy's cooperative principle; but then the ceiling is indexed to actual compliance, and it follows that in a world of saintly compliance, morality demands the full consequentialist load again. The objection then proves nothing about consequentialism itself; it proves only that nonideal worlds license prerogatives — which consequentialists can happily grant.

Second, the agent-protective route succeeds, but it does not refute consequentialism; it amends it. This is essentially the move Scheffler made in proposing an agent-centered prerogative: agents may devote a proportion of their effort to their own concerns even when promoting the good overall would better serve others. Once the prerogative is granted, the resulting view is a hybrid, and hybrids are typically presented as concessions extracted from consequentialism. But notice what has happened dialectically: the objection began as a refutation of consequentialism and ended as a revision of it. The opponent of consequentialism who takes this route has not shown that maximizing is false; she has shown that one reasonable view permits moderate deviations from it.

Third, and this is the section's most contentious claim: any ceiling on demandingness that is not grounded in either of these two ways can, I argue, only be grounded consequentialistically — by appeal to the bad effects of teaching or internalizing an over-demanding morality, such as burnout, resentment, and motivational collapse. If the only reason to cap moral demands is that capped moralities produce better people and better outcomes, then the objection is consequentialist reasoning operating at the level of theory-selection while pretending to operate at the level of first-order morality. So the trilemma is: either a fair-share ceiling that collapses into a compliance-indexed prerogative, an agent-protective prerogative that revises rather than refutes consequentialism, or a covertly consequentialist ceiling. None of these is what the demandingness objection was supposed to deliver.

My questions for you:

1. Soundness first. Is the trilemma's exhaustive structure defensible, or am I smuggling in a false dilemma? In particular, can a contractualist ceiling — one fixed by what no one could reasonably reject, where the desperately needy person herself could not reasonably demand the whole of my life — be classified under either of my two headings? It feels beneficiary-limited, but it doesn't have the fair-share structure I attack. Have I refuted the wrong version?

2. Does the argument turn on an ambiguity I haven't separated: ceilings on what morality demands, versus ceilings on what a person may be blamed for not doing, versus ceilings on what it would be admirable to do? I suspect these come apart, and that some versions of the demandingness objection only ever needed the middle one. If so, does my trilemma still bite, or does it bite only against the strongest and least popular version of the objection?

3. Am I too quick about the agent-protective route being a mere amendment? A defender could say that once the prerogative is in, maximizing no longer plays any role in the theory — the hybrid is a rival, not a revision. Where exactly is the line between amending a view and abandoning it, and which side of the line does the prerogative-laden hybrid really fall on?

4. Accuracy and fairness. Does my characterization of the fair-share and cooperative views do them justice, or have I stacked the deck with the indivisibility point? Similarly, does presenting the prerogative as a revision of consequentialism rather than an alternative to it beg the question in a way a referee would flag?

5. Finally, an honest assessment: if you think the argument is sound in its parts but fails to add up to anything — that demandingness objections were never meant to refute consequentialism outright, only to make it uncomfortable — then say so, and tell me whether the section still earns its place in the chapter.
