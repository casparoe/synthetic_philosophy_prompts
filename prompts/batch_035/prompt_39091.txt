In June 2014, at a contest held at the Royal Society to mark the sixtieth anniversary of Alan Turing's death, a chatbot named Eugene Goostman — presented as a thirteen-year-old Ukrainian boy with patchy English and a pet guinea pig — was declared the first program ever to pass the Turing test, after convincing 33% of a panel of thirty judges, over five-minute text conversations, that it was human. The organizer, Kevin Warwick of the University of Reading, defended the pass threshold by appealing to a prediction in Turing's 1950 paper that by the year 2000 the average interrogator would have no better than a 70% chance of correctly identifying the machine after five minutes of questioning — though Turing offered that as a forecast about the state of technology, not as a criterion for mind, and the bot had already persuaded 29% of judges at a Bletchley Park event in 2012, while earlier programs had done comparably well under looser conditions.

Analyze this episode as a case study in behavioral criteria for mind, running the analysis in both directions. First, the mapping: what exactly does the Turing test operationalize — the presence of thought, or the capacity to produce a certain conversational performance — and how faithfully did the 2014 event instantiate what Turing actually proposed? It is worth taking seriously that the 1950 imitation game was originally framed as a man imitating a woman against a woman, with the machine only later substituted into the man's role, and that Turing's own paper presented the game as a dissolving of the question of whether machines think rather than as an experiment. Second, the breakdowns: the persona strategy (a bored, evasive, non-native-speaking teenager as a systematically lowered evidential bar — when the computer scientist Scott Aaronson asked whether a shoebox or Mount Everest was bigger, the bot said it would have to think about that later and changed the subject), five minutes as a rationed evidence window, and the quietly shifted burden of turning a prediction into a threshold. Third, the reflexive question: does the episode show that any behavioral criterion for mind is hostage to how the test is staged — judges, time limits, personas, and what counts as a pass — or does it rather vindicate Turing's deeper point, that the original question was too vague to answer and only an operationalized substitute is answerable at all? Please do not simply rehearse the standard objections to the test; say precisely where the behavioral criterion is silent about this episode, and where it speaks clearly against Warwick's interpretation.
