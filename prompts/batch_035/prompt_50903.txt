I got into a long argument with my roommate (math grad student, insufferable about it) about the two-envelope paradox, and I want you to write an essay that actually takes a side instead of just surveying the terrain. Quick setup so we're on the same page: one envelope contains twice as much money as the other, you pick one at random, and before opening it you're offered the chance to switch. The tempting argument says: whatever's in mine, call it X, the other envelope has either 2X or X/2, and these seem equally likely, so the expected value of switching is 1.25X — switch! Except the same argument, run from the other envelope's perspective, says switch back, and so on forever, which is obviously absurd. The standard cure is to say the "equally likely" step is where it goes wrong: for any sensible prior over the amounts, once you condition on the value you actually observe, the other envelope is more likely smaller than larger for most values, and the 1.25X calculation falls apart. Fine. That handles the naive version, and my roommate says this means it was never a real paradox, just a howler, and that anyone still puzzled by it hasn't done the algebra.

But here's the part I can't shake, and what I want the essay to really dig into. There are variants where the switching argument apparently isn't fallacious at all. In one, the amounts are generated so that, whatever value you end up observing, the conditional probability that the other envelope holds more is slightly above one half — for every possible observed amount — even though you picked your envelope at random, so neither envelope was special in advance. In another, the expected value in each envelope is infinite, so "both envelopes have the same expectation" is true but useless, and the 1.25X comparison quietly trades infinities it has no right to trade. My roommate waves these off as "pathological distributions, nobody's prior is like that," but that response feels question-begging to me: the paradox was supposed to be cured by conditioning properly, and in these cases you *can* condition properly and the switch still looks favorable.

So the question for the essay: is the two-envelope paradox just a fallacy with a textbook cure, or do these weird setups show it has genuine content — that our reasoning about expected value breaks somewhere deeper than one botched probability assignment?
