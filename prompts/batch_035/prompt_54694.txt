A colleague objected that when we write that the model "expects" a transaction series to continue or "believes" a record is anomalous, we're trafficking in mere metaphor, since the model has no beliefs in any respectable sense. The trouble is that I now suspect some of these commitments don't sit comfortably together, and I can't see where the problem is. Here is the list:

1. Global physicalism: the complete microphysical state of the universe, together with the laws, fixes all the facts there are.
2. The real-patterns criterion: an intentional state is real when, and only when, adopting the intentional stance toward the system yields predictive compression — you get more output of prediction for less descriptive input than you could get without it. This is roughly the line Dennett takes in his 1991 paper on real patterns.
3. Realism about belief: because folk psychology is a robustly projectible pattern, most ordinary beliefs and desires exist. I am not an eliminativist about propositional attitudes.
4. The physical stance is not metaphysically privileged. The three stances — physical, design, intentional — are perspectives for compressing the same world at different grains, and the physical description is itself just the currently most general and powerful such compression, not a metaphysically absolute inventory of what there is.
5. There is a determinate, categorical fact of the matter about whether a given system has beliefs: systems either genuinely believe things or they don't. This matters practically, because our reports need to say whether the model has expectations, full stop, not that it's belief-ish to degree 0.7.
6. Where intentional-stance attributions are inexact at the margins — whether the system's confidence that a record is anomalous is best described as a belief or as a sub-doxastic state — there is no stance-independent fact of the matter; the pattern simply doesn't partition things that finely, and that's fine.
