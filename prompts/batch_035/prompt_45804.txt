I'm taking Philosophy of Medicine this term. My tutorial essay argues that disease-status can't be read off the biological statistics alone: whether a condition is a disease depends, at least in part, on what it does to a person's life. The usual contested examples won't carry that argument — with menopause, aging, deafness, or short stature, either the harm is disputed or the dysfunction is, so both sides keep room to maneuver. What I need is a case built to order.

The scenario should be one in which a Nordenfelt-style normativist account — on which a disease is, roughly, a condition that involuntarily prevents a person from achieving her vital goals, with no separate biological-dysfunction requirement — must classify a condition as a disease, while Boorse's biostatistical theory cannot. This is the hard direction: the harm has to be as uncontroversial as harm gets, and the absence of dysfunction has to come from the statistics rather than from stipulation.

Constraints on the scenario: one paragraph; medically realistic, modelled on conditions medicine already manages (degenerative, age-graded), no genetic engineering or far-future technology; the harm is chronic pain and lost mobility, not anything that turns on contested values, and the patient must not welcome the condition — her attitudes may do no work; keep it bodily rather than psychiatric; avoid the standard parade of examples (menopause, aging as such, pregnancy, deafness, short stature, ordinary sadness); and engineer the split through where the relevant biological measures sit relative to the statistically normal range for the reference class — not through the condition being rare or exotic.
