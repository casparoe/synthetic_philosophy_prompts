I'm in a philosophy of mind course this semester and our last unit was panpsychism — we spent a couple weeks on Russellian monism, the idea that physics only tells us about the structural/relational features of matter and leaves the intrinsic nature of things blank, and that maybe that intrinsic nature is experiential. I actually loved it even though half the class hated it. But the final session was on the combination problem, and that's where everything got messy: if the fundamental constituents each have some tiny experiential character, how do you get from a swarm of micro-experiences to ONE unified subject, one consciousness that has all of it from the inside? My professor kept stressing that this is supposed to be disanalogous to physical composition — when you combine particles you're just arranging structure on top of structure, but phenomenal properties aren't structural, so there's no obvious recipe for them to sum. She also mentioned William James's old worry, paraphrased roughly: he thought it was as unintelligible that experiences in many distinct minds could add up to a single bigger experience as it would be for the individual experiences to be piled on top of each other in the first place. No matter how many you line up, you always end up with just a crowd of little experiences, never one big one.

For our last seminar we all bring something instead of writing a response paper, and I want to bring a poem (someone's doing a comic, someone baked something shaped like a brain, whatever). Here's my idea: a sestina. The six end-words should each be a single-word stand-in for a micro-experience — like a flicker, a hum, a warmth, that kind of thing, you pick them — and the rotation of the sestina should do the philosophical work: the same six experiences keep getting rearranged, stanza after stanza, shuffled into new orders and new sentences, but they never fuse. Each stanza should be vivid enough to read as a little moment of experience, and the reader should gradually feel the worry that no amount of reshuffling produces a single subject that has all six at once. Then the envoi — which has to use all six end-words in three lines — is where the poem should pose, but NOT answer, the question of whether gathering all six into one final stanza is genuine combination or just proximity: the words finally share a stanza the way the micro-experiences allegedly share a mind, and it should be deliberately unclear whether that settles anything. My TA is a panpsychist so she'll notice if the poem just strawmans the view — it shouldn't be a hit piece, more like an honest dramatization of why the problem is hard. Something like 39 lines total, standard sestina rules, but beyond that you've got freedom on tone (lyrical is fine, weird is fine, darkly funny is fine too).
