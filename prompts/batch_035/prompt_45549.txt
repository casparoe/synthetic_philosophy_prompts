Commercial aviation investigates accidents under an explicitly non-punitive protocol: investigators reconstruct what happened, their records are in many jurisdictions shielded from use in disciplinary or legal proceedings, and the standard output is not a verdict of fault but a redesign — a revised checklist, a changed instrument layout, different automation logic, a new training syllabus. On this protocol the pilot's memory, the laminated checklist on the yoke, the flow of information between crew members, and the flight-management software are treated as one engineered system, and a finding of "pilot error" is typically diagnosed as a failure of some component of that system — often an external one, with the remedy applied to the scaffold rather than the skull. Yet the very same industry revokes licenses, grounds pilots who fail proficiency checks, and holds the individual captain legally responsible for the flight. What conception of agency, skill, and the boundary of the mind does this practice presuppose? Does the no-blame investigation regime presuppose something like the extended-mind thesis — the view that when the coupling is constant, reliable, and automatically consulted, the checklist and the cockpit avionics are literally parts of the pilot's cognitive system, so that "pilot error" misdescribes a failure of an extended agent — or does it require no such theory at all, being merely a pragmatic bargain in which immunity is traded for honest reporting? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, say whether the licensing-and-enforcement half of the industry presupposes an inconsistent internalism about the very same mind, and tell me what would actually have to change in investigation practice if aviation explicitly adopted the extended-mind view — and what, if anything, would change if it explicitly rejected it.
