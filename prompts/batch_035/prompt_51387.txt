I'm a journalist who writes long features on science and ideas, and I've become preoccupied with the bootstrapping problem in epistemology: the family of cases in which someone apparently reasons their way to knowledge that a source is reliable using nothing but the deliverances of that very source, thereby acquiring what has come to be called easy knowledge. The stock illustration is the traveler who concludes his map of an unfamiliar region is accurate by checking each landmark against the map itself; the canonical epistemological versions involve someone concluding that their color vision is reliable because things they see as red look red, or that an instrument is trustworthy because its readings cohere with each other. I want to work out what this problem actually implies for my trade, because journalism is full of practices that resemble that traveler: citing a study because other outlets cited it, treating peer review as evidence of a paper's reliability rather than as a filter we have no independent way of auditing, deciding a dataset is trustworthy on the strength of its internal consistency, or deferring to an expert consensus partly on the basis of the very reporting that made that consensus visible to me in the first place. Please describe the most important implications of the bootstrapping problem for working journalists: which of our deference practices it genuinely condemns and which it leaves untouched; what separates legitimate trust in an instrument, a method, or an expert community from illegitimate bootstrapping, and whether the standard proposed separators — calibration against independent checks, background information about competence, gradual earned trust — actually do the work they are advertised to do; and what the problem teaches about the difference between reporting that a claim is true and reporting that a community of experts takes it to be true. I am not asking for a history of the debate or a survey of who said what; I want the implications worked out on the page, including any points at which the philosophical lesson and the practical realities of deadline-driven reporting pull in opposite directions, since those tensions are what I most need to understand before I write the piece.
