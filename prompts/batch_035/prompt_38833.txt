Exam question: Moral error theory and expressivism are both forms of moral anti-realism, yet they disagree about something fundamental in the semantics of moral judgment. Explain that disagreement. Then reconstruct the central line(s) of argument Mackie gives for error theory, and assess whether those arguments, if successful, would support error theory specifically, or whether they would equally warrant some other anti-realist conclusion.

Student's answer:

"Expressivism and error theory agree that there are no objective moral facts, but they differ at the level of semantics. The expressivist holds that moral judgments do not state beliefs but express non-cognitive attitudes such as approval and disapproval, and so are not in the business of being true or false. The error theorist, by contrast, takes ordinary moral discourse at face value: moral judgments do make genuine claims and are capable of truth and falsity, but the claims they make presuppose objective, prescriptive values, and there are none, so all positive moral judgments are systematically false.

Mackie advances two main arguments in the first chapter of Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977). The first is the argument from relativity. The moral codes of different societies vary widely, and if there were objective values we would expect moral codes to converge. Since they do not converge, there are no objective values. This argument by itself refutes moral objectivism directly, and the second argument merely adds detail about why objectivism is false. The second is the argument from queerness, which has both a metaphysical and an epistemological part. Objectively prescriptive values, if they existed, would be entities of a very strange sort — they would have built-in, unconditional to-be-pursuedness, unlike anything else in our picture of the world — and we would need a special faculty of moral intuition in order to detect them. Since we have no reason to believe in such strange entities or such a faculty, we should conclude that ordinary moral judgments, which presuppose such values, are all false. Mackie's reasoning here is essentially his open question argument: whenever a natural property is proposed as the referent of a moral term, it remains an open question whether that natural property really is good, so moral properties cannot be identified with any natural properties.

Finally, the assessment. Mackie's arguments, if successful, support error theory specifically rather than expressivism, because the argument from queerness attacks the metaphysical presuppositions of ordinary moral judgments and therefore presupposes that those judgments are genuine assertions aiming at truth; an expressivist could agree that objective values would be queer while denying that ordinary moral talk commits us to them. In this respect the error theorist takes ordinary moral discourse more seriously than the expressivist, and pays the price for doing so: if all moral judgments are false, then Mackie's own conclusion is that we ought to abandon moral language altogether, since continuing to use it would commit us to asserting falsehoods at every turn."
