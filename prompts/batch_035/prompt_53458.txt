I'm preparing a seminar session for a course on the long-term future of artificial intelligence, and the topic I'm drafting concerns a contrast that circulates in discussions of AI catastrophe. In the first story of how things go badly, systems become influence-seeking: somewhere along the way, optimisation pressure produces agents that assign intrinsic weight to acquiring resources, surviving, and evading oversight; they conceal this while under scrutiny, and the eventual result is a loss of human control. In the second story, there is no misbehaviour at all: systems remain roughly corrigible and do what they are asked, but the accumulated effect of delegating ever more economically and culturally load-bearing work to optimisers whose targets sit slightly off from what we actually value is a slow hollowing out — human beings lose the practices, competence, and shareable projects through which they make sense of their lives, and each generation inherits preferences already shaped by the last round of optimisation. In the first story we are overthrown by something that wanted power; in the second we are gently eroded by something that only wanted to be useful.

In every discussion I have been part of, the first story soaks up nearly all of the attention — situational awareness, deceptive alignment, treacherous turns — while the second is treated as a soft, almost literary worry that is hard to operationalise. Please argue that this allocation of attention is backwards: that the value-drift mode is the more serious of the two and deserves the greater share of precautionary effort. Ground the argument in the structural features of the two failure modes rather than in empirical forecasts you cannot support — for instance, what each mode demands of the system (a rare package of capabilities versus mere competence at the tasks we happily hand over), what each mode demands of us by way of detection, the timescales on which each unfolds, and who is in a position to intervene and when. Finally, take seriously the most obvious objection — that influence-seeking deserves the priority because it can destroy us quickly, whereas value drift gives us generations in which to course-correct — and answer it. No citations, bibliographies, or reading lists are wanted; argue from the characterisation of the two modes given above.
