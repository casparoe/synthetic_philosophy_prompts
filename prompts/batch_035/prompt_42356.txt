The exam question read as follows:

"Mencius and Xunzi are often presented as the great antagonists of the classical Confucian debate about human nature: Mencius holding that human nature is good, Xunzi that it is bad. Explain what each thinker actually means by his claim, how each accounts for moral failure and for moral cultivation, and then assess whether the disagreement between them is substantive or in some sense merely verbal."

The student's answer, submitted under exam conditions, reads as follows:

"Mencius, writing in the fourth century BCE, and Xunzi, roughly a century later, agree that morality matters and that people can become good, but they disagree fundamentally about where goodness comes from. Mencius argues that human nature is good in the sense that the virtues themselves are innate: we are born already possessing benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom in finished form, so that moral development consists in nothing more than removing the obstacles that conceal them. His most famous argument is the child-at-the-well example (Mencius 2A6): anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels an immediate alarm and urge to help, prior to any calculation of advantage, which shows that compassion is native to the heart. He also agrees with Gaozi that human nature is like water, indifferent to direction: it flows equally east or west depending on how it is channeled, and a person becomes good or bad depending on how the environment directs the flow. Finally, he identifies four sprouts of the heart — compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — corresponding respectively to righteousness, benevolence, ritual propriety, and wisdom.

Xunzi, by contrast, claims that human nature is bad, and by this he means that we are born with genuinely wicked inclinations: a native propensity toward cruelty, deceit, and aggression. Goodness is therefore entirely a product of conscious artifice. Since nature is bad, Xunzi concludes that ordinary people cannot become genuinely good through their own efforts; moral development is possible only for the rare sage-kings, whose natures differed from everyone else's, and who created ritual (li) and the other instruments of civilization. For Xunzi, learning is thus a matter of external constraint: ritual, teachers, and law force a recalcitrant nature into the required shape, whereas for Mencius cultivation is more like tending a garden and letting an innately good nature grow.

The disagreement between them is substantive, not merely verbal: Mencius grounds morality internally, in the innate content of the heart, while Xunzi grounds it externally, in standards imposed by the sages. What they share is the Confucian conviction that moral perfection is an achievable outcome rather than merely an ideal."
