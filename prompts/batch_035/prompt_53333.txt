I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, where Carroll's tortoise gets trotted out every few years as the canonical demonstration that deduction cannot be justified without circularity — usually followed by a shrug and the admission that we simply stop at some point. For a while I accepted this as the standard wisdom, but I've been drafting an essay that tries to do better, and I'd appreciate comments on whether the argument actually works or merely relocates the problem.

Here is the idea, in condensed form. The tortoise accepts every premise of a modus ponens inference and accepts, as an additional premise, the conditional statement that the premises imply the conclusion — yet still refuses to accept the conclusion. The standard lesson drawn is that deduction needs a justification it cannot itself supply. My essay argues this is the wrong lesson: the tortoise works its mischief only because the interlocutor treats the rule of inference as something to be added to the premises, and a rule-as-premise is inert in exactly the way the dialogue shows. There is a categorical difference between asserting "this form of inference is valid" and actually being moved by an inference of that form — between, as I put it in the draft, rules as license and rules as legislation. On my view, the regress reveals not a gap in the justification of deduction but a defect in a particular picture of justification: the picture on which all warrant must be propositional, passable from person to person as one more item on the ledger. The warrant for a deductive step is instead a competence, a disposition to move from premise to conclusion, and asking for a premise-style justification of it is a category error. The second half of the essay addresses the obvious rejoinder: what distinguishes a competent reasoner from a merely disposed one, and doesn't this smuggle in Wittgensteinian rule-following considerations that raise difficulties of their own? I concede that it does, but argue that these are the genuine difficulties, and that the tortoise is best read as the entrance to them rather than as a self-contained paradox. I also have a section I'm unsure about, which claims that a rule's justification can be bootstrapped from its track record — that we trust modus ponens the way we trust a well-calibrated instrument — before acknowledging that any appeal to track record is itself an induction that presupposes deductive norms, so the circularity returns, though perhaps as a benign, Quinean circle rather than a vicious one.

What I'd like from you: comments on whether the central distinction (rules as license versus rules as legislation) genuinely dissolves the regress or just hides it — in particular, whether my claim that the tortoise is guilty of a category error would strike a philosopher as a real argument or as question-begging against someone who insists that non-propositional warrant is no warrant at all. I'd also like your honest assessment of whether the essay is interesting or merely a rehash of the standard takes — I have no way of knowing how much of this is already folklore in philosophy departments, and I'd rather find out now than embarrass myself by presenting as a discovery what everyone treats as obvious. Finally, tell me frankly whether the track-record section earns its place: if the circularity it admits is benign, the section needs a stronger argument for that benignity than the gesture towards pragmatism I currently give, and if it isn't benign, the section may need to be cut entirely, with the essay standing on the competence point alone.
