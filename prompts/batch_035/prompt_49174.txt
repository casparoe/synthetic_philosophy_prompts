I run a fortnightly methods seminar for philosophy graduate students, and my next session — fifty minutes, whiteboard only, no assigned reading — covers the accuracy-first program in formal epistemology: the claim that credences have a single epistemic good, namely accuracy, and that norms like probabilism can be vindicated non-pragmatically by showing that any credence function violating them is dominated by some probability function with respect to every strictly proper scoring rule. The audience is eight students, mostly from ethics and history of philosophy: quick with an argument, but they have never seen a dominance or convexity-style argument, and my last formal session left some of them feeling ambushed by notation, so the plan must ration the mathematics rather than apologize for it.

To be clear about the deliverable: please don't explain the accuracy-first program to me — I know the material and will be doing the talking. What I want is a plan for the explanation. Specifically:

First, the dependency structure. What has to be established before the central argument can even be stated — presumably scoring rules, then properness, then dominance — and where in the sequence the expected-utility machinery enters. Can the idea of an expectation be smuggled in through a single well-chosen example, or does the group need a short decision-theory detour first, and if so, how short can I get away with?

Second, the carrying example. I have been torn between a weather forecaster being scored on past forecasts and a trader whose payoff depends on the price they post; my worry about the forecaster is that being scored feels like being paid, which may invite exactly the pragmatist confusion I want to avoid. Recommend the one case that should carry the whole session, tell me what it must contain and what it must not, and why.

Third, the misconception map. My guesses at what will go wrong: (a) the circularity charge, that using expectations of epistemic utility presupposes probabilities and so can't vindicate them; (b) the sense that "epistemic utility" is either an oxymoron or a capitulation to instrumental reasoning, collapsing the epistemic into the pragmatic; (c) the assumption that the Brier score is somehow canonical, so that the whole argument looks like an artifact of one arbitrary choice. Tell me which of these are genuine risks, at what point in the sequence each will surface if I've ordered things badly, exactly where to pre-empt each, and what predictable misconception I've missed entirely.

Fourth, a check: one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answer would reveal whether the students have grasped the dominance structure of the argument — as opposed to merely having absorbed "inaccuracy is bad."

Finally, the refusals: what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the geometric proof sketch and anything about imprecise credences — and how to acknowledge those exclusions to the group in a way that reads as triage rather than evasion.
