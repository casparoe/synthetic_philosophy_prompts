I teach an upper-level seminar on the ethics and politics of drug policy, and I want a decision-tree handout that sorts students by what they actually believe about prohibition, decriminalization, and legalization, rather than by which slogan they find themselves attached to. The tree should begin from a commitment everyone in the room accepts — that recreational drugs can cause serious harm to those who use them — and terminate in named positions: prohibition; Portuguese-style decriminalization, in which use remains formally prohibited but is met by referral to a dissuasion commission rather than a court; Dutch-style de facto tolerance of retail sale within a framework that remains prohibitionist on paper; medical-only regimes; commercial cannabis legalization of the Canadian sort; and legalization without commercialization, whether through home cultivation, cannabis social clubs, or a state monopoly on sales with no advertising and no profit motive.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first fix the diagnostic questions, then locate the actual regimes among the combinations of answers, and report where nobody stands — with a diagnosis of each unoccupied combination as incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried. The questions I want available for the tree, in whatever order does the most sorting work per question:

1. May the criminal law punish conduct that harms only the actor, or must some harm to others — or some failure of voluntariness — be established first?
2. Does heavy addiction defeat the voluntariness that the first question relies on, such that coercive treatment for the user's own good is sometimes justified?
3. Does legal permission imply state endorsement of use, and if that implication can be severed — by plain packaging, public monopolies, health warnings — how much force does the endorsement objection retain?
4. Is the asymmetry between user and supplier principled — the supplier profits from another's vice — or an unstable compromise between camps?
5. If a drug is legal, may it be sold for profit and promoted, or only through non-commercial channels?
6. Does a more permissive stance toward cannabis than toward, say, heroin track a principled difference in harm and dependence, or is it politics all the way down — and can the tree represent a principled graded position without that branch collapsing into ad hocery?

Constraints on the instrument. Every node must be answerable by someone who has never heard the names of the positions; no question may be a disguised "are you a prohibitionist?". Flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — I particularly want to know whether it can distinguish Portuguese decriminalization from Dutch tolerance at all, and whether "medical use only" is a genuine leaf or merely prohibition with an exception carved out. The theoretical question underneath, which the handout should make visible without preaching at the students: the standard liberal argument from the harm principle appears to establish at most permission, while what most actual legalization regimes deliver is a commercial market. So I want at least one branch in which a student who accepts the harm principle ends up at "legal but non-commercial" rather than at "legalize and tax it," and I want the handout to say plainly whether that leaf is a real, defensible position or a philosopher's artifact.

The audience is undergraduates with one course in political philosophy behind them. Keep the deliverable to roughly one page of tree and one page of commentary, and do not include citations or a reading list — I will supply those myself.
