Two friends of mine have been arguing about the death penalty for literally years and I want to force them to do the adversarial-collaboration exercise they keep promising each other. One is a retributivist retentionist -- he thinks some crimes just deserve death, and that the state expressing society's condemnation at its maximum intensity is part of what justice requires. The other is an abolitionist whose center of gravity is epistemic: any system run by fallible humans will eventually execute innocent people, and no amount of retributive value can outweigh that. She's also honestly kind of skeptical of retribution as a justification at all, though she's never fully cashed that out.

Write the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign. I want the following pieces:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept. Please make these non-vacuous -- not "murder is bad" but things like what the deterrence evidence can and cannot currently show (both agree the empirical literature is too noisy to hang your hat on either way, or if they can't even agree on that, say so in the disagreements section instead), and that no appeals process can drive the probability of executing an innocent to literal zero.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement, phrased so that the side holding that view would actually sign off on the wording. If you find any part of their fight is verbal -- I suspect "dignity" is doing double duty, since he says execution affirms the victim's dignity and she says it degrades the state's -- flag which rows are that and why.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I have a suspicion one crux is what happens in the "slam-dunk" subset of cases -- imagine a defendant on uninterrupted 4K video, with DNA, who confessed with counsel present and re-confesses knowing exactly what awaits him. At that point her fallibility argument has almost no purchase, so does her position collapse into pure anti-retributivism, or does she have residual grounds (irreversibility as a categorical property of the punishment itself, the state's standing to kill)? And correspondingly: if he's right that desert can ground execution in slam-dunk cases, is his view hostage to the claim that desert verdicts can be distributed as reliably as slam-dunk evidence? Whether real capital systems contain any slam-dunk cases is itself a sub-question worth putting in the table.

4. For each crux, one concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it -- and here's the part they never actually do: write down each side's prediction in advance, before running the case. E.g. if the crux is whether retributive intuitions survive being made to actually specify execution criteria (aggravators, ages of defendants, the video case above), what does each predict they'd feel after working through it.

Don't try to adjudicate who's right -- I've had enough of that from them. The deliverable is the document they could both put their name on, with the leftover disagreement made exact enough that a reader could tell what measurement or argument would move one of them.
