I teach a historiography seminar for undergrads (14 students, one 80-minute session) and I want them to actually feel hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy rather than just read about them. Design an activity built around predictions: students should be asked to forecast how some real historical episode turned out before they know the outcome, get the outcome revealed, then be asked to explain it -- and the punchline should be them realizing how inevitable and over-determined their explanation makes the outcome feel, even though they just failed to predict it. Ideally there's a second phase where they rate how predictable the outcome was, so we can catch them rating it as more obvious than their own failed forecasts could possibly justify. Use an episode that's real but obscure enough that most of them won't already know how it went -- suggest one or two candidates and tell me why they work. I want concrete rules, timing, and worksheets/questionnaires I can print. Then a debrief that clearly separates the two ideas: hindsight bias as a memory/knowledge-contamination effect versus the narrative fallacy as the urge to compress messy contingency into a tidy causal story. Finally, be honest about what a 80-minute classroom version can't reproduce (e.g. the emotional distance of real historical distance, or the months/years over which real analysts sat with their uncertainty), and what I'll have to just assert in the debrief.
