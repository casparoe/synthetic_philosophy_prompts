I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on personal identity and medical ethics, and to think carefully before answering rather than reaching for the first label that fits.

Some background to my question. My mother has advanced dementia. Years ago she signed an advance directive refusing life-prolonging treatment, saying she did not want to be kept going in a state like this. Now she is in that state, and she seems, most days, content — she enjoys her meals, sings along to the radio, does not appear to be suffering. When I read the arguments about what ought to happen, I keep noticing something about their shape. Nearly everyone who defends honouring the directive, and nearly everyone who attacks it, ends up arguing about whether the woman in the care home is the same person as the woman who signed the document. The defenders say the real patient is the one with the values and plans, and that she would be wronged if we let her later condition speak for her; her past wishes are hers, not a stranger's. The attackers say the woman in the home is the patient now, and that past wishes are at best a bequest from someone who no longer exists. Odysseus and the mast gets mentioned a lot. What strikes me is that both sides treat that question — same person or not? — as the thing that settles the matter, as if once you have fixed the label, the ethics follows automatically. But I can imagine someone saying yes, same person, and that is exactly why her later, calmer self gets to change her mind, and someone else saying no, not the same person, and that is exactly why we should keep the promise the earlier one extracted from us. It looks as though the practical conclusions are not really flowing from the label; the disputants choose the label that fits the conclusion they arrived at anyway, and then present it as their reason. And yet I hesitate to call the whole thing empty, because the label does seem to shift the burden of proof: if the signer is a stranger to the patient, the case for the directive has to travel by some other route — promising, perhaps, or the value of living by one's commitments — whereas if they are one person, something like self-rule seems to do the job directly. I have noticed the same shape elsewhere: arguments about whether the dead can be wronged, and arguments about punishment that turn on whether someone "is" the same agent as the person who committed the crime decades earlier.

So my question is this. Is there an established concept for this kind of dispute — one framed as a substantive question whose answer is supposed to settle a practical matter, but where it appears that any practical conclusion can be attached to either answer with a bit of ingenuity, so that the real work is being done elsewhere? If there are several nearby concepts, I want to know which fits best and what the differences are, because I suspect I am conflating distinct things. And if nothing established fits, please coin a term and define it precisely enough that I can tell which cases it covers and which it does not. I am not after a bare name; I want the geography of the issue — what exactly is going on in these arguments, what is not going on, and where the edges are.

Two requests about how you answer. Please distinguish what is genuinely settled among specialists from what is contested — I have noticed that writers on this topic tend to present their preferred view as though everyone already agrees with it, and I would like to know where the actual fault lines lie. And please write in continuous prose, as you would in a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
