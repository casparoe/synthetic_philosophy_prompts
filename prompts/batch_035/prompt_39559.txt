I run a discussion section on democratic theory for undergraduates, and every term at least one student dismisses the entire unit with the line that one vote doesn't matter: no election they will ever vote in has been decided by a single ballot, and none ever will be. This term, rather than answering with a vague appeal to civic duty, I want to take the line seriously and dissect it. Please treat "one vote doesn't matter" not as a single claim but as a sentence that could be expressing several distinct theses, and lay those readings out as a menu I can bring to class.

I'd like you to cover, at minimum, the following range. Start with the near-trivial statistical reading: that the probability of any given vote being pivotal is vanishingly small. Then move to expected-utility readings that are meant to yield an irrationality verdict: ones that count the total benefit a better outcome would confer on everyone, and ones that count only the voter's own stake. For each reading, state precisely what it claims, what assumptions it needs about how to value collective outcomes and how to estimate pivotal probabilities, how sensitive the verdict is to the size of the electorate and to how close the election is predicted to be, and whether it is plausible. Then consider the stronger readings the line is sometimes used for: that voting is irrational all things considered, that voting can't be a duty, that a rational agent is permitted not to vote, and the most radical one, that the practice of mass elections is thereby discredited as a mechanism for translating individual judgments into collective ones. Say which of these follow from which weaker readings and which require additional premises. I'm especially interested in the direction of the close-election case: which readings get weaker as the election gets closer, and whether any reading gets stronger instead.

Finally, tell me which reading my student most likely has in mind, given that he used the line to justify indifference rather than to argue for abolishing elections; and, for that reading, what the sharpest precise reply would be — one that engages the arithmetic rather than sidestepping it. If some readings are true but harmless, and others would be devastating but are false or unsupported, say which are which.
