So this is embarrassingly on the nose: I got married two months ago, and my best man -- doing a philosophy PhD, the worst possible combination of traits in a person to have standing next to you at an altar -- has since got me completely twisted up about the vows. I keep going over his argument and can't find the clean exit. Here it is as he gave it to me:

(1) A promise can only bind me if I made it freely -- otherwise it's just coercion with extra steps.
(2) If I'm bound by my own will, that binding must remain an expression of my will at every moment it holds; my will now can't be overruled by my will then, because both are equally me.
(3) At the wedding I exercised my will to promise. Today I'm exercising my will to, well, feel differently about some of the specifics.
(4) If my past will has authority over my present will, then by symmetry my present will can revoke that authority whenever it likes, since it's the very same authority doing both jobs.
(5) So the promise binds only for as long as I go on wanting it to bind.
(6) But then my vows were just a forecast of my future behaviour, and it would be absurd to tell my wife that "I promise" means "I currently predict". Everyone present, including her, understood something stronger.
(7) So either promises don't bind in the strong sense everyone took my vows in, or one of (1)-(4) is false.

For the record I'm not planning to invoke any of this, I'd just like to sleep at night. I'd obviously rather keep (1) and (6) and concede something in the middle -- (4) smells funny to me but I can't say why, and I genuinely can't tell whether the problem is the symmetry claim or something slippier in (2) about what "an expression of my will" means. He also claims whatever premise I drop will cost me elsewhere -- that giving up any of them has consequences for consent, contracts, or telling my mate I'll pick him up from the airport -- and I want to understand the price tag, not just the receipt.

Pretend you're a professional moral philosopher who's taught promising for years: think it through carefully, briefly run two or three candidate diagnoses past me before committing to one, then name exactly which step you'd reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs compared to the alternatives. Under 450 words, and British English please (I've had enough American spellings from the best man, who is from Ohio).
