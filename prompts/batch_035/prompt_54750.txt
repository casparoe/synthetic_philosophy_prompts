Can you write me a dialogue between a philosopher who works on weakness of will in roughly the Davidson tradition and a behavioral economist who thinks hyperbolic discounting makes the whole akrasia debate dissolve? It's for a paper I'm sketching on whether the economist's "preference reversal" machinery actually captures what philosophers meant by acting against your better judgment, so I need both sides at their best, not a warm-up act and a punchline.

The setting: they're watching a blitz chess game (five minutes per player, no increment) and arguing about one specific move. White, down to about forty seconds on the clock, grabs a hanging rook with her queen — and in the post-game interview she says she was aware, even while her hand was moving, that taking the rook allowed a forced mate, but that the win she'd get by declining the capture was at least six moves away. The philosopher wants to say this is about as clean a case of acting against one's own better judgment as you'll ever find. The economist says it's a textbook preferance reversal: "rook in hand right now" beats "checkmate six moves later" on a steeply hyperbolic curve, and all the philosophical machinery of "better judgment" just redescribes a flat discount rate.

Requirements on the two of them:

- The economist has to actually engage with the Davidsonian point that on the philosopher's view the incontinent action is still intentional and the contrary judgment isn't causally inert — the agent acts in the light of the thing she knows, which is supposed to be why akrasia is puzzling rather than just ignorance or compulsion.
- The philosopher has to actually engage with the economist's best point, which is that "better judgment" looks suspiciously like just another preference, one with a flatter discount curve, so positing a separate faculty of practical reason on top of the preferences may be double-counting.
- The philosopher's strongest card should be the timing: the information about the mating net was fully available before the move, so this isn't an intertemporal information asymmetry, and it's hard to see what the discounting curve is trading off when both the danger and the safer alternative are visible simultaneously.
- The economist should have a real response to that — e.g. that pausing to calculate the long refutation has its own immediate cost in a blitz game (clock time, cognitive fatigue), so even "seeing it now" involves trading a now against a later, and maybe the further point that a self-aware hyperbolic discounter who declines to precommit — say, by drilling the refutation beforehand so the safe move is automatic — is pursuing a sophisticated strategy, not suffering from a defect.

Have them actually get somewhere on whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal — one of them should concede something concrete by the end, not just agree to disagree.

Format: exactly five exchanges, strict alternation, each speaker's turn only a sentence or two. No narration, no scene-setting, no intro or conclusion — drop me straight into the argument mid-sentence if you can.
