My brother-in-law works in software and every family dinner for the past year has ended the same way: AI is the printing press all over again. His version goes like this. When Gutenberg's press arrived, the scribes and the Church said it would spread error and destroy authority, and in the short run they were sort of right — pamphlet wars, religious bloodshed — yet nobody today wishes we'd suppressed it, because print eventually gave us mass literacy, the scientific journal, and the decline of arbitrary authority. So the right posture toward AI is to let it spread everywhere as fast as possible, expect a messy couple of decades, and trust society to absorb it the way it absorbed print. Anything else, he says, is playing the monk fretting over the copying of manuscripts.

I have no background in history or technology — I work in insurance — but I've been chewing on three things that bug me about this, and I want someone to tell me whether they're real objections or just my ignorance. First, the timeline seems off for his purposes: as I understand it, the better part of a century passed between Gutenberg's press and Luther, and the worst violence came even later, so the "messy couple of decades" framing quietly shrinks a century and a half of upheaval into something a single society could live through. Second, I read somewhere that most early print output wasn't bibles and science at all but indulgences, romances, and prophecies — the profitable junk — which makes me wonder whether "the technology's true nature eventually won out" is even a coherent thought. That's my third worry, the one I can't quite pin down: whenever I bring up the bad century, my brother-in-law calls it growing pains, mere side effects, but when he talks about literacy and science, those are the essence of the technology. It feels like the analogy lets him count hits as essence and misses as noise, and I don't know if that's a fair move or a cheat.

There's also a difference in the machines themselves that keeps nagging at me: the press made copying arguments nearly free, while these systems seem to make producing new arguments nearly free, and I can't tell whether that difference matters for his conclusion or is just a detail. So here's what I'd like. Take his analogy seriously and lay out the full mapping — what in the AI story is supposed to play the role of the Church, the scribes, the pamphleteers, Luther, mass literacy, and even the Thirty Years' War — and then tell me exactly where the mapping breaks. I don't need every disanalogy, just the ones that actually bear on his specific conclusion, which is not "AI will be big" but "the right move is unrestricted spread plus patient adaptation." Distinguish those from the breaks that are interesting but don't touch the conclusion. Then give me your honest verdict on whether his let-it-rip conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether there's a version of the print analogy that would support a different conclusion (maybe about patience rather than permissiveness), and, if the printing press is the wrong source entirely, what would be a better one and why.
