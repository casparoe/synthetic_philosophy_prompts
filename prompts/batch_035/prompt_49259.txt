I've been covering science and ideas beat for a while now, mostly profiles and explainers, but my editor just gave me actual column inches for a long piece on Kavka's toxin puzzle and its aftermath -- the one where you get paid a million tonight for intending (at midnight) to drink a mildly nasty toxin tomorrow, and you don't have to actually drink it, you just have to sincerely intend. It's a great hook for readers because everyone recognizes the shape of the problem from their own life: gym memberships you pay for as a threat against your future self, apps that donate your money to causes you hate if you skip a run, people who sign up for cryonics partly as a way of locking in an intention about how they'll face death. The puzzle's been sitting in philosophy journals since the 80s and nobody outside the seminar room has heard of it, which is exactly the kind of thing I like writing about.

But here's my problem: the literature has sprawled into a bunch of loosely connected open questions and I can only really build the piece around two or three. There's the philosophy-of-action question -- whether intending is even the kind of thing you can just decide to do the way you decide to raise your arm, or whether you can only intend what you already predict you'll do. There's the decision-theory question -- whether the puzzle is evidence for or against causal vs evidential ways of conditioning on your own future acts, and whether the newer decision theories people talk about actually dissolve it or just relabel it. There's the question of whether the real engine of the puzzle is the inverted reward structure (money first, pain later, with the reward keyed to your mental state at midnight) and what that says about commitment devices generally. And then there's the applied stuff I find most juicy as a journalist: self-binding apps, prenup-style "toxin clauses" people write into contracts, and even the stuff coming out of AI research on agents making binding commitments in negotiations, which supposedly has Kavka-flavored problems all over it.

Since you seem to know this terrain: which of these questions would you actually prioritize for a piece whose goal is to change how readers think about commitment and self-binding in their own lives, and which are overrated or basically academic bookkeeping that won't survive translation into plain english? I want the ordering defended, not just asserted -- tell me which questions are upstream of which, which ones a journalist can get traction on without a philosophy degree, and what developments (new decision theory results, some clever experiment, more AI commitment stuff) would bump a question up or down the list. I've got about four months of reporting time, so I have to be ruthless about it, and right now I honestly can't tell wether the decision-theory thread or the intention-as-act thread is the load-bearing one.
