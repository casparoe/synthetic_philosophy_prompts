My coworker lent me a pop-sociology book about standards and coordination in tech (think: why QWERTY stuck, why we all agreed on TCP/IP, network effects, that kind of thing), and there's a chapter about the philosopher David Lewis and his idea of a "convention" that I keep coming back to. The book's summary goes roughly like this: a convention is a solution to a coordination problem, where everyone wants to match everyone else's behavior and doesn't much care which solution they end up with, like everyone driving on the same side of the road. Lewis's big innovation was requiring common knowledge, which the book glosses as everyone having actually agreed at some point, so conventions are essentially tacit contracts. It then says the arbitrariness of conventions means any convention is as good as any other, so Lewis's account can't possibly explain why some conventions survive and spread while others die out. The chapter wraps up by saying Lewis's framework is purely static and rational-choice based — for Lewis, a convention just appears fully formed — and that this gap was only filled in later by evolutionary game theorists, who basically refuted him.

I found this pretty persuasive when I read it, but I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I have no idea if this is a fair reading of Lewis or if the author is beating up a strawman to make the evolutionary stuff sound more revolutionary. A friend who took a philosophy course mentioned something about salience being a big deal in Lewis (like, how do you even get a convention started without communication if there are multiple equally good options?), and also that common knowledge for Lewis is some recursive "I know that you know that I know..." thing rather than an actual meeting where people shake hands, which made me suspicious of the "tacit contract" line. But the friend was half-remembering too.

So: how much of this summary is right? Go claim by claim and tell me what a Lewis scholar would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — especially (a) whether common knowledge really implies any kind of agreement, (b) whether arbitrariness means all conventions are equally good, and (c) whether Lewis really had nothing to say about how conventions arise and change, or whether the book is ignoring stuff he actually addressed. And finally, if you correct the summary, does the book's punchline survive — is there still some real limitation in Lewis that the evolutionary accounts of norm formation were responding to, or does the whole "they refuted him" framing fall apart?
