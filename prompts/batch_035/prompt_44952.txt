Next month I'm presenting at my department's work-in-progress seminar in philosophy of mind, defending a componential view of the badness of pain, and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, in brief: the case of pain asymbolia — patients, typically after insular or cingulate lesions, who report that they feel pain, can locate and describe it, yet are entirely undisturbed by it and may smile at pinpricks — shows that the painfulness of an episode decomposes into a sensory-discriminative component and a distinct affective-motivational component of aversiveness, and that only the latter is responsible for pain's badness. The sensation on its own carries no intrinsic disvalue. I want to argue this as a general metaphysical claim, not just a clinical curiosity: what we call the badness of pain is not a property of a quale but the output of a valuation that attaches to the sensation. I also want to extend the dissociation beyond the lesion data, arguing that the same component can come apart in reverse — there are chronic pain patients who insist their pain is not really worse in intensity than before, but who have come to dread it, and whose suffering seems clearly to outrun the sensory facts — suggesting aversiveness is at least partly independent of, and can amplify, the sensation it attaches to.

The audience is a specific and somewhat awkward mix. About half the room consists of committed intentionalists and representationalists about perceptual experience, who will want pain to be a perceptual representation of bodily damage and will smell an ad hoc epicycle in my extra affective component. Two of the senior people are welfare theorists and sympathetic hedonists: for them, pleasure and pain just are the prudential bedrock, and my thesis looks like it dissolves the one thing that makes hedonism plausible — they will not like being told that pain is not intrinsically bad. There is also a neuroscientist from the medical school who attends the seminar and has actual pain patients; she knows the lesion literature far better than I do and will pounce on any sloppy use of the clinical material. The rest are assorted and generally reasonable.

Please draft the prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I'm most likely to actually receive, ordered by how much damage they could do to the talk, each phrased roughly the way that segment of the audience would phrase it, with a short reply I could give in two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Please include, first, the epistemic worry that asymbolics' self-reports are not straightforward evidence — that the lesions may have disconnected report from evaluation rather than removing badness, so that the patients are not disclosing an intact sensation stripped of disvalue but misreporting a state they can no longer properly assess; second, the hedonist's normative objection that if aversiveness rather than the sensation itself grounds the badness, I owe an account of what makes aversiveness bad that doesn't just restate the problem one component downstream; and third, the intentionalist's complaint that I've misdescribed the dissociation because what the lesion spares is mere somatosensory detection, not pain proper. Do include the question I'm hoping nobody in that room asks. Throughout, please distinguish clearly between what is settled among people who know this literature — for instance, the basic clinical facts about asymbolia and the standard two-component functional decomposition of pain — and what is genuinely contested, such as whether asymbolia actually supports the componential metaphysics I'm claiming it does; where my position rests on a contested reading of the evidence rather than a consensus one, say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
