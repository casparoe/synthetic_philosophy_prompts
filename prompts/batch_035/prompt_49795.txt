I'm preparing a short position paper on how a rational agent should treat risk in decisions involving money, and before I circulate it I'd like to be sure the views in it actually form a coherent package. Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. For small-stakes decisions — where the amounts at play are a few percent of my wealth or less — expected monetary value is the right standard: accept any bet with positive expected value and decline any bet with negative expected value, no matter how favorable or unfavorable the odds are otherwise.

2. My utility in money is very nearly linear across any range I will ever personally encounter, from my current wealth up to the largest windfall I could realistically receive. I don't think diminishing marginal utility is doing real work at these scales, and I'm suspicious when people conjure up a concave utility curve precisely where they need one to reach a conclusion they already wanted.

3. In my actual investing I size positions by the Kelly criterion: for a favorable bet I wager a specific fraction of my bankroll, never everything, and in practice I halve that fraction for prudence. My justification is that Kelly wagers maximize the long-run growth rate of wealth and never expose you to ruin, whereas betting everything on a favorable gamble guarantees eventual bankruptcy under repetition. I'm also somewhat sympathetic to the complaint that ordinary expected-value maximization compares outcomes across an ensemble of possible agents that no single person gets to be.

4. Whether a decision occurs once or recurs is irrelevant to what's rational. A sequence of decisions should be evaluable as one composite decision, and what's rational for the composite must agree with what's rational for each part taken in sequence. I'd be embarrassed to defend a theory under which each individual bet is fine but the series is a mistake, or the reverse.

5. Declining a positive-expected-value bet is not automatically irrational. If someone offers me a double-or-nothing coin flip, weighted 60/40 in my favor, on my entire net worth, I decline without feeling that I've made an error; if the very same offer is scaled to 1% of my net worth, I take it without hesitation. Both verdicts strike me as obviously right.

Please audit this set of commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore coherence — and what would that revision cost me elsewhere? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally damning, and be explicit about which commitment you believe has to go and why the others can stay.
