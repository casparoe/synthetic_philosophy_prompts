I spent most of my career as a quantitative geneticist at an agricultural research station, estimating heritabilities and watching selection response accumulate in barley lines, and since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophy of biology — Dawkins on replicators and vehicles, Williams's case for parsimony, the Hull–Sober–Lewontin literature on what "a unit of selection" is even supposed to mean. My reading group has distilled from this a gene-selectionist argument that I initially found irresistible, and which I now suspect proves far too much. It runs as follows. (1) A unit of selection must be an entity whose frequency in a population selection can change, and an entity can only be selected if it persists across generations in recognizable copies — it must be a replicator. (2) Genes are copied with high fidelity: the allele present in a parent is present, token for token, in the offspring. (3) Organisms do not copy themselves; the offspring of a diploid sexual organism are recombined mosaics, and no individual is a copy of another. (4) Genomes are not copied either, since segregation and recombination break them up every generation. (5) So whenever selection "acts on" an organism — the faster deer escaping the wolves — what is actually being sorted is the alleles that built the deer, and organism-level talk is just a bookkeeping device for gene-frequency change. (6) Therefore the gene is the one and only unit of selection.

The trouble begins when we turn the same criterion on the gene itself, as some of our newer members insist we must. (7) With epistasis and gene interaction, an allele's phenotypic effect — and hence its contribution to its own propagation — depends on which other alleles share its genome; after recombination, the "copy" in the offspring does its causal work against a different genetic background, just as the deer's traits are reassembled rather than transmitted. If the organism is disqualified because what it does is not transmitted intact, then the allele is transmitted intact only in the base-pair sense, and whether that counts as preserving the "same" unit is precisely what step (3) denied the organism. (8) Run consistently, the argument disqualifies everything — gene, genome, organism, group — so that natural selection, taken at face value, selects nothing at all. Yet I have seen rust-resistance frequencies shift visibly in my own plots over a decade, so evolution by selection is certainly doing something.

I cannot see which step to give up, and I would most like to keep (1), since the replicator criterion is what made gene selectionism feel rigorous rather than metaphorical — though I notice that relaxing it seems to let groups, or even whole ecosystems, back in through the same door. Tell me which premise or inference I should reject, why that step looked sound when I first accepted it, and what rejecting it costs: in particular, whether the gene can survive as a unit on some weaker criterion that does not also license units I am confident do not exist, and whether rejecting (7) instead amounts to assuming away epistasis rather than answering it.
