so i'm a novelist and somewhere in drafting my current book i realized i'm basically an eternalist -- not on purpose, it just fell out of how i work. the book has this structure where you can enter at any chapter, past and future scenes sit side by side on the page, no chapter is "now" unless you happen to be reading it, and moving the bookmark is what makes a passage present, not the passage itself. a friend with a philosophy MA told me what i've reinvented is the moving spotlight theory, with my bookmark as the spotlight. i kind of love that, and somewhere along the way i started holding the real thing too: that past and future events exist tenselessly, that 1849 and 2089 are elsewhere rather than gone or not-yet.

now i want to stress-test it. You're a professional metaphysician who has spent twenty years on the philosophy of time, so think carefully before answering -- start with some brainstorming, list everything you can think of that would even mildy count against eternalism, and then rank the defeaters from fatal through serious through merely awkward. roughly what i'm fishing for:

- what would actually have to be true for eternalism to be wrong? not just presentism is also coherent, but the observations, arguments, and intuitions it stands or falls with. i take it special relativity is on my side (no privileged now, simultaneity being frame-relative and so on), so flip it for me: what physics result would hurt? would some discovered preferred frame, a Lorentz-violating experiment, actually resuscitate the presentist -- or is that too thin to lean on?
- the phenomenology of passage. the felt whoosh of time moving is exactly what my bookmark metaphor smuggles in and exactly what bare eternalism has the most trouble with. is the argument from experience a real defeater, or do eternalists just concede passage is a cognitive artifact and sleep fine? and is that concession an insulation move -- if the view keeps retreating to the whoosh being all in your head, has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that even a problem for a metaphysics of time as opposed to a physics claim?
- internal collapse risks: i've heard people argue eternalism can't state change without quietly becoming presentist, or that it makes causation mysterious (if the later events already exist, what is the earlier one even doing to them?). how bad are those really?
- which of these would a committed eternalist concede counts against their view, and which would they wave off? where do you personally think the view is genuinely exposed versus merely awkward? and be honest about whether the moving-spotlight hybrid i accidentally adopted is a stable place to stand or just a way of keeping the whoosh while paying for it in extra ontology.

and dont be diplomatic -- if you think the whole dispute is partly verbal and the two camps talk past each other, say so, but then tell me what a novelist loses or gains structurally by commiting to the eternalist picture anyway. my editor thinks the all-times-exist structure is a gimmick and i want to know if its also bad metaphysics.
