For roughly fifteen years, the decision-theory community centered on the LessWrong forums has been arguing about two thought experiments of its own devising, and the argument has barely moved. In counterfactual mugging, a perfect predictor flips a fair coin: if it lands tails, it demands $100 from you; if heads, it pays you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails world. Once the coin shows tails, updateful agents — causal and evidential decision theorists alike — refuse to pay, since the money is gone either way and no influence of theirs reaches the heads branch; updateless agents pay, on the ground that the decision procedure now at work is the same one that determined the prediction in that branch. In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer who is a perfect predictor sends her letter only if she predicted you would pay on receiving it, and the case is engineered so that, from inside it, paying looks prudent by ordinary causal and evidential lights, while updateless agents refuse: being the sort of agent who pays is precisely what got the letter sent in the first place. Critics outside the community, and a few defectors inside it, complain that the two verdicts are cherry-picked — paying the mugger while stiffing the blackmailer — and neither camp has budged. My hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about decision rules at all but about what a counterfactual is: the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about which act to perform given what you now know, the other about which policy you should have been all along. Is that right? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything — a formal result, a new distinction, a realistic embedding of the cases — could count as progress, or whether the two questions should simply be split apart and pursued separately.
