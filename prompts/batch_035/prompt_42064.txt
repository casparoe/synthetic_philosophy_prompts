I'm on the board of a small family foundation that's spending down its last $10M this year, and we've ended up in a genuinely nasty fight over which of two finalists to fund, so I need a decision memo I can circulate before our vote in two weeks. Option A is boring but proven: funding micronutrient supplementation (vitamin A and the like) through a partner NGO, which our numbers people say would very reliably avert something on the order of 5,000 under-five deaths, give or take. Option B is a three-person team working on early detection methods for engineered pandemics. Their own best estimate is maybe a 1-in-10,000 chance their work ends up mattering at all, but in the world where it does, it stops a catastrophe that could kill tens of millions — so the straightforward expected-value math puts B ahead of A by a factor of two or three, depending on how you count.

Here's where it gets philosophical and where I need the memo to actually do work. One of our board members, a retired decision theorist, says funding B is the only rational choice and that anyone who can't bite this bullet is just squeamish about tiny probabilities — he calls refusing to maximize expected value a form of self-flattery, telling yourself you're "sensible" when really you're just not multiplying. Another board member wants us to adopt a formal rule before voting: any outcome branch with probability below 1% gets discounted to zero for decision purposes, or at least severely penalized, on the grounds that our probability estimates at that range are basically made up (she points out that the 1-in-10,000 figure is the founders' own guess, not something we could ever calibrate). And a third thinks we should just split the money, though everyone agrees splitting is a way of not deciding rather than a decision.

Please draft the memo recommending one concrete course of action (fund A, fund B, split, or something else you think is better framed), with reasoning the losing board members could recognize as fair to their position — especially the strongest version of the "tiny probabilities are epistemically fake" argument versus the strongest version of "expected value is expected value." You can invent plausible details about our bylaws and the two proposals where it helps. Please keep it under 900 words and end with a line of the form "Verdict: [recommended action in a few words]" so I can extract it easily.
