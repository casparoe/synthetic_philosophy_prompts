I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been trying to get my head around Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two idealized rational agents who start from the same prior and whose posterior beliefs about some proposition become common knowledge between them cannot end up with different posteriors on that proposition. I want to understand it in the only epistemology I actually practice, which is the newsroom's. Translate the theorem and the main debate around it into the vocabulary of my trade, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: what the translation fails to carry.

The vocabulary I'm fluent in: sourcing tiers (a named official on the record, a background briefing, a single anonymous source with an obvious axe to grind), corroboration standards, embargoes, corrections policies, beats, the difference between what a reporter privately believes and what the paper will print, and the habit of checking in with a colleague who covers the same territory before you publish. I am not fluent in measure theory or Bayesian formalism beyond the bare idea that degrees of belief can be quantified.

Work through these candidate mappings one at a time, and label each as clean, distorting, or impossible:

1. Common prior. Is there anything in newsroom practice that corresponds to two reporters walking in with the same prior? My instinct is that the prior maps onto something like a shared internal ranking of which kinds of sources tend to be reliable — official denials, think-tank reports, leaked documents. Tell me whether that's a faithful rendering or a fudge that quietly replaces the theorem's assumption with a different, weaker one.
2. Information partitions. Does a reporter's network of sources function the way the theorem's private information does — evidence conditioned on, then aggregated? Where does the analogy strain? I'm thinking, for instance, of a source who tells me something partly in order to change what I write, which doesn't seem to have a formal analogue in the theorem as I understand it.
3. Common knowledge of posteriors. The theorem, as I understand it, doesn't require the two agents to share their evidence — only that each one's current belief becomes common knowledge between them. What in my world resembles that? Two reporters openly telling each other how confident they are that a story will hold up? A public corrections column? Or is even that a distortion, since newsroom exchanges of confidence are rarely truthful or common knowledge in the strict sense?
4. The conclusion. I have colleagues I deeply respect with whom I have disagreed for years about the same body of evidence. Translate the theorem's verdict on that situation honestly, without flattering my side of it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I most want. The debate around the theorem includes, at least, these strands: objections to the common prior assumption itself; the question of whether the theorem tells us anything about real human disagreement or only about an idealized case; the follow-on results about agents iteratively announcing posteriors back and forth; and the point that the whole machinery assumes the agents report their beliefs honestly rather than strategically. Which of these strands has a genuine counterpart in newsroom vocabulary, which has to be distorted to fit, and which has no expression at all? And where there is no expression, tell me which side is deficient: is my vocabulary missing something the formalism captures, or does my vocabulary contain distinctions — above all the fact that my sources and even my colleagues often have interests in what I believe — that the formalism has to idealize away?

Finally, I've drafted my own working rendering for a piece I may write: two beat reporters who, before every story, exchange a complete and truthful assessment of how likely each of their claims is to hold up, and who know that they have done so, must end up with matching assessments. Is that faithful to the theorem? If not, identify precisely where it goes wrong — I would much rather learn it now than in print.
