I cover physics and adjacent fields for a general-interest magazine, and I have two sources I trust: a particle physicist who spent years inside one of the large LHC collaborations, and a philosopher of science I often call for background when a story turns on how scientists justify their claims. In July 2012, after the CERN seminar at which the two experiments reported the Higgs result, the three of us got together, and my two sources fell into an argument about the language of the announcement. I stayed out of it, and I've never been sure who had the better of it. I'll reconstruct the exchange as fairly as I can.

(1) The philosopher opened by objecting to the spokespeople's formulations at the seminar and in the accompanying press materials, where the finding was presented as an established discovery. His view was that the norm of assertion is knowledge — the position associated with Timothy Williamson, on which one is entitled to assert only what one knows — and that a single facility's result, however impressive, reported before any genuinely outside confirmation, is not yet knowledge. On his account, the assertions were norm-violating even if every word turned out true, because a public claim to a lay audience, which cannot check the evidence, should be held to the strictest standard available.

(2) The physicist replied that this conflates knowledge with certainty. Knowledge requires a reliably produced true belief, not Cartesian security; a blind analysis, pre-registered selection criteria, and significance at the five-sigma level is about as reliable as empirical knowledge ever gets. He added that the replication demand was in fact already met: ATLAS and CMS are separate detectors, separate teams, separate analyses, and both saw the same thing. By the philosopher's standard, nothing would ever be known until a second laboratory somewhere reproduced it, which no one in science actually believes.

(3) The philosopher answered that two experiments sharing one accelerator, overlapping modeling assumptions, and a single professional community are not independent in the sense that matters. He pointed to BICEP2 in 2014: that team reported a highly significant detection of primordial gravitational waves, and the claim collapsed once the galactic dust foreground was properly modeled. The lesson, he said, is that the reported significance does not capture the probability that the whole result is an artifact of a shared systematic, and the statistics can look overwhelming while the substantive claim is false. The defect in such an assertion is not captured by asking whether the speakers knew; it is captured by asking whether their belief was responsibly formed given everything that could go wrong — which suggests the operative norm is closer to justified belief, not knowledge.

(4) The physicist pushed back on the audience premise. Inside the seminar room, among peers, nobody batted an eye at the discovery language; the philosopher's complaint was that the same words become less entitled merely because laypeople are listening. But the proposition asserted is the same, and whether one is entitled to assert it should not depend on who happens to be in the room. If it did, he said, there would be no single norm of assertion at all, just an ever-shifting target, and public communication of science would become an endless exercise in hedging that informs no one.

(5) The philosopher's closing move was to accept that cost. Maybe assertion norms are context-sensitive, he said — stricter for public claims to non-experts than for shop talk among specialists — and if that means scientists must hedge more in press releases than in internal meetings, so much the better for the public. The physicist said that was an admission that the knowledge norm had failed as a description of scientific practice, and that a norm which science must constantly violate to do its work is the wrong norm.

I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange step by step. For each of the five moves, say who is right and why, and identify which candidate norm of assertion — knowledge, truth, or justified (responsibly held) belief — is actually doing the work in that step. Then address the two larger questions their argument raised: whether entitlement to assert can legitimately vary with the audience, as the philosopher's final position requires; and which of the three norms best fits what working scientists actually treat as proper on occasions like the 2012 announcement. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of my sources missed.
