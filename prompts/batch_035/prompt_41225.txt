So I've come round to the view that money pump arguments settle the diachronic question: if an agent's preferences across time are structured so that a trader who knows them could cycle the agent back to their starting option while skimming a fee at each step, the preference pattern is irrational -- full stop. The mere possibility of the pump is enough for me; I don't need the pump to ever actually happen, and I don't much care that real-world traders rarely know your preferences. What would count against this?

I'm aware of the standard escape routes -- agents who precommit and stick to the commitment even when they'd pay to revise it, the complaint that pumps presuppose a persistent agent whose later choices reveal current irrationality, and the "painful" pumps where the trader loses too. But I want an actual map of the exposure, not a list. Which of these would a committed defender of the pump argument concede as counting against it, and which would they wave away as confusing the argument's target (the preference structure) with its delivery vehicle (the trader)? Is there a surviving version of the view -- say, one that only condemns strict intransitivity and lets agents index preferences to times, or one that requires the exploit situation to be live rather than merely possible -- and if so, does that version still do real work, or has it been qualified into harmlessness? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what you'd personally find most persuasive.
