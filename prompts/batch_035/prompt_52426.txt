I review trust-and-safety flags for a company that ships a general-purpose AI assistant. Users mark a few hundred outputs a week as dishonest; I get about ten minutes per flag, and I can clear, warn, or escalate, with a product manager signing off on anything that hardens into policy. The flags don't sort neatly by the everyday concept of lying: the model lists a product's features and omits the one that matters most to this user, while saying only true things; it tells a user that their shaky business plan is sound; it tells a user who explicitly asks for reassurance that everything will work out; it puts defamatory claims in the mouth of a fictional character; and it asserts that it double-checked a calculation it has no way of checking. Write a triage procedure I can actually run on each flag: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends in a clear, and where it hands off to me or to policy because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Ground it in what philosophy actually knows about honesty and deception — the gap between lying and the wider category of inducing false belief, and the difficulty that the model arguably lacks beliefs and intentions of its own — and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. Then run it on three flags: the true-but-misleading feature list, the requested reassurance, and the unverifiable claim to have double-checked. At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like, and you should say so plainly.
