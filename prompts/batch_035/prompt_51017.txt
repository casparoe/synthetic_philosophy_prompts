My labmate has this argument that knowing about your own biases makes you irrational whenever you believe anything you care about, and its been bugging me for a week because it seems to prove way too much.

(1) It's irrational to believe p while also believing your evidence doesn't support p. He says nobody can deny this one — it's just the anti-akrasia requirement, higher-order evidence has to be able to veto first-order confidence.

(2) What your evidence supports isn't fixed by how it feels from the inside. You can be wrong about your own evidence, same as anyone else can be wrong about it.

(3) You know motivated reasoning is real — everyone knows it, it's textbook. So on any topic you personally care about, you know there's a real chance your confidence there got pumped up by something other than evidence.

(4) From the inside, a motivated belief feels exactly like a well-supported one. That's the whole trouble with it — if you could spot it happening, it wouldn't count as a bias.

(5) So on topics you care about, you can never rule out, from the inside, that your belief outruns your evidence.

(6) If you can't rule that out, then you believe there's a serious chance your evidence doesn't support p. And by (1), believing that is incompatible with rationally believing p at full confidence.

(7) So on every topic you care about, you either hold the belief at full tilt (akrasia, irrational) or discount yourself down to whatever a cold outside observer would say about you.

(8) And here's the part that got him grinning: one of the topics you care about is whether the bias research feeding step (3) is any good. So the argument saws off the branch it's sitting on — nobody can rationally be confident in the very findings it needs.

He says the moral is just that real intellectual humility means discounting yourself everywhere you care, and if that lands you in skepticism about the bias science itself, well, tough. I dont buy it — the woman who's spent her career on memory bias can presumably be rational and confident that the misinformation effect replicates. But I cant name the broken step. My gut says something slides between (5) and (6), a "serious chance that" quietly turning into a plain "belief that," but I can't tell whether that's the real flaw or whether the trouble starts back in (3), with a generic fact about people getting applied to every specific belief I happen to hold. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you?
