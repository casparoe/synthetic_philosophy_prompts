I've been repeating a summary of Adam Smith's views on empire that I picked up from a popular history podcast, and I've recently been told it's unfair to him in ways I can't identify myself. The segment's account, as I took it in, went roughly like this: in the Wealth of Nations, Smith demonstrated that the American colonies had been a net financial drain on Britain essentially from the start; the empire as a whole existed to serve a London merchant lobby through the monopoly of the colony trade; and Smith's conclusion was a clean call for Britain to let the colonies go immediately — which, the host noted, the book was well placed to make, since Smith wrote it after Britain had already lost America, so it reads as a calm vindication of the outcome. The host added that Smith attributed no value to the empire at all: no strategic value, no fiscal value, and none for the colonists themselves, whom the segment characterized as stripped bare by the monopoly. It closed by describing Smith as an anti-colonialist two centuries ahead of his time, and by observing that his economic case had been refuted a generation ago, when economic historians showed the empire had in fact turned a healthy profit for Britain.

I found this persuasive, partly because it fits what I already believed about Smith, but a friend who has studied him carefully says the segment misrepresents him in several places and refused to tell me which. Please give me a proper audit. Go claim by claim: which parts would a Smith scholar accept as fair, which are overstatements or conflations of two things Smith kept distinct, and which are simply mistaken on the facts? I'd especially like you to address three things. First, whether Smith's target was empire as such or something narrower — the summary slides from his criticism of a particular commercial arrangement into a blanket anti-imperialism, and I'd like to know whether that slide is licensed. Second, what Smith actually proposed Britain do about the colonies: I'm told that an unconditional and immediate renunciation of the colonies was not his stated recommendation, and that his writings contain a menu of other options, at least one of which sounds surprising coming from him. Third, whether the closing claim of refutation follows: even granting what later economic historians argued about the profitability of empire, does that refute the specific arguments Smith made, or does it at most bear on some of them? Finally, tell me how much of what I've been repeating survives the audit, and give me a sentence or two on what Smith's considered position on empire actually amounts to once the misrepresentations are stripped away.
