I'm taking a seminar on global history that keeps circling the question of whether the past can be told "from everywhere at once," and one phrase has now been used in it to do two opposite jobs. Early in the semester, when we discussed working with colonial archives, the professor invoked 'history is written by the victors' to justify reading sources against the grain: since the surviving documents largely reflect the perspectives of conquerors and administrators, she argued, the historian's craft partly consists in reconstructing what the archive's silences suppress. But last week, during a discussion of "decentered" and polycentric histories, a classmate used the same line to dismiss the whole enterprise. On his telling, the slogan shows that there is no neutral vantage point at all: global history is just the West writing everyone else's past under a new, friendlier name, and any narrative that claims to speak from everywhere is merely crowning new victors — so the decentering project either fails or hides its own hegemony.

I'm not satisfied with either use, but I can't yet say precisely why, because I can't pin down what the slogan actually asserts. It seems to be doing several distinct things at once: maybe a trivial sociological observation (states and conquerors produce and preserve more records), maybe a claim about distortion (victors' accounts systematically misdescribe the defeated), maybe a fully general epistemic thesis (all history is perspectival, so no account is better placed than any other), maybe a methodological maxim (privilege the losers' testimony), or maybe even a self-undermining paradox (if it's true, we could never know it, since our knowledge of who won and what happened would itself come from the victors). And some of these readings seem false as a matter of historiographic fact: plenty of influential history has been written by the defeated and the colonized, and there are odd cases like the American South, which lost a war but arguably won the ensuing memory battle.

Could you lay the plausible readings of 'history is written by the victors' out as a menu, from the most deflationary to the most radical, and for each say whether it's true, and what kind of evidence or argument would bear on it? Then tell me which readings would actually support my professor's use (that we should read archives against the grain), which would support my classmate's use (that a history told from everywhere at once is impossible or self-deceiving), and whether any single reading supports both — or whether the two of them are, despite quoting the same words, really invoking two different slogans. I'd like something I can bring to the next seminar, so please be explicit about which readings are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are false or self-undermining.
