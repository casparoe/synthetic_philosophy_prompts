For the death-and-dying unit of a first-year medical school bioethics course, I am preparing a handout: a decision tree whose purpose is to sort students into a position on when a human being is dead, based on what they say about cases rather than on which label they prefer when asked directly. The audience are future clinicians who will actually attend death determinations and organ procurement; the handout should work even for students who have never heard of the standard positions. Please build the tree from cases rather than from doctrine: every branch node should be a concrete scenario answerable by someone with no prior vocabulary, and only after the student has answered the nodes should they learn the name of the leaf they've landed on.

The tree must find its way to at least these leaves: the whole-brain criterion; the higher-brain or consciousness-based criterion; the traditional circulatory-respiratory criterion; somatic views on which death is the loss of the body's self-integrating unity as a whole, independent of the brain's role in maintaining it; and two leaves that are stances rather than criteria — first, the view that the legal definition of death is a policy line we draw for good reasons, not a biological fact we discover; second, the view that the entire criterion debate should be dissolved by abandoning the dead-donor rule and revising the ethics of transplantation outright instead.

The following cases should appear among the branch nodes, and you may add others of your own:

(a) Chronic brain death: the documented cases, collected most prominently by a neurologist who began as a defender of brain death and became its most serious critic, in which bodies declared brain dead were maintained on ventilators for months — including one that gestated a fetus to viability and cases in which a body passed through puberty or survived infections without intensive reversal of the brain-death diagnosis. Ask the student whether such a body is a living human organism, a dead one, or something their vocabulary has no name for.

(b) Donation after circulatory death: the heart has stopped, the autoresuscitation window has passed, and the family has refused resuscitation, so the loss of circulation is permanent but not strictly irreversible — the heart could be mechanically restarted if anyone chose to. Ask at which minute the patient died, and make the student's answer to whether death here depends on facts about the patient or facts about what anyone will do an explicit node.

(c) A patient in a permanent vegetative state, with a destroyed cortex but an intact brainstem who breathes, sleeps, and wakes.

(d) An anencephalic infant.

(e) At least one case of your own devising that stresses a joint the standard cases leave alone — I suspect the usual examples never force a student to choose between the death of the organism and the death of the person, so design at least one node that does.

Two structural requirements. First, a hypothesis I want the tree to test: that everyone's path through the cases is really fixed by one upstream question — whether death is something a body does or something we declare about it — and I want you either to place that question at the root, or to tell me plainly why putting it at the root would misrepresent the debate and where it belongs instead. Second, after the tree, include a short appendix flagging any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate (for instance, whether it can distinguish a higher-brain theorist from someone who merely thinks personhood, not death, tracks consciousness) and any coherent-looking combination of answers that lands nowhere, with one sentence diagnosing whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere unexplored territory. Keep the whole instrument to at most ten nodes, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
