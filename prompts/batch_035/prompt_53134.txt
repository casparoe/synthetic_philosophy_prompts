I've been in the same philosophy reading group for about three years and we have now circled back to the analytic–synthetic thing three separate times, because Quine's Two Dogmas keeps getting assigned like a bad penny. And I've noticed something that honestly bugs me more than the issue itself: nobody in this fight has changed their mind in seventy-odd years. Quine says the idea of a statement that's true purely by virtue of meaning never gets explained — his point about definitions and synonymy and interchangeability all leaning on each other in a circle is the famous move. Then a few years later Grice and Strawson fired back with basically two things: that Quine can't even state his own thesis without relying on the distinction he's rejecting, and that everybody perfectly well knows which side "all bachelors are unmarried" is on even if the messy penumbra cases are messy. And then... basically nothing. There was a revival of interest in the nineties, but as far as I can tell the same handful of moves just get re-run with newer vocabulary — implicit definition, conceptual role, whatever the term of the decade is. New bottles, old wine.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not another tour of the arguments — we know the arguments, that's the problem. What kind of disagreement is this actually? Here's my working theory, tell me if it's dumb. Both sides quietly agree on all the facts: that there are no perfectly sharp cases, that "bachelor" has fuzzy edges, that any statement can be held come what may if you're stubborn enough about adjusting other things. The real fight is over what follows from that. One side thinks a distinction without sharp cases is a distinction in name only — that's the Quinean taste. The other side thinks almost nothing has sharp cases (they'd say the same about "chair" or "alive"), so fuzziness can't be grounds for elimination, or you'd end up eliminating nearly everything, including the distinction Quine himself needs between revising a statement for empirical reasons and revising it for expedience. If I'm right, the whole thing is a fight about what it takes for a distinction to exist at all — not a fight about meaning, a fight about whether categories need sharp boundaries to be real, wearing a semantics costume. Which would be a weird thing to be stuck on for seventy years.

Is that diagnosis right, partially right, or am I missing a genuinely substantive question that both sides keep failing to state cleanly — something about how justification actually works in, say, mathematics versus physics, hiding under the semantic vocabulary? And be honest with me about whether anything could move this debate. Would a better account of vagueness do it, or does the impasse go all the way down to bedrock intuitions about what a "fact of meaning" is even supposed to be, in which case what would count as progress here — a distinction, a reformulated question, or just admitting the thing is ill-posed?
