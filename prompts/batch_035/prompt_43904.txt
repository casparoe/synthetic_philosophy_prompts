My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Hempel's raven paradox. Her view is that it's a trick of language, a pun on "confirms"; mine is that Nicod's criterion plus the equivalence condition really do commit you to logging the contents of your kitchen as evidence about birds. We've agreed the fairest test is whether the paradox can be made funny without being made cheap, so I'd like you to write the following piece.

Write a satirical newsletter, roughly 450-550 words, from an amateur ornithological society that has voted to take Nicod's criterion fully seriously: since "all ravens are black" is equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," members now record every non-black non-raven they encounter — white shoes, green apples, a beige cardigan — as confirmations of the club's raven hypothesis, and the annual raven report's confidence rating climbs accordingly. Use a deadpan society-newsletter voice: committee minutes, a treasurer's note, a letter or two from members. The humor must come from getting the confirmation theory right rather than from inventing absurdities. The equivalence should do real structural work in the piece (someone should be able to explain, in-world, why the club's procedure follows from it); the club should at some point notice that no member has actually observed a raven in several years and that this hasn't slowed the project down at all; one censured member should object that her non-raven observations have nothing whatsoever to do with ravens, and the committee must answer her without cheating; and, if you can manage it, include a procedural quarrel over whether an observation counts differently depending on how the object was selected — a red pen logged while out birding versus one logged during a stationery inventory. Keep it straight-faced throughout, with no winking at the reader; a philosopher of science should find it fair to the paradox and wish it applied to their own club. Begin your reply with the headline and the piece itself, with no preface or commentary, and have the piece's opening sentence state the club's most important news outright before the minutes and letters follow.
