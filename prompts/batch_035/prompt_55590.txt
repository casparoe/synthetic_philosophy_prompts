I'm a PhD student in philosophy at a department with a strong history-and-philosophy-of-science orientation, and I need help refereeing an argument I witnessed. After last week's colloquium, two of my fellow doctoral students — Marcus, who works in formal epistemology, and Elena, a historian of early modern philosophy — spent nearly two hours at the pub arguing about whether philosophy makes progress, and they finally asked me to adjudicate, which I don't feel confident doing on my own. The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Marcus opened by claiming that philosophy obviously makes progress wherever it stays in contact with mathematics or the sciences: logic after Frege, decision theory, the causal modeling literature, and the way philosophy of mind was transformed by cognitive science. (2) Elena replied that this concedes too much: those are precisely the cases where questions got settled by becoming empirical or formal — that is, by ceasing to be philosophy — so what looks like philosophical progress is really the export of problems to other disciplines, while the questions that remain distinctively philosophical (free will, the status of normativity, the point of consciousness) show no convergence after two and a half thousand years. (3) Marcus answered that "no convergence" is false at the level of distinctions and tools rather than headline answers: hardly anyone now thinks "good" names a simple natural property discoverable the way a chemical element is, compatibilism about free will has gone from looking like a dodge to being the default position, and the field's conceptual toolkit is incomparably sharper than it was in 1850 — which is progress even if the big questions stay open. (4) Elena's rejoinder was that Marcus keeps sliding from "the field moved" to "the field improved": those shifts were driven by fashion and sociological dynamics — who trained whom, which writers a generation found exciting — and a thoroughgoing Kuhnian could describe every one of them without ever invoking a notion of getting things right; a distinction can die because someone wrote elegantly at the right moment, not because it was refuted. (5) Marcus's last word was that Elena's deflationary standard proves too much, since the same deflationary story could be told about pre-paradigm science, and that the real test of progress is not convergence but whether the field's current best theories would defeat its historical best theories in open argument — which he said they obviously would, since no modern epistemologist would lose to Descartes.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails, and justify each rating. Then identify what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether there are conceptions of progress or of philosophy's function available to each side that neither considered. Finally, tell me who comes out more right overall, and how confident you are in that verdict. Please answer in American English.
