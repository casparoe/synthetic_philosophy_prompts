I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar titled "Carving Nature: Natural Kinds in Science." I would like you to draft one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, subject to the constraints below. Please write everything in American English.

The question must concern natural kinds and whether the sciences succeed in carving nature at its joints, but it must not be answerable by reciting the standard historical narrative (Aristotelian essences, Locke and Mill on real kinds, Putnam and Kripke on Twin Earth, or Boyd's homeostatic property clusters as a rehearsed list). To force genuine philosophical reasoning rather than recall, build the entire question around the following hypothetical case, which you should present in full as part of the exam question:

For decades, clinicians have diagnosed a chronic neurological condition, "Larrian syndrome," on the basis of a stable symptom cluster, and epidemiological research on it has produced a large, coherent literature. A genomic study then shows that diagnosed patients fall into two groups with distinct underlying etiologies, and only one group responds to an existing drug. Shortly afterward, molecular work suggests that one of these two subgroups is itself heterogeneous: some of its members have the condition through one causal pathway, others through a second pathway that produces similar but not identical symptoms, with the two sets of patients interleaved in the population rather than reproductively or geographically separated. Meanwhile, an organized patient community resists the proposed subdivision, arguing that the shared lived experience of the condition is itself medically real and should anchor the category.

The exam question should ask students: is "Larrian syndrome" one natural kind, two, three, or none; what would have to be true for a principled answer to be available; and whether the answer is hostage to which science is doing the carving (molecular biology, clinical medicine, or epidemiology). The question should be answerable in roughly seventy-five minutes.

Alongside the question, please provide a model answer of about 700 words that resolves the case explicitly rather than merely cataloguing positions, and a marking scheme worth 25 points total with the following structure: five must-hit points, each with its point value and a one-line note on how students typically miss it; a further line explaining what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer in terms of the specific additional moves an A answer must contain (for instance, addressing whether the patient community's argument is relevant to natural-kind status at all, or what the interleaved heterogeneity implies about the possibility of any joint-based partition); and, finally, a short list of the two or three errors that should cost the most points (such as conflating the question of whether a kind is real with the question of whether its label should be kept, or assuming without argument that etiology is privileged over symptom profile as the individuating dimension).
