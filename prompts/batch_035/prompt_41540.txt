I'm a retired ecotoxicologist -- spent about 30 years doing pesticide risk assessments for a state agency, and now that I finally have time I've fallen down the philosophy of science rabbit hole instead of doing real work. Something keeps nagging at me. All through the 90s and 2000s, whenever our advisory panel wanted to restrict a chemical on thin evidence, someone would invoke the precautionary principle, usually via the Wingspread statement: "When an activity raises threats of harm to human health or the environment, precautionary measures should be taken even if some cause and effect relationships are not fully established scientifically." We all nodded along. But looking back, I don't think the people nodding actually agreed with each other. The toxicologists seemed to mean something like shifting the burden of proof onto the proponent, the NGO reps on the panel meant something closer to "if in doubt, ban," and one lawyer seemed to use it to mean only that uncertainty breaks ties in favor of the stricter option -- which, now that I think about it, sounds like a claim about the shape of our loss function rather than a decision rule at all. And I've also noticed the principle somehow never got applied to the replacement chemicals we waved through, or to the precautionary measures themselves (a ban is an "activity" too, last I checked). So help me pull that sentence apart: what are the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the trivially true to the genuinely radical, and for each one, is it defensible, and would it actually license the stuff we did with it? Lay the readings out as a menu I can chew on, with your verdicts on each, and tell me which reading is doing the real work when the principle gets invoked against a new pesticide but never against banning it.
