I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of a decade, and the post that rearranged my intuitions more than almost anything else was the whole cluster of discussion around Amartya Sen's work on famines: the observation that many of the great famines happened with no aggregate food shortage at all, that grain was often being exported out of the famine region while people starved in it, and that the real story was one of entitlement failure — people whose wages or rights to food collapsed, not food that vanished. My favorite version of the point is the political one: famines look less like hurricanes and more like policy failures with a very forgiving public-relations strategy, in which "natural disaster" is the label that does the exculpatory work. Some of us in a reading group have been arguing about whether the "famine as act of God" framing is the single most load-bearing misclassification in political discourse, up there with calling an epidemic an act of weather.

What I'd like from you is a piece of sustained satire in the form of a bureaucratic determination letter: a fictional national Disaster Classification Board (or similarly named agency, your choice) issuing its official finding on whether an ongoing famine qualifies as a "Natural Disaster" under a relief statute that compensates victims of natural disasters but not of "man-made events subject to political responsibility." The voice should be deadpan officialese — findings of fact, enumerated criteria, a conclusion — around 600 to 700 words. The humor has to come from the actual mechanics of famines rather than generic bureaucrat jokes: the letter should solemnly note, as exculpatory evidence of naturalness, things like record grain exports during the period in question, warehouses that stayed full, wages that failed to rise with food prices, and the suspicious regularity with which the famine politely spared those whose entitlements were secure. Somewhere in the letter, an economist character (invented, not a real person) should be given a box or an annex to raise the entitlement argument, and the Board should dismiss it with exactly the kind of technicality that makes the reader feel the agency understands the objection perfectly well and is not confused, merely committed.

Two constraints I care about: the satire should be fair enough that someone who actually knows the development economics would recognize the piece as landing Sen's point rather than garbling it, and the irony should stay legible without winking — no authorial nudging, no exclamation points, nothing but the Board's own impeccable reasoning revealing it. Aim for the register where the reader's dawning discomfort is the punchline.
