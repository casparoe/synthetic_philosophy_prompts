In my philosophy of science reading group we keep circling the claim that the sciences discover natural kinds rather than invent convenient categories. One member defends it with an analogy that I suspect is quietly carrying her whole argument: classifying, she says, is like a butcher carving an animal at its joints. The joints are there before the butcher arrives; the skilled butcher cuts along them, while the clumsy one hacks across the bone and makes a mess; so good classifications are discovered rather than decided, and when a science carves in the wrong place that is a genuine mistake, not a mere change of convention — as she puts it, phlogiston was a cut across the joint, and one day the category of major depressive disorder may be seen the same way. She adds that the analogy supports only realism about joints, not a fixed list of kinds: a butcher can be wrong about an unfamiliar animal, and splitting one joint into two cuts (as with a loin) is doing something different from hacking, which she takes to cover biologists who multiply species concepts.

I want this analogy audited rather than accepted or waved away. Please provide:

1. An explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the animal, the joints, the knife, the skilled butcher, the clumsy one, and the mess a bad cut makes — filled in with real classificatory episodes where possible (chemical elements, the species problem, the Pluto reclassification, the DSM, the definition of temperature as a measurable quantity).

2. The points where the mapping breaks, with a distinction between disanalogies that threaten her conclusion and those that don't. In particular, I want your view on cases where the category seems partly manufactured at the same time as it is discovered — temperature as a quantity fixed by the instruments and procedures used to measure it, or kinds defined by the very tests used to detect them. Are these counterexamples to the analogy, or, as she would insist, just instances of clumsy or refined carving?

3. A verdict on whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the analogy establish realism about natural kinds, merely illustrate it, or simply restate it — and if what counts as a joint is itself what's in dispute, is the analogy smuggling in the very conclusion it was supposed to support?

4. If one exists, a better analogy for classificatory practice in the sciences — one that preserves what is right in hers without importing its commitments — together with a sentence on what that replacement analogy predicts about a live dispute, such as whether depressive disorders will turn out to be one kind or several.
