Two of the people in my department have been at each other for about three years now, and they've finally agreed to do an actual adversarial collaboration. One of them is basically a modernist in the Hobsbawm-and-Anderson mold: nations as we know them are a product of the last couple centuries, and the older a national tradition claims to be, the more suspicious we should be of it. The other is more of a perennialist or ethnosymbolist in Anthony Smith's direction: she thinks modern nations built on genuine pre-modern ethnic cores, and that the "everything was invented in the 1800s" story wildly oversells how much you can conjure a people out of nothing. They asked me, as the neutral one, to draft the joint statement they'd both sign at the end. Write that document for me.

Some things I want in it. First, the claims both can actually sign — and I want these to be real commitments, not pablum. I'd expect, for instance, that they can both agree the clan-tartan thing is mostly a modern concoction and yet still disagree about what that shows, so the agreed claims need to be worded so that signing them doesn't already concede the other side's thesis. Second, the precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording — e.g. the modernist needs a formulation of "national consciousness requires mass communication" that he'd actually sign, and she needs a formulation of "nations rest on pre-modern ethnic cores" that she'd sign, and neither formulation can be a strawman of the other. Third, the cruxes: I suspect one is whether mass literacy and print were strictly necessary for national consciousness or just accelerated something that was already there, and another is whether a pre-modern identity built on shared myth of descent counts as national or merely ethnic or religious — and I want them to say explicitly which of their disagreements is substantive and which is just a definitional fight over the word "nation." If some of it turns out to be verbal, they should say which part and why, since both would rather concede a word than a fact.

For each substantive crux, I want a case or kind of evidence both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Make up a fictional test case if that helps — something like a Ruritania with a medieval chronicle full of origin myths, a vernacular bible in the 1700s, railways and cheap newspapers in the 1830s, compulsory schooling in the 1860s, and a national anthem written in 1902 by a government committee — and have each side say which of those developments they think made the inhabitants actually national, and what they'd count as evidence either way. Another wrinkle they keep tripping on and should address: cases where the inventors of a tradition sincerely believed in its antiquity, and cases where an invented tradition fails to take root and gets dropped after a generation. What does each side think stickiness and failure show?

One formatting thing: I know joint statements usually come with numbered lists and a big disagreements table, but I want this as plain continuous prose, like a letter both of them could actually put their names under — no headings, no bullets, no tables, nothing bolded. They can still number the agreed claims in the running text if they want, just keep it flowing like prose. And dont have them end with a fake handshake reconciliation — the whole point is that the residual disagreement comes out sharper, not blurrier.
