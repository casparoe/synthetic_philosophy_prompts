I spent the better part of my career in agricultural research, estimating heritabilities for yield and disease-resistance traits in field trials, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in earnest. I have now been invited to lead a single ninety-minute session for a mixed graduate group: philosophers of science with little quantitative background, plus a few psychology students who, I am warned, will arrive equating "heritable" with "genetically fixed." My topic is what heritability does and does not mean.

I should be clear about what I am asking for: not the lecture itself, but a plan for it, in five parts.

First, an ordering of the components with the dependencies made explicit. My candidate sequence, natural to a breeder, is: (i) heritability as a ratio of variances, and therefore a property of a population in a particular distribution of environments; (ii) the distinction between broad-sense and narrow-sense heritability; (iii) the breeder's equation, where the concept does its work; (iv) the human case and the twin design; and only then (v) the fallacies. But I suspect this ordering is wrong for this audience, and I want your considered verdict, including whether the sequence should instead begin from the controversies the students arrive with and work backward to the statistics.

Second, the choice of carrying example. In less formal settings I have used something like Lewontin's two-field illustration — one batch of seed sown in a well-fertilized field and a starved one, genotype explaining much of the variation within each field and none of it between them. Should that carry the whole session, or does the phenylketonuria case — fully heritable, yet fully treatable by diet — do more work? Tell me which should carry it, at what point in the sequence it should be deployed, and what the chosen example must have and must lack.

Third, a misconception map. The ones I anticipate: heritability read as a fact about individuals ("this person's height is eighty percent genetic"); high heritability taken to imply immutability; heritability within a population taken to license claims about the basis of differences between populations; and the assumption that a twin-study figure is a stable property of the trait rather than of the sample and its circumstances. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off, and which one is likeliest to survive the defusal anyway.

Fourth, a closing comprehension check — one question whose answer would show the students have grasped the locality of the estimate. My rough idea is a scenario in which a breeder raises the heritability of milk yield without altering a single gene, and the students must say what changed instead. Improve this or replace it.

Fifth, what to openly refuse to cover at this level — I assume the biometrical derivations, the parameterization of gene–environment correlation, and the dispute over missing heritability — and how to say so without condescension.

Finally, a request about the status of your advice rather than its content. Mark for me where the plan is most likely to fail: the point in the sequence where a defusal is most fragile, and the misconception likeliest to reassert itself after the session ends. And tell me what would change your mind about the central verdicts: which consideration, if it held, would lead you to reverse your recommendation on where the sequence should start, or on which example should carry the session. I would rather have honest notice of the load-bearing points and their vulnerabilities than confident advice sustained throughout.
