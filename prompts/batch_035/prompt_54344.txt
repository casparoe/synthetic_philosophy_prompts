I've been asked to write a short position piece for a workshop on practical rationality — the audience is a mix of management scholars and philosophers, and the assigned topic is whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational. Before drafting it I want to know whether the views I've drifted into over the years actually form a consistent package, and I suspect I'm too close to them to tell. Here is the set. (1) I hold the standard forward-looking principle: when deciding what to do next, only considerations about what happens from now on can count in favor of an option; resources already spent are gone, and citing them as a reason is the paradigm of irrational choice. (2) I believe an agent's record of finishing what she starts is a genuine asset — people who can credibly commit themselves get better projects, better partners, better terms — and that the only way to acquire such a record is to sometimes carry a project past the point where the forward-looking case for it has gone negative; so I endorse finishing things in order to prove, to others and to one's future self, that one is a finisher. (3) I think one's own past investment in a project is genuine evidence about its value and about one's fit for it, since one's earlier choices to keep investing filtered information over time; on this basis, saying that one has put ten years into something can be a legitimate probabilistic consideration rather than a fallacy. (4) I think there are cases where the past generates reasons all by itself: if I promise a dying friend that I will keep her project going, I am obligated to give real weight to that promise even in a scenario where no one will ever know whether I kept it and where the project is doomed regardless — the history itself binds. (5) And yet, when my brother says the ten years he has sunk into his failing restaurant justify keeping it open, I tell him he is making the textbook sunk-cost mistake, and I have never doubted that I am right to say so.

What I want is an audit of this package, pair by pair where it matters: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest repair would cost me in each case. I'm particularly unsure whether (2) and (3) are loyal extensions of (1) or quiet betrayals of it — whether finishing something to protect one's credibility, or weighing one's own past investment as evidence, are forward-looking reasons wearing a backward-looking costume or the fallacy reentering through the back door — and whether my confidence about my brother in (5) survives (3), since his ten years of investment seem to be exactly the kind of self-filtered evidence I claim to take seriously elsewhere.

Please be committed rather than even-handed: rank the tensions by severity, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and if you think nothing has to go, say so plainly and state explicitly which reading of (1) makes the other four consistent with it — do not hedge by laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and no restating my list back at me before you get to work.
