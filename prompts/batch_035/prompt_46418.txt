I'm building a set of discussion prompts on fakes, forgeries and the value of originals, and I want a thought experiment with a specific structure rather than the usual perfect-duplicate cases. Those cases hold the causal history fixed: the original is the one made first AND by the artist, so they only isolate authenticity against aesthetic value. What I want is a case that splits the artist's own hand from "being the original".

The scenario should work roughly like this: a painter makes a small compositional study on canvas A, then paints the definitive full-size version on canvas B, which becomes the famous one. Years later, asked for a second version, the artist doesn't start fresh — they go back to canvas A and work the study up into a finished painting indistinguishable from B. Same hand, same materials, and arguably MORE of the artist's actual decision-making visibly layered in it, since A carries the exploratory history. Everything documented, no deception anywhere. The point of the case is to test whether the premium on originals tracks the artist's physical/causal connection to the object or something else — so a scenario that just stipulates the answer or smuggles in epistemic uncertainty fails.

Your job:

1. Write the scenario itself as one realistic paragraph (studio setting, no gallery swaps or provenance tricks — everything is above board).
2. Say which canvas the museum should treat as "the original" and what grounds that, given that a causal-history view points towards A while the "which was completed as the definitive work" story points towards B.
3. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the value gap if you remove that feature.
4. Give the best attack a defender of B's priority could make, and say whether it survives.

Please answer in British English (so "colour", "recognise" etc). Thanks!
