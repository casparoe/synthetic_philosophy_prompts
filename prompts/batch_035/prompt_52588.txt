I'm on the economic development comittee of our city council (mid-sized city, roughly 200k people, post-industrial, struggling high street) and we've got a live one coming to a vote next month. A local business association wants to launch a "civic pound" — a local currency, paper notes plus an app, where you buy in at a bonus (pay 100 real pounds, get 110 civic pounds), spendable only at participating local businesses. They're asking the council to formally endorse it, put £50k of seed funding toward the admin, and let council staff opt to recieve a slice of their salery in it. Half the committee thinks it's a nice shop-local gesture, the other half thinks it's funny money that'll fizzle out in eighteen months and embarrass us.

Before making up my mind I've been rereading Hume's political essays, and it seems like he has more to say about this than you'd expect from a guy writing in the 1750s. As I understand him: money isn't wealth, it's just the convention or counter we use to represent actual labor and commodities, so a community can't make itself richer by minting more counters; if half the money in a country vanished overnight, you'd get a painful adjustment and then prices and wages adapt and the country ends up in basically the same position as before, because what matters is people's habits of industry and frugality, not the quantity of coin; and interest tracks those habits, not the money supply. He's also pretty down on the notion that any scheme can enlarge trade beyond what the underlying industry supports — the tokens follow the trade, not the other way round.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a clear bottom line — endorse, reject, or endorse with conditions — with concrete terms (what we fund, what we don't, what has to happen before the salary option goes live, that kind of thing), and it should take Hume seriously on both sides. Where would he say the 10% purchase bonus is just a subsidy that can't create wealth, since the city's labor and commodities are what they are? And where might he actually go along with it — e.g. if the scheme works less as money and more as a device for changing habits, keeping circulation local, or steering demand toward local industry, that seems like the sort of thing he thought actually mattered. Please also deal honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side: the boosters keep repeating that shop-local keeps money circulating here instead of leaking out to chains and online retailers, and I want the memo to answer that, not sneer at it. And if there are points where Hume's framework just doesn't fit a modern situation — fractional reserve banking, digital payments, the fact that this "currency" is really a voucher system with a loyalty bonus — flag those honestly instead of stretching him to cover them.
