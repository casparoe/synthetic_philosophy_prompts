I am a novelist working on a book set at a small residential colloquium in a villa outside Florence, and I need your help with the interview that anchors the book's central chapter.

Here is the situation. The year is 1972. My protagonist, a young historian, has been given the task of publicly questioning a visiting scholar -- an aging and famously irascible Italian émigré whose entire career has been devoted to the theme of fortune and virtù in Machiavelli. The scene must run about ten pages, and its dramatic engine is that my protagonist slowly realizes, over the course of the interview, that the scholar's reading of Machiavelli is also an unflinching confession about the scholar's own life: a man who rebuilt himself in exile after his early career was destroyed, and who has spent decades arguing that a whole life can be re-forged by audacity only after it has been broken by luck. I want the interview questions to carry that double weight -- textual and personal -- without either the protagonist or the reader being able to say exactly where commentary on the Prince ends and commentary on the man begins.

Since you cannot search, here is what the prompt requires you to know, all of which is standard, well-memorized territory, and which I am setting out so we are working from the same page.

First, the famous closing movement of The Prince, chapter 25: Machiavelli argues there that fortune governs about half of our affairs and leaves the other half, or nearly so, to us; he compares fortune to one of those violent rivers that, when it floods, tears up trees and buildings and carries off earth from one place to deposit it in another -- with everyone fleeing before its fury, yielding to its force, unable to place any dike or embankment against it. But, he continues, this is the character of fortune: she shows her power where no measures have been prepared to resist her, and turns her force in the directions where she knows no dikes and embankments have been built to constrain her. He then adds the comparison I want placed in the scholar's mouth, that fortune is a woman, and that she is the friend of the young, because they are less cautious, more ferocious, and command her with more audacity. And he closes the chapter with the claim that it is better to be impetuous than cautious, because fortune is a woman, and it is necessary, if you wish to keep her down, to beat her and push her; and he says, with the insouciance that has scandalized readers for four and a half centuries, that she lets herself be won more by those who are impetuous than by those who make cold advances. You can treat that whole cluster of images -- the river in flood, the dikes and embankments, the woman who yields to audacity, the preference for impetuosity over caution -- as reliably in the text, and paraphrase it freely in the scholar's answers; but do not put any of it inside quotation marks, since I cannot verify exact wording, and a novel can live perfectly well on faithful paraphrase.

Second, the other passage my novel turns on. In The Prince, chapter 8, Machiavelli distinguishes two ways of rising to power: by ability, or by wickedness. His example of the second is Agathocles of Sicily, who rose from the lowest station to become king of Syracuse, and who -- as the whole world knows, Machiavelli says -- betrayed and slaughtered his fellow citizens, seized the government through a single act of criminal treachery, and then ruled long and successfully over the very city he had wronged. And yet, having granted all of this, Machiavelli famously withholds the word that elsewhere in the book he hands out with real generosity: he cannot call it virtù, he says, to kill one's fellow citizens, to betray one's friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may win empire, but not glory. Again, paraphrase this without quotation marks, and again treat the underlying content as reliable: Agathocles is the example of criminal, treacherous rise; the act is acknowledged to work; the honorific is nonetheless denied; and this denial -- the moment Machiavelli, having told us to learn from anyone who succeeds, suddenly refuses to call a successful method by his favorite word -- is one of the genuinely unresolved cruxes in the book.

Third, the scholar's own thesis, which I am inventing for the novel, so you may quote it freely as coming from him. Call it his double-ledger reading. His claim, argued across a lifetime of essays, runs as follows: chapter 8's refusal to dignify Agathocles with the name of virtù is not, as everyone assumes, a residual pang of conventional morality that the rest of the book has outgrown. It is a bookkeeping necessity. Virtù, in Machiavelli, is not simply skill, and it is not simply audacity; it is specifically the quality that lets a man take his bearings by the break in the weather, by the moment when fortune's flood recedes. And that requires two entries in the ledger: a first half of one's life in which one is broken, in which everything one built is carried off by the water -- and a second half in which, precisely because nothing remains to lose, one can be impetuous in the right way. The scholar's formula, which my protagonist should quote back to him at the pivot of the interview, is this: no one deserves the second half who has not been ruined by the first. His gloss: Machiavelli's famous arithmetic in chapter 25, that fortune governs half of our affairs and leaves the rest to us, is a description of a single life cut at its midpoint, not of two populations of men -- the cautious and the bold. The cautious man and the bold man are the same man, before and after the flood. Agathocles fails the test not because his methods were criminal, but because he committed the one crime Machiavelli cannot forgive: he never had a first half. He came up from nothing straight into treachery; there was no break in his weather; so the audacity that succeeded in him was not virtù answering fortune but mere ferocity, the flood imitating the dike. On the scholar's reading, this also explains why chapter 25's audacity is reserved for the young, and simultaneously for men whose youth is already behind them: they are young in the second life only.

Now, what I need from you. Please take the role of an expert in Renaissance political thought who also has a working novelist's sense of how an interview can go wrong on the page. Before drafting anything, think through, briefly and in the open, the following questions, so that the interview questions you hand me are the output of real deliberation rather than paraphrase of Machiavelli scholarship at second hand. First: at which points should my protagonist ask about the text, and at which should he ask about the scholar's life, such that the reader experiences the discovery that these are the same question? Second: the scholar must never be caught simply lecturing; each of my protagonist's questions must either genuinely corner him or invite him to say something he has never said in print -- what would a man who has argued the double-ledger reading for forty years, and lived it, be compelled to add if pressed on the parts his published work conspicuously avoids? Third: the interview needs one moment where the reading visibly fails, or the scholar evades, and the protagonist chooses tact over truth -- where should that moment sit relative to the others, and what should its content be?

Then draft the interview itself: eight to ten questions, in the order my protagonist should ask them, each followed by a note on the answer the scholar is most likely to give and, where needed, the follow-up that answer invites. At least one question early on must turn on the arithmetic of chapter 25 -- whether half and half is a real proportion, a division of a single life, or a description of two kinds of men, since this is the thesis's load-bearing wall and the reader must see it built. At least one must press on Agathocles and the withholding of the word virtù, and specifically on the scholar's claim that the withholding is bookkeeping rather than morality. One must ask about the dikes and embankments: if fortune shows her power only where nothing has been built to resist her, is the scholar saying that the ruined man's advantage is that he has nothing left to defend -- and does the protagonist's own generation, whose dikes were all built by others and never tested, therefore lack not fortune but the flood? And one, near the end, must be the question the scholar cannot have anticipated, the one my protagonist constructs out of the earlier answers, about whether a man who needs to be ruined before he can be bold ought to wish ruin on those he loves -- which is where the scholar either confesses or evades, and where I have not yet decided, and would like your view, whether the scene is stronger if he evades.

Two final constraints. The scene is set in 1972, so nothing after that date can appear; and the questions must be speakable aloud by a young man who is brilliant, intimidated, and angry -- in that order, with the anger arriving only in the second half of the interview, after he has understood what the scholar's thesis implies about his own unruined, untested life. Please do not give my protagonist any questions that a scholar could answer from a textbook; every question must be one that could only be asked in that room, on that afternoon, by that particular young man who has just read the essays and is looking at the scar.
