I teach an introductory political philosophy course (22 students, one 75-minute session), and last week a friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov — specifically about whether it can be taught by experience at all. His position was that the chapter is at bottom a warning about tyranny, and that any classroom staging of it would merely demonstrate the trivial fact that people surrender freedom when frightened, which every student already claims to believe. I took the opposite line: the chapter's real bite is that the Inquisitor never threatens anyone, every offer he makes is one that a reasonable person would accept on its own terms, and the horror is that unfreedom arrives through a sequence of individually sensible choices rather than through fear. There is also, I insisted, a second and quieter argument in the chapter that my friend kept collapsing into the first: not merely that most people cannot bear freedom, but that even those who can would be cruel to leave the weaker majority to perish with it — and that these two arguments license different complaints and different politics. We could not settle it, which is exactly why I want the classroom version.

The students will not have read the chapter — I have assigned it for the following week, so the exercise must run without naming Dostoevsky, and the reveal belongs to the debrief. Design the activity accordingly. I need rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme with stakes that are real but small and involve no money — you propose the currency, but you must defend it, because if the stakes are trivial students will trade everything for sport, and if they are too heavy the thing becomes manipulation rather than philosophy. The facilitator plays a "steward" who only ever makes offers and never coerces. The sequence of offers must include, in whatever order you judge most seductive, one that functions as bread (a benefit addressing a scarcity the game itself has deliberately created), one that functions as miracle (a guaranteed outcome, the removal of uncertainty), and one that functions as authority (the transfer of a decision the participants would each have made differently, made attractive by fatigue or by coordination failure). There must also be a phase in which a minority refuses the final offer and the majority must decide, openly, what to do with them — keep them, expel them, or force them.

For the debrief: map each concrete moment of the game onto its corresponding temptation before you name the source, and then put the two Inquisitor arguments in front of the students and ask them to identify which one they actually enacted — in my experience of these things, participants are confident it was the first and the record shows it was the second. Finally, be honest with me about the failure modes and the limits. I want to hear about strategic refusal (students who smell a lesson and decline everything in order to perform their autonomy), about cynical compliance, and about refusers playing martyr. And I want a frank statement of what no classroom game of this kind can reproduce: nobody in the room is genuinely starving or genuinely at risk, the tempter is a facilitator rather than a sincere and suffering man, and there is no silent answerer standing in the corner. Tell me how the debrief must supply those absences, because if it does not, the exercise will collapse into a show of hands about whether twenty-two-year-olds like freedom.
