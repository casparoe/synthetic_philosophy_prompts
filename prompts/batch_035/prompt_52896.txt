Okay so last friday I got into a genuinely two-hour argument with my friend Dara (math grad student, very smug) about Skolem's paradox. I'm in a philosophy of math reading group and I have to run next week's session on it, and she kept insisting the whole thing is "trivial from the inside" while I kept feeling like there's something genuinely vertiginous about the fact that a first-order theory whose whole point is to talk about uncountable infinity has, if it's consistent, a countable model. Like the theory says "there is a set bigger than the naturals" and then the universe hands you a model where everything is countable anyway and the theory is somehow satisfied. We ended up semi-agreeing that the crux is the split between what the model can see (no bijection anywhere inside it) and what we can see from outside (the model itself is countable, so there IS a bijection, just not one that lives in the model). But I fully believe if I'd explained it that way to the reading group cold, half of them would've left more confused than they started.

The group is like eight people, mixed -- a couple who've done serious logic, a couple who've only read about Cantor's diagonal argument secondhand, and the rest somewhere in between. It's a 90 minute evening session with a whiteboard. My bet is somebody says "so this proves set theory is broken" first, then "so uncountable sets don't really exist," then someone clever says "so countability is relative and Skolem was right that set-theoretic concepts have no absolute meaning," and honestly that last one is closest to where Dara and I got stuck so I'd love to know where in the sequence to take it on. Also: what should I flat-out refuse to prove at this level (the compactness stuff that gets you the countable model in the first place? something else?), and one question I can ask at the end that would tell me they actually got the inside/outside distinction rather than just nodding along.
