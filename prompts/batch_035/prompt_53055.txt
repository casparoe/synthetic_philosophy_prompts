I'm defending epistemic structural realism in a term paper, and my own argument now looks like it proves far too much. It runs as follows.

(1) The best explanation of the empirical success of our best theories is that those theories are at least approximately true. (2) Yet the history of science is a graveyard of successful theories later judged false, often about the very entities they posited — the ether went, while the Lorentz equations survived into relativity. (3) So our knowledge of the unobservable world is knowledge of structure — the formal relations — and not of the intrinsic natures of things. That was my paper's core, and I still believe it.

Then I tried applying the same reasoning to the structural claims themselves. (4) Our epistemic handle on any structural claim — an equation, a symmetry principle — comes only through its relations to the rest of the theory: its place in the axiomatization, its derivation roles, its behavior under reformulation. (5) Whatever "intrinsic character" it has over and above all that plays exactly the role that the electron's hidden nature played in step (3). (6) So by parity, we know only the structure of the structure, not the structure itself. (7) The same reasoning applies again, yielding a regress without end. (8) Either the regress terminates at some level known non-relationally — in which case I owe an account of why the first level wasn't like that — or it doesn't, in which case we know nothing at all about the unobservable world. (9) But we do know something.

My seminar partner says the culprit is (3). I can't accept that: (4)-(6) look like nothing more than the reasoning I used to derive (3), which I thought was sound. Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked fine, and say what rejecting it costs. Don't give me a menu of options — commit to one verdict and defend it.
