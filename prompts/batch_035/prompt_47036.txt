I've been part of a graduate reading group on decision theory that spent most of the term on risk-sensitive theories, and I've been asked to circulate a short position statement on where I've landed. Before I send it around, I'd like a consistency check, because I suspect I'm holding views whose motivations don't all fit together, and I honestly can't see where the friction is. Here is my package: (1) I endorse the risk-weighted expected utility framework developed by Buchak — rational preferences over acts need not be linear in probabilities, and an agent who weights the lower end of an outcome distribution more heavily, via a convex risk function, is not thereby irrational; risk sensitivity is a genuine feature of rational preference, not just a euphemism for a concave utility function. (2) I think the standard Allais preferences are rational: taking the sure payoff in the first choice and the gamble in the second, in open violation of the independence axiom, and I think a theory that convicts these preferences of irrationality is the worse for it. (3) I hold that any acceptable decision theory must respect stochastic dominance: if one act's outcome distribution is at least as good as another's in every state of the world, with a strict advantage somewhere, the agent must not choose the dominated act.

Two more commitments, which I'll confess I've never tried to square with the first three. (4) I think the Ellsberg preferences are rational as well — I would rather bet on the urn whose composition I know than the one I don't, switching away from the unknown urn no matter which color is called, and I don't believe this makes me irrational. (5) I am deeply moved by exploitability arguments: if an agent's preferences leave them open to a money pump — a sequence of individually acceptable trades that returns them to exactly their starting position at a cost — then something has gone wrong with those preferences, and a decision theory that licenses such an agent is deficient. (6) Relatedly, I am suspicious of dynamic-choice escape hatches: a decision theory should tell the agent what to do at the moment of choice, and I distrust resolutions on which the agent avoids exploitation only by binding themselves in advance to plans, or by counting counterfactual pump payments they would have refused as compensating the ones they actually make.

So: which of these six commitments pull against each other, which pairs only look to be in tension but dissolve on inspection, and what would the cheapest repair be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating every mismatch as equally damning, and for each one tell me what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list — I'm not attached to any particular member of the set, so don't spare my favorites. I'd also like to know whether my suspicion is even right that there's a real conflict somewhere, as opposed to my having misdescribed one of the positions in a way that makes it look unstable.
