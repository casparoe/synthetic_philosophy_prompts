I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on international criminal law, and we've just started the unit on atrocity crimes. Before the first session, our instructor distributed a short course-pack introduction that is supposed to give us the background on the concept of genocide. It reads, in substance, as follows: "The term 'genocide' was coined by Raphael Lemkin, a Polish-Jewish jurist who lost most of his family in the Holocaust, in his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe. Lemkin, drawing on the Nazi occupation as his model, defined genocide as the deliberate physical extermination of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, carried out with hatred of the group as such. This is the definition the United Nations adopted in the 1948 Convention, essentially unchanged. Lemkin deliberately kept the category narrow so that it would capture only the worst crime: he intentionally excluded cultural and political groups from protection, which is why the destruction of a group's language, institutions, and way of life, absent mass killing, does not count as genocide in international law. In addition, because the Convention requires an intent to destroy the group in whole, a perpetrator who targets only part of a group — say, its educated elite or its inhabitants of a single region — does not commit genocide."

I have two reasons to distrust this. First, in another course I was told that Lemkin's own conception of genocide was considerably broader than the Convention's, and that the Convention's narrower text was the product of political compromise among states rather than of Lemkin's preferences. Second, I vaguely remember that the Convention's intent requirement is phrased as intent to destroy a group "in whole or in part," which seems to contradict the last sentence of the course-pack paragraph. But the introduction was written by our instructor, who is a professional in this field, and the paragraph is mostly plausible to me.

Please audit it claim by claim. For each claim, tell me (a) what a scholar of genocide studies or an international criminal lawyer would accept as accurate, (b) what is a defensible simplification, and (c) what is a misrepresentation — in particular, I'd like to know how the actual history of the Convention negotiations bears on the claim that Lemkin "deliberately" excluded cultural and political groups, and whether the paragraph conflates the motive requirement with the intent requirement in the legal sense. Finally, please assess whether the paragraph's implied conclusion — that we should treat legal genocide and Lemkin's concept as the same thing, with the boundaries fixed by Lemkin's own choices — survives the corrections.
