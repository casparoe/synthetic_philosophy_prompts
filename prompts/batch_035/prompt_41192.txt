my wife died in march and im a novelist, which turns out to be no help at all with this. a friend lent me Hofstadter's book — the one where, after his own wife dies, he argues she goes on existing in a partial but literal sense: her pattern, her way of seeing things, still running on other brains, mostly his, at lower and lower resolution as the specific memories decay. i don't know if i believe it but i can't stop thinking about it, and i want the epigraph of my next book to be a poem that does the idea rather than states it. here's what i'm imagining: the same four or five lines come back over and over like a refrain, but each time slightly corrupted — a word swapped out, a tense slipping, a name only half-remembered — the way a file degrades everytime it's opened and re-saved by someone else. the poem should never quite decide whether the degraded copy is still her or just my grief wearing her clothes, and it should end on that question, not answer it, because honestly thats where i live now. someone who knows Hofstadter should be able to recognize the argument in the form itself, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem about memory. free verse, no rhyme, maybe 20-25 lines. and please don't make it comforting — she would have hated that.
