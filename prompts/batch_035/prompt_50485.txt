I write literary fiction with a speculative bent — three published novels, loosely a trilogy, set in a world where omens are established by the narration as infallible: nothing foretold in my fiction has ever failed, and the reader knows this. My editor now claims that after three books she can predict, before I write a word, how I will resolve any prophecy plot: which character's deliberation the narration will vindicate and which it will mock. We argued about this over dinner and she is, annoyingly, probably right. Before I plot the fourth book, I want to know whether my own record actually determines the new scene in front of me — and, if it does, what it commits me to.

Here is the record, stated as rulings. In the first book, a pilgrim stands before an oracle with a perfect record and two caskets, one sealed long ago, one open holding a modest certain prize; everyone in the book's history who took only the sealed casket found it full, everyone who took both found it empty, and the oracle has never once misjudged a pilgrim's choice. My heroine took only the sealed casket and was rewarded; the pilgrim who hedged took both, was ruined, and the narration sneered at him: never try to get the better of a perfect record, because the foresight is already a fact about you. In the second book, a seer publicly foretells that a riot will tear the city apart on a fixed day; because the prophecy is heard, the magistrates prepare, the day passes quietly, and the narration blames the elders for treating prophecy as a passive picture of the world while vindicating those who prepared — a prediction that is heard becomes a cause, and the script is fixed only through your response to reading it. In the third book, a prophetess knows a flood is coming with certainty, no one believes her, and nothing she says changes anything; the narration treats her warnings as futile but her preparations as right, and it gives the townsfolk who ignored her no excuse. So: trust perfect foresight as a fact about you; treat heard predictions as causes; treat certain foreknowledge of others' acts as settled, not arguable.

The new scene, for the fourth book, is this. My narrator meets a pale stranger in the Damascus souk, and the stranger is visibly startled to see him there. Without naming a city, the stranger tells him he will come for him tomorrow. Everything above is in force: the stranger keeps exactly one appointment, in exactly one city, and is never wrong about whom he will meet. Moreover, the surest sign of which city the appointment is in is the narrator's own conduct — if the narrator is the sort who stays, the appointment will turn out to have been in Damascus all along, so staying is fatal and flight would have saved him; if he is the sort who flees to Aleppo, the appointment is Aleppo, and staying would have saved him. So whichever plan he adopts, the moment he adopts it the other plan looks like the one that would have saved him, and the stranger's startle in Damascus is meanwhile the strongest evidence that he is expected to flee. A friend at that dinner said this is some textbook problem called Death in Damascus; ignore that — my world has its own rules, and I have to decide the scene as its author. What I want from you is not the resolution but the analysis: which rules are my three earlier rulings jointly consistent with, where exactly do those rules diverge on this scene, and what does each predict I will do — have the narrator stay, have him flee, or have the narration treat the deliberation itself as the trap? Give me your best guess at the ruling I will deliver, with your reasons, and then tell me what further plot element — a second omen, a tossed coin, a companion whose choice mirrors his, the startle reinterpreted — I could introduce that would best decide between the candidate rules, so that I can either prove my editor wrong or concede, knowingly, that she read me correctly.
