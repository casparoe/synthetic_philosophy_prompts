I teach philosophy of mind at a small liberal arts college, and this semester I am rebuilding my unit on Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater. I have read Consciousness Explained twice and find myself arguing with it in the margins both times, and I would rather not open the seminar with the standard positions lined up in their usual order. Instead, I want to open with my own verdicts on particular cases and let the theory fall out of them, so my students can see the commitments we actually bring to the debate before anyone names a position. The question I want to put to them — and now to you — is whether my verdicts form a coherent pattern, and whether the principle that captures them is compatible with the multiple drafts model or quietly requires a theater of exactly the kind Dennett wants to abolish.

Here are my verdicts. A blindsight patient who can guess the orientation of a line at well above chance while sincerely reporting that he sees nothing: not conscious. A word masked so that the subject cannot report it but which still speeds the naming of a related word: not conscious, even though something of it is clearly getting through. A stimulus the subject can freely report a second later: conscious, full stop — so far, availability to report seems to be doing all the work. But now the color phi phenomenon: two spots flash at separate positions, the first red and the second green, and the subject experiences a single moving spot that changes color midway through its journey. My verdict is that the subject really did experience the motion and the color change, and that the arrival of the second flash fixed how the earlier portion of the experience went; the cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist and near the elbow produce felt taps at points in between, strikes me as the same. And a dream recalled in vivid detail on waking: conscious while it was happening, not merely made conscious by the act of recall. Finally, the case of someone shown a stimulus who reports seeing it confidently but whose discrimination performance is at chance — I find myself wanting to say the seeing itself was not conscious, only the confidence.

Can these verdicts be captured by a single principle about when a mental content is conscious, and if so, what exactly is the principle? The trouble, as I see it, is that the dream verdict suggests consciousness can occur without concurrent availability to report, while the masking verdict suggests availability is precisely the mark; and my firm sense that the phi subject's experience was really had — that there is a fact about what it was like and about when it was like that — seems to commit me to a moment at which content is fixed and presented, which is what the multiple drafts model denies there is room for. Please state the principle, or the small set of candidates, precisely; tell me which clause is built to save which of my verdicts; and be candid about which of my verdicts no workable principle can save. Then apply the result to a case I keep going back and forth on: a patient diagnosed as being in a vegetative state whose brain scans show the right responses when asked to imagine playing tennis and to imagine walking through their home. What does the account that emerges from my verdicts predict in that case, and does the prediction match the judgment I cannot stop myself from making?
