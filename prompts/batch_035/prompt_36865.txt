For years I've read the rationalist blogs and forums, long enough that phrases like "epistemic circularity" and "the evidence is only good if the method already is" feel obvious to me — but I've recently discovered that I can't turn that instinct into anything actionable, and I now need to.

The situation: I help review member-proposed self-tracking protocols for a quantified-self community. Members regularly submit validation schemes for their instruments, and a disconcerting number of them have the shape epistemologists call the "easy knowledge" or bootstrapping problem: the method up for validation is used, quietly, to produce the very evidence offered as validating it. I can usually smell a bad case, but not reliably, and I have no articulated standard to point to when telling a member their validation doesn't count.

Please write me a review procedure I can run on a proposed validation in about twenty minutes: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a fatal answer at each question; where the procedure stops with a rejection; and which stages are genuinely irreducible judgment calls that no checklist can make for me. It should also tell me what to do about circularity that seems benign rather than vicious — calibrating one ruler against another of the same make feels fine in a way that other cases don't, and I'd like the procedure to say when and why a loop is harmless rather than just flagging every loop.

Then run it on three proposals currently in our queue:

(1) A member wants to validate a daily mood scale by asking users, after a month, whether the scale's past readings matched how they remember feeling at the time — where those memories were partly formed by reading the scale's outputs at the time.

(2) A member runs a calibration-training app and proposes to demonstrate that the training works by showing that users' scores improved — where the scores are computed by the app's own scoring rule, which is exactly what some members dispute.

(3) A member proposes validating a homemade sleep-stage classifier against the user's own report of when they fell asleep and woke, as recorded in a journal the classifier timestamps.

For at least one of these, the procedure should reach a verdict the proposing member will find unfair, and you should be upfront about that. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, the kinds of covert circularity it will miss entirely.
