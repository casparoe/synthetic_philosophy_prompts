I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized regional hospital, and every bad flu season — and certainly if we get another pandemic-scale surge — we run short of ICU beds and ventilators, and the allocation decisions land on us. I want to ground our written procedure in the doctrine of double effect, for two reasons. First, several of our committee members are devout and already reason in those terms, and the rest of us would rather have the doctrine made explicit and testable than smuggled in. Second, the standard trolley-problem literature tells me the intending/foreseeing distinction is exactly what's supposed to separate permissible from impermissible life-and-death tradeoffs, and our ICU decisions seem to be live instances of it: withholding a ventilator from a new patient, withdrawing it from a patient already on it, redirecting scarce equipment from one unit to another where the flow of patients differs, escalating opioids in a DNR patient whose breathing may be suppressed. But I can't run a philosophy seminar at three in the morning, and the procedure has to produce a documented decision in under ten minutes that survives after-action legal review.

What I need is the doctrine turned into something runnable. Please give me the ordered questions a triage officer should ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers terminate the procedure with a no, and which hand the decision back to a named human because the remaining question is one of values rather than the doctrine. Be explicit about how the procedure should classify the act itself — whether the choice in front of the officer is best described as withholding, withdrawing, or redirecting a threat, and whether that classification step is doing real work or just relabeling the hard part. The intention condition needs especially careful handling: when death is foreseen with near-certainty but not aimed at, what evidence (chart notes, the framing of the order, the existence of a palliative alternative) separates a genuine difference in intention from a rationalization? And proportionality needs a usable form even when the goods and harms are incommensurable.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Case one: the last ventilator, and a choice between an existing referral — a 74-year-old with multiple comorbidities and perhaps a 15 percent chance of surviving to discharge — and a new arrival in his thirties with much better odds. Case two: that same 74-year-old has been on the ventilator for six days, his prospects have fallen below the threshold, and the 30-year-old has just arrived. Case three: the palliative escalation of opioids in a patient who is DNR and in visible distress, where the dose needed for comfort may plausibly stop her breathing. Finally, tell me where the procedure breaks down: where double effect is simply silent on questions my committee actually faces (aggregating survival probabilities, age weighting, lotteries versus first-come-first-served), and whether the doctrine can distinguish case one from case two at all, given that some philosophers deny the withholding/withdrawal distinction is real. If the honest answer is that the procedure cannot distinguish them, say so, and tell me what the written policy should say instead — I would rather have a document that admits its limits than one that pretends the doctrine decides everything.
