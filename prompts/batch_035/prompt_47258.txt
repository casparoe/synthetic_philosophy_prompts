I chair the board of a grant-making foundation established thirty years ago by a founder who died in 2019. The trust deed commits us to broad purposes — it speaks of relieving need and expanding opportunity — and leaves nearly everything else to the trustees' discretion. The founder also left a body of letters and interview transcripts in which he explained his thinking: he favored prevention over remediation, distrusted large institutions, wanted grants to reach people directly rather than through layers of administration, and expressed views about the deserving and undeserving poor that most of the board now finds repugnant and that were, in several cases, grounded in factual claims we know to be false.

Our disagreements have stopped being about particular grants and have become about a deeper question: when the deed doesn't settle a proposal, whose judgment are we actually exercising? One faction says we should ask what the founder would say today if we could put the proposal to him. A second says we should apply his documented preferences as faithfully as museum conservators apply a restoration policy. A third — the position I find myself drifting toward — says both are wrong: we should ask what he would have concluded under conditions he never actually enjoyed: fully informed, free of his factual errors, required to make his values consistent with one another, and deliberating calmly rather than from grievance. If I understand the philosophical literature, this is roughly what is called indirect normativity: deriving what to do not directly, but from the output of an idealized deliberative process. And I understand it faces a known objection I want the procedure to confront rather than ignore: that the idealized founder becomes a screen onto which each trustee projects her own politics, so that consultation with the ideal turns out to be consultation with ourselves.

What I need is a decision procedure the board can actually run, in a meeting, on any proposal the deed doesn't settle. It should specify: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which it returns "the deed settles it, apply the deed"; and the point at which it returns "founder's intent cannot be constructed here, decide on other grounds" — and I want that final branch to be real, not decorative. Above all, it must contain a step that tests for projection: some check the trustees can run to notice when the idealized founder has started agreeing with whoever is describing him.

Then run it on three cases.

1. A proposal to fund a conditional cash transfer program administered by a large international NGO. The founder distrusted large institutions and wanted money to reach people directly, but the transfers do reach people directly, and the evaluation evidence is strong. His letters never mention cash transfers; the first major trial postdates his death.
2. A proposal to fund needle exchange. The founder would almost certainly have opposed this in writing — his letters on drug use are among the harshest things he left — but his opposition traces entirely to factual beliefs about addiction that are false, while his own most explicit principle, reducing preventable death, points the other way.
3. A case where the procedure's answer will be one I dislike: the founder's documented preferences and his idealized ones come apart in favor of a restriction I would vote against, and I want the procedure to say so anyway rather than bend.

Constraints: the board meets quarterly, and the procedure must run in fifteen minutes per proposal; no trustee has philosophy training beyond what we have picked up for this dispute; and the minutes must record which branch of the procedure decided each case, because our regulator asks how we exercised discretion.

Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: "The procedure" — the steps, numbered, each with its sufficient-answer condition stated, about 600 words; "The three cases" — the procedure run on each, showing the projection check at work, about 450 words total; "Where judgment remains" — the points at which the procedure cannot decide and a trustee must, and what the minutes should say at those points, about 200 words; "What this procedure cannot do" — an honest accounting, including whether it genuinely answers the regulator's question or merely repackages board politics in founder-shaped language, about 200 words.
