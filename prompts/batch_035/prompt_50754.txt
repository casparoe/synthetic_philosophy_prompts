I'm a high school senior taking an elective on game theory and rational choice, which is the first place I've met any of this material, so forgive me if the puzzle I'm about to describe was dissolved decades ago. Our teacher spent two weeks on what she called Dutch book arguments: the idea, as I absorbed it, is that if your degrees of belief and your preferences fail to obey the axioms of expected utility theory, then a patient bookie who knows your valuations can offer you a series of bets, each one acceptable by your own lights, that is guaranteed to leave you with less money than you started with. She presented this as an argument that rationality itself pushes you toward being an expected utility maximizer. After class, a classmate of mine decided to push the argument as far as it would go, and now neither of us can find the exit. Here is his version, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) If an agent's probabilities and preferences violate the expected utility axioms, then a bookie who knows them can get the agent to accept a sequence of bets that together leave the agent poorer with certainty. (2) An agent that anyone with enough information can drain of money for sure is thereby irrational; whatever else rationality is, it must at least include immunity to guaranteed exploitation. (3) So a rational agent must satisfy the axioms: it must carry a precise probability for every proposition, a precise utility for every outcome, and choose whichever act maximizes expected utility. (4) Therefore any agent that is not an expected utility maximizer -- one with vague credences, or incomplete preferences, or a settled habit of declining gambles -- is irrational. (5) But my grandmother declines every offer ever made to her: she keeps what she has, bets on nothing, trades nothing, and takes no bets at any odds, favorable or not; no bookie can extract a cent from her however well he knows her mind. (6) So either my grandmother is irrational for refusing all offers, which strikes me as absurd, or she is rational without being an expected utility maximizer, and my classmate's conclusion overreaches.

He says the lesson is that refusing all offers is itself a form of irrationality, since it means her preferences don't even extend to the gambles in question, and that she is simply escaping the theorem's conclusion by failing to meet its subject matter. That strikes me as too quick, but I can't say exactly why. My own suspicion is that something slips between (1) and (3) -- that the first premise, as our teacher actually stated it, doesn't quite license the third -- but I cannot name the move. Which step should be rejected, what exactly did it look plausible at the time, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I would like to know whether my grandmother shows that this whole style of argument is broken, or only that it needs an extra assumption that she, for one, declines to satisfy -- and if it's the latter, whether the assumption can be stated in a way that doesn't make it look like it was smuggled in precisely to make the theorem come out true.
