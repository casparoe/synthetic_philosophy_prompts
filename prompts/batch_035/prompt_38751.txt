I'm a machine learning engineer whose daily work is ensembles: bagging, random forests, stacking, and the general business of aggregating many noisy but individually weak predictors into one that is less wrong. I also have a political scientist friend who keeps invoking the Condorcet jury theorem — something about how if each voter is even slightly more likely than chance to pick the right option, large electorates become almost surely correct, and this is supposed to tell us something deep about democracy.

Restate that theorem and the main lines of its modern debate in the vocabulary I actually use — weak learners, ensemble size, correlated errors, diversity, calibration, bias, distribution shift, information pooling — as faithfully as you can. Assume I'm fluent; don't gloss basic terms. In particular, please cover these items as they arise, in whatever translation you find honest:

1. The competence assumption (each voter better than a coin flip against a fixed correct answer). I want to know what "competence" becomes when there is no labeled ground truth at the polling place — whether it's calibration, whether it's a bias condition on individual error rates, or something my vocabulary genuinely lacks.

2. The independence assumption, which is where my friend always gets vague. In my world, correlated errors among base learners are the main enemy, and we fight them by deliberately decorrelating the ensemble — random feature subsets, bootstrap resampling. I'm told the political equivalent of this discussion involves voters sharing the same information sources, deliberation making opinions move together, and something called the "miracle of aggregation" (the claim that a large electorate of mostly low-information voters plus a small informed minority still lands near the informed answer, because errors wash out). Tell me whether that aggregation claim is anything more than what I'd call averaging away unbiased noise, and whether the institutional devices people propose — secret ballots, insulated expert committees, staggered terms — are best understood as diversity engineering or as something else wearing that costume.

3. The swing voter's curse, at least as my friend describes it: rational voters who know others are better informed should sometimes abstain rather than vote sincerely, because their own vote conditioned on being pivotal carries adverse information. Does this have a counterpart in how I'd think about ensembles — is it akin to a member deferring to the committee, or to dropping a noisy feature at inference time — or does the conditioning-on-pivotality logic have no expression in my vocabulary at all?

Second — and this is the question I actually want answered — the translation looks suspiciously painless, and I want to know whether that's because the philosophical debate is secretly just a statistics debate that never learned the notation, or whether something real is being left behind. The leading candidate for what's left behind, as I understand it, is that the theorem is used to do normative work: to argue that majority rule doesn't just predict the truth but is entitled to obedience because of how it tracks the truth. Ensemble methods, as far as I can tell, make no analogous claim — a stacked model is more accurate, not more legitimate. So tell me: is the normative residue a sign that my vocabulary is missing something (and if so, what would I have to add to it), or a sign that the original claim was never as clear as its proponents thought?
