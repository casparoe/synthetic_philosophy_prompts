I'm writing the concluding essay for a graduate seminar on rational agency, and before I commit anything to print I'd like an audit of the package of views I've drifted into over the semester. My topic is weakness of will, and my holdings are these. First, I take akrasia to be a real and distinctive phenomenon: someone acts against their better judgment while the judgment still stands, unclouded — not out of ignorance, not under compulsion, and not because they changed their mind in the interval between judging and acting. I think this is a failure internal to practical rationality rather than a mere performance error, and I want to preserve the thought that it differs in kind from ordinary preference change. Second, I accept hyperbolic discounting as the best psychological account of the diachronic pattern: our valuations of rewards fall steeply and non-exponentially with delay, and this predicts the textbook preference reversals — preferring the larger, later reward when both options are distant, then flipping to the smaller, sooner reward as it becomes imminent. Third, I hold that exponential discounting is the uniquely rational form: any other curve yields preferences that reverse with the mere passage of time, and dynamic inconsistency of that kind is precisely what is wrong with the weak-willed agent. Fourth — and this is the one my friend, who does behavioral economics, keeps hammering — I've become convinced by the preference-construction literature that there is no context-independent fact of the matter about what an agent really wants prior to deliberation; preferences are elicitation-sensitive and are partly constructed in the choosing. Fifth, I find the intrapersonal bargaining picture very attractive: the person as a succession of temporally located selves with partially conflicting interests, willpower as the self-enforcement of personal rules, and temptation-resistance as the bundling of choices into classes so that each choice is made as though a whole string were at stake. On that picture, the procrastinating choice can be locally optimal for the self that makes it. Sixth, I take the sophisticated-choice idea from the consistent-planning literature to be right: if you know your future selves will cave, the rational present strategy is the best one given that prediction, so arranging your life around your own predicted weakness can be more rational than planning to resist. Seventh, I hold that akratic agents are fully responsible — no excuse, no diminished blame — because their action expressed what they, at that moment, most strongly wanted.

My friend says several of these can't all be true, but her objections all feel like the same one to me — that I'm simultaneously deflating and inflating akrasia — and I can't tell whether that's one problem or three. I also can't see which of the seven I'd give up if I had to, and I'd rather find out before submitting the essay than after. Please write your answer in American English.

So what I want is an audit, not a survey. Go through the seven commitments, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every friction as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the surviving package still deserves to be called a view about weakness of will rather than a view about something else.
