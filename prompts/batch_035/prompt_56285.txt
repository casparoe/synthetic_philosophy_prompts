I run a small monthly philosophy discussion group for non-specialists — people who enjoy a serious argument but have no formal training — and our next session is on when, if ever, it is rational to believe an extraordinary report on the strength of testimony alone. Rather than assigning a paper, I would like to hand out a short dialogue as the centerpiece of the session, and I would like you to write it for me.

The dialogue should be built around a specific invented case. At a stage magician's performance, a statistician in the audience — by hobby a tireless exposer of fake psychics, and about as hard-nosed as they come — is invited to test the magician's assistant. She brings her own deck of cards, still sealed, shuffles it herself, and keeps it in her hands throughout. Before a single card is turned, the assistant calls out the entire sequence, card by card, face down, with no corrections permitted — and names all fifty-two cards in exact order. The statistician reports that she checked everything checkable: she supplied the deck, the shuffle was hers, the cards never left her sight. She has no explanation. I want the dialogue to turn on a feature of this case that I find genuinely puzzling: nothing in it violates a law of nature. Every one of the astronomically many possible orderings was, in advance, equally probable; the world did not break any of its rules, it simply landed on the one ordering that had been named beforehand. Whether that difference matters, and how much, is what the two characters should fight about.

Write the exchange between two named characters of your choosing. One is a Humean: he holds that a rational person proportions belief to the evidence, that testimony for an extraordinary event must be discounted in proportion to how far the event departs from ordinary experience, and that in practice this almost always means declining to believe it. The other is a critic of that maxim: she holds that evidence is to be weighed, not amputated, and that a rule telling you in advance that no amount of testimony could ever establish a given claim is not a rule about evidence at all. Both should be equally competent — neither a strawman — and they must engage each other's actual points rather than talk past each other. In particular, the critic should press the lottery point: every lottery draw is astronomically improbable in advance, yet we rationally believe a newspaper report of the winning numbers on testimony alone; and the Humean should have a considered reply, perhaps by distinguishing events that violate laws of nature from events that are merely improbable but consistent with them, and arguing that the case for discounting testimony differs in kind, not just degree, between the two. Let the exchange end only as far as the arguments honestly allow; do not hand either side a cheap victory. Aim for roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in language a non-specialist can follow without a glossary.
