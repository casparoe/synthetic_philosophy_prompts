I am a PhD student in sociocultural anthropology, and at a recent departmental seminar on my dissertation fieldwork a colleague ended discussion of a painful initiation rite I had documented — one performed on adolescents who, as adults, overwhelmingly endorse it — with the line "who are we to judge?", delivered not as an open question but as a settled principle that forecloses evaluation altogether. A philosopher on my committee pressed me afterward on what exactly that sentence claims, and I have come to suspect it conflates several distinct theses, only some of which would actually license declining to evaluate the practice rather than merely explain why outsiders often evaluate it badly. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, and nothing before item 1 or after item 5. Each item should state one distinct precise claim the slogan could be expressing — for example a semantic thesis about whether judgments like "this is wrong" have context-independent truth conditions, an epistemic thesis about outsiders' access to the thick local meanings that make the rite intelligible, a normative thesis about toleration or non-interference, a thesis about who has standing to criticize, or a thesis about what the participants' later retrospective endorsement shows — and for each item say whether that claim is more or less trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether, if true, it would genuinely support my colleague's refusal to judge or fall short of it.
