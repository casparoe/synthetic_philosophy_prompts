Consider the proposition I'll call C: for ordinary vague predicates like 'is a heap' as used in present-day English, there is a determinate, sharp boundary — some number n of grains such that n grains determinately compose a heap and n−1 grains determinately do not — even though no one could ever know where that boundary lies. Epistemicism (in the style of Williamson) affirms C; supervaluationism, which diagnoses vagueness as semantic indecision and says truth is truth on all admissible precisifications, denies it, holding that each precisification draws a line but that meaning itself draws none.

My colleague puts his credence in C at roughly 0.75; I'm around 0.3. Please give me your own number, but the number is the least interesting part. What I want is the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each of the following, plus any further terms you think the credence should factor through:

1. That vagueness is a semantic phenomenon at all — a feature of our words or concepts — rather than of the world or of our epistemic access to an independently sharp reality. (My colleague and I agree here, so it does little work for locating our disagreement, but it does work for the number.)
2. That preserving classical logic and bivalence is a non-negotiable constraint on the semantics of vague language, in the sense that any non-classical approach either incurs equivalent costs in other coin or quietly collapses back toward epistemicism.
3. That semantic indecision genuinely precludes determinate meaning facts — that is, that it is coherent and correct to say that what a community's usage settles it is, all of it, that there is to what their predicate means, so that where usage runs out, there is nothing left for a sharp boundary to be.
4. That supervaluationism can handle higher-order vagueness — the vagueness of the admissible-precisification relation itself — without either arbitrary stipulation or a slide into treating admissibility as itself sharply bounded, which would reintroduce epistemicism one level up.
5. That the supervaluationist's artifact problem is serious enough to count against the view: on supervaluationism, the existential claim that there is a boundary between heaps and non-heaps comes out supertrue (true on every precisification) even though the view's whole point is that there is no determinate boundary — and the tradeoffs involved in trying to keep the disquotational truth schema while blocking this artifact are a genuine cost rather than a technical curiosity.

Then combine the terms into your overall credence in C, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and — most important for my purposes — identify where the disagreement between the two of us most likely lives. Relatedly: do you think the fact that the dispute appears to be empirically idle — that no possible corpus of usage data, speaker judgments, or surveys could distinguish C from its negation — should push the credence toward 0.5, or is that a confusion of evidential symmetry with credal symmetry when theoretical virtues are still doing real work? And what argument or consideration, if you encountered it, would move your number the most?
