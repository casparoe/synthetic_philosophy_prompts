Two research teams have spent eighteen months arguing about how "honesty" and "deception" should be defined for language models, and they have now agreed to draft a joint statement. Team A, the truth-conditions camp, holds that honesty is an output–world relation: a response is honest to the extent that what it literally asserts is true, and deception consists in making false assertions. Team B, the impression-management camp, holds that honesty is an output–mind relation: a response is honest to the extent that the impression it predictably creates in the user matches the model's own epistemic state, so that a technically true answer can be deceptive — paltering, exploitable implicature, true-but-incomplete answers — and a confident falsehood can fall short of being deception at all.

Draft the joint statement. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both teams can sign, covering, where possible, the classification of concrete cases — sycophantic agreement with a user's arithmetic mistake, a false "I don't know," flattery with no assertoric content, and the way what counts as a complete true answer depends on the question under discussion; (2) a disagreements table, with each row phrased so that the team holding the view would accept the wording — at minimum: the definitions themselves, whether a hallucinated citation counts as deception or as some distinct wrong, whether a literally true but predictably misleading answer (telling a patient with a five-percent survival prognosis that she has "a fighting chance") is dishonest, whether formulaic self-reports like "As an AI, I don't have preferences" are honest, and where Frankfurt-style indifference-to-truth bullshit falls inside or outside the category of deception; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether language models have belief-like states robust enough to ground Team B's definition at all, and another is whether the wrong of a misleading truth is the same wrong as the wrong of a lie — and, for each crux, a concrete study, probe, or argument both teams would accept as bearing on it, with each team's prediction recorded in advance. Finally, if part of their dispute strikes you as merely verbal — for instance, a fight over which of two distinct properties deserves the word "honesty" rather than over anything substantive — identify that part, explain why, and state exactly what would remain in dispute once the vocabulary is fixed.
