Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of commodification cases, and I'd love to know if there's a principle behind them. A stranger selling one of their kidneys to a broker, fully informed, at a fair price: I actually think that's fine, or close to it. A pharmacy tripling the price of insulin during a shortage: clearly wrong. A citizen selling their vote in an election: wrong. A poor country selling citizenship to wealthy investors: somehow less bad than vote-selling, though it feels related. A stadium offering standing-room tickets where you can pay extra to throw chairs... no wait, bad example, everyone throwing chairs is bad regardless of payment. A radio station paying listeners $100 an hour to be sterilized as a publicity stunt: wrong. A person selling their blood plasma regularly to make rent: fine, maybe even good that the option exists. Fans paying celebrities for personalized video messages: fine, a bit distasteful maybe. Parents auctioning off naming rights to their baby to a corporation: wrong, but I can't say why that's worse than the video messages. And dwarf tossing, with consent and good pay: I flip back and forth on it. My tentative thought is that something is wrong when the sale corrupts the meaning of the thing sold -- votes are about expressing political judgment, so buying them corrupts them -- but that makes the citizenship case hard, since citizenship isn't sold as a political judgment in the first place, and it makes the baby-naming case depend on some story about what names are *for*. Is there a principle (or two) that gets most of these verdicts, what does it predict for a case I haven't mentioned, like commercial surrogacy or selling the right to jump a queue at a public hospital, and which of my verdicts should I give up as just confused? Please answer in American English.
