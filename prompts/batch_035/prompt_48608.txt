Our seminar on the ethics of future generations has spent a whole term stuck on the nonidentity problem, and I have begun to suspect the impasse is not really a moral one. One side holds, following Parfit's Depletion scenario, that the people who would exist under a reckless long-term policy could not have existed at all had the policy been different, and that a life worth living is not made worse for someone merely by being caused; so if the policy is wrong, the wrongness cannot consist in a harm to any particular future person. The other side grants every one of those identity facts and still insists that we do wrong those very people, and that they, specifically, are the victims. No argument has moved anyone in months. My working hypothesis is that the standoff conceals a semantic disagreement about the word 'harm': whether harm is comparative, so that a person is harmed only if things go worse for her than they otherwise would have, or noncomparative, in the spirit of Seana Shiffrin's suggestion that causing someone to exist in foreseeably bad conditions can harm her even when no better life was available to her. Is that hypothesis right, or does a genuine moral question remain underneath — for instance a dispute over whether wrongness requires an identifiable victim, or a clash over which intuitions count as data, the interventionists' sense that 'they owe their existence to the wrong, so they can't have been wronged' cuts no moral ice, versus the other side's sense that it does? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new distinction, a test case that splits the two positions, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that the dispute is ill-posed and no progress should be expected.
