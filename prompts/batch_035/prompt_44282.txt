I am drafting a novel set among foragers on the eve of the first sowings, and my editor has seized on a framing I now fear will steer the whole book. She read Marshall Sahlins years ago and wants my hunter-gatherer characters portrayed as, in effect, Zen monks: affluence, she says, is having one's wants met, and Sahlins distinguished between the Western road to affluence, where wants are great and means limited, and what he called the Zen road, where wants are few and means adequate. So my foragers are to be written as people who want little, trust the land to provide, and spend their days in unhurried contentment, while my farmers become anxious strivers hoarding grain against failure — and the arc of the book, she argues, should follow naturally: the transition to agriculture as a fall from that serenity into toil, inequality, and worry. Before I commit to a novel full of serene foragers, I want the analogy audited rather than merely felt. What exactly maps onto what in the comparison between foragers and Zen ascetics — the renunciation, the discipline, the doctrine of wanting little, the monastery that guarantees a floor of provision? Which parts of the monk picture quietly import things foragers lack: monks renounce after tasting plenty and choose poverty as a spiritual achievement, whereas foragers, being mobile, simply cannot accumulate; their wanting little may be a constraint of the life rather than a philosophy of it. And do they even want little, given the evidence of feasts, status rivalries, and the occasional fat burial? I also want to know whether the editor's larger conclusion — that agriculture was therefore a mistake, a fall into misery — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether it depends on the monk picture doing work it cannot do. Finally, if the Zen monk is the wrong analogy for a forager's contentment, is there a better one, something that keeps what is true in Sahlins without the borrowed serenity? Please answer in continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, though do lay out the correspondences and the breaks between them plainly as you go.
