In three weeks I defend, before our department's logic-and-cognition reading group, the thesis that the Gödelian analogy running through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach is literal rather than decorative: a mind's self-awareness is the same phenomenon as a formal system's capacity to represent itself — the diagonal lemma supplies self-reference without paradox, Gödel and Tarski show that any such system must harbor a gap between what it can do and what it can say about itself, and the felt "I," at once the thinker and the thing thought about, is that gap iterated into a self-model.

The room: two logicians (a proof theorist who considers the book's mathematics fine but its psychology loose, and a model theorist), a cognitive scientist who runs a metacognition lab, a graduate student writing a dissertation sympathetic to Penrose's Gödelian argument against mechanism, and the organizer, a Kantian who suspects the "strange loop" is apperception in punning dress. Nobody in the room is a dualist; everyone has read the book.

My argument sketch: (a) sufficiently structured systems can refer to themselves without collapsing into the liar; (b) any system that represents itself must be incomplete or inconsistent about itself; (c) minds demonstrably cannot fully audit their own reasoning, yet refer to themselves stably; (d) so the best explanation is that minds carry internal self-predicates, and the "I" is their fixed point.
