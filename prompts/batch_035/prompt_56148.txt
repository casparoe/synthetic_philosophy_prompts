2. Since L is true, the disjunction "L or Q" is true, for any sentence Q whatsoever.
3. Since L is not true, and "L or Q" is true, Q is true.
4. Q was arbitrary, so every sentence is true.
5. Not every sentence is true — even the dialetheist insists that not everything is true, since trivialism would make the position vacuous.
6. Therefore some step must be abandoned, and the only honest candidate is (1): the Liar reasoning must be rejected, and with it dialetheism.

He adds a flourish: he claims there is no equivocation anywhere — "true" means the same thing in every premise, and steps (2) and (3) are not hidden conditionals but ordinary inferences (disjunction introduction and disjunctive syllogism, respectively) that we use constantly in mathematics and everyday life, so I cannot quietly claim some premise was read two ways.

I cannot find the exit. I cannot tell whether the problem is in the premises, in the inferences, or in some assumption the argument never states out loud.
