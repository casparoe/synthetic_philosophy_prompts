i write about science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and for the last few years i keep getting assigned the birth-rate panic piece — you know the genre, some new fertility number drops and everyone writes a column about civilisational decline. but the thing that actually interests me is the policy mix goverments reach for when they decide the birth rate is a problem: baby bonuses, subsidised childcare, parental leave, tax breaks per child, hungary exempting mothers of four from income tax for life, korea handing out cash for every newborn, france's entire large-family apparatus. all of it aimed at getting more people born. and the same states also pay freely for contraception and abortion, fund ivf, run adoption systems, and — this is the part I keep chewing on — restrict immigration even when they say they desperately need workers and young taxpayers. so, as a non-philosopher: what theory of population ethics does this package actually presuppose? is it the total view, where additional happy lives are good in themselves and the sum is what counts? is it person-affecting, where extra lives are at best instrumentally useful to the people already here? and what do we make of the non-identity angle — if a child exists because of a tax break, who exactly has been benefited, given the alternative was no such child at all? also the immigration bit, which seems to give the game away: if it were really about population size, importing adults is vastly cheaper than manufacturing babies, so the preference for births over migrants must smuggle in some extra premise about whose lives count or what nations are for. i'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory here — what the policy mix strictly presupposes versus what it's merely compatible with, whether the parts presuppose consistent things, and where the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival views of population value would license the same cheques. format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro and no conclusion, and please don't let 'it's complicated' be one of the five.
