Each spring I teach the aggregation unit of my graduate seminar in moral theory, and each spring it ends in the same place: the contractualists leave convinced that one person's larger burden can never be outweighed however many smaller burdens are on the other side, the utilitarians leave convinced that refusing to sum across persons is a kind of superstition, and I have no principled ground on which to tell either party they are wrong. I have concluded that what the seminar needs before it reads another word of the literature is not another theory but a specification: the list of things any adequate account of when, and how much, numbers count must deliver. I want you to write that specification. Treat it as the document I will hand out on the first day of the unit.

Set the stage as briefly as you need to, but the deliverable is the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum, the list should engage the following, which the seminar will treat as fixed points of the course design: Taurek's 1977 rescue case, where I can save one drowning swimmer or five others and must choose, together with Scanlon's much-discussed observation that his contractualism, despite its anti-aggregating reputation, saves the greater number there, on the ground that the one left behind has no complaint that outweighs any of the five's complaints under the rival principle; the contrast case in What We Owe to Each Other (1998), where a burden falling on one person is so much greater than the burdens falling on each of a great many that almost no one is willing to let the many small losses be summed against the single great one; and Parfit's challenge, where one person would suffer a pain only slightly worse than the pain each of a very large number would otherwise suffer, so that refusing to aggregate here seems to mean accepting an outcome containing vastly more total suffering in order to honor a difference between complaints that is nearly invisible. You should also say what the theory must do with a case I have been building for the seminar and cannot yet get into focus: a ladder of rescue cases in which the burden on each of the many decreases by a small step from one rung to the next while the number of the many increases, arranged so that a theory which counts numbers near the top of the ladder and refuses to count them near the bottom must cross a rung somewhere, and I do not know what could make one rung rather than its neighbor the place where the verdict flips. Tell me which of your desiderata the ladder strains most.

Beyond cases, the specification should say which distinctions an adequate theory must respect rather than collapse: interpersonal versus intrapersonal aggregation (I will trade my own year of minor discomforts against my own one great harm without controversy, so the crux is precisely what may be done with your headache and my headache jointly); the strength of a single person's claim versus the number of claimants; and ex ante versus ex post evaluation, where a lottery over who gets the last dose of a medicine can seem acceptable from the perspective of every participant before the draw yet produce the same distribution the ex post verdict condemns. For each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as the prejudice of one camp, and justify the marking. Then give me the two parts I care about most. First, tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself constitutes a result about what no theory in this area can achieve; if you think the answer turns on rejecting a requirement most theorists treat as trivially correct, say which one. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work against the familiar contenders, namely utilitarianism, Scanlon-style contractualism, prioritarianism, and the Taurek-inspired view that numbers have no significance of their own. Do not score the contenders against the list; the seminar will do that, and I want the students to arrive at it fresh. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the theories.
