I'm a high school senior writing an essay for an elective on economics and public policy, and I want to test something that has been bothering me. Almost everywhere I look, the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal gets presented as the textbook illustration of Goodhart's law, and I'm not convinced that's right, or at least not the whole story. So: write an essay on whether Goodhart's law, properly understood, actually explains the Wells Fargo scandal. Weigh the case for and against, and defend a verdict — don't end on a "it's a bit of both" note.

Since I want the essay to be self-contained, here are the facts to take as given, so that nothing turns on looking anything up: between roughly 2011 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees opened millions of accounts and issued credit cards and debit cards that customers never asked for, in order to meet daily sales quotas that a 2013 Los Angeles Times investigation described as aggressive to the point of being mathematically impossible; the bank had a long-standing strategy, inherited from Norwest under Dick Kovacevich, of selling each customer as many products as possible, at one point expressed in a push called "Going for Gr-Eight," roughly eight financial products per household; management reported cross-sell ratios to investors as evidence of deep customer relationships; when the scandal surfaced in 2016, regulators fined the bank a combined $185 million, around 5,300 employees were fired, CEO John Stumpf resigned after congressional hearings, the count of unauthorized accounts was later revised upward toward three and a half million, and the bank eventually paid a $3 billion settlement in 2020.

The crux I want addressed: Goodhart's law in its original, narrow form is about regression — once a proxy is targeted, the correlation between the proxy and the thing you actually care about quietly degrades, typically through drift in ordinary behavior. Wells Fargo looks different. Employees didn't gradually start behaving in ways that made the cross-sell number less meaningful; they fabricated the underlying records outright — enrolling customers in online banking without consent, setting PINs themselves so customers wouldn't notice, even signing up homeless people for fee-accruing products. That looks less like a measure slowly losing its meaning and more like outright fraud aimed at the measurement channel itself. So the essay should ask: does the popular, broad reading of Goodhart's law (roughly, any quantitative proxy that becomes a performance target will be gamed) earn its keep here, or is it just relabeling "fraud happens under pressure" with a famous name? Consider where Campbell's law — the companion idea about quantitative social indicators inviting corruption pressures — fits, and whether the case is better described as a principal specifying a target it had every reason to expect would be gamed: if the quotas were mathematically impossible, then perhaps the executives weren't being fooled by a decaying proxy at all, which would take the case outside Goodhart's law entirely. Then say what the episode shows about the theory itself: whether Goodhart's law needs to be split into distinct failure modes — a proxy silently decaying versus an agent attacking or fabricating the measurement — and whether cases like this should make us more or less confident that the law generalizes to things like algorithmic performance metrics and AI training objectives, where the same distinction between drift and active gaming seems to matter.

Begin the essay with its bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question, and no bibliography or citations.
