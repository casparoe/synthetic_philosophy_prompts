I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and this term I've been working on the species problem — specifically on whether the biological species concept's reliance on reproductive isolation can survive cases where interbreeding relations aren't transitive. The case I keep coming back to is the classic textbook example of a ring species: the Ensatina salamanders that spread around California's Central Valley in two arcs, with neighboring populations interbreeding all along the way, but with the terminal forms meeting at the ring's closure as reproductively isolated strangers. I find this case genuinely beautiful, and I want to close a departmental reading with a poem about it rather than a paragraph of prose. The trouble is that I can't find a form that does the philosophical work rather than just decorating it, and I suspect you can.

Here is the formal constraint I've landed on, which you should follow exactly. Write a chain poem of about eight to ten quatrains in which each stanza shares its dominant end-rhyme with the stanza that follows it — stanza one and stanza two rhyme with each other, stanza two and stanza three rhyme with each other, and so on down the chain — but the first stanza and the last stanza share no rhyme sound whatsoever. Two adjacent stanzas should sound like members of one natural kind; the first and the last should sound like they were written by strangers who never met. The reader should be able to verify this by ear, so make the shared rhymes substantial (at least two end-words per shared rhyme) rather than a single threadbare pair.

On content: the poem should track a walk (or a lineage) around the ring, with each stanza advancing one step along the arc of populations, so that the stanza-to-stanza rhyme relation enacts gene flow between neighbors. Somewhere near the closure of the loop, the terminal forms should meet and fail to recognize each other, and the rhyme scheme should already have prepared that failure: every local judgment of "same species, these two interbreed" is correct, yet the global judgment contradicts them. Resist the temptation to editorialize about species realism versus nominalism, or to name-drop alternative species concepts like the phylogenetic one — let the chain do the arguing. A philosopher of biology who knows the ring species literature should recognize the case from the poem's structure before they recognize it from any salamander mentioned by name; a poet who knows nothing about it should still hear a poem about two things that are obviously kin and obviously not.
