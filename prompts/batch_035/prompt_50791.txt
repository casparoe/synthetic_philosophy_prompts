Two of my roommates have been arguing for a week about whether you can trust introspection, and I got roped into judging because I supposedly took a philosophy of mind class once. Context: Lena came back from a 10-day silent meditation retreat convinced her introspection got way sharper — she now claims to know that her mind wanders basically 80% of the time, and that she's never really angry, just anxious. Marco, who did his psych undergrad thesis on confabulation, thinks this is hilarious. Their exchange, roughly as it went down at dinner:

(1) Marco said the choice blindness experiments — where people confidently explain and defend choices they never actually made, because the cards got secretly swapped — plus the split-brain patients who invent stories about why their left hand did what it did, show introspection failing flat on its face, so we shouldn't trust anything it tells us.

(2) Lena said those are circus cases: hypnotized subjects, brains cut in half, manipulated choices under time pressure. None of that tests ordinary reports about ordinary ongoing mental states, which is what she's making now.

(3) Marco said the reason those cases are so revealing is that introspection has no built-in error signal — you literally cannot catch yourself in the middle of a confabulation, that's what makes it a confabulation. So the failures showing up in weird cases give you no reason to think the same machinery runs clean in everyday life; the errors would just be invisible.

(4) Lena said he's also conflating two different targets: the classic studies where people misreport why they chose something are about the *causes* of behavior, and she never claimed introspection reveals causes. She's claiming access to occurrent states — what's happening in her experience right now — and none of his examples touch that.

(5) Marco said nobody, including her, actually uses introspection that narrowly — her retreat insight that she's never angry is a claim about her dispositions across weeks and situations, exactly the territory where introspection embarrasses itself. He also pointed to the studies where people carry a beeper and write down whatever was in their head the second it goes off, and they're consistantly surprised by their own samples — which means their ordinary introspective estimates were off.

(6) Lena said surprise only shows refinement, not failure — astronomers were surprised by Mercury's orbit and got better telescopes, they didn't conclude telescopes are useless. Training sharpens the instrument, that's the entire point of a retreat.

(7) Marco said fine, but then equally well-trained meditators should converge on what they find in there, and they don't — they basically each rediscover whatever map their tradition handed them in advance, which smells like theory-driven reporting dressed up as observation.

They want a verdict and I honestly can't give one. Can you go through the moves and say who's right about which specific points, where (if anywhere) they're talking past each other, and who comes out more right overall?
