Write an insurance rider -- deadpan, corporate, in the voice of a mid-size insurer's policy document -- for coverage of what the company calls "extended cognitive assets": the notebooks, phones, and cloud accounts a policyholder relies on to think with. The document should be around 700 words, formatted with a definitions section and numbered clauses covering: what qualifies an artifact for coverage under the company's trust-and-reliability criteria (constant availability, automatic endorsement, past access -- so a phone that is usually charged and always on the nightstand is covered, while the laptop you consult once a month is explicitly excluded as "merely environmental"); claim procedures when a covered asset is lost (including how the company handles the resulting gap, given that the policyholder cannot be compensated for memories she never had, only for the dispositional states she has now lost); a subrogation clause assigning responsibility when the failure is in the coupling rather than the coupled item; and an exclusions section that quietly takes positions on the real objections -- the coupling-versus-constitution problem, the worry that parity would make half the town part of your mind, and the question of whether the company is insuring part of a person or merely her property, with premium rates differing accordingly. The humor should come from getting Clark-and-Chalmers-style extended mind right rather than from caricaturing it: a philosopher working on embedded and extended cognition should recognize each clause as a compressed version of a live debate and find the treatment fair, while a lay reader should still find the fine print funny as fine print. Do not include any footnotes, citations, or references to the literature by name; the document must read as an actual rider, and all allusion must be in the clauses themselves.
