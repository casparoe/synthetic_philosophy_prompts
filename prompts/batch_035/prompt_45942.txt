My reading group is working through Kant's 1795 sketch on perpetual peace, and one member — a committed Hobbesian — closes every debate the same way. International politics, she says, simply is the state of nature writ large: each state, like each person in Hobbes, remains judge in its own cause, there is no common power to appeal to, and so the condition is one of latent war. And just as individuals escape that condition only by handing their judgment over to a sovereign through covenant, states can escape war only by instituting a world state above them. She points out that Kant seems to grant her the premise: he treats the absence of a rightful condition among states as itself a state of war, insists that a mere cessation of hostilities is not yet peace, and even suggests that the problem of organizing a commonwealth is easy enough to be solved by a nation of devils, provided they are intelligent — so that morality is not doing the interesting work. Yet Kant refuses her conclusion. Instead of a world republic with coercive authority over its members, he settles for a federation of free republics that leaves the sovereignty of each state intact, a league he seems to allow may be dissolved at any time; and he warns that a single state that had swallowed all the others would lapse into a despotism without spirit. She thinks the refusal is sentimental: once the state-of-nature diagnosis is accepted, the Hobbesian cure follows, and the federation is just the state of nature with better manners.

Please audit the analogy she is relying on. Give me exactly five numbered points and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion — with each point a sentence or two (compressed notation is fine for the mapping). (1) The correspondence: what plays the role of the individual, the natural liberty surrendered, the covenant, and the sovereign; and how Kant's federation distorts each of these. (2) The disanalogy Kant actually seems to use to stop short of a world state — what a state is, for him, that a person is not, namely a moral person with a claim not to be absorbed into another — and what that buys him. (3) The disanalogy she can safely concede: which difference between persons and states does no work for her argument, and what it fails to answer on her opponent's side. (4) Your verdict: does the world-state conclusion stand on its own once the disanalogies are on the table, or did the analogy smuggle it in — and, if the conclusion fails, which better analogy fits Kant's federation, considering his own rival images of states as trees in a forest crowding one another and thereby growing tall, and of the league as an association members may quit. (5) The self-critique: identify where your audit is most likely to go wrong — what a Kantian or a Hobbesian could press that would flip the verdict in point four, and what you would have to concede. Two constraints on the answer: do not put anything in quotation marks attributed to Kant or Hobbes — paraphrase throughout — and if you are unsure whether any claim I have reported is genuinely Kant's, say so rather than quietly shoring it up.
