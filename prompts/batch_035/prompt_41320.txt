I've just finished a graduate seminar on care ethics — we read Gilligan, Noddings, Held and Kittay — and rather than a settled view I've come away with a pile of gut verdicts on cases, some of which don't sit well together. I'd like your help doing the reflective-equilibrium work properly: tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, which of them has to go, and what the principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on. Here are the cases and my verdicts. First: a live-in carer who looks after a stroke patient six days a week — she anticipates his needs, reads his moods, does everything skilfully — and feels nothing for him; it's a job. My verdict: nothing is morally amiss, she is a good carer. Second: a hospice nurse who genuinely grieves every patient and, after eight months, is exhausted and beginning to miss things. My verdict: admirable, but not a better carer than her steadier colleague who feels less. Third: a daughter who visits her mother with dementia every Sunday out of grim duty, resenting it and going through the motions badly. My verdict: something is morally wrong here, and not merely as a matter of technical incompetence — the resentment itself seems to count against her. Fourth: I don't think my duty to my own child is reducible to her needs, since I may pay for her violin lessons rather than send the money where it would relieve far greater need, and I don't think I do wrong. Fifth: someone who gives effectively to distant strangers but never notices that their flatmate is sliding into depression has failed in a way the donations don't offset, and the failure seems to be specifically a failure of caring. Sixth: a friend refuses all help after a bereavement in order to preserve his independence, and I think turning up anyway with soup can be the genuinely caring thing — care sometimes involves refusing to accept a refusal. Seventh: a father deliberately steps back and lets his adult son's business struggle, because hovering would undermine him, and I think that can be not just permissible but better care than intervening.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the carer and the daughter: both go through the motions without warm feeling, yet I judge the daughter harshly and the carer not at all. My instinct is to reach for the relationship — intimacy demands more of the motive than a professional role does — but that clause feels ad hoc unless relationships are doing real justificatory work, which then collides with the child-favouring verdict (if relationships ground duties, do they ground them even when the relationship is rotten?). The unresolved case, then: a woman whose mother was coldly neglectful throughout her childhood now has dementia and needs looking after. Does care ethics say the daughter owes her care, because the relationship itself grounds the duty; or that she doesn't, because a relationship without any history of care generates no claim; or something more complicated, such as a duty to arrange care rather than give it?

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose from: state your principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it overturns and why that one is the right one to lose, and give a committed verdict on the abusive-mother case — including, if you like, whether arranging care rather than giving it counts as discharging whatever duty there is. I'd also like to hear what the principle predicts about a case I haven't listed, of your choosing, where it delivers an uncomfortable verdict. Please write in British English, and end your reply with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a short label for your principle (three to six words) followed by your one-line verdict on the abusive-mother case, e.g. \answer{Care as skilled practice | the daughter owes arranged care but not personal care} — with your actual labels and verdict, of course.
