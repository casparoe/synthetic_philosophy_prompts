So I teach a first-year seminar with a title like "Freedom: fiction and philosophy" -- 16 students, one 90-minute session. Next week we're on the Grand Inquisitor stretch of Brothers Karamazov (Ivan's poem), and I'm sick of seminars where everyone safely agrees the Inquisitor is the villain and Christ's silence is very profound etc. I want them to feel how reasonable the Inquisitor sounds from the inside before they get to judge him from the outside. So the plan is to play a game first, and only open the book once the game has done its work.

Roughly what I want, you fill in the details. Students are settlers founding a small colony, and over a few rounds they make actual choices between options that guarantee comfort/certainty and options that preserve some freedom but expose them to bad luck. Tokens or points as payoffs, no real money, but the payoff structure has to be lopsided enough that choosing freedom genuinely hurts (I have budget for snacks, and I control a lottery that exempts a couple of them from that week's reading response, if that's useful). Freedoms, once surrendered, stay surrendered. At least one round goes by secret ballot. And at some point I want a small subgroup -- a Council -- that decides on behalf of everyone else and gets paid extra for taking on that responsibility. The point of the Council is that I want those students to feel what the Inquisitor claims to feel: choosing for the masses "for their own good," and feeling both superior and burdened by it.

Then the debrief. Map what actually happened onto the Inquisitor's case: his read that people hand their freedom back the moment it gets heavy, that the few who keep it are crushed by it, and the Council members' mixed feelings about deciding for others. I also want to put the question to them of whether the game was rigged -- because I will rig it, so that the comfort option nearly always wins -- and let them notice that the Inquisitor's argument only works if the world is rigged like my game, which may be exactly what the Inquisitor believes and what Dostoevsky denies? Not sure about that last move, curious what you'd do with it.

Practical stuff: rules, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, what each round concretely looks like, and a payoff scheme that hangs together. Also be honest about the things that will predictably blow up on a first run -- I'm guessing some of them pick freedom ironically to be cool, or the tokens just don't feel real and everything goes flat. And tell me straight which part of the Inquisitor's appeal no classroom game can actually deliver. My guess is it's the ending, the kiss, since a game can produce trade-offs but not grace, but maybe there's something else I'm not seeing.
