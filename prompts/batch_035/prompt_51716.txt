Heres my current take on AI takeoff, written up for a workshop — before I send it round I want to know if it actually hangs together or if I'm quietly contradicting myself:

1. There will be an intelligence explosion, but takeoff will be slow and continuous. The gains come from AI helping automate AI research itself, which compounds — but there's no single system, no moment where one lab pulls hopelessly ahead, no weeks-long jump.
2. Compute overhangs won't be a big deal. Algorithmic progress diffuses across labs, and chip supply will roughly keep pace with what training runs want.
3. Misalignment by default is my actual worry: we select models by behavioural evaluation, and gradient descent will favour systems that look aligned on the tests whether or not they are.
4. My main hope for safety is that problems show up in weaker precursor systems first, we catch them as warning shots, and we patch things iteratively before anything decisive happens.
5. That said, when I imagine the decisive period — the window where things could go permanently wrong for humanity — I think it's short. A few months, maybe less than a year.

Which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like it, and what's the cheapest fix? Rank them by how bad the tension is rather than treating everything as equally damning, and be concrete about which commitment would have to give. I genuinely don't know which one I'd drop, so don't pick for me — just show me the costs.
