You are a philosopher of history and a methodologist who spends more time thinking about how historians know things than about what they know. I am preparing to write a critical essay on causal explanation in history — the kind of work that explains why the Berlin Wall fell, why the First World War broke out, or why Rome's western empire collapsed — and before I evaluate any particular historian's explanations, I want the standard of evaluation itself worked out. Write me the desiderata.

Specifically: write the list of requirements that any adequate explanation of a particular historical outcome must meet in order to count as genuinely explanatory rather than a retrospective story that merely feels right because we already know the ending. The list should include, at minimum, conditions drawn from (a) the psychology of hindsight — once people know an outcome occurred, they systematically overestimate how foreseeable it was, and an adequate historical explanation must not merely exploit that distortion; (b) the narrative fallacy — the human tendency to compress messy sequences into stories with a manageable cast, a small number of turning points, and a satisfying arc, where the story's coherence is mistaken for evidential support; and (c) the distinction between explaining and merely re-describing: an explanation that could be retold, unchanged, to "explain" the opposite outcome should be disqualified. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration — a concrete historical episode or a stylized experiment makes it vivid — and say whether it is non-negotiable or instead a preference of a particular school of historiography (analytical philosophy of history, cliometrics, traditional narrative history, the Annales school), so that I can tell genuine requirements from camp prejudices.

Two candidate desiderata I want you to weigh explicitly, and I don't know how much weight they deserve:

1. The twin-outcome test: an explanation of why X happened earns its keep only if, had X not happened, the same explanatory apparatus would have counted against X, and its author could say in advance what evidence would have pushed them toward the non-occurrence. Consider whether any actual historical practice could pass this, or whether it is jointly unsatisfiable with the requirement that explanations be intelligible and persuasive to readers who already know the outcome — in which case the tension itself is the finding.

2. Narrative economy: the suspicion that the demand that an explanation be a good story — sequential, focused, with a climax — is not a neutral virtue of historical writing but the very engine of hindsight bias, so that requiring perspicuity and requiring bias-resistance may pull against each other.

Begin with a short brainstorm of candidate desiderata before committing to the final list; I want to see which ones you considered and discarded, and why. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that joint-unsatisfiability result is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly and only briefly, note how a covering-law account, a counterfactual account, and a plain narrative account of historical explanation fare against the list — a few lines each; the criteria are where I want the thinking, and I will do the full scorecard myself. Do not cite literature or give references; argue from the concepts and the cases directly. Think carefully and take your time — the value here is in the reasoning behind each requirement, not in the length of the list.
