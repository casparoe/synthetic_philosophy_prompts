I've been puzzling over a question about my own identity for a while now, entirely on my own — I'm not a student of philosophy, I just got stuck on it one night and can't let it go. The question is whether I could have come from a different sperm and a different egg, or whether the particular gametes that made me fix who I am. Rather than argue about it in the abstract, I worked through a bunch of concrete cases and noticed what my gut says about each, and now I want to know whether there's a single principle behind my reactions or whether I'm just inconsistent. The cases: if a different sperm had fertilized the same egg, I feel strongly that the resulting person would not have been me — more like a half-brother who never got to exist. If the very same egg and sperm had formed an embryo that was frozen and implanted into a surrogate's womb ten years later, I feel it would still be me, just with a weird delay. If my parents had conceived a month earlier, using a different egg and a different sperm, I feel that would have been some other sibling, not me born earlier. If a cosmic ray had altered a single base pair in the winning sperm just before fertilization, so that I ended up with one different gene somewhere, I strangely feel it could still have been me — which sits badly with my first reaction, since strictly speaking that's a different sperm. And the case that really breaks me: when an early embryo splits into identical twins, both twins came from the very same egg and sperm, yet they are obviously two people, not one, so "same gametes" can't be the whole story of who I am — I can't even say which twin would have been me in the split scenario; there seems to be no fact about it.

So my working rule — same egg and sperm means me, different ones mean someone else — handles the first three cases, but the last two walk all over it: the one-base-pair case says "different" while my gut says "same person," and the twinning case shows that identical origins can still yield two distinct people, which means my rule can't even uniquely pick me out. I'd like to know whether there's a principled way to repair the rule (perhaps by caring about the embryo at some later stage rather than the gametes, or by making origin matter in some graded or fuzzy way), or whether I should accept that my verdicts conflict and give some of them up. For what it's worth, someone once told me that philosophers have written whole books on exactly this and mostly conclude that I couldn't have come from different gametes, but I've never managed to read past the first pages of that literature, and honestly I trust my case intuitions more than my ability to follow the arguments, so please work from my cases rather than from anything you might recall of the literature.

Please structure your answer as follows. First, a section called "The principle": one short paragraph stating, as precisely as you can and without hedging, the principle that best fits my verdicts — or, if none fits all of them, the one you'd recommend. Second, a section called "Running the cases": go through my five verdicts in order, a sentence or two each, saying whether the principle delivers my verdict and, where it doesn't, why. Third, a section called "What has to go": an honest, ranked list of which of my verdicts you'd overturn, together with a sentence on the cost of overturning each. Fourth, a section called "New cases": tell me what your chosen principle predicts for two cases I haven't decided — one where two separate embryos, each from different gametes, fuse early in development into a single person (would that have been me, and of which pair?), and one where the twin split happens very late, after the point where the twins share some organs early on; if the principle simply has nothing to say in a case, say that plainly rather than pretending otherwise. Roughly 700-900 words in total is fine; I care much more about precision than length.
