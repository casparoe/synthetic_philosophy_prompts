The question students were asked (worth 25% of the midterm):

"C. Thi Nguyen distinguishes epistemic bubbles from echo chambers. (a) Explain the difference in terms of what each structure does to the agent's access to, and trust in, outside sources. (b) Explain why Nguyen thinks the remedy that works for epistemic bubbles fails for echo chambers. (c) Give one example of each, and say why each is an example of the thing you claim it is."

Here's the answer I'm grading, copied exactly as submitted:

"Epistemic bubbles are social structures where the relevant other voices have been left out. Echo chambers are structures where those voices have been actively discredited. In a bubble the agent simply never hears the other side -- the sources arent reaching her, so she holds no belief about them either way. In an echo chamber she has heard the other side, but the chamber has trained her to trust it less, to hear it as unreliable or as coming from people who are out to get her. Nguyen takes this distinction from Cass Sunstein's Republic.com, where Sunstein warned that the internet was sorting us into echo chambers and driving group polarization.

The difference matters for the cure. Bubbles are easy: expose the agent to the missing sources and she will update, since nothing is blocking her except lack of contact. Echo chambers are harder, but not hopeless. If members keep encountering outside sources, the mistrust should gradually erode, since repeated exposure is how trust in any source gets built in the first place. So the echo chamber is really just a bubble plus a trust deficit, and the remedy is the same remedy applied for longer.

Example of an echo chamber: my uncle. His feed only ever shows him one side of things, so he never hears the other side, and if he did he would probably reconsider. Example of a bubble: someone who only follows cooking content and so never encounters political news at all, and would update if she did."
