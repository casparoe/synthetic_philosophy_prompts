For the final project in my applied-ethics course ("Moral Status at the Margins," an upper-level seminar), I'm building an interactive sorting quiz to run during our last session, and I'd like your help drafting it. The question it sorts on is: do video-game characters matter morally, and if so, to whom and why? The complication is that my classmates have read all the same survey material I have, so any question phrased at the level of labels — "do you think NPCs have moral status?" — will just prompt them to pick the position they already identify with rather than revealing what their actual instincts about cases commit them to. So I need eight to ten diagnostic questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, where the cases are what do the work and the labels never appear.

A few cases I'm fairly sure I want on the quiz, though I'm happy for you to add or swap others if they carve better: (i) a fantasy RPG in which NPCs, when you draw your weapon, drop to their knees and beg for their lives with fully voiced, seemingly improvised lines — a friend of mine plays specifically to trigger these reactions, finding it funny; (ii) a sandbox life-simulation game in which a streamer walls a character into an empty room until it dies of neglect, narrating it for an audience; (iii) a hypothetical future game whose NPCs are behaviorally indistinguishable from human beings and insist they suffer; (iv) the same, except the game's documentation proves the NPCs are running a simple lookup table; (v) a historical wargame in which the enemy civilians are modeled on a real ethnic group; (vi) whether deleting a save file, or quitting mid-conversation, wrongs anyone; and (vii) whether it matters that a game could in principle flip a single hidden flag converting "suffering" into a bare damage counter without changing anything observable. If some of these don't actually pull apart the positions, cut them and tell me why.

For the scoring key, please build the space of positions from the answer combinations first and only then attach names: at minimum I'd expect leaves for (a) a phenomenal-consciousness gate (nothing in current games matters directly, but sufficiently rich simulations might), (b) a substrate-independent functionalist view on which the right simulation is a moral patient now, (c) the view that no NPC is ever a patient but cruelty toward them corrodes the player's own character, (d) the view that the wrongs, when there are any, are wrongs to third parties — spectators, the desensitized player's future behavior, the group caricatured — and never to the characters, (e) an expressivist or fictionalist view on which harming a character wrongs no patient but can still be an ugly act, the way desecrating something symbolic can be, and (f) the deflationary view that nothing done inside a game could ever be wrong in itself. Two further requirements: flag explicitly any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate (I suspect the player-virtue and third-party views predict the same answers on at least one of my cases, and I'd like to know whether any question could split them), and note any answer pattern that maps onto no position, with a sentence diagnosing whether that combination is unoccupied because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. The audience is fifteen philosophy majors who will be answering honestly but competitively, so nothing should be answerable by remembering which philosopher said what.
