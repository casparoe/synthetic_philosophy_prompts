So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all, but I've ended up pretty deep in ML-adjacent work -- I've trained models, done a ton of feature engineering, I know my way around embeddings and attention and all that stuff. A while back I fell down a rabbit hole reading about Hofstadter's Copycat project, and I keep wondering how it looks if you translate it into the vocab I actually use every day, because I suspect the translation is either trivial or impossible and I can't tell which.

The deal, as I understand it: Copycat is this old program that solves letter-string analogy puzzles. You give it something like abc → abd, and then ask it to complete iijk → ? based on that one example. And the interesting part isn't getting the right answer, it's that the program doesn't have fixed concepts -- the concept of "successorship" or "leftmost" or "the letter i" is fluid, it gets stretched or slippage'd depending on context. Sometimes i is treated as just a letter at the start of the alphabet, sometimes it's allowed to slip into being "the h of this little sequence", and which reading wins depends on the run -- it's stochastic, different temperatures give different answers. Hofstadter's big claim, as I understand it, is that this kind of fluid analogy-making isn't some cute side effect of cognition, it basically IS cognition, and rigid concepts are an illusion we impose after the fact. He thought a tiny microdomain done in insane detail teaches you more than a broad shallow system.

So: restate the whole Copycat worldview in terms I actually use. Embeddings and cosine similarity, nearest-neighbor retrieval, attention heads doing soft token matching, inductive bias, sampling temperature, distribution shift, whatever fits. Do it as faithfully as you can, and then give me the honest residue report. Which parts translate cleanly -- is "conceptual slippage" literally just what soft matching over learned representations already does, such that Hofstadter anticipated something we now do routinely? Which parts only translate with distortion -- like, does his idea that concepts are built out of a perpetual top-down/bottom-up pressure have any counterpart in how attention actually behaves, or do I have to squint and lie to make it fit? And which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary -- is his claim that analogy is the core of cognition even the kind of thing that shows up as a claim about architectures and losses, or does it just evaporate?

And then the part I actually care about: for each bit of residue, tell me whether that's a defect of my vocabulary -- something ML people have a blind spot about, maybe because we only measure benchmark accuracy and never ask what a system is doing when it's *not* being evaluated -- or whether it's a sign Hofstadter's claim was vaguer than it looked, poetry about cognition rather than a hypothesis about it. Don't be nice about it either way. If the honest answer is that Copycat is a neat 1980s curiosity that modern soft matching subsumes, say that. If the honest answer is that there's something in there about concepts bending under context pressure that embedding-similarity talk genuinely can't capture, say that instead, and say what it would take to capture it.
