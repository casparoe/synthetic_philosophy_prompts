I'm a graduate TA running a fifty-minute section in a third-year course on free will and moral responsibility. Next week's reading is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and my students have just spent two weeks on compatibilism, so they'll arrive primed to file the paper under 'hard determinism' and move on. Plan the section for me rather than explaining the argument to me: the order in which the pieces must be introduced (I suspect the distinction between ultimate and ordinary responsibility has to come first, so the argument can't be heard as a rehash of the determinism debate, but check whether that's right and whether anything else has to precede even that); the single case I should carry the whole session through — it should be one in which the students start out genuinely wanting to blame the person, and it should be set up so that letting indeterminism into the story at any point visibly fails to help, since that is the move that separates the Basic Argument from hard determinism; the misconceptions they'll form, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off — my guesses are that they'll hear it as an argument that determinism is true, that they'll read the claim that true responsibility would require being causa sui as the claim that you'd have to have caused your own existence, and that they'll take the argument to depend on character being fixed rather than on the structure of self-creation; then one question I can ask in the last five minutes whose answer would show whether they've grasped that the argument turns on the structure of responsibility rather than on the truth of determinism; and finally what I should openly decline to get into at this level, together with what I should tell them about why I'm declining.
