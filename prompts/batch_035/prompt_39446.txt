My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether health and disease are purely factual categories or partly value-laden ones, and we ended up talking past each other for an hour before hitting the genuinely hard cases. She defended something like a biostatistical view: a disease is simply a part of an organism making a subnormal contribution to the survival and reproduction that is statistically typical for the species, with no evaluative judgment involved. I took the normativist line: nothing counts as a disease unless it is bad for the person, relative to their goals, their plans, and their circumstances. We tested both views on three cases that exposed the fault lines: situs inversus, where all the viscera are mirrored yet every part functions normally; evolutionary mismatch, where a fondness for calorie-dense food was adaptive in scarcity but is now harmful amid abundance; and a hypothetical isolated community in which nearly everyone goes deaf in middle age, so that deafness is species-typical for them and hearing past fifty is the statistical anomaly. Please write a dialogue between the two of us in which we work through these cases and press on each other's actual commitments — for instance, whether her statistical view can explain why we would treat the mirrored organs but leave the deaf elder alone, and whether my value-laden view can avoid making disease hostage to fashion or prejudice. The two characters should be equally competent, each conceding genuinely strong points to the other, and each must respond to what the other actually says rather than lecture past her. Please write the dialogue in American English.
