I'm a backend engineer who's been reading the AI safety stuff people at work share around, and I keep bumping into a distinction I don't think I really get in my gut: "alignment" as in make the model actually want the right things, vs "control" as in assume it might be misaligned and design the surrounding system so a misaligned model still can't do damage. The trusted-monitor / untrusted-model setups, defense in depth, that whole flavor of thinking. I've been asked to run a 2-hour workshop for ~12 engineers (all technical, zero philosophy background) about why you'd even bother with the second thing, and lecturing at them never works, so: design a game/exercise where they feel the difference themselves.

My rough idea: one group secretly plays a misaligned model inside some deployment pipeline, the others build defenses, but I'm open to better structures. Whatever you come up with, I want: concrete rules and materials (cards, chat relay, whatever -- no special software), roles, some way to score it, and the key constraint baked into the rules that the defenders can never look inside the box or verify anything about the "model's" goals -- they can only shape what it can see and do. A debrief that maps what actually happened onto the real concepts (why you monitor with a weaker trusted model, why you limit action space instead of trying to filter outputs, why one layer always leaks). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the real control problem a classroom game just cannot reproduce. If you can sneak in a moment where players reinvent "we can't verify alignment so we assume misalignment" on their own before I name it, that's the dream.
