Something I've been chewing on: nearly every country that officially believes in equal opportunity still lets parents pass wealth on to their kids more or less untaxed below a very high threshold, and even where inheritance taxes exist they're usually the least enforced and least popular tax around. Yet the same legal order is quite happy to break up other accumulations of unearned advantage — we don't let public offices be bought and inherited, and the rule against perpetuities (plus its cousins) exists precisely to stop the dead controlling property forever. So what view of fairness does the way we actually tax — or mostly don't tax — inheritances presuppose? Is it that the family counts as the legitimate vehicle of opportunity, so "equal opportunity" means equal starting points for families rather than for individuals? Or a quasi-desert view on which the earner's claim over their wealth extends to directing it after death? And do the parts of the practice even point the same way — e.g. why is a big lump-sum bequest at death acceptable while a trust that pays out gradually over a century feels like something the law needs to guard against, if both are just dynastic transfers? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, say which rival views of equality the practice would also be consistent with (i.e. where it's genuinely underdetermined), and tell me what would have to change if a luck-egalitarian took inheritances seriously.
