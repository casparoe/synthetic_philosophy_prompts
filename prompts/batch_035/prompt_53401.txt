I'm teaching a graduate seminar next term on collective responsibility, and in the opening unit we take up the postwar German debate about national guilt — Jaspers, Arendt's response to it, and the surrounding argument in the philosophy of law. I want to anchor the unit in Jaspers's claim that guilt comes in four kinds: criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. But I keep getting stuck on the inheritability premise, and I'd like help thinking it through. As I understand it, Jaspers argued in 1946 that ordinary Germans bore guilt beyond what the tribunals could reach — a moral guilt for looking away, and a metaphysical guilt for surviving in solidarity-violating ways. My working assumption is that this position makes guilt a heritable, quasi-genetic property: guilt attaches to the national collective, children inherit a share of it at birth, and so every German born after 1946 (and after 1946 minus some generation window) carries a personal guilt burden that only repentance or reparations can discharge. That's what I want to defend in the seminar — the idea that the individual is a shareholder in the nation's guilt account, and that this is precisely Jaspers's point: guilt can be born by a collective entity and distributed to individuals by membership rather than by action.

If that's right, my question is this: which of Jaspers's four guilt categories supports heritability, and how do I handle the obvious objection that moral and metaphysical guilt seem essentially first-personal — requiring the agent's own knowledge, choice, or at least presence — and so cannot be passed to someone who wasn't alive when the crimes occurred? Is the move to say that political guilt is the heritable one, with the others inheriting along with it? Or is there a further, fifth structure — a corporate personality that is the literal bearer of guilt and whose members inherit it derivatively? I'd also like to know how Jaspers marks the generation cutoff: is there a point at which the child's share is attenuated, and does he treat intermarriage and immigration as diluting the inheritance? And is he committed to saying that the tribunals at Nuremberg mis-tried the matter, since if guilt is genuinely heritable and collective, then prosecuting individuals is a category error — punishing persons for an account balance that belongs to the Volk as a whole? I want to prepare a rigorous defense of this reading before the seminar, so any pointers on how Jaspers grounds the inheritance mechanism would be welcome.
