I've been reading a pop-finance book about market efficiency, and there's a passage near the end of one chapter I keep going back to. Paraphrasing from memory: "Economists proved a remarkable result called the no-trade theorem. If two traders are both rational and start out with the same prior beliefs, they can never disagree once they've pooled their private information — so persistent disagreement between market participants is itself proof of irrationality. It follows that speculating on information is pointless: the very fact that a counterparty accepts your bet is evidence you're on the wrong side of it. Rational traders with common priors shouldn't trade against each other at all; the only rational trades are rebalancing for consumption purposes. Since real markets clear trillions of dollars in speculative volume, the assumptions must be false in practice, and the efficient market hypothesis is dead on arrival."

I found this pretty compelling but a few things nag at me. The bit about the counterparty's acceptance being evidence against you sounds right at first (it reminds me of the old line about not wanting to join a club that would have me), but does the no-trade result actually hinge on the common prior, or is something else doing the work? And is it really true that disagreement between rational people is proof of *irrationality* — could there be some other assumption quietly failing? The last sentence especially feels like a jump: even if all that speculative volume shows the assumptions break down somewhere, how does that get you to prices not reflecting any rational consensus? To complicate things further, I vaguely remember reading about some paradox involving Stiglitz saying perfectly informationally efficient markets are impossible anyway (something about nobody having an incentive to gather information if prices already reflect everything), but I cant see how that fits with the book's conclusion either — they both seem to want to bury the efficient market hypothesis but for opposite reasons.

Can you go through the summary bit by bit and tell me what a financial economist or a Bayesian type would sign off on, where they'd push back, and whether the book's final conclusion follows from the parts that survive? I'm personally inclined to accept the book's moral that markets are just full of non-rational traders, so tell me if thats not the natural lesson to draw.
