Been reading lesswrong and the effective altruism forum on and off for like 12 years now and there's this one fight that never goes anywhere: does my individual burger / flight / vote actually matter, or is it true that "one more car on the road changes nothing" and only coordinated action is real. You know the shape of it. One side does the expected value math -- there's some small probability my consumption is the one that tips a threshold (Kagan style, the ten millionth customer gets the free burger, except here it's the marginal coal plant staying on one more year), so the EV of eating meat is some horrifying number of animal-years of suffering, so yes it matters. The other side says no, in most real cases the effect is indefinite or spread out, not a clean threshold, so there's no fact of the matter about whether YOU caused anything, and Norcross has this old line about how it's nuts to think torturing a puppy yourself is fine because puppy 1 through 1000 wouldve been tortured anyway. And then every few months someone rediscovers the whole thing and posts it like it's new and the comments section reruns it again.

Heres what I actually want to know: what KIND of disagreement is this? Because it feels stalled in a specific way that makes me suspicious. Both sides agree on the physics and the economics, both agree suffering is bad, both can do the math, and yet nobody budges. My working hypothesis is that it's not really about causal thresholds at all, and it's secretly the debate about whether ethics should use decision theory at all -- like whether "expected" value is a legitimate input to moral verdicts about an individual act, or whether it's only ok for institutional agents / for choosing between gambles but not for "did I harm anyone". One side treats moral responsibility as tracking expected effects, the other treats it as tracking actual difference-making, and those come apart when effects are tiny and diffused, so the two camps can pass each other forever without contradiction. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually bedrock intuitions about cases (the guy who says "I'd pay $10 to un-torture the puppy but $0 to be a 1-in-a-million co-cause"), or maybe it's a verbal fight about the word "cause."

So: diagnose it for me. Tell me which disagreement it really is -- verbal, substantive-but-empirical, different questions under one heading, or competing standards for what makes an act wrong -- and use what the sides actually say to back it up, not a survey of everyone who's written on it. And then be honest about what, if anything, would count as progress: is there some distinction or argument that should end this, or is it the kind of thing where the rationalist-adjacent instinct to "just shut up and multiply" and the philosopher's instinct to look at actual causal structure are genuinely incommensurable? Also if you think my decision-theory-in-ethics hypothesis is wrong, say so and say what's better about yours.

Please write in American English.
