so I've been reading up on molyneux's problem and I ran into the sensorimotor contingency view of vision (O'Regan and Noë's thing), where seeing gets compared to a blind person skillfully using a cane. the idea is that perceiving isn't having inner pictures, it's mastering a set of rules about how sensory input changes when you move -- how the visual field shifts when you turn your head, how an object's look changes as you walk around it. vision is a skill, like cane use, and a well-worn cane basically becomes part of the perceiver's body.

the podcast where I heard this used it to argue that molyneux's problem pretty much dissolves: there's nothing to transfer from touch to vision, because there are no visual contents at all until you've learned the visual contingencies. so of course the newly sighted man can't tell the cube from the sphere by eye -- asking him to do it on day one is like asking whether a good swimmer can immediatly ski.

audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the cane, the arm movements, the felt tip contact, the skill itself, the object touched -- and then say where the mapping breaks down. and here's the thing that bugs me: the whole point of the cane example is that the experienced cane user is perceiving distal objects, the chair leg out there in the world and not the buzzing in his palm. touch itself is already delivering the geometry of objects out there via a sensorimotor skill. so shouldn't the analogy actually predict some transfer to vision, rather than none? if both modalities are skills for exploring the same external shapes, maybe the molyneux man isn't a swimmer-turned-skier at all -- more like a skier handed a snowboard. does the "molyneux dissolves" conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy quietly doing all the work? and is there a better analogy for the newly sighted person's situation than the one the podcast gave?
