For my decision theory seminar we've been on expected utility and Pascal's mugging, and it made me realize my gut verdicts on small-probability-huge-stakes cases are kind of all over the palce, so I'm trying to figure out if there's actually a principle behind them. Here's the list. A guy at a bus stop tells me he'll torture a googolplex of people unless I hand over $5: not paying, obviously. Donating $50 to a group that tracks near-earth asteroids: seems fine to me, even though the chance my $50 actually stops and impact is astronomically small. My friend offers me 1% of his startup for $200 and swears there's a 1-in-10,000 shot it sells for $100M, so it's supposedly worth $100k in expectation: taking it would still be dumb. Signing up for cryonics at $100/month on the theory that there's maybe a 1% chance it eventually gets me centuries more life: I go back and forth but lean yes. A lottery site selling $1.20 tickets for a genuinely random 1-in-500-million shot at $600M: I wouldn't buy one, and I wouldn't even be tempted. A fair coin flip for $20 with my roommate: I take it, so it's not like I'm just scared of risk in general.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? The lottery verdict and the cryonics verdict seem hardest to hold together — structurally they look almost identical, tiny chance of an enormous payoff, except cryonics costs me way more per shot and I still lean toward it?? And what does the principle say about the case I genuinely can't settle: giving $1000 to a group that works on making sure future superintelligent AI doesn't wipe out humanity, where they'd tell me there's some small but real chance of saving literally everyone who will ever live? If the principle has to overturn some of my verdicts, I'd rather know which ones, and I'd also like to hear what it predicts on a case I haven't thought of.
