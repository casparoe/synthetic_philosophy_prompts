I teach an undergraduate law and philosophy seminar, and this term two students have been locked in a running argument that started with the 1950s and 60s exchange between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart over whether the state may enforce majority morality as such — the debate spawned by the Wolfenden Committee's recommendation on homosexuality and prostitution, Devlin's argument that a shared morality is part of the social bond and may be defended like anything else essential to a society's existence, and Hart's insistence, drawing on Mill, that immorality as such is never a sufficient ground for criminal punishment. One of my students takes the Devlin side, though in a sophisticated form: he accepts that popular disgust at consensual conduct is an unreliable guide, but thinks some enforcement of shared moral norms is defensible insofar as those norms genuinely do the work of holding the society together — the question is where toleration begins to dissolve the social fabric, not whether the fabric matters. The other takes the Hart side but is uncomfortable with the pure harm principle: she worries that a society's moral environment is a real object of concern, that people can be wronged in ways that involve no identifiable harmed individual (think of corruption, cruelty indulged in private, or degradation of public standards), and that Hart never fully answers what to do about that. They argue past each other constantly: one says the other wants the tyranny of the majority, the other says her opponent wants an unhappiness of the moralized state.

I want them to stop and produce something better. Draft the joint statement these two would issue after a properly conducted adversarial collaboration, and I'd like it to do the things such documents are supposed to do. Lay out, as numbered claims in continuous prose (write the numbers inline, e.g., "first, ...; second, ..." — I want no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just continuous prose like a letter), the propositions both could sign their names to — about the harm principle, about what Devlin's "society" must mean for his argument to even get started, about the distinction between morality as a fact and morality as a standard, about the epistemic unreliability of moral disgust, and about the limits of what criminal law can achieve through enforcement. Then set out precisely where they still disagree, with each disagreement phrased in wording the person on that side would actually accept — I don't want the Devlinian described as licensing persecution, nor the Hartian as indifferent to the social fabric. Identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and I suspect at least one crux is whether there are genuine victimless wrongs, and another is whether the fact that a morality is shared gives anyone standing to impose it. For each crux, name the kind of argument or case both would agree bears on it — a historical episode, a thought experiment, a comparative case about a society whose enforced morality changed or collapsed — and record each side's prediction about what that case would show, written down in advance so neither can claim victory retroactively.

Two things I especially want the document to contain. First, both should confront the asymmetry their positions run into: my Devlinian has to admit that his argument licenses enforcing whatever morality is in fact shared, including a newly shared progressive morality, and my Hartian has to say whether that consequence embarrasses him or her opponent more. Second, they should say explicitly which parts of their year-long dispute turned out to be verbal — e.g., whether they were using "immorality" and "harm" to pick out different things — and which parts are genuine and unlikely to be settled by any evidence either can currently imagine. The point of the exercise is to show my class what honest disagreement looks like when it is made exact. Keep it in continuous prose throughout, no headings or lists.
