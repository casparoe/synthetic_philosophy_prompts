After a long argument with a friend who teaches ethics, I'd like a case built to order. She takes what I understand to be the standard "no genuine dilemmas" line: whenever duties conflict, one is simply weightier, the agent thereby does her actual, all-things-considered duty, and any guilt she feels is really regret about how circumstances went rather than remorse for a wrong done — a residue in the agent, not a verdict against her. I want to see whether that line can be made to strain, and I'd rather test it on a case engineered for the purpose than on the stock examples.

Construct a thought experiment meeting all of the following specifications: (i) an ordinary, realistic scenario, describable in a single paragraph — no trolleys, no ticking bombs, no collapsing institutions, and no one dies; (ii) the agent genuinely bears both duties, with the conflict arising through no negligence of her own, so she cannot be accused of having overpromised her way into the predicament; (iii) the case is built so that a Rossian weighing of prima facie duties and a straightforward aggregate-welfare maximizer converge on the same choice without either theory having to strain; (iv) nevertheless, the person on the unchosen side retains, given only the facts internal to the case, a grievance against the agent — a claim to have been wronged, not merely to have lost out; and (v) the residue is undischargable: no apology, compensation, or explanation can reach the wronged party, so the guilt cannot be discharged by after-the-fact repair.

After the scenario, demonstrate explicitly that it satisfies each of the five specifications, identify which feature is doing the philosophical work and what happens to the case if that feature is deleted, and then give the strongest countermove available to the no-dilemma theorist — I anticipate my friend saying either that the grievance is a projection onto the agent of what is really the world's fault, or that the guilt, however intelligible, is unfitting in the way fear of a harmless ghost is unfitting. Say whether either countermove succeeds. Conclude with your own verdict on whether the case establishes that genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and be candid about where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong and what argument or consideration would move you to the opposite verdict.
