I'd like a careful explanation of why the backward induction argument for the centipede game is considered paradoxical, rather than merely surprising. I understand the setup — two players alternate between "pass" and "take," with payoffs increasing the longer both pass, and the induction concluding that a rational player takes at the very first node. What I want explained is the knot in the reasoning itself.

As I understand it, the trouble is something like this: to run the induction, each player must reason about how her opponent would choose at nodes that the argument itself establishes will never be reached if both are rational. But if a node is reached, someone has already deviated from what the argument said rationality requires — so what grounds are there for still attributing rationality, and common knowledge of it, at that node? The presuppositions seem to be self-undermining in a way that doesn't happen in simpler cases like the one-shot prisoner's dilemma.

Please address the following, using a concrete four-move centipede with explicit payoffs throughout so nothing stays abstract:

(ii) Diagnose the self-undermining character of those presuppositions. Is the problem that the premises are jointly inconsistent (common knowledge of rationality at a node that can only be reached given a prior lapse of rationality)? Or is it that the counterfactuals the argument needs are ill-defined, so that it's indeterminate what a rational player would do at a node that her own reasoning says couldn't occur? Explain the difference between these diagnoses and why it matters for what a repair must accomplish.
