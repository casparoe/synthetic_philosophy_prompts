I'm preparing a seminar exercise on extrapolating philosophical frameworks rather than reciting them, and I'd like your help drafting the model answer. The setup: suppose Jeremy Bentham, revived with his utilitarian commitments fully intact but given a full briefing on modern conditions, testified before a parliamentary committee reviewing the regulation of sex and intimate life. His record on the issues of his own day, as I understand it: he argued that private consensual sexual conduct between adults occasioned no primary mischief and so should never be criminal; he held that condemnation rooted in religious asceticism or mere disgust is prejudice dressed up as principle; he took pleasure to be intrinsically good, so that no sexual act could be bad considered merely as pleasure; but he also took remote and structural harms seriously — damage to reputation, to family, to population — and he insisted that such alleged harms actually be demonstrated with evidence rather than asserted. Now the committee wants his testimony on matters that did not exist in his time, and I want you to give it in his voice and by his methods. The cases: (1) AI companions marketed as romantic and sexual partners — does his no-harm verdict carry over automatically, or would he count alleged erosion of users' capacities for reciprocal relationships as a real remote mischief, and how would he demand it be evidenced? (2) The Nordic model for commercial sex — criminalizing purchase while decriminalizing sale — given that he would weigh the mischief of the trade against the mischief of the constraint; would a partial criminalization strike him as a sensible middle path or as the worst of both? (3) Platform-mediated informal exchanges of intimacy for housing or tuition support — he treated the legal institution of marriage as an artificial arrangement whose justification must be consequential; would that make these arrangements straightforwardly tolerable, or introduce new risks he would regulate? (4) Nonconsensual distribution of intimate images — the interesting question is whether, for him, the wrongness resides in the breach of privacy, in the reputational injury (a currency he understood well), or in the distress itself, and whether that matters for how the law should be drafted. For each case: give the verdict you predict, derive it explicitly from his framework rather than from modern liberal sentiment, and say which of his principles is doing the work. Then tell me where the framework genuinely underdetermines the verdict — where two strands of his thought (say, his hostility to asceticism and his willingness to count structural harms) pull apart — and what empirical findings, if any, he would treat as settling the matter. Finally, flag the one case on which you think the extrapolation is least secure, and why. Please write in American English, in a reasonably formal register, and keep his felicific-calculus style of reasoning visible: tally the pleasures, tally the pains, name the sanction at stake.
