I'm a retired chemist -- thirty-some years in a spectroscopy lab, and since retiring I've been making a mess of philosophy and economics the way only a person with too much library access can. The idea I keep chewing on lately is revealed preference, which as I understand it goes back to Samuelson: stop asking people what they want (surveys are cheap talk), watch what they choose when prices bind, and read their "utility" off the choices. It reminds me uncomfortably of the operationalism I was raised on in physics -- don't define length with metaphysics, define it by the ruler you actually lay down -- except economists seem to have applied it to happiness. And the more I stare at it, the more I can't decide whether it's the most honest idea in social science or the most elegant circular argument ever constructed: he chose it, so he preferred it, and we know he preferred it because he chose it. My nephew the econ grad student says it's all been formalized and my worry is naive, which is exactly the kind of confidence I'd like to see punctured.

So here's what I want: a satirical newspaper advice column, roughly 700 words, written by a strict revealed-preference economist -- call her anything you like. Three or four reader letters with replies. Suggested letters, use or invent your own: a woman who keeps renewing a gym membership she's used twice in four years; a smoker who "wants to quit" but keeps buying cigarettes; a father whose daughter called him crying about her job, which she has not quit; and one letter from a skeptical behavioral economist, which the columnist should answer with serene condescension. The rules of the bit: the columnist must never break doctrine. Nobody ever acts against their own preferences, regret is just a data point about a later self, complaining is itself a consumption good, and every miserbale choice is quietly reinterpreted as optimal. The humor should come from getting the actual theory right -- ordinal utility, the circularity worry, revealed preference vs. stated preference, maybe de gustibus non est disputandum -- so that an economist laughs rather than winces, but it has to stay readable for someone like me who never took an econ course. Ideally, by the end, the reader feels the real question pressing through the jokes: is this a science of what people want, or a very fancy way of saying that people do what they do?

Start your reply with the column itself, no preface, no commentary, nothing after it either -- if you want a one-line editor's note as part of the column, that's fine, but nothing from you personally.
