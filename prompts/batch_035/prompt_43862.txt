Jurisdictions that have legalized euthanasia or physician-assisted dying typically surround it with heavy safeguards: the request must come from a competent adult, be repeated and considered rather than made in a single moment of despair, be assessed by at least one independent physician, and in some systems be followed by a waiting period. The very same medical systems treat the refusal or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment by a competent patient as an ordinary exercise of the right to refuse care — no second opinion, no waiting period, no particular scrutiny of whether the decision was made in despair. And while advance directives refusing treatment are honored after the patient loses capacity, advance requests for euthanasia written while competent are in most of these systems not acted upon once the patient can no longer confirm them. What theory of the wrongness of killing, and of the difference between killing and letting die, does this pattern of safeguards presuppose? Does the asymmetry presuppose that active killing is morally worse than letting die, or merely that it is more dangerous — more open to abuse, harder to reverse, more corrosive of trust in medicine — so that the safeguards track risk rather than any difference in moral status? And what view of autonomy and personal identity does the refusal of advance requests presuppose: that only a contemporaneous, competent self can authorize a death, or that the person who wrote the directive no longer exists in the relevant sense by the time it is to be carried out? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the safeguard asymmetries, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely consistent with, and tell me what would have to change in the law if the asymmetry were instead grounded in a straightforwardly consequentialist, risk-based picture of killing and letting die.
