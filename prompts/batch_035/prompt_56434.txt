I teach social epistemology, and every spring I get to the unit on testimony and can't resist going on a tangent about hearsay law that my students probably find mildly derailing but which I genuinely think is philosophically underexplored. The practice, in outline: courts in the American system (and many others) have a blanket rule that out-of-court statements can't be offered to prove their content. If you want to establish that the light was red, you can't just have a witness report that a bystander told them it was red; the bystander herself has to take the stand, be put under oath, and get cross-examined. But the rule is then riddled with dozens of exceptions — dying declarations, excited utterances made under the stress of the event, business records kept in the regular course, statements against the declarant's own interest, and so on — where the absent speaker's words come in anyway, without oath or cross-examination.

Here's what I want to work out with my class, and I'd take the same exercise from you. Does the exclusionary rule presuppose something like a reductionist view of testimony, where a secondhand report only transmits justification if the trier of fact can somehow get back to the original perception, with cross-examination playing the role of the verification mechanism that reductionism says we need? And then do the exceptions quietly presuppose the opposite, something closer to the anti-reductionist line associated with Reid, on which testimony is a basic source of justification rather than a degraded copy of perception — since business records, for instance, are admitted precisely because the record-keeping practice is reliable, even though nobody who directly perceived the recorded event ever testifies? It's tempting to say the exceptions track the conditions under which a speaker would have been reliable at the source (a dying man has no motive to lie, an excited speaker no time to confabulate), which is still a kind of indirect checking, but it's checking by general assumptions about situations rather than anything the jury actually does.

So: reconstruct the implicit epistemology of testimony in this body of law, tell me whether its parts presuppose consistent things or whether it's a philosophically incoherent patchwork that happens to work, and say where the practice is underdetermined so that both a reductionist and an anti-reductionist could endorse the same ruleset for different reasons. Then tell me what would actually have to change in courtroom practice if the law were rebuilt on the rival view — would we still demand live witnesses, or would sworn statements plus reliability evidence about the speaker's circumstances do the job? Also worth addressing whether cross-examination is doing epistemic work at all, as opposed to political or moral work (the defendant's right to face an accuser), since if it's the latter the reductionist reading of the rule collapses a bit. Please write it as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a long email, since I want to fold pieces of your answer into my lecture notes.
