(1) If an actually infinite collection of things could exist, then a hotel with infinitely many occupied rooms could exist — it's the same kind of thing, just more of it.
(2) In that hotel every room is occupied. Call that "full." And a hotel is full exactly when it has no spare capacity, i.e. it can't take another guest without someone leaving or being turned away.
(3) The hotelier can move every guest from room n to room n+1, freeing room 1, then check in a new guest. No one leaves, no one is turned away, no room gets double-booked.
(4) By repeating (3), the hotelier can take in ten new guests, or a thousand, or any finite number you want, without anyone ever leaving.
(5) So the hotel both IS full (every room occupied) and HAS unlimited spare capacity (steps 3 and 4). But "full" and "has spare capacity" are contradictories — that's just what the words mean.
(6) Therefore an actually infinite collection cannot exist, because if any one could exist, this contradiction would be true.

the reason i think it proves too much: run the exact same argument on the natural numbers. every natural number is "taken" (each one is already some number), so the naturals are full; but you can always shift everything and make room for one more. so by his own logic there are only finitely many natural numbers. he says he doesn't want to say that, his move is "numbers aren't real things so it's different," but i don't see where in the argument he gets to make that move when step (6) says ANY actually infinite collection. also, and maybe this is the same issue, in step (5) he ends up saying the hotel is full and not-full at the same time, which feels like it should be illegal even in thought experiments.
