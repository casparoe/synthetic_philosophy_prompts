I've spent enough years reading Tetlock's forecasting work, Taleb's complaints about narrators, and the LessWrong archives on hindsight bias that I've developed a set of gut verdicts about when a retrospective explanation of a historical event is legitimate and when it's a narrative fallacy — an explanation manufactured after the fact that would have "explained" any outcome equally well. The problem is that I can't seem to make my own verdicts consistent, and I'd like your help extracting whatever principle, if any, actually captures them.

My verdicts, concretely:

1. After the 2008 crisis, an analyst says the crash was obvious in retrospect: household debt at record levels, lending standards collapsed, the ratings agencies captured. I distrust this. The base rate of people crying bubble in advance is high enough that the eventual crash vindicated mostly stopped clocks, and the tell is that this analysis is only ever produced after the fact.

2. Darwin's explanation of the Galápagos finches as adaptive radiation from a common ancestor was constructed entirely after the observations. Yet I trust it completely, and would even if Darwin had never made a single successful forward prediction. So mere retroactivity can't be the mark of the fallacy.

3. I distrust the claim that the assassination at Sarajevo "caused" the First World War — too many contingent paths to war, and the trigger narrative feels like privileging whatever happened to happen. But I'm fairly comfortable saying the Soviet collapse was caused by economic stagnation, imperial overstretch, and the nationalities problem — a structural explanation of a unique event that seems to me to be under the same suspicion.

4. A forensic team reconstructs a plane crash from the wreckage and concludes that a fatigue crack in a specific bracket caused the failure. Fully retrospective, about a unique event, and I trust it without reservation.

5. The hard one. I said the "obvious in retrospect" housing analysis was illegitimate. But I also believe that a handful of people, using essentially that same analysis, identified the bubble correctly in 2005–2007, and that their reasoning was legitimate — genuinely good, not lucky. If the analysis was legitimate in 2006, what exactly makes the identical analysis illegitimate when voiced in 2009?

My tentative principle is this: a retrospective explanation earns trust only to the extent that, before the fact, it would have licensed a risky prediction — a forecast that could have failed — that then came true. This handles 1 (no risky prediction, just narration), 4 (the reconstruction implies dangerous failure modes in the identical brackets of the rest of the fleet, a checkable forward claim), and arguably 5. But it seems to condemn 2 more than I want it to, and it has trouble with 3: even a perfect analyst in 1913 could not have predicted the timing or trigger of the war, so what work is the alliance-structure explanation actually doing, and why do I trust the Soviet-collapse version of it?

Please tell me: is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, or a small set of candidate principles? State it precisely, motivate each clause by the case it handles, and say what it predicts about two cases I haven't considered — say, an economic historian in 2045 explaining a hypothetical war over Taiwan as the inevitable result of semiconductor supply concentration, and a doctor telling a patient that their rare diagnosis "was visible in the chart all along." Then be honest about which of my five verdicts your principle cannot save, and whether the choice is between a complex principle that preserves all of them and a simpler one that requires me to give one up — and if so, which one should go.
