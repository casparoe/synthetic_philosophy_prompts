I run a small evolution blog and my next post is one of those "which X are you actually" quizzes — this time, what kind of selectionist the reader really is. My audience is smart laypeople: most of them read The Selfish Gene once, years ago, and came away with the vibe that group selection is for suckers, without ever touching Williams or Sober and Wilson or anyone who complicated that. So the quiz should sort them by their actual intuitions, not by which label they like.

You're a philosopher of biology who's been around the levels-of-selection block many times. Before writing, brainstorm silently: which concrete cases actually split the positions, and which only feel like they do. If a question could be answered on vibes alone, cut it.

The deliverable: 8-10 questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The leaves have to include at least these: hard-line gene-centrism (Dawkins flavor), orthodox organism-level selectionism, pragmatic multilevel selectionism of the Okasha-ish kind, group-selection revivalism, and inclusive-fitness loyalism. Species selection can be a leaf if you can honestly make it fit, otherwise say why not.

Case coverage I want: meiotic drive or something like it, cancer (yes, cancer — it's the best test of whether someone thinks "selection" requires benefiting the individual), sterile workers in eusocial insects, one ordinary-adaptation control case where all positions land in the same place so the quiz doesn't smell like ideology in every question, and one built on my own invented scenario — a desert shrub that reproduces clonally, so a whole hillside is one genetic individual, with a toxin gene that slightly damages the shrub's own tissue but kills seedlings of rival genotypes nearby. Use that clone-shrub case; it's the one I'm proudest of and I want to see what you do with it.

Two hard parts I want you to handle explicitly:

1) Kin selection vs group selection. As I understand it the formalisms are basically equivalent, so a case-based quiz can't seperate them by answers alone. Decide what to do about that — merge them into one leaf, or find some way to split readers that isn't just asking which label they prefer — and be upfront about which you chose and why.

2) After the scoring key, note any answer-pattern combinations that no real position occupies, and say for each whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it. Don't paper over gaps in the tree.

Rules: no question may use the technical labels — a reader should be able to answer the sterile-worker question without knowing inclusive fitness is a thing. No reading lists or citations, obviously, this is a quiz. And the whole thing (questions, options, key, notes) has to come in under 800 words — tight but doable if you keep the cases lean. Definately don't pad the options with hedges.
