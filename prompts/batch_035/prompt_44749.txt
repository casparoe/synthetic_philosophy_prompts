Title: What the Diffractometer Never Told Us

Physicists and chemists of my generation spent our careers extracting structures from shadows. In a diffraction experiment, one never observes an atom; one observes an interference pattern, and from its symmetry, intensities, and systematic absences one reconstructs a lattice and a set of positions — a structure, in the strictest sense. No one in the laboratory ever believed this was a limitation we might someday overcome. The shadows are not an obstacle standing between us and the atoms; they are, as far as any operational sense can be given to the claim, all there is to be had. After retiring, I discovered that philosophers had put a name to a nearby idea: structural realism, the view that what science delivers, and all it can be said to deliver, is structure — the network of relations among things, not the intrinsic natures of the things themselves. Worrall famously defended this by pointing to the continuity of structure across scientific revolutions: Fresnel's equations for light were not discarded when Maxwell's theory replaced the elastic aether; they were absorbed into it. What survived was the mathematics of the relations, not the ontology of the medium.

My contribution — and I am aware this may be naive — is to argue that this epistemic modesty is not a philosophical discovery about science but a description of what laboratory instrumentation has always been doing. I call the position instrumental structural realism. Its claim is that measurement itself is constitutively structural: an instrument is calibrated against other instruments, its outputs are defined only against reference standards, and every quantity it registers is a relation to something else. Nowhere in a physics laboratory does one encounter an intrinsic nature. When two rival theories are empirically equivalent but ontologically discordant — the textbook underdetermination scenarios — the instrumental structural realist should say that this equivalence is not an embarrassment to be explained away but a fact about the world that our instruments are trying to tell us: the rival ontologies are two presentations of one and the same structure.

I believe this position also answers the objection Newman raised against Russell's structuralism in 1928: that if all we know is structure, then almost any cardinality-sufficient domain can carry that structure, and the view collapses into triviality. But Newman's objection concerns any and all relations in the abstract, whereas instruments register only the specific, spatiotemporally anchored relations that physical devices can be coupled to. The structure instrumental structural realism commits us to is not the set-theorist's free constructions but the measurer's stubbornly local facts.
