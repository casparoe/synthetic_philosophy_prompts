Two philosophers — a committed non-reductive physicalist and a philosopher persuaded by the causal exclusion argument that mental properties, if irreducible, are causally idle — have spent three years arguing about mental causation and have agreed to run an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they should produce.

The document must contain, in this order:

1. An agreed-claims section (numbered) covering what both can sign: the causal closure of physics as they will jointly understand it (including any agreed limits of that principle — e.g., whether "closed under physics" means no non-physical causes, not that physics is complete), the causal inheritance intuition each concedes in his own idiom, and the shared insistence that the dispute does not turn on dualism, since both are physicalists.

2. A disagreements section, as a table, stating the residual disputes in wording each side would accept for its own view: at minimum, (a) whether causal overdetermination is an option or a cost; (b) whether the exclusion principle's "sufficient cause" is a notion of production, of contrastive explanatory relevance, or something the two parties were using differently all along; (c) whether reduction of the mental preserves what mattered about mentality or forfeits it; and (d) whether the argument generalizes — whether, if it works against mental causes, it equally threatens the causal efficacy of biological, geological, and thermodynamic properties, and whether that generalization is a reductio of the argument or of higher-level causation generally.

3. A cruxes section with, for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance. At least two cruxes should engage engineered systems where the relevant facts are uncontested — for example, a simulated reinforcement-learning agent whose "beliefs" are explicitly represented as high-level variables over a fully known substrate, or a virtual machine whose instruction-level states are multiply realizable across processors. State what each philosopher predicts an interventionist account of causation would find in such systems, and where those predictions come apart.

4. A short closing note, signed jointly, addressing the question the two cannot resolve in a signed document: state how confident the drafter is in the formulations above, and identify the single numbered agreed claim or table entry the drafter is least sure both parties would actually endorse as worded, with a sentence on why.

Do not quote either party or any published author verbatim; attribute the exclusion argument to Jaegwon Kim by name once, and otherwise argue from the structure of the views. Avoid vacuous agreed claims — anything both would sign but that no one disputes in this debate — and avoid formulations that quietly decide a contested question (e.g., don't stipulate that causation is production when contrastive explanation is at issue).
