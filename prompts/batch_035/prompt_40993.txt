A guy in my weekly ethics discussion group has a track record on trolley-type cases, and next week we're voting on three new ones, so I want to predict him before we meet. Here's everything he's committed to so far:

- Bystander switch: trolley heading for five, he can turn it onto a spur where one person is standing. Fine to turn. (In our version there's a buffer behind the guy on the spur, so his body isn't actually what stops the trolley.)
- Loop: same setup, except the spur curves back around and rejoins the main line, so if the one guy wasn't there the trolley would just come around and kill the five anyway — his body is the only thing that stops it. Still fine to turn, he says.
- Footbridge: not fine to push the guy in front of the trolley.
- Shoving a bystander onto the track right in the trolley's path to stop it: not fine.
- Surgeon case (one healthy visitor, five dying patients who need his organs): not fine, obviosly.

The new cases for next week:

(1) You're in the crane booth over the main line. You can drop a crate in front of the trolley; the crate stops it on its own, no body needed. But a maintenance worker is standing on that stretch of track, and the crate will crush him on the way down. His death does literally nothing toward stopping the trolley — you're just certain it will happen, becuase the crate's path goes right over where he's standing.

(2) Same crane, except this time the worker's body would cushion the crate's landing just enough that the crate stays put and stops the trolley.

(3) Standard footbridge, except you don't push him — you press a lever that opens a trapdoor under him, so he drops onto the track in front of the trolley and his body stops it.

Will he say fine or not fine on each of the three? Honestly I care less about the answers than about the rule he's running underneath. As far as I can tell his record so far is consistent with at least a couple of different rules — something like double effect (harming as a means vs. as a foreseen side effect), or something like "you may redirect an existing threat but may not start a new process of your own," or maybe those two secretly collapse into one rule and I'm missing it. Spell out which rules fit his record, what each one predicts for cases (1)–(3), which of the three best splits the rules apart, and then what further case you'd want his verdict on to really pin him down.
