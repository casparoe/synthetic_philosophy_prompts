In a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion we've been reading Freud's account of religion as illusion — the analysis in The Future of an Illusion, where the key distinction is that an illusion is a belief held not because of its evidential warrant but because it fulfills a deep human wish, most centrally the wish that the benevolent father-figure of childhood be found again behind the impersonal forces of nature, and the companion wish for compensation for suffering and, ultimately, for death. A friend of mine in computer science insists that this analysis simply cannot touch the contemporary transhumanist movement — cryonics, radical life extension, mind uploading, and the expectation of a benevolent superintelligence — because, he says, these projects are explicitly secular, invoke no supernatural agents, and rest on extrapolations from current science rather than on revealed doctrine. He thinks Freud's category of illusion only applies to supernatural, culturally transmitted belief systems, not to technological prognostication.

I'm not sure he's right, and I'd like you to play out what Freud himself would have said had he been confronted with these movements. Specifically: would the central claim — that a belief qualifies as an illusion in his technical sense when its motivating ground is a wish, regardless of whether the belief happens to be true — reach the transhumanist's hope for immortality through uploading, or the singularitarian's hope for a superhumanly competent and caring intelligence? Map the correspondences carefully: what, if anything, plays the role of the omnipotent father; whether the oceanic feeling Freud discussed in correspondence with Romain Rolland and at the opening of Civilization and Its Discontents reappears in transhumanist rhetoric about transcendence and cosmic-scale futures; and whether the promise that death is merely a technical problem awaiting a technical solution is a continuation of, rather than a break with, the religious wish Freud anatomized. Then consider the complications in both directions: the death drive from Beyond the Pleasure Principle might suggest that no human being could actually *want* literal endless existence, which would make transhumanism not wish-fulfillment but something stranger; and Freud's insistence that religion must yield to the scientific spirit raises the awkwardness that the transhumanists present themselves precisely as the party of scientific rationality. So my question is twofold: would Freud have classified these movements as religion's heir, religion's continuation in secular dress, or something his framework genuinely fails to cover, and where do you think his framework breaks down as a diagnostic instrument here? Please note explicitly where you're reporting Freud's own reasoning and where you're extrapolating beyond anything he wrote.
