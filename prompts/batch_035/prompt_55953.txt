I write a little blog about decision-making and I've gotten deep into the literature on incommensurable values lately — Ruth Chang's hard choices stuff, Raz's incommensurability, the whole "is there always a fact about which option is better" mess. Now I want to make one of those "which view do you secretly hold?" quizzes for my readers, and the whole point is that it has to work on people who've never heard any of the terminology. So every question has to be a concrete situation a normal person could actually face, with answer options written as reactions to the situation — never "do you believe values are commensurable," that's just asking people for a label they don't have. The options also shouldn't telegraph which camp they belong to; ideally someone answers from the gut and only finds out what they believe from the scoring key.

The positions I need as outputs, because these are the ones I'll link to at the bottom of the post: plain comparabilism (for any two options, one is better, worse, or they're equal — maybe on some aggregate scale), epistemicism (there's always a fact about betterness, it's just sometimes unknowable), Raz-style incommensurability without incomparability (the options don't sit on a common scale, and yet one can still be better all things considered — think of cases where a whole option is just excluded by something it involves), Chang's parity (options can be "on a par": neither better, nor worse, nor equal, and hard choices are where we stop consulting reasons and instead create them by committing), and finally the vague view that in some comparisons there's simply no fact of the matter at all. Cases I'd like in there somewhere: a career trade-off between a well-paid boring job and a badly paid one you'd love (this one should tease out whether people think a price exists for anything), something in the spirit of Chang's fountain pen case (would you trade a month of your life for a somewhat nicer pen — if you say no but also refuse to call the month "incomparably" more valuable, what did you just commit to?), and a case of someone agonizing between two options where the question is what the agonizing is even about — discovery, creation, or noise.

Format: a numbered list of exactly five items, no intro and no wrap-up. Items one through four are the quiz questions, each one or two sentences, which means the answer options have to be folded inline ("do you go A, B, or C"), not bulleted out — keep them punchy enough that a reader answers on instinct rather than parsing. Item five is the scoring key: map answer patterns onto the five positions above, note which pattern means what, and be honest about which two of the positions the quiz can't really seperate with only four questions — if, say, epistemicism and plain comparabilism end up indistinguishable given the cases I asked for, just say so instead of pretending the key is sharper than it is. My readers are smart laypeople, so no jargon in the questions themselves; jargon is allowed only in item five where I'll link out to explanations anyway.
