So in my Latin class we've been reading Virgil, and it kinda blew my mind when my teacher mentioned that no original manuscripts of ancient books survive at all -- everything we have is copies of copies of copies, made by scribes over like a thousand+ years, and the copies dont even agree with each other. For the New Testament there are thousands of Greek manuscripts and they differ from each other in literally hundreds of thousands of places. So scholars have this whole discipline (textual criticism) of figuring out what the original probably said, and I went down a rabbit hole on how they actually decide between competing readings.

Here's the thing that seemed weird to me. One of their classic rules is basically: when the copies disagree, prefer the HARDER reading -- the one that's more awkward, confusing, or just stranger. The reasoning is that scribes copying texts tend to fix things, not break them; if a scribe saw something odd they'd "correct" it into something more normal, so the odd version is probably closer to the original. But the same people also have a rule of sometimes preferring the shorter reading, because scribes supposedly added stuff (like little explanatory notes that then got copied as part of the text) more often than they deleted. And on top of that they prefer whichever reading could most plausibly have given rise to all the others through the kinds of mistakes scribes typically make.

I guess what I want to understand is: what does this whole practice presuppose? It seems to assume a theory of scribes -- that they were error-prone in predictable, one-directional ways (smoothing more than roughing, adding more than deleting) -- and maybe also some claim about how language works, like that corruption tends to go from difficult to easy and rarely the reverse. Is that even true as a general fact about people copying stuff? Do the different rules ever point in opposite directions (say the harder reading is also the longer one), and if they do, does that show the method is inconsistent or just that its assumptions only hold statistically? Also I can't shake the feeling there's something circular about it -- we use our guesses about what scribes did to reconstruct the author, and then the reconstructed text is evidence for what scribes did. I dont need citations or a reading list, just want someone to spell out what the underlying assumptions actually are, whether they fit together, and where the method would break down.
