I teach a second-year ethics seminar at a university in the north of England — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session — and last night I had a long, genuinely unresolved argument with an old friend that I would like to turn into next week's class. She holds the ordinary view: a driver who texts, mounts the pavement and kills a child is worse than the driver who texts and happens to arrive home uneventfully; outcomes are part of what we judge, and pretending otherwise is philosophy's fastidiousness. I found myself defending something like the control principle — that we only blame people for what was under their control — and noticed that neither of us could budge the other, largely because we were both reasoning from the armchair.

So I want an activity in which students feel the tension before it is named. My sketch, which you should improve or replace: students in pairs play "drivers" making a decision (drive tired, send one more text, take the risky overtake) while other students sit on "panels" that assign blame and punishment. A die or a shuffled deck of cards — rigged or otherwise — determines the outcome of the identical decision, and crucially the panels record sealed judgements of blameworthiness before the outcome is revealed and then re-judge afterwards. I want the design to expose the gap between the two verdicts, and ideally to separate at least resultant luck from circumstantial luck (some drivers should never even encounter the hazard). Please supply full rules, materials, timing, and a payoff or penalty scheme that doesn't involve real money but still makes verdicts feel consequential.

Then the debrief: map the moment of the reveal onto the philosophical positions, without lecturing up front — I'd rather the students discover that their own blame tracked the die, and then have to decide whether that was a mistake or their correct verdict all along. Do tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect students will behave strategically once they smell the point), and be honest about which part of the problem — constitutive luck, I assume — no game with dice can reproduce and I will have to supply by talking. If it helps, the students will have read nothing on this topic beforehand; assume they meet the literature only after the activity.
