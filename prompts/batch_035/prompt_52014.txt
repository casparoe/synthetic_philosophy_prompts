I am a novelist two-thirds of the way through a book about estranged siblings in a community reckoning, decades later, with an atrocity both took part in. One sibling was raised to believe that only guilt — the acute, act-specific dread of having wronged a particular victim, answerable through confession and reparation — can carry moral weight; the other was raised inside an older ethic of shame, in which the self is seen through the eyes of an internalized community and wrongdoing threatens who one is, not merely what one did. In the climactic scene the two must co-author their family's public accounting of the atrocity, and I refuse to write it as a caricature in which one ethic simply wins. To write their drafting scenes honestly, I need a rigorously fair statement of what each position can and cannot sign.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of emotion and moral psychologist, long experienced in moderating adversarial collaborations, and think carefully before you draft — first working out where the two camps have been talking past each other, and only then composing the document.

Draft the joint statement that a committed guilt-defender and a committed shame-defender could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, covering both of the questions on which they entered: (a) which emotion is the better moral emotion, where "better" means better for moral life — motivating repair, restraining cruelty before the fact, tracking wrongdoing accurately, surviving forgiveness — and not which feels better; and (b) whether a culture can deliberately choose its dominant moral emotion, or whether such a shift is something that only happens to cultures retrospectively. Bear in mind that the shame-defender is not defending humiliation, stigma, or face-saving, but shame in roughly the philosophical sense of the self judged through an internalized other; and that the guilt-defender is not defending scrupulosity or self-punishment, but guilt as precise, act-specific, and dischargeable through reparation. The document must record the claims both sides accept (about what each emotion registers, what it moves people to do, and where each predictably misfires); the exact residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes — including at least one about whether shame is inherently global and self-condemning or can be as act-specific as guilt, and at least one about whether an emotion embedded in an audience can survive a community's disintegration; and, for each crux, the kind of argument or evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Do not cite specific studies, authors, or works, and do not include references — where a crux is empirical, describe the evidence abstractly, in terms of what would be observed rather than who observed it.

Structure the statement as exactly five numbered sections, in this order: (1) agreed claims; (2) disagreements, each given in both camps' accepted wording; (3) cruxes; (4) for each crux, the argument or evidence both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded; (5) each side's closing paragraph on which disagreements struck it, in drafting, as genuinely substantive and which now strike it as verbal or the product of earlier talking-past-each-other — the one place in the document where the single-voice convention may lapse. Nowhere else should the joint voice break.
