My brother and his girlfriend were home for the holidays and got into their now-annual fight about evolutionary psychology, and this year my brother decided to settle it by reading aloud from some pop-science book he's been pushing on everyone (I didn't catch the title, honestly I was half listening until I realized I was going to be asked to pick a side). The paragraph he read went roughly like this:

"In their famous 1979 paper, Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin demolished adaptationism. They showed that most traits are not adaptations at all but architectural byproducts — spandrels — like the spaces between the arches of San Marco's cathedral in Venice, which look designed to hold the gorgeous paintings they contain but are really just inevitable consequences of putting arches next to each other. Because natural selection can only optimize one thing at a time, and because organisms are bundles of compromises, the search for an adaptive story behind every trait is pseudo-science, just-so storytelling. In effect, they proved that most of what passes for evolutionary explanation, especially where human behavior is concerned, is unfalsifiable."

He then declared victory: his girlfriend's favorite claim, that music is an adaptation, is therefore junk science and she should stop believing it. I'm honestly inclined to take his side — the just-so story thing has always bugged me about evo psych — but a couple of things about the summary felt off. One is the San Marco bit: I thought the whole point of the analogy was that the spandrels DO end up doing something (holding the paintings) even though that's not why they exist, which seems different from the summary's version. Another is the "selection can only optimize one thing at a time" line, which sounds like a real constraint but also sounds like something a pop writer would invent to make the logic tighter than it is.

Can you go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a philosopher of biology would accept as a fair gloss, which are subtle distortions of what Gould and Lewontin actually argued, and which are just wrong? And then the part I actually care about: does my brother's conclusion about music follow from whichever accurate parts survive? Relatedly, does it matter for our argument that "spandrel" strictly means a byproduct of some other structure's growth (something that's there as a consequence, not something that's merely "non-adaptive" for whatever reason)? And am I right that there's some nerdy footnote about whether the San Marco things Gould and Lewontin pointed at are even technically spandrels, or did I dream that? I'd like to walk into next Christmas with more than a vibe.
