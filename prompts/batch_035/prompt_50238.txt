I help run a graduate reading group on well-being, and our session on hedonism ends with the heterogeneity problem — the objection, running from Butler's remark that pleasure attends our satisfactions rather than being the thing we pursue, to its modern form: that the pleasures of a good meal, a solved proof, an act of revenge, and the end of a headache seem to share no single property, so that there may be no one thing for the hedonist to be right about. I would like to close the session with a villanelle on this.

The two refrains should be the two horns of the problem: one line the hedonist's flat reply — that what these things share is simply that each feels good — and the other the objector's retort that a property that thin is no property at all. Someone who knows the literature should be able to tell which line is which from the line alone.

Each stanza should stage one concrete pleasure, and I want the instances chosen deliberately far apart in texture: the first sip of water during a fever, the click at the end of a long proof, schadenfreude at a rival's stumble, the calm after weeping, the smell of rain on hot pavement. No two of them should read as species of a common genus.

Most importantly, let the form do the arguing. In a villanelle the same two lines return whether or not the content can bear them; make each return read differently — vindicated by one instance, embarrassed by the next, stretched, quietly reinterpreted — so that the poem's real question becomes whether the recurring line is a genuine commonality or merely a word we keep saying. The closing couplet, where the two refrains finally sit side by side, should leave it genuinely open whether hedonism's thin thread holds (family resemblance, perhaps) or snaps under the last stanza's weight. Our discussion ended exactly at that unresolved point, and I want the poem to end there too.

Assume an audience that already knows what a hedonist is and what family resemblance is; allude to either freely without explaining them.
