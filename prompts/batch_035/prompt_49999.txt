"Distinguish the etiological (selected-effect) account of biological function from the causal-role account. Then apply both accounts to: (a) the human appendix, which recent work suggests shelters 'good' gut bacteria, though it presumably didn't originally form for that reason; (b) a mutant bacterial enzyme that has acquired a new catalytic activity but hasn't yet made any difference to the survival or reproduction of its bearers; (c) the non-functional eyes of a cave fish. And here's the answer I got assigned (copied as-is, typos and all):

"The etiological account says a trait's function is what it was selected for doing in the past — the reason the trait is there. The causal-role account, developed by Larry Wright, says the function is whatever the trait currently does that contributes to some capacity of the organism, like survival or reproduction. On the appendix: the etiological account gives it its old function, whatever our ancestors' appendices were selected for (probably helping digest plant matter), not harboring bacteria. The causal-role account says harboring bacteria is a genuine current function, since the appendix contributes to gut health now. So they come apart on this case: etiological gives the old function, causal-role the new one. For the mutant enzyme, since it hasn't affected fitness yet, neither account assigns a function — etiological needs selection to have acted, and the causal-role account only applies to traits that make a difference to the organism's capacities, and this one doesn't make any difference. For the cave fish eyes, the etiological account says no function, since the eyes don't do anything for the fish now; the causal-role account agrees, and this counts in its favor, because it shows causal-role is more parsimonious and doesn't posit functions for useless traits. Overall the causal-role account handles these cases better, so it looks like the correct account and the etiological one should be abandoned."
