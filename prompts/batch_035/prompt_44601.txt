I'm a high school junior writing my extended essay for my Theory of Knowledge class on whether what goes on in our minds can change what we literally see, and before I outline the paper, I want to make sure the position I've drifted into is actually consistent. My teacher suggested I stress-test my commitments before drafting rather than after, so here is where I stand, as best I can state it:

1. Perception is sometimes cognitively penetrated: our beliefs, expectations, and desires can directly alter the character of perceptual experience itself, not merely the judgments we make on top of it. I'm moved by the studies I've read about in which subjects who wanted something — thirsty people considering a bottle of water — judged it to appear closer or larger, and by first-person cases like a hill looking steeper when you are tired or carrying a heavy backpack.

2. The persistence of the Müller-Lyer illusion after you have measured the lines shows that at least the earliest stage of visual processing is encapsulated: knowledge cannot reach in and correct it. What you know changes what you judge, not what you see.

3. Perception is a basic source of justification. In ordinary conditions, when your senses are functioning and you have no reason to suspect trouble, what your experience presents gives you the right to believe it. I take it that this is what everything else — testimony, science, my own essay — ultimately rests on.

4. Expertise genuinely penetrates perception rather than post-perceptual judgment. My piano teacher literally hears voicings and errors in a recording that I cannot hear, and a trained sommelier literally tastes things a beginner does not. It is not that she notices more and then reasons quickly; the experience itself is richer.

5. I do not think there is a sharp line between "just seeing" and "seeing as." Recognizing a friend's face in a crowd simply is seeing your friend; it is not a cognitive act layered on top of some purer, interpretation-free visual input.

Something about this list feels unstable to me, but I cannot locate the problem, and I suspect that if I try to fix it myself I will just flatten it into whatever view I already find comfortable.

So: please tell me which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision, if I am allowed exactly one, would clean up the most. I would rather repair the position now than discover the crack halfway through drafting.
