A colleague in comp lit lent me a popular introduction to Borges that she swears by, and its section on his views about time says, roughly in its own words: "In his great 1946 essay Borges proved that time does not exist. Berkeley and Hume had already shown that the mind never encounters a flowing time, only memories and expectations; with the present dissolved and the past and future already gone, the self turns out to be a series of disconnected selves with no history. The Garden of Forking Paths teaches the same lesson as fiction: all possible times exist at once, and our single timeline is an illusion, which is why the story is often read as anticipating many-worlds interpretations of quantum mechanics."

My students keep arriving with versions of this and I'd like to know how much to correct. I'm inclined to think the 1946 essay is closer to a reductio he only half-believed — I remeber he somewhere describes it as a game, or as scaffolding for a bigger book he abandoned — but I don't want to overcorrect into the opposite cliché, that it was all irony and he believed nothing at all. So: which parts of the book's summary would a careful Borges reader accept, where exactly does it conflate the essay's argument with the stories' imagery, and is the all-times-at-once reading of Forking Paths actually what that story is doing, or is that something people import onto it from later physics? Go claim by claim and tell me what to correct and what to let stand.
