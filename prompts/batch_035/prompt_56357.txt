I'm a second-year student on a mathematics-and-economics programme, and our game theory module reached iterated elimination of dominated strategies this week. In the problem class we worked through a 3×3 example the lecturer constructed, and afterwards two coursemates, Julian and Farida, spent the whole coffee break arguing about what the exercise actually licenses us to conclude. I've been asked to write a short reflective summary of the seminar discussion for Friday, and I honestly cannot referee their exchange myself, so I would be grateful for a careful adjudication. Please write your answer in British English.

Here is the game (row's payoff first in each cell):

|       | L       | M       | R       |
|-------|---------|---------|---------|
| **A** | (4, 0)  | (0, 1)  | (1, 2)  |
| **B** | (1, 0)  | (4, 1)  | (2, 3)  |
| **C** | (1, 5)  | (1, 4)  | (0, 1)  |

The interesting step is the first one: C is not dominated by A or by B individually, but the 50/50 mixture of A and B yields expected payoffs of (2.5, 2, 1.5) against C's (1, 1, 0), so C is strictly dominated by the mixture. Once C is deleted, both L and M become strictly dominated for the column player by R; once those are gone, A becomes strictly dominated by B; and iterated elimination therefore "solves" the game at (B, R). I checked afterwards, incidentally, and (B, R) also happens to be the unique Nash equilibrium of the game, if that matters to the diagnosis.

The argument between them ran as follows. (1) Julian said deleting C costs nothing in the way of assumptions: whatever the column player does, the coin-flip between A and B does strictly better, so no rational player keeps C, and strict domination by a mixture stands on exactly the same footing as strict domination by a pure strategy. (2) Farida replied that it does not: "strictly better" here means strictly better *in expectation*, which only bites if you are an expected-utility maximiser with utilities that aggregate linearly across mixtures, and who can actually implement the randomisation — a player who cannot toss coins, or whose preferences violate the independence axiom, might permissibly keep C, so even the first step smuggles in more than "you would never knowingly choose something worse". (3) Julian conceded nothing but moved on: the deeper issue, he said, is that the later rounds are different in kind from the first — deleting column's L and M only makes sense if the column player has worked out that row deleted C, and deleting A needs a further layer still, so the procedure presupposes a tower of beliefs about the opponent's rationality that nobody has ever observed; but that tower is precisely what common knowledge of rationality means, it is a normative ideal rather than an empirical hypothesis, and so the derivation stands as an account of ideal play. (4) Farida answered that a normative ideal nobody satisfies is a terrible prediction: in a one-shot game against a stranger, the choice between A and B is a judgement about how many rounds of reasoning she will do — an empirical question about people — and not a consequence of any axiom, which is why iterated dominance works as a self-fulfilling convention among the trained (it will serve us all perfectly in the exam) and tells you nearly nothing about a pub game. (5) Julian closed by saying she had thereby conceded the axioms and was only complaining that people are irrational, which was never in dispute.

Could you adjudicate this move by move — who is right at each of the five steps, and why — rather than just declaring an overall winner? In particular, I'd like to know whether Farida's expected-utility objection to the first step is a real objection or a technicality; whether Julian's distinction between normative ideal and empirical prediction can carry the weight he puts on it; and whether there is anything both of them are missing about what iterated elimination with mixed strategies does and does not establish in a game like this one.
