My friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether groups can be right or wrong, as opposed to just more or less satisfied, and I came away with a set of verdicts I can't fully square. I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them. The cases:

(1) The three-judge court: Judge A accepts that the defendant made a promise and that the promise was legally binding, and finds for the plaintiff; Judge B accepts that there was a promise but not that it was binding, and finds for the defendant; Judge C accepts that the promise was binding but denies there was any promise at all, and finds for the defendant. So a majority accepts each premise, and a majority rejects the conclusion. Here I'm firmly on the side of taking the premise majorities and finding for the plaintiff; deciding the case by majority on the conclusion alone feels like it gets the law wrong, not just the count.

(2) A hiring committee where five members score candidates on research, teaching, and collegiality and then each gives an overall recommendation. Here I think taking the majority overall recommendation is perfectly fine even if it disagrees with what the majority's premise scores would imply. I don't feel the tug I felt in the courtroom at all.

(3) The same court as in (1), except the chair's vote on each premise counts double. My gut says this might actually be acceptable, which surprised my friend, since I also think collective judgments should track the group's reasons rather than anyone's authority.

(4) A legislature voting on budget lines, where the majority's preferred figure for each line, taken together, produces a deficit the constitution forbids. Here the premise-by-premise approach seems clearly wrong — the group shouldn't walk away committed to a package it deems unconstitutional — and overriding line-by-line majorities in favor of an overall constraint seems right.

So: I favor premise-based aggregation in the courtroom, conclusion-based in hiring, weighted votes on premises in a tribunal, and constraint-overriding in the budget. My friend says these can't all come from one principle, and that at least one has to go. Is he right? If there's a principle — perhaps something about which contexts demand that the collective view follow from reasons the members actually hold, and which allow the group to simply choose — I want it stated precisely enough to tell me what it implies for the case I keep going back and forth on: a faculty committee deciding tenure, where members vote on research, teaching, and service, but the final decision must be one the committee can defend in writing as following from those votes. And I want to be told honestly which of my four verdicts the principle can't accommodate.
