I direct operations at a regional crisis and emotional-support hotline. Our funding comes partly from a state contract and partly from donors, both of whom want numbers; our staff are paid supervisors and volunteer responders; and the thing we actually exist for — callers leaving less desperate than they arrived — is exactly the thing we cannot measure directly. I have been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for about a decade: long enough to know the difference between regressional, causal, extremal, and adversarial Goodhart, to have read the Manheim–Garrabrant elaboration of the taxonomy, and to know that the tails come apart every time a dashboard singles out our top performers. But knowing the taxonomy has never once helped me at ten in the morning on a Thursday when a board member proposes a new number.

The decisions are constant, and mostly mine. Every quarter someone — a board member, a contract officer, a supervisor, a well-meaning data analyst — proposes adopting, changing, or attaching consequences to a metric, and I have to bless it, kill it, or redesign it, usually within days, with no one above or below me who has read any of this. Average handle time, abandonment rate, volunteer retention, successful referrals, first-call resolution, response speed on the text line: each proposal arrives looking obviously reasonable, and some of them are quietly lethal.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run on one proposed metric in under an hour, on paper, without having to remember the taxonomy. It should list the questions in order; for each, state what a sufficient answer looks like and what a disqualifying one looks like; distinguish clearly the outcome of a plain no (never attach consequences to this) from the outcome of usable-for-monitoring-only; and mark explicitly the points at which the procedure cannot decide and a human must, so that those points do not masquerade as findings. The theory must survive the translation: I want the four Goodhart modes and the underlying structure — what a proxy is a proxy for, and how the relationship between proxy and goal breaks under optimization pressure — doing real work inside the steps rather than name-checked in a preamble, but expressed in plain words my supervisors could apply without me. And where the modes pull in different directions, resolve the tension with a definite rule rather than presenting it to me as a difficulty for further study. For example: regressional drift is everywhere and unavoidable, while adversarial pressure only begins once consequences are attached; so the procedure cannot treat this metric is imperfect and this metric determines someone's bonus as the same kind of problem — I want a rule for when a metric may be tracked despite being imperfect and when its imperfection alone disqualifies it from ever being attached to rewards, not a shrug in both directions.

Then run the procedure, step by step and in writing, on three cases:

1. A board member proposes giving supervisors a quarterly bonus for reducing average handle time below the network average. My guess is this should fail; I want the procedure to fail it at a specific step, for specific reasons, not by general apprehension.
2. Our state contract officer asks us to begin reporting successful warm transfers, defined as transfers the receiving agency accepts — a number the receiving agency controls half of and we control none of. My instinct is that this is harmless because we would only be reporting it, not rewarding anyone on it. Test that instinct and say whether it survives.
3. First-call resolution: the metric I trust most and the one our best responders are proudest of. I already suspect the procedure will tell me something here I do not want to hear — that we have let a number we cannot audit stand in for a question we never ask. Run it honestly, and if the answer is that this metric should be demoted from performance measure to open question, say exactly that.

Two commitments I need from you. First, take positions throughout: where the procedure is silent, extend it; where two steps conflict, choose one and say why; where a case is genuinely borderline, decide it one way and give the reason, rather than returning a balanced maybe for me to mull over. Second, at the end, describe the procedure's own failure modes as candidly as the ones it diagnoses — including the most predictable one, which is that I will deploy it selectively against metrics I dislike and discover reasons to waive it for metrics I like — and state plainly what the procedure cannot do. It will not tell us whether a caller was helped. I want to know exactly where its reach ends, stated as a genuine limit and not as a humility flourish.
