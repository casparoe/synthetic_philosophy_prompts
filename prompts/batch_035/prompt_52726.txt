In my bioethics course we have spent several weeks on the determination of death, and the debate over brain death strikes me as completely stalled. On one side, critics led by the neurologist D. Alan Shewmon point to well-documented cases of patients declared brain-dead whose bodies, kept on support, survived for months or even years — healing wounds, fighting infections, and in one widely discussed case gestating a fetus until viability. He takes this as evidence that the brain is not, in fact, the integrator of the body, as the whole-brain rationale requires. On the other side, defenders of the neurological standard reply that such cases are rare, machine-dependent curiosities, or that the integration of the organism was always partly a stipulation rather than an empirical discovery. Meanwhile, the practice has quietly moved on: organs are now routinely recovered after circulatory death following only a few minutes of absent pulse, on an irreversibility standard that nobody claims would satisfy the brain-death tests, and the Uniform Law Commission's recent effort to revise the Uniform Determination of Death Act collapsed in 2023 amid public distrust rather than technical disagreement. My question is not who is right but what kind of disagreement this actually is: a substantive dispute about the biological nature of death, a verbal dispute about the word "dead," a covert policy fight about organ transplantation dressed up in metaphysical language, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that no evidence could move? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — new empirical findings, a conceptual distinction, or abandoning the definitional question in favor of asking directly which criteria should govern which practices, and why.
