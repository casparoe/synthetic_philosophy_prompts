I had a long argument this weekend with a close friend, a convinced Epicurean about death, about the following: is the extinction of humanity worse than the deaths it involves, or is it just the sum of those deaths plus a lot of grief and dread? He granted that extinction would be terrible for us who anticipate it, but he insisted that if extinction were instantaneous, painless, and unannounced — a planet-sterilizing event, say, that no one ever learns is coming — then there is no further badness over and above the deaths. Nobody is deprived in a way they experience, no one suffers in the ruination, and there are no survivors left to mourn; so any claim of "surplus badness" beyond the deaths seems to require a badness that bad things can have without being bad for anyone. He thinks that's incoherent; I want to say it's real, and I'm trying to work out how. I've decided to write a short essay on this and would like your comments on my plan before I draft it.

My thesis is that extinction is worse than the aggregate of its deaths, and that the surplus is structural rather than experiential. I want to organize the essay around three candidate sources of surplus badness that I think my friend and I ran together in our argument: (a) the deprivation of the people who would otherwise have existed — the standard, familiar line; (b) the effect on the living of believing the future is secured, which I take from Samuel Scheffler's work on what he calls the afterlife conjecture — the observation that much of what makes present life and present projects meaningful depends on the assumption that humanity will continue after our deaths, so that even the sudden disappearance of the future would, if known, drain significance out of the present; and (c) what I want to call incompleteness — the thought that a world whose history is cut off is like a story whose final pages are blank, bad not because anyone is bothered by the blankness but because the value of a history, like the value of a life or a narrative, is partly a matter of how it goes on and comes to an end. My friend's no-announcement case, which he deploys against (a) and (b), is meant to be answered by (c) alone, and I'm honestly unsure whether (c) can carry that weight without collapsing into either a mere aestheticism about the universe or a controversial metaphysics on which value can exist with no valuers at all.

Specifically, I'd like your comments on four things. First, is my three-way distinction between deprivation, effects on the living, and incompleteness actually clean, or does (c) secretly reduce to (a) or (b) once spelled out? Second, is the no-announcement case a decisive test of my thesis or merely a curiosity that pushes the issue onto (c)? Third, do you see any way to defend the surplus badness while remaining agnostic about whether value requires valuers — some middle position between my friend's subjectivism and a strong objectivism about value — or does the essay have to choose? And fourth, more generally: is this thesis interesting enough to be worth writing, and is there any angle my friend and I missed entirely in our argument?
