I'm co-writing the final for an upper-level seminar on decision theory and formal epistemology. The students have already worked through the St. Petersburg paradox, the standard utility-based responses to it, and the basics of expected utility theory, so they can be assumed to know what a St. Petersburg-style game is — but I want the essay question on the Pasadena paradox to be unanswerable by recitation. A student who has merely memorized the sentence "the Pasadena game's expectation is undefined" should not be able to pass it. The question I have in mind should force three things. First, the student must explain precisely why the Pasadena game's expectation is undefined rather than merely infinite, and why that difference matters for what a resolution owes us — the St. Petersburg literature's tools were built for the infinite case, and I want the students to see that the undefined case breaks them in new ways. Second, the question should present a proposed repair and ask the student to evaluate it: imagine a colleague who suggests ranking gambles by the Cesàro mean of the partial sums of the expectation series — that is, averaging the oscillating partial sums, which happens to deliver a clean finite value for the Pasadena game — and who claims this is the principled successor to expected value. Third, the question should include a dominance twist: an "Altadena" variant in which the probabilities are the same as in the Pasadena game but every payoff is one dollar higher, and the student must say whether the proposed rule — or any rule of that general shape — can both assign the Pasadena game a definite value and still respect the intuition that a pointwise-better gamble must be preferred.

Please write the question as it would appear on the exam, assuming students have 90 minutes to answer it. Since I don't want the question to depend on recall, include a short preamble within the question itself that states both games explicitly (payoffs and probabilities) and defines the Cesàro rule, so everything needed is on the page and the intellectual work is all evaluation rather than retrieval. Then give a model answer of about 600 words, followed by a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points with marks summing to 20, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically botch it — for instance, conflating "the series diverges" with "the series fails to converge because it oscillates," or praising the Cesàro rule for its mathematical elegance without ever asking what happens to its verdicts when the game is truncated after finitely many terms or when finitely many payoffs are altered.

My own sense is that the difference lives in whether the student notices that the Cesàro rule's ranking can be made to flip under changes that leave the underlying gamble almost surely unchanged in character — or in whether they see exactly which of the intuitions at stake (definiteness of value, dominance, independence from ordering) must be sacrificed and can say why their chosen sacrifice is the cheapest one — but exercise your own judgment about where the real crux sits.
