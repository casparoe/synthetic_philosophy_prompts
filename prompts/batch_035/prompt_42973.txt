Here's the exam question as it appeared on the test:

"Consider a three-judge court deciding a contract case. Let P = 'the contract is valid,' Q = 'the defendant breached it,' and R = 'the defendant is liable,' where R is true iff both P and Q are. Judge 1 accepts P and Q; Judge 2 accepts P but rejects Q; Judge 3 rejects P but accepts Q. (a) Explain why proposition-wise majority voting generates collectively inconsistent judgments in this case. (b) Distinguish the premise-based from the conclusion-based procedure for resolving it. (c) State the impossibility result associated with List and Pettit's work on judgment aggregation: which conditions, taken together, force what kind of aggregation rule, and what exactly does the independence condition require?"

And here's the student's answer (typos theirs, not mine):

"The doctrinal paradox actually goes all the way back to Condorcet's work on juries in the 1780s, though it was rediscovered later by legal scholars debating panel decisions.

(a) With the three judges as described, a majority accepts P (judges 1 and 2) and a majority accepts Q (judges 1 and 3), but a majority rejects R (judges 2 and 3). So if the court takes a straight majority vote on each proposition, the collective accepts P, accepts Q, and rejects R, which is inconsistent given that P and Q together entail R. Majority voting is therefore inconsistent whenever the propositions on the agenda are logically interconnected in this way.

(b) There are two natural fixes. The premise-based procedure has the court vote only on the premises P and Q, and derive the collective verdict on R from the collective verdicts on those. The conclusion-based procedure takes a direct vote on R and ignores the premises entirely. The premise-based procedure is clearly the more democratic of the two, because it respects the reasons behind each judge's vote, whereas the conclusion-based one throws those reasons away.

(c) The List–Pettit result says: suppose you want an aggregation rule defined for all possible profiles of individual judgment sets (universal domain), that always outputs a consistent and complete collective judgment (collective rationality), that respects unanimity (if everyone accepts a proposition, so does the group), and that treats propositions in a systematic way -- which is basically just the anonymity requirement that everyone's vote counts equally. Then you're stuck with a dictatorship: there must be some one individual whose judgments automatically become the group's judgments. So majority rule fails collective rationality on interconnected agendas, and the theorem shows that no other anonymous rule can do any better."
