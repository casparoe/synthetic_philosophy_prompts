I write about science and ideas for a broadsheet newspaper and a long-form magazine, and I am midway through drafting a feature on the afterlife of Gödel's incompleteness theorems outside mathematics — the way they surface in arguments about artificial minds, in popular claims about the limits of scientific knowledge, and in the long-running row over the Lucas–Penrose argument that started with Lucas's 1961 paper and was renewed in Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. The half of the essay that dismantles popular misuses is going fine. My difficulty is the other half: in the course of reporting on this for years, I have accumulated a set of working beliefs about what the theorems do and do not show, and I want to know whether they are jointly tenable before anything resembling them appears in print. Here is the set.

(1) The first incompleteness theorem is a strictly mathematical result about consistent, effectively axiomatised formal theories strong enough for arithmetic, and I hold that essentially every invocation of it outside mathematics — to show that minds outstrip machines, that science cannot explain everything, that economic systems or legal systems or the internet are "Gödel-incomplete" — is rhetorical spillover that a careful editor should strike.

(2) I am nonetheless a mechanist about mathematical cognition: I see no reason why the work of doing, checking, and understanding mathematics requires anything a Turing machine could not in principle do, and if someone told me a machine faithfully simulates a competent working mathematician indefinitely, I would have no Gödelian reason to disbelieve them.

(3) At the same time, I believe that for any formal system I can specify and whose consistency I have no reason to doubt, I can in principle construct its Gödel sentence and come to accept it as true — and that this reflective step has no ceiling, since I can perform it again on the stronger system that results from adding that sentence as an axiom, and again, without end.

(4) I believe the totality of arithmetic that the human mathematical community would ever come to endorse is not the theorem set of any single formal system we could now write down — that formalisation of mathematics is always extendable and never finished, which I take to be a real and important fact, not just a sociological one.

(5) Gödel himself drew a disjunction from his results — either the human mind's mathematical capacities cannot be matched by any machine, or there exist mathematical problems that are undecidable in an absolute sense, undecidable by any human means — and I firmly reject the first horn and accept the second: there are arithmetical truths that no human procedure, however long-lived, will ever settle or even establish.

(6) The second incompleteness theorem says that a consistent theory cannot demonstrate its own consistency, yet I believe working mathematicians' everyday confidence that Peano Arithmetic is consistent is rational rather than delusional, and that it does not rest on anything embarrassingly circular.

(7) I think Penrose's argument is unsound, but I also think the standard dismissals of it are too quick — in particular, I am not persuaded that the whole thing collapses at the familiar point where the sceptic says we could never know which machine supposedly models us.

(8) Despite all my editorial striking in (1), I find that whenever I try to say what the theorems genuinely teach a lay reader, I write some version of the claim that any framework rich enough to represent its own workings cannot certify its own reliability — and I believe this one extrapolation, unlike the others, earns its keep.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture: assume I know what the first and second theorems actually state. Go through these commitments, individually and in whatever combinations matter, and tell me which combinations are genuinely in tension and which only look tense. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave; for each, say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me; and distinguish outright contradictions from merely uncomfortable cohabitations. Where a tension dissolves on inspection, show me how, rather than leaving me to wonder whether I missed something. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your answer in British English.
