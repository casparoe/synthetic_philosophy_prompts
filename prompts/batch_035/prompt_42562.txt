I'm part of a small reading group that has spent this term on decision theory, and we've just reached the material on the value of information. The result we keep circling back to is Good's theorem — the observation, going back to I. J. Good's work on the principle of total evidence, that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can never be made worse off by receiving additional information before making a decision, provided the information is free and received in time to act on it. The reasoning is that you can always plan to ignore what you learn, so having the option to condition can't lower your maximum. My group takes this to settle the matter: a rational person should accept free, reliable, decision-relevant news whenever it's offered. But when I go through my own gut reactions to concrete cases, I'm not so sure, and I'd like help figuring out what principle, if any, actually captures my verdicts — or which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are my reactions to a set of cases. A free, reliable forecast of the weather for the day of an outdoor wedding I'm planning: obviously yes, and I'd even pay a fair amount for it. A free and perfectly accurate test that would tell me whether I carry a gene that predicts a devastating untreatable disease decades out — information that would matter for my savings, my career, and whether I have children: no, I don't want it, and I don't think I'm being irrational, even though the news bears on major decisions. A tip about a small company from an acquaintance whose judgment I half-trust: I'd rather not even hear it — not because the information is likely wrong, but because I know that once it's in my head I'll act on it with more confidence than it deserves, and I'd be better off never having received it. A postmortem of a negotiation I just concluded badly, itemizing exactly which of my moves cost me what: yes, I want that, even though it comes too late to change that outcome at all. A candid account, from a close friend, of everything he privately thinks about my work and my judgment — information that would be moderately uncomfortable to hear and would bear on decisions about how much I rely on him: no, though I notice I'd say yes if the same report concerned a third party's opinion of a colleague I was considering hiring.

So the obvious principle — take information when you can act on it, refuse it when you can't — is clearly wrong: I refuse the genetic test and the friend's candor though both are actionable, and I want the postmortem though it's too late to act on. My tentative principle is something like: accept free information if and only if (i) receiving it has no direct hedonic cost, and (ii) I trust myself to update on it properly rather than overweight or underweight it. That handles the forecast, the genetic test (the dread itself is a real cost), the tip (I distrust my conditioning), and maybe the postmortem (no hedonic cost, updates my priors for next time). But I'm not confident it saves my verdict on the friend's candor — the discomfort there seems too small to be doing the work, yet refusing feels different from refusing the genetic test. Could you state the principle, or a small set of candidates, as precisely as possible, showing which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate? I'd also like its prediction on cases I haven't considered: a genetic test for a condition whose course I could substantially manage through behavior; learning the exact date of my death; and being told, before deciding whether to volunteer for a campaign, the precise margin by which the candidate will win or lose.
