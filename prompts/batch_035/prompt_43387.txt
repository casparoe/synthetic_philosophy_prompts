You are a professional philosopher of language, specializing in theories of reference, and you have been asked to advise a colleague who knows Kripke's and Putnam's core arguments well but is not a specialist in philosophy of science. Before writing anything, take a few moments to brainstorm: sketch the possible mappings of the case onto the theory, note where each mapping strains, and only then commit to the strongest one. Do not pad the answer with textbook exposition of material the reader already knows; use it only where it does dialectical work.

Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 vote in Prague as a case study in the dispute between descriptivism and the causal-historical theory of reference, and more specifically in the cluster of ideas around rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori. The salient facts, all common knowledge: when Giuseppe Piazzi discovered Ceres on New Year's Day in 1801, it was announced as a new planet, and it retained planetary status until the accumulating flood of similar bodies between Mars and Jupiter led astronomers to quietly stop calling the asteroids planets; when Eris, a trans-Neptunian body more massive than Pluto, was discovered in January 2005, the resulting pressure forced the IAU to do something it had never done — vote on an explicit definition of "planet" — and Pluto fell outside the resulting category, failing the criterion that a planet must have cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit.

Take the mapping questions seriously rather than treating them as rhetorical. "Pluto," it would seem, rigidly designated the very same object before and after the vote; nothing about the object changed. The trouble is with "planet." Did the IAU change the meaning of "planet," or did it discover something about planets — so that dynamical dominance is a hidden essence the term had all along, discovered a posteriori, making it necessarily true (though unknowably in 1930, when Tombaugh found Pluto looking for a planet) that Pluto was never a planet? Or was the vote a stipulative redrawing of a term whose extension is fixed by convention, closer to a legislative redefinition than to a discovery — and if so, does that show "planet" is unlike "water" and "gold" in a way Putnam's semantics of natural-kind terms cannot accommodate? Work the parallels and disanalogies with the canonical examples: whales turning out not to be fish, jade turning out to be two distinct minerals, Hesperus turning out to be Phosphorus. In each of those cases we say the world, not the word, was corrected; ask candidly whether the Pluto case belongs in that company, and what the disanalogy (if any) consists in — that the IAU is an authoritative body voting on definitions, that "planet" names a role rather than an underlying stuff, that there is no obvious microstructure to defer to.

Then say where the reference-theoretic account breaks down against how the astronomical community actually behaves. Note that many astronomers still reject the 2006 definition and defend a geophysical one on which Pluto plainly qualifies — is their disagreement about reference or about classification? Note the linguistic datum that people naturally say Pluto "stopped being a planet" rather than that we discovered it never was one, while nobody says we discovered Ceres was never a planet; and note the oddity of flatly asserting that the sentence "Pluto was the ninth planet" was always false. Finally, answer the two closing questions directly: does the episode vindicate descriptivism in some restricted domain, vindicate the causal theorist, or show that "planet" sits uneasily between kind terms and terms of art? And does the whole affair teach anything at all about rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, or is it merely a case about words, in which the philosophical apparatus adds drama but not understanding? Structure your answer so the mapping is explicit, and be honest about the points where you are forcing the theory to speak.
