I teach high school physics, and last week a student stayed behind after class to ask whether the wavefunction is a real thing out there in the world or just math we do to predict stuff. I gave her the line I've been leaning on for years, that I picked up from some teacher workshop or pop-science book ages ago: it's like the odds a bookmaker posts. A probability distribution is a summary of someone's knowledge, and when you measure and the wavefunction "collapses," that's just you updating your info when the race is run. It's a nice line — it makes the kids relax about all the "the particle is in two places at once" spookiness.

But my brother-in-law, who works in quantum computing, was over for dinner and basically told me that analogy is the actual live fight in philosophy of physics, not a settled thing at all — he muttered something about psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic — and he said there's a no-go theorem from around 2012 that's supposed to show the wavefunction can't be just knowledge, as long as quantum mechanics itself is right. So now I'm worried I've been confidently teaching something dodgy at a blackboard for a decade.

Can you audit the bookmaker analogy for me? Like, a real correspondance table — what's supposed to play the role of the bookmaker, the odds, the race, the update — and then where the mapping breaks down. I gather interference is supposed to be a problem (amplitudes can cancel in a way probabilities don't?) but I don't see why that's fatal rather than just bookkeeping. And does that theorem actually settle it, or do the knowledge-side people have a way out? Also, what does the analogy quietly smuggle in — my gut says it's telling the kids "there's nothing weird at the bottom of physics," which sounds like a philosophical add-on rather than something quantum mechanics itself licenses. And if the analogy doesn't survive, is there a better one I could actually use in class that's honest but won't freak out fifteen-year-olds?

sorry for the wall of text, this is bothering me way more than I expected it to.
