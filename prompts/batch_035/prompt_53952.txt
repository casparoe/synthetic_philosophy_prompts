I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, but this term I've been roped into TA-ing an intro-ish course on vagueness, and I want to hand my students something better than the standard "here are five -isms, memorize them for the exam" sheet. What I'd like is a decision tree that starts from intuitions about cases and only *then* attaches names, so it sorts people by what they actually believe rather than by which label they've decided to like. The root should be something like the sorites series itself (bald men / heaps / tall people, whatever concrete case you prefer), and the branch questions should be answerable by someone who has never heard the word "supervaluationism" — things like: would a single hair ever make the difference between bald and not bald; when you point at a clear borderline case, is "that's red" true, false, neither, or does it depend on what the conversation has fixed; could an omniscient being in principle know the exact cutoff, or is there nothing there to know; do you accept bivalence for vague predicates or not, and if not, whether the failure shows up in truth itself or only in assertibility; do "definitely red"-style operators iterate in a way that should worry us.

The leaves need to include at least epistemicism, supervaluationism, a degree-theoretic/fuzzy option, the nihilist escape ("nobody is bald, nothing is a heap"), and some contextualist or pragmatic option, plus whatever else falls out of the logical space you build — and I'd like you to construct the tree from the questions first, then locate the positions inside it, rather than working backwards from the labels. If some cell of the logical space is unoccupied (say: sharp cutoffs exist but are unknowable-in-principle rather than merely unknown, or: no cutoffs but bivalence survives anyway), flag it and say a sentence about why nobody lives there — incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Also please be honest about pairs of positions your tree can't actually seperate, like certain forms of supervaluationism and certain forms of contextualism, rather than inventing fake criteria that only work if you already know the answer.

Two more things. First, size: aim for somewhere between six and nine branch questions and no more than ten leaves, since it's a one-page handout. Second, and this is partly for my own curiosity: I'll tell you my own answers, and at the very end I want you to run me through the tree. I say a single hair can never make the difference; I say "that's red" said of a borderline patch is neither true nor false; but I get nervous about gaps for simple present-tense observation statements, and I kind of want "there's no fact of the matter" to be a fact about our linguistic practice rather than about the world; and I find the idea that there's a sharp unknown cutoff genuinely repugnant. So end with a line like \answer{...} containing just the name of the leaf where I land (or the closest match, if I'm inconsistent — in which case tell me which pair of my answers is doing the fighting).
