"The fitting-attitude analysis holds that x is valuable iff it is fitting to have a pro-attitude toward x -- to desire it, admire it, be pleased by it, and so on. The wrong kind of reasons problem starts from cases where we seem to have a reason to hold an attitude that has nothing to do with its object's value. Example: a demon tells you he will torment you for the rest of your life unless you fear him. The threat gives you a reason to fear the demon, yet it makes him no more fearsome: fear produced by the threat would be unfitting, and the demon does not become any more valuable for being feared under duress. Such reasons are of the wrong kind because they bear on the attitude itself rather than on its object -- they give you a reason to be afraid, not a reason to find him fearsome. The line of thought goes back to Brentano, who grounded value in emotions that are appropriate to their objects, and it received its biggest boost from Moore's open question argument in Principia Ethica, which showed that goodness is a non-natural property -- specifically, the property of being a fitting object of pro-attitudes. As for a response: notice that the demon's threat cites nothing fearsome about the demon. A consideration that could not possibly make the fear fitting is not a reason at all -- it is mere causal pressure, like a gun to the head that makes you hand over your wallet: the gun doesn't give you a reason to hand it over, it just makes you do it. So the threat was never in the running as a reason, and once this is seen the problem dissolves: the analysis was only ever restricted to genuine reasons, and the wrong kind never existed -- we simply mistook compulsion for justification. The demon case then teaches the right lesson: you can be made to fear without having any reason to fear, which is all the FA analysis ever needed."
