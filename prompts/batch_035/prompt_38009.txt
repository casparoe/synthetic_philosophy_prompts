I am a novelist at work on a book whose central relationship is between a poet and her most devoted reader: a woman who has trusted the poet's taste for thirty years, reads whatever the poet recommends, and once abandoned a novel she was halfway through and loving because the poet dismissed it over dinner. I want the book to engage the philosophical problem of aesthetic testimony — roughly, whether a judgment about beauty or artistic merit can ever be genuinely known on another person's say-so, rather than merely believed — but dramatized through scene rather than argued in lecture.

Please construct a worked example of the conceptual issue for me. Specifically, I want a pair of situations involving these two characters: one in which the reader's deference to the poet's verdict seems to amount to real knowledge — accepting the testimony got her something she could not otherwise have obtained — and one in which it seems to leave her with a belief she is entitled to hold but could never defend or, perhaps, even fully understand. The difference between the two situations should turn on something more precise than the crude fact that she read the work herself in one case and not the other. You might vary, for instance, the kind of judgment being deferred to: whether some work is worth reading at all versus a verdict on the merit of a poem she has already read and loved; or the role the judgment plays in her own life and future choices; or whether the poet offers reasons alongside the verdict or the verdict alone.

Then, in a closing paragraph I can pin above my desk while drafting, explain what the pair of examples is supposed to show: which assumption of the view that aesthetic knowledge can never be testimonial do they jointly put pressure on, and where does that view still have a reply.

One constraint: no citations, no bibliography, no names of philosophers or papers. I am not collecting references; I need the shape of the difficulty itself, stated so that I can bend a scene around it.
