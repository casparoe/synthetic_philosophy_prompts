For three years I have been arguing with my flatmate about how our college's discretionary hardship fund should be spent, and I have recently started writing down her verdicts, because I suspect there is a single principle behind them that she has never once stated out loud. The track record so far: for a first-year with a spinal injury who needs a ground-floor room and a powered chair, and whose family cannot help, she ruled that the college must pay for both in full, not merely the room; for a student who arrived indifferent to sport, got passionately into competitive rowing, and now claims his life is blighted unless the fund covers his equipment and race travel, she ruled not a penny — that is his project, not a hardship; for a student raised in genuine poverty, on the minimum grant, who has declined every top-up, travel award, and room upgrade offered to her, says she wants for nothing, and is by all appearances as content as anyone in college, my flatmate ruled that the college should raise her grant anyway and not take no for an answer; for a student whose chronic depression simply arrived, with no triggering misfortune behind it, she ruled that a full course of private therapy should be funded; and for a well-off, healthy student who asked for extra money purely on the grounds that he was persistently miserable — homesickness, mostly — she ruled no, saying that being unhappy is not by itself a claim on the fund.

Next term three new cases come up, and I would like to predict her verdicts before she delivers them. The first is a student whose stipend and resources are identical to everyone else's, but who comes from a family and community in which it is taken for granted that a daughter does not travel alone, dine out, or spend money on herself: her allowance goes home each month or sits untouched, she insists she is content, and she has asked for nothing. The second is a student with no impairment, no diagnosis, the full grant, and — as far as anyone can tell — every real freedom her classmates enjoy, who has been gloomy for as long as anyone can remember and now requests a larger room allowance on the sole ground that pleasanter surroundings would raise his wellbeing. The third is a student deaf from birth, a fluent signer, settled and flourishing in the Deaf community, who asks for nothing, while the fund sits on an unspent earmark for hearing technology that will otherwise lapse at year's end; here I want to know whether she would let the earmark lapse, convert it into unrestricted money he may spend as he pleases, or hold that the college ought in some way to press the technology on him.

Our moral philosophy tutor frames these rows as a single question — equality of what? — with welfare, resources, and capabilities as the candidate answers, and I suspect my flatmate has an answer to it that she has never articulated. So what I want from you is this: first, the rule or rules that fit her track record equally well (welfare looks dead on arrival, but I cannot tell which of the surviving candidates she is really running, or whether she needs the brute-luck / option-luck distinction I have seen invoked somewhere in this literature); second, for each of the three new cases, what each surviving rule predicts and precisely where the rules come apart on it; third, your best guess at her actual verdicts, with your reasons, including any point at which her own record seems internally strained; and finally, the single further case, of your own devising, that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules — the case I should engineer our next argument around.
