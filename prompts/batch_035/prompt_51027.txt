I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about what a group's judgment is when its members disagree, and I want to know whether one principle captures them, or whether I have to give something up. Case one: a three-judge panel decides a negligence claim, where liability requires both that a duty of care was owed and that it was breached. Judge A accepts duty, breach, and liability. Judge B accepts duty but denies breach, and so finds no liability. Judge C denies duty but accepts breach, and so finds no liability. Majorities accept each premise, yet a majority finds no liability, and my verdict is that the panel's judgment is no liability: count heads on the bottom line. Case two: a five-member hiring committee governed by a written rule that a candidate is appointed iff the research record clears the bar and the teaching record clears the bar. Two members accept both premises and vote to appoint; a third accepts research but not teaching and votes against; a fourth accepts teaching but not research and votes against; a fifth accepts neither and votes against. So majorities clear both bars, while a majority votes against appointing. My verdict here is that the committee appoints: the premises are the committee's own official inputs, so majorities on the inputs determine the output. Case three: a seven-member advisory panel reports on whether a dam is unsafe, where unsafe means the structural survey flags a fault or the load test fails. Majorities say the survey flags nothing and the test does not fail, but a majority reports the dam unsafe, and my verdict is that the report should say unsafe: they were asked for a bottom-line judgment, and that is what should be aggregated. Now the case that keeps me up at night: the hiring committee again, identical rule, identical tallies, except that one of the three voting against is a member who accepted both premises — research clears, teaching clears — and still voted not to appoint, offering no further reason. My gut says don't appoint: his vote looks like an error or an unspoken reservation, and it seems wrong for the premise-majority machinery to override a majority that includes him. But if that is right, the rationale I gave for case two, official rule plus official inputs, cannot be the whole story, since it delivers the same verdict in both. Finally, a case I genuinely cannot decide: the same committee when the rule book is silent on how votes are combined and the chair must simply declare a result. Is there a principle that fits all my verdicts, or close to it? State it precisely enough to decide new cases, tell me what it says about the silent-rulebook case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — I would rather know whether to keep them all at the price of a baroque principle or accept a simpler one that overturns a verdict. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter to a colleague.
