At the last meeting of my writers' circle, two of the members fell out over a passage from my novel-in-progress, and I would value a careful adjudication of their exchange. The passage concerns my protagonist, a woman in her fifties who declines to report her brother's fraud, telling herself that she has no choice in the matter: she was raised to keep family business inside the family, and she simply is not the sort of person who could walk into a police station. Marcus, who studied philosophy years ago, said this is bad faith in Sartre's sense almost by definition: she is a consciousness pretending to be a fixed object with a nature, fleeing the anguish of her own freedom by treating her upbringing and her character as things that decide on her behalf. Ingrid replied that the accusation presupposes she could have done otherwise, and no fictional character can — I wrote her declination into the manuscript, and in a strict sense every choice she appears to make is mine; her insistence that she has no choice is, uniquely in her case, simply true, and a truth cannot be a lie she tells herself. Marcus countered that Ingrid's point proves too much: our genes, our upbringing and our circumstances also fix a great deal about each of us, and if background determination dissolved responsibility, no one could ever be in bad faith at all, whereas Sartre's claim is precisely that a person is never identical with her facticity — she is her relation to it — so the novelist's determinism adds nothing that ordinary facticity does not already supply. Ingrid's final word was that Marcus is equivocating on what "could have done otherwise" means: for a real person, the counterfactual is grounded in facts about her own deliberative powers, but for my character there is no fact of the matter at all, since the version of her who telephones the police is not a manuscript I ever wrote; if she is in bad faith, it is only because readers choose to take her as free, and the freedom resides in the reading rather than in her. They then turned to me, and I discovered that I cannot answer the question of my own protagonist's freedom from the author's chair. Please reply in exactly five numbered points, each no more than a sentence or two, with nothing before the first point or after the last: the first four should adjudicate each of the four moves in their exchange in the order given, and the fifth should say what, if anything, both of them have missed about how Sartre's radical freedom applies — or fails to apply — to a written character, and which of them comes out more nearly right overall.
