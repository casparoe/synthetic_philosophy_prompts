I'm taking a phil of language course this semester and the sorites stuff is breaking my brain a bit, so I want to try to figure out what I actually think by laying out my gut verdicts and seeing if there's a principle behind them. Here's where my gut currently stands. On the forced march (asked one at a time whether n grains is a heap, starting at 10,000), every individual "yes" feels fine, and there's no single step where flipping to "no" feels justified — but I don't think that means anything goes. A friend who says "not a heap" at 9,999 grains but "heap" at 9,998, for no reason, is doing something wrong. At the same time, if you show me the conditional "if 9,999 grains is a heap then 9,998 grains is a heap," point blank and out of context, I'm happy to deny it. And yet in the sequence, answering "yes" then "no" at two adjacent steps strikes me as flatly contradictory, worse than the weird friend. Relatedly, for the "small numbers" version (if 0 is small, so is 1, etc.), the universal generalization "every instance holds" feels basically true to me, but each instance taken alone feels deniable. That pair I genuinely cannot reconcile.

Then there's the hidden-removal version: if someone steals one grain from my sandpile every night for years, and I only ever see the pile at the beginning and the end, I have the strong intuition that there was a last night on which it was a heap — some specific night, even if nobody could ever know which. That sounds like it commits me to a sharp cutoff, which I don't want: I'd swear up and down that there's no number n such that n grains is a heap and n−1 is not. My flatmate says these two verdicts can't both be true and that I'm secretly an epistemicist in denial, which is infuriating but might be right. One more wrinkle: I think there's a real difference between whether the pile IS a heap and whether I could KNOW it's a heap. If a pile is right at whatever the boundary zone is, someone confidently asserting "that's a heap" seems to be guessing rather than knowing — like there's a margin for error on knowledge but not on truth. But I'm suspicious of how far that generalizes. And separately, with legally sharp lines (say a theft statute drawing a dollar threshold for a felony), I'm totally fine with the sharpness being stipulated, in a way I'm not fine with for "heap" — not sure if that's principled or just me being used to law.

So: is there one account that captures all or most of these verdicts — the sequence/instance asymmetry, the hidden-removal intuition, the no-sharp-cutoff commitment, the knowledge-margin thing, the law/heap contrast — and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up and why? I know the standard families of views (supervaluationism, epistemicism, degree/many-valued stuff, contextualism) well enough at this point, so feel free to build on or hack apart any of them, but I care more about getting a principle that fits MY cases than about which famous view it resembles. Please also tell me what the principle predicts about a case I haven't mentioned — I'm curious about the one where you remove 5,000 grains from the pile and then add the exact same 5,000 grains back. And since I know how motivated my own reasoning about this can be, tell me honestly where your proposed principle is most likely to fail and what would actually get you to switch to a different verdict on the cases above — don't just present it as obviously correct.
