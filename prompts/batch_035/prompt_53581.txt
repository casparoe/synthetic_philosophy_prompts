I'm a software engineer who spends most of my working life on search and retrieval infrastructure, and I recently reread Borges's "The Library of Babel" for the first time since college. I know it's usually read as a fable about infinity, or information, or theology, but what struck me this time is how much of it is really about the thing I do for a living. The library contains every possible 410-page book over twenty-five characters, so every coherent text — the true history of the future, the vindication of every person, every book anyone has ever wanted — is guaranteed to be in there somewhere, surrounded by overwhelmingly more noise. Nobody in the story doubts the books exist; the whole tragedy is that nobody can find them. That looks a lot like a distinction I work with constantly: between a dataset containing an answer and an index that can actually deliver it. So I'd like a reading of the story through the philosophy of computational complexity — tractability, brute force versus clever search, compressibility as a marker of meaning — rather than the usual lenses.

A few angles I'd want you to take seriously, and to push back on where they don't fit. First, the sects: the Purifiers who destroy gibberish books, and the believers in a total catalogue somewhere in the library. Can these be read as two rival strategies for making an intractable search problem tractable — one tries to prune the search space, the other to build an oracle — and is Borges right that both fail, or is he quietly assuming something like P ≠ NP as a fact about the universe? Second, the narrator's observation that for almost any gibberish book, some language could be invented in which it counts as a coherent and profound text: is that a claim that meaning is just a matter of decompression given the right model, and does it threaten the idea that meaningful texts are the compressible ones? Third, and most important to me: the story seems to dramatize the position that existence and epistemic availability come apart — the true books exist but are as good as nonexistent, and finding them is not merely slow but effectively impossible. I gather some philosophers think that if hard problems are genuinely intractable, this changes what agents can know rather than just how long knowing takes. Does the story support that stronger view, or does it cheat by making the noise so close to measure one in a way real search problems never are?

I should admit I have no philosophy background — I know the terms P and NP in an engineering way, not a careful way — so please be explicit about where you're using complexity informally as a metaphor and where something rigorous is actually being claimed. And where the story resists the computational reading, say so plainly: the narrator's closing faith that the library's order proves a demiurge feels like it's about something search can't capture, and I'd like to know whether that's a limitation of my lens or precisely the point Borges is making.
