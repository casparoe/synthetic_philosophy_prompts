so a bit about me: I spent 40 years in condensed matter physics, retired two years ago, and have since fallen down the philosophy of math rabbit hole. one thing I keep running into is what people call Benacerraf's dilemma — the tension between wanting our account of mathematical truth to treat math sentences like any other true sentences (truth conditions, reference, the works) and wanting an account of how creatures like us, causally stuck in spacetime, know anything about objects that aren't anywhere and don't do anything. my instinct, from decades of building models, is to write the spec first: what any adequate theory of mathematical knowledge has to deliver, before I go shopping among platonism, structuralism, formalism, nominalism, the rest.

so write me that requirements list. roughly what I have in mind, but use your judgement:

- which phenomena it has to reproduce: the certainty of proof compared to empirical conjecture, the objectivity of mathematical disagreement (two mathematicians arguing isn't like two people arguing about taste), the convergence of mathematicians who never met, the fact that we do sometimes get it wrong (flawed proofs that fooled everyone for a while), and the fact that math keeps working in physics — unreasonably well, as Wigner famously complained
- the distinctions it cant collapse: knowing a theorem vs knowing the theory is consistent, truth vs provability (gödel is presumably relevant here), and whatever distinction separates counting on my fingers from transfinite set theory
- what it has to be consistent with on the cognitive side: our number sense evolved for counting and tracking, so the theory can't pretend we have some magic faculty with no story about where it came from

then the part I most want: mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are one camp's prejudice smuggled in as a neutral demand — I suspect causal contact with abstract objects is the platonist's opponent demanding a physics-style explanation where none should be expected, but maybe its the other way around, you tell me. and crucially, is there a subset of the requirements that no theory could jointly satisfy? if so the spec itself is the finding, like a no-go theorem, and I'd want that stated plainly rather than hidden in footnotes.

no scorecard against the isms, I'll do that in my reading group myself. what I need is the requirements with the case behind each one. a table is fine. plain language please — I've only been at this a few years and never formally.
