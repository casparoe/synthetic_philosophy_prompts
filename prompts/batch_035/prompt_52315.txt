I teach an evening class on the philosophy of money (mostly retirees, very opinionated ones), and this term's unit is Aristotle. We're doing the exchange material in Nicomachean Ethics Book V — the builder and the shoemaker, the idea that there's no exchange without equality and no equality without commensurability, money as the convention that makes dissimilar things commensurable because everything is measured by it — alongside the oikonomia versus chrematistikē contrast from Politics Book I, where natural household wealth-getting is fine and the unlimited money-making kind is disparaged. The students always walk away with wildly different impressions of what Aristotle actually thought money *is*, so this time I want to catch that with a quiz before they've read any secondary literature and hardened into labels.

So: please write me a "which reading of Aristotle do you actually hold?" quiz, eight to ten questions, each a concrete case the students can answer without knowing any of the scholarly terminology. Cases like: the shoemaker demands five pairs of shoes for one house and insists he's owed nothing more, on the grounds that both sides freely agreed; or a merchant who buys grain low in one city and sells high in another, never lying to anyone; or a farmer who refuses to sell at all because he needs the grain for his household — that sort of thing. Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the interpretive positions: money as a mere convention that creates commensurability by agreement, commensurability as genuinely grounded in need (the chrēmata reading), money as measure whose value itself has to come from somewhere, the reading where Aristotle's demand for equality in exchange is really about sustaining the association between craftspeople rather than about prices, and so on. I'd also like a leaf for the position that the whole apparatus is a bad fit and Aristotle is doing something else entirely, if you think that's a real position someone could hold.

One complication my co-tutor and I keep fighting about: he reckons Aristotle's talk of need amounts to a proto labour theory of value, and I think that's anachronistic — we're reading 19th-century economics backwards into a discussion about how a polis holds together. Could you make sure one of your branch questions sorts those two stances apart, phrased as a case rather than a question about labels? For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the thing a student would wince at being committed to), and please flag any two leaves your scoring key can't actually separate. And since the scholarly ground here is shakier than a quiz format makes it look, I'd like a closing note on where you think the key is most likely to misfire: which branch questions secretly turn on a contested translation or reading of the Greek, and what would have to be true of the text for you to redesign the tree rather than just tweak a question or two.
