I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background who has been drifting into causal inference for practical reasons — our growth team's dashboards keep drawing causal conclusions from observational data, and I've been trying to understand when that's defensible and when it isn't. At an internal tech talk on "why you can't get causation from correlation alone," a senior data scientist gave us a capsule history of the philosophy of causation, roughly as follows: there are two rival theories. The older, counterfactual theory says a cause is what makes a difference — if the match hadn't been struck, there would have been no fire, so the striking caused the fire. The newer, interventionist theory associated with Judea Pearl rejects this as circular and unscientific, because we can never observe the counterfactual world where the match wasn't struck. Instead, it defines causation in terms of manipulation: X causes Y if intervening to set X changes Y, where an intervention is like a surgical operation that cuts off all other influences on X. She added two corollaries. First, interventionism implies there is no causation in domains where no one could possibly intervene — so on this view there is no causation in fundamental physics, since no experimenter can manipulate the gravitational constant. Second, once you look closely, setting X by intervention is really just conditioning on X after you've controlled for the confounders, which brings the whole apparatus back to ordinary statistics. Her takeaway was that Pearl's framework is a rigorous repackaging of the old counterfactual idea rather than a genuinely different theory, and that the philosophical rivalry is mostly a matter of style.

I found this pretty compelling at the time, but when I tried to repeat it to a friend who studied philosophy, she looked skeptical at several points without explaining exactly why — she muttered something about Pearl's framework itself being "thoroughly counterfactual," which confused me, since I had understood the whole point of interventionism to be that it replaced counterfactual talk. Could you go through the summary part by part and tell me which claims a careful philosopher of causation, or a causal-inference researcher, would accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations? I'm especially interested in three things: whether the counterfactual and interventionist approaches are really rivals in the way the talk implied, or whether the relationship between them is more complicated; whether interventionism really commits its defenders to denying that there is causation in fundamental physics, or whether that conclusion trades on an ambiguity in what counts as an intervention; and whether the claim that intervention just is conditioning after controlling for confounders is correct — that last one matters most to me practically, since if it were right, quite a lot of our internal experiment documentation would need to be read differently than I've been reading it.
