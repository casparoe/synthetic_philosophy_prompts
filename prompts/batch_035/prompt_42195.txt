I run a weekly philosophy of language reading group at a university, 14 students, mix of final-year undergrads and MA students, one 90-minute session. Next term we hit Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and I want to design an in-class exercise where they run into the underdetermination themselves before they've read a single page of Word and Object — the word "gavagai" shouldn't appear anywhere until the debrief.

Here's roughly what I'm imagining, but you do the actual design. A staged field-linguistics scenario: one or two students play native speakers of an invented language, the rest play linguists trying to crack it, with strict limits on what counts as admissible evidence — pointing, showing things, and yes/no questions the natives can only answer with assent or dissent in their own language. You'll need to invent the target word and, crucially, a secret crib sheet for the native speakers: a lookup table of assent/dissent answers covering a good range of situations (a live rabbit in view, a dead one, a rabbit briefly glimpsed then gone, a picture of a rabbit, rabbit stew, one rabbit next to another, the same rabbit seen again an hour later). The trick I want built in: the crib sheet should be deliberately constructed so that at least two incompatible translation manuals — say "rabbit" versus undetached rabbit parts versus rabbit time-slices — fit every entry in it. And it should also handle the obvious escape routes: when the linguists try to break the tie by asking identity questions ("is this the same blork as that one?") or counting questions ("is there one blork there or two?"), the crib sheet's answers must still be compatible with both manuals, because the auxiliary hypotheses bend.

I'd also like a second phase where the linguists get to consult each other and write down their translation manuals, and then you hit them with a fresh test sentence or scenario that the rival manuals treat differently even though they agreed on all the data so far — that's the moment I most want them to feel, the empirical-equivalence moment rather than just "we ran out of time to gather data."

Please give me: the full rules and any materials (write out maybe 10-15 actual crib sheet entries so I can see the underdetermination is airtight), a timeline for the 90 minutes, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve real money, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the real apparatus — stimulus meaning, assent and dissent, the role of analytical hypotheses, and the distinction between "our evidence doesn't settle it yet" and Quine's stronger claim that nothing behavioural settles it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing: natives improvising off-script and accidentally being consistent, linguists smuggling in English meta-language, someone having heard of gavagai already and spoiling it — how do I handle that?), and be honest about which bit of the thesis the game simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to just assert in the debrief.

Finally — and I actually mean this part, don't skip it — include a section saying where you think this design most plausibly misfires. My worry is that students walk out having learnt "translation is hard and our data was limited," which is just underdetermination of evidence and something a confirmation holist is perfectly happy with, not the radical indeterminacy thesis. What exactly would show you that the activity failed in that way — what would a student have to say in the debrief for you to conclude they got it — and what alternative design would you reach for if you concluded this one can't do the job? If you think no classroom exercise can get from finite observed behaviour to the stronger claim without the facilitator pushing it, say so plainly rather than papering over the gap.
