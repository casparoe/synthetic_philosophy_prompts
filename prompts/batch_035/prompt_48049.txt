The debate over whether ancient societies "collapsed" has now run for roughly two decades in essentially the same configuration: on one side the catastrophists, for whom the end of the Classic Maya kingdoms, the Late Bronze Age palatial system, and Easter Island's deforestation are single, dramatic failures demanding causal explanation — think of Tainter's diminishing returns on complexity or Diamond's environmental mismanagement; on the other the transformationists, crystallized in the 2010 volume Questioning Collapse, who reply that Maya populations persisted and relocated rather than vanished, that the Bronze Age "systems collapse" destroyed a network of palaces and scribal elites while the villages between them carried on, and that Easter Island's society weathered its own ecological history better than it weathered European contact and slave raids. What strikes me is how little of the dispute is actually about the record: both camps will accept the same drought proxies, the same site-abandonment chronologies, the same epigraphic end-dates for the last Maya kings, and yet the argument has not moved in years. Each side publishes its case and the other treats it as confirming evidence for its own diagnosis of the opposition's naivety.

I have a working hypothesis I want tested rather than endorsed. It is that the whole dispute turns on what I call the unit of persistence: the entity whose disappearance would count as collapse. The catastrophist implicitly takes the unit to be the political and cultural formation — the kingdom, the palace economy, the literate elite tradition — and so reads the loss of institutions and inscriptions as a civilizational death. The transformationist takes the unit to be the population and its lifeways — the farmers, the villages, the descendants still there today — and so reads the same events as reorganization, sometimes even liberation. If that is right, the two camps never actually contradict each other; they are indexing the word "collapse" to different survivals, and the impasse is structural, not evidential. But I can also see the argument going another way: that this is a normative disagreement in disguise, between people who want the concept of collapse as a warning device for our own century and people who want it retired as a piece of narrative melodrama with an ugly history of being aimed at other people's ancestors.

Diagnose the disagreement properly. Is it verbal, resolvable by stipulation or by splitting the concept into two? Substantive, with some identifiable empirical or theoretical crux — for instance, whether loss of complexity is a distinctive phenomenon requiring distinctive explanation, as Tainter's framework implies, or just one cell in a table of transformations? A difference in explanatory standards, single-cause narrative versus multicausal process? Or two different questions — a descriptive archaeological one and a prescriptive futurist one — traveling under a single word? Say which of my hypotheses, if either, you would defend, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis: whether a definition, a distinction, a body of evidence, or a reframing of the question could actually end the argument, or whether we should expect it to persist indefinitely and why. I am as interested in whether the question is ill-posed as in who is right.
