I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about how the replication crisis rewired the machinery of empirical research. My editor wants more than a roundup of reforms; she wants the underlying conception of evidence brought to the surface. I've hit a knot in that project that I can't untie on my own, so what I need is a careful conceptual analysis, not reporting help.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. Across psychology, medicine, and much of experimental biology, a finding still becomes publishable only by passing through a ritual inherited from the early twentieth century: state a null hypothesis, collect data, compute the probability of results at least as extreme as yours under the assumption that the null is true, and treat 0.05 as the line between discovery and noise. In the last fifteen years, the reform movement has bolted a second set of requirements onto this one. Researchers are now asked to preregister their hypotheses and analysis plans before seeing the data; some journals accept papers based on the registered plan alone, committing in advance to publish the results however they come out; and data-peeking, flexible stopping rules, and unreported analytic choices have been rebranded from sloppiness into a species of misconduct.

The two halves seem to pull in opposite directions, and each pulls against a third view. The significance-testing half appears to define the evidential meaning of a dataset by long-run error rates: a p-value is a property of a whole experimental procedure, including hypothetical replications you never ran. That has a strange consequence — if I commit in advance to sampling exactly 100 subjects, versus committing to peek after every subject and stop whenever the results look good, the very same final dataset can yield different p-values. Bayesians object that this is irrational: what the data tell you about a hypothesis should depend only on the data you actually obtained, not on what you might have obtained but didn't — a position philosophers of statistics call the likelihood principle. Yet the preregistration half seems to violate that principle from the other side: it says that identical numbers, produced by identical analyses, carry different evidential weight depending on the researcher's prior intentions and private discipline — the same table counts as strong evidence under a locked advance plan and as weak or tainted evidence when it emerges from flexible exploration. So my field appears to be running two philosophies of evidence simultaneously, each making the data hostage to intentions in its own way, while the Bayesian position, which would sever evidence from intentions entirely, fits neither half. And it is the Bayesians, ironically, who keep insisting that preregistration is what saves us.

Help me reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this institutional practice. What does the combined regime — the 0.05 threshold plus preregistration — strictly presuppose about what evidence is, and what does it merely sit comfortably with? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of evidence would license it as-is? Is the tension I've described real, or have I misdescribed something — for instance, is preregistration best understood as tracking the trustworthiness of the researcher rather than the evidential force of the data, in which case the two halves may not conflict at all? Some defenders of the significance-testing tradition argue that what matters is not the likelihood of the data given a hypothesis but how severely a hypothesis has been probed — how badly the test could have exposed it as false had it been false. Could that kind of view license both the threshold and the preregistration requirement at once, or does it too have to choose? Finally, and most importantly for my article: suppose the major journals abandoned p-values tomorrow in favor of posterior probabilities or Bayes factors. On your reconstruction, would preregistration still have any rationale, or would the reform movement have sawn off the branch it was sitting on — and if the latter, what would the reformed institution have to look like to remain coherent? I'd like an answer I can paraphrase for readers who have never heard of any of this, which means it had better be right.
