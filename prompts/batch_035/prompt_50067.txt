Act as a philosopher of history with a strong line in historiography and the debates over periodization, and reason carefully before committing to anything. My doctoral advisor, a historian who has spent her career on how history gets carved into periods, has a settled track record of verdicts on which proposed periods are real, and I want a prediction about her next ruling. Her verdicts so far:

- The Renaissance is not a real period: a label minted by art historians and then projected onto politics, economy, and thought, where it carves nothing.
- The Scientific Revolution is a real period, dated to the middle and later seventeenth century, when mathematical-experimental natural philosophy became the dominant way of producing knowledge about nature — and here she insists historians, not philosophers of science, own the dating.
- The Iron Age is real but properly dated region by region, each region from its own adoption of iron, with no global date — and historians must defer to the archaeologists' dating, since the evidence is theirs.
- The Little Ice Age is real despite an onset that is diachronous across proxies and regions (roughly 1300–1450 depending on where you look), because it is driven by one physical process; and its boundaries belong to the climate scientists.
- The Cold War is a real period even though its endpoints are conventional political events, because the structure it names — a bipolar nuclear order — was globally synchronous and organized nearly everything inside it.
- The long eighteenth century is not a real period, just a syllabus convenience.

Next month her research seminar takes up the Anthropocene, and stipulate the following context so nothing depends on how the geologists' process actually ends: their commission has just voted down the formal proposal to mark it as a geological epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century, dated by bomb-test radionuclides, while climate scientists, environmental historians, and much of the reading public keep using the term regardless. Start by sketching the candidate rules that fit her track record — the ones I can see all have to do with whether a period needs a single globally synchronous marker, or is individuated by a driving process with a threshold, or is suspect as a label projected backward from present concerns, or something else — and what each rule predicts here. Then: (1) will she accept the Anthropocene as a real period? (2) If so, when does it start — the mid-century radionuclide marker, an earlier threshold such as the Columbian-era changes visible in atmospheric chemistry, or something regionally staggered, like her Iron Age? (3) Who does she think has standing to decide, given her deferrals to archaeologists on the Iron Age and climate scientists on the Little Ice Age, but her claim that historians own the Scientific Revolution? I'm less interested in the answers themselves than in the rule she seems to be running, the rival rules that fit her verdicts at least as well, and the further case — some candidate period she hasn't yet ruled on — that would best tell those rules apart. Give your best guess and your reasons for preferring it. Please say how confident you are overall and single out the point you are least sure of. Then end with one final line of the form

Verdict: <real period / not a real period>; <start, if any>; <who decides>

keeping that line to fifteen words or fewer.
