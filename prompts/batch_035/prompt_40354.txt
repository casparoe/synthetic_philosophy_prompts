I teach a mid-size metaphysics seminar (14 students, one 80-minute session) and I'm tired of how flat the statue-and-clay stuff lands on the page — everyone nods at "the statue isn't identical to the clay" and then five minutes later nods at "obviously only one thing is sitting on the pedestal," and nothing forces them to notice they've done that. I want an activity where students have to act as the ontological authority for a fictional sculptor's studio across a sequence of events, and they dig their own grave. My rough idea: split them into small "registry offices" that keep a ledger of what exists in the studio — one file per object, opened when it comes to be, closed when it's destroyed — and every ruling has to be a clean yes/no on whether a new file opens or an old one closes, no "it's sort of both." I'd want the sequence of cases engineered so that whatever file-opening policy they adopt, a later case makes it look terrible: a pre-existing lump gets shaped into a statue (do you open a second file for the same matter?), the statue is squashed flat and the lump obviously survives (so they can't share a file after all), the clay is remolded into a new statue, a single grain is picked off the surface, and two overlapping "artworks" get made out of the very same clay in the same fortnight. Then some kind of cross-examination round where rival registry offices try to catch each other in contradictions they've actually committed to on paper. Design the full thing: rules, what the ledger materials are, how to split the class, a timeline, how to run the cross-examination, and a debrief that maps their actual rulings onto the real moves in the debate — the Leibniz's Law point from differing survival conditions, the problem of the many (why exactly one statue and not a thousand clay-minus-a-grain candidates standing in the same spot), and the arbitrariness complaint about admitting artifacts into your ontology at all. Also be honest about which feature of the whole dispute the game can't reproduce, since picking between responses like four-dimensionalism or just denying there are statues is a matter of weighing theoretical virtues, and no classroom ruling can make that cost anything. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
