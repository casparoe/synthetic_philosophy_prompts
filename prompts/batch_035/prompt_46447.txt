I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working on decision theory, with a prior background in analysis and probability theory. I have to choose a dissertation direction within the next few months, and the area I keep coming back to is the problem of undefined and order-dependent expected values, exemplified by the Pasadena game: a St. Petersburg-style gamble whose payoff series alternates in sign in such a way that the expectation becomes a conditionally convergent series — one whose sum can be made to equal any real number depending on the order in which the outcomes are summed. What draws me in is that the case doesn't just say expectations are infinite or undefined in some technical corner; it suggests that the very operation of aggregating payoffs into a single expectation can be sensitive to arbitrary choices of ordering, which feels like a deeper wound to expected utility theory than the more familiar paradoxes. My three-year constraint is real: I can do serious mathematics, but I won't be running experiments or doing empirical psychology.

Within this broad problem, I see several candidate directions, and I'd like help prioritizing. First, there are attempts to repair expectations: truncating the payoff series at some finite point, or requiring convergence in a stronger sense before a gamble gets a value at all — the question is whether any of these restrictions are principled rather than ad hoc. Second, there are decision theories that sidestep expectations altogether, aggregating payoffs in an order-sensitive or rank-sensitive way, and the question of whether they handle Pasadena-type cases without simply relocating the arbitrariness. Third, there's the question of practical reach: whether Pasadena-style phenomena show up in real decisions with heavy-tailed payoff distributions — catastrophic-risk cost-benefit analysis, philanthropic prioritization under uncertainty about the far future — or whether they are pure mathematical pathology with no decision-relevant analogue. Fourth, there's the more purely mathematical question of characterizing which classes of gambles can produce this kind of order-dependence, which might yield a clean classification theorem as a dissertation. There may be a fifth direction I'm not seeing, and if so I'd want to hear about it.

Please give me a dependency-aware ordering of these lines of work for someone with my constraints: which are upstream of which, which are tractable in three years for someone with analysis skills but no appetite for empirical work, and which would leave me with a publishable result even in the pessimistic scenario. I'm also interested in your criteria and how you weighed them — for instance, I genuinely don't know how much the practical-reach question should count, since it could either be the entire point of the exercise or a distraction from the foundational issue. And please tell me what would change your ranking: e.g., if a clean classification result for order-dependent gambles already exists in the literature, or if someone demonstrated a realistic decision problem exhibiting genuine Pasadena-style sensitivity. I'd rather not spend three years rediscovering something or polishing a corner nobody cares about.
