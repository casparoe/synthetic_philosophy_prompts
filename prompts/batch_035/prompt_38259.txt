I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of biology — fifteen students, one ninety-minute session — and we're at the point in the semester where we move from etiological (selected-effect) accounts of biological function to causal-role (systems-analysis) accounts. The students can recite the positions, but they treat them as two ways of describing the same fact, like choosing between Celsius and Fahrenheit. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the accounts genuinely come apart: participants should run into cases where the two frameworks assign different functions to the same trait, or assign a function to different traits, before anyone names the positions.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though you should improve on it: a role-play in which the students sit on a regulatory "Function Bureau" that must issue one official function designation per trait — like a patent office or licensing board, but for biological structures. The class is divided into boards, and each board contains members with conflicting mandates: some are only permitted to argue from a trait's current contribution to the workings of the organism ("performance officers"), while others are only permitted to argue from the trait's history of selection ("provenance officers"). The boards are evaluated on cross-case consistency: they earn accreditation points for issuing rulings that cohere with their own earlier rulings, not for matching any answer key. So when the cases diverge, the pressure is internal — they discover they must choose a criterion, and the criterion has costs.

For cases, use real, well-known examples that split the accounts: a vestigial structure with a long selective history and no present job; an exaptation with a modest history of selection for one effect that now performs a different, indispensable job (something along the lines of feathers originating in thermoregulation and later being co-opted for flight); a byproduct of some other structure's growth that nonetheless currently supports an essential process; and, for a final round, an artificially selected trait in a domesticated species where the "history of selection" is itself recent and human-directed, which should make the etiologists in the room uncomfortable. Run the activity in rounds: in the first, boards see only the trait's present role in the organism's economy; only afterwards do they open a sealed dossier revealing the selection history. That staging matters — I want the causal-role intuition to settle in before the historical information destabilizes it.

Please include: the rules and materials (index cards, dossiers, whatever scoring sheet is needed); a payoff scheme that uses no real money; a round-by-round timeline that fits ninety minutes; a full debrief script that maps the specific moments of the activity onto the parts of the theoretical dispute — including where each side's rulings were vindicated and where they had to bite a bullet; a candid section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (student buy-in, boards that try to have it both ways, the provenance officers smuggling in present-day considerations, and so on) and how I should handle it; and an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself — in particular, the fact that working biologists seem to use "function" in both senses depending on context, so that a participant might reasonably conclude the dispute is verbal, and I don't want that conclusion foreclosed by the game's structure. No lecture up front; the terms "etiological," "selected effect," and "causal role" should not appear until the debrief.
