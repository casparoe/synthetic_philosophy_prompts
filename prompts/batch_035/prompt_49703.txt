I'm a condensed matter physicist, retired four years now, and lately I've been wading into philosophy (the reading group here has been very patient with me). Next month I present to them my take on the St. Petersburg paradox: that its not really a paradox at all, just a broken idealization. The infinite expected value only appears because the game can't physically exist — no counterparty can cover unbounded payoffs, and the coin flips couldn't outlast the heat death of the universe anyway. Cap the game by the bankroll of any real casino and the whole thing evaporates. In physics we know what to do when an idealization fails (the frictionless plane, the infinite heat reservoir): you say the model broke, you don't declare nature paradoxical, and I want to say rationality deserves the same courtesy. The group is mostly formal epistemology types plus a couple of grad students and one retired actuary who loves Dutch book arguments. Prep my Q&A sheet: the objections they'll actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way this crowd phrases them, each with a short reply I can give in two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a feeling somebody there knows a construction that makes physical caps like mine look quaint, and I'd much rather hear it from you first.
