I'm teaching a unit that pairs Golding's Lord of the Flies with the lifeboat ethics tradition — Hardin's argument that in conditions of scarcity a group's first duty is to its own survival, that sharing capacity past viability sinks everyone, and that his preferred metaphor of the lifeboat must replace the softer picture of spaceship Earth. Read the novel through that framework. On the surface the island looks like a lifeboat with the adult world stripped away: limited food, no external authority, and the boys forced to decide who eats, who works, and who counts as a member of the group at all. But the fit is strange on inspection. The island is not genuinely scarce — fruit, fish, and pigs are available — and the boys never starve; what collapses is the signal fire, the conch, and any capacity to keep agreements. The scarce resource seems to be credibility and enforceable cooperation, not calories, which suggests the novel stages an inversion of the standard tragedy of the commons: not the overuse of a shared resource but the underprovision of a shared project, as hunting for the private good of meat crowds out tending the public good of rescue. So I want to know whether the novel vindicates Hardin's harsh logic, refutes it, or shows that his scarcity assumption is doing all the work in the argument. Consider whether the split between Ralph's camp and Jack's is a Hardin-style fragmentation into rival lifeboats; what Piggy's fate says about what the lifeboat framework predicts for those who can't contribute to survival; whether Simon's death is a failure of triage reasoning or something the framework has no vocabulary for; and whether the naval officer and the warship at the end make the metaphor recursive — the rescuers themselves adrift on a larger lifeboat, mid-war. Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that says where Golding dramatizes something lifeboat ethics cannot see, and where the framework illuminates scenes the novel leaves dark. I'm not looking for an illustration of Hardin with the boys as props, nor for a defense of the novel that dodges the argument.
