I am the safety officer at a research organization of roughly two hundred scientists working at the intersection of synthetic biology and software tools. The day-to-day work is unremarkable, but it occasionally brushes against genuinely dual-use territory. Under our rules, individual researchers can proceed with most work without sign-off; only a short list of triggers — particular organisms, quantities, named agents — forces review. The problem is the cases the list doesn't anticipate, of which we get maybe a dozen a quarter. A postdoc wants to publish a protocol that would materially shorten someone else's path to harm, and judges the legitimate benefit to outweigh it. Another wants to demo an unreleased tool at a conference ahead of internal review. A third orders a construct from a commercial vendor whose screening we consider sloppy. In each case the researcher is probably right that nothing bad will come of it: individually reasonable, collectively alarming.

A colleague in risk management told me this is an instance of the unilateralist's curse: when several people can each independently take some action, and the action is bad if anyone takes it, then even if every individual's judgment is unbiased, the action will be taken considerably more often than any one of them would endorse — because it takes only one optimist. He said the standard remedy is some form of precautionary default: when a unilateral action is contested, side with the more cautious assessment, and centralize the decision rather than leaving it to whoever moves first. I want to build that into a procedure my office can actually run, because the de facto rule right now is that a case gets escalated only if some individual researcher has the nerve to escalate it, and that is exactly the wrong dependence.

Please write a decision procedure for cases like this, with the following elements:

- An ordered list of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, separating the factual questions my office can settle from the value questions we must hand upward.
- Specification of where the procedure terminates in a no, where in a yes, and where in "consult" — including the fallback rule when consultation is impossible in the time available (the demo is the day after tomorrow).
- Guidance on how the procedure should treat the number of people positioned to act unilaterally, since I gather the logic differs when only one person could possibly do the thing at all, and I don't want us invoking the curse as a rhetorical cover for ordinary caution.
- Worked applications to the three cases above, with at least one coming out in a way I won't like; I would rather see the procedure's costs than have them hidden.

Finally, be candid about where it will misfire. I am told the curse argument depends on assumptions about how errors are distributed across people, and I would like to know when requiring consultation is mere prudence with a fancy name and when the structure of the situation genuinely changes. Also state plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether it can be honest about the fact that the costs of its false positives fall on the researchers, while the costs of its false negatives would fall on me, and what that asymmetry predicts about how the procedure will be applied in practice.
