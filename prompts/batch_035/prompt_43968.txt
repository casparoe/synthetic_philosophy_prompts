I've been chewing on the utility monster thought experiment for a few months now — the being, usually attributed to the philosopher Robert Nozick, whose capacity for wellbeing is so vast that sacrificing everyone else's happiness to feed it would maximize total utility. A friend of mine who's a convinced utilitarian thinks the whole thing is a cheap strawman: no actual creature could have effectively unbounded utility, so the case tells us nothing about whether utilitarianism is true. I'm not so sure — to me it feels like it exposes a structural feature of the view (adding up wellbeing across beings, on a single scale, with no cap per person) rather than a fantasy about a particular monster. Neither of us is a philosopher, so I'd like your help figuring out where our disagreement actually lives.

Here is what I want from you. Give me your credence — an actual number — that the following proposition is true: if maximizing aggregate wellbeing is the whole of morality, then in a world where a utility monster could be created or fed at modest cost, we would be morally obligated to devote essentially all our resources to it. Then decompose that credence into the sub-questions it depends on, with a rough probability for each: whether wellbeing across different beings is genuinely comparable on a common scale; whether the right way to aggregate is additive summation rather than something with a per-person cap, average, or diminishing returns; whether a being whose utility could dominate the total is even physically or conceptually possible; and whether, if all that holds, the correct utilitarian response is to bite the bullet rather than treat the case as a refutation. Feel free to add terms I haven't listed if you think they belong in the chain.

One twist I've been turning over that I'd like you to weigh in on: the monster is usually imagined as one gigantic being, but I suspect the deep problem doesn't need one. Suppose instead you could run a trillion copies of a single very happy digital mind — is that a utility monster, a trillion ordinary people, or something the aggregation question handles differently depending on how you answer the identity question about the copies? If the copies count as one being, the monster seems to sneak back in; if they count as many, maybe the case dissolves — or maybe it just becomes worse. Tell me which of these sub-questions your overall number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it the most. My own gut number is around 0.5, my friend's around 0.1, so if you land near either of us, tell me which of us you think is making the identifiable mistake.
