I help organize an interfaith discussion series, and at nearly every session someone reaches for the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each man touches a different part, reports a different animal, and the moral drawn is that every tradition, perhaps every conceptual scheme, grasps only part of the truth, so no one is in a position to correct anyone else. A colleague who has been reading about Jain epistemology says that if you put the parable next to anekāntavāda — the doctrine that reality is many-sided, elaborated through syād-vāda, the sevenfold scheme of qualified judgment, and through the theory of nayas, the partial standpoints from which any true judgment is made — the parable does not support that moral at all, and may support something nearer its opposite. I would like an audit of the parable as my panelists use it. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the elephant, of the blind men and their hands, of their reports, and of the teller of the story — noting that the story as usually told does have a sighted teller, someone who sees the whole animal and finds the blind men's disputes comic. Then set out where the mapping breaks: which features of the parable the pluralist conclusion quietly imports, such as a single bounded object with one determinate shape, reports that come out simply wrong rather than true-from-a-particular-standpoint, and an observer with the full view; and say for each such feature whether the Jain doctrine endorses it, rejects it, or complicates it — the complication that interests me most being that a tradition built on the many-sidedness of truth also claimed that the liberated kevalin knows things in all their aspects. Then tell me whether the panelists' tolerance conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the parable was carrying it the whole time; and if anekāntavāda itself supplies a better comparison for their purposes, say what work that comparison can do and where it in turn breaks down. If you think the parable and the doctrine are too loosely related for any of this to be more than table-making, or that my colleague and I have misdescribed the doctrine in a way that matters, please say so directly rather than working around it.
