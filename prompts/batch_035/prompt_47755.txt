I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a mid-size daily, and I also run a lunch thing at the office called Explainer Club, where once a month somebody walks the newsroom through an idea they've been reporting on. No slides, just the whiteboard and forty minutes before everyone's edits are due. Attendance is reporters plus a few editors: sharp people, comfortable with numbers in a "I can read a chart and I know what a confidence interval is" sort of way, but no formal probability past whatever they half remember from a stats-for-journalists seminar.

Next month it's my turn, and I want to do the thing that's been bugging me ever since I profiled a forecasting shop: what do you do with evidence that is itself conditional? The classic case is van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, and I want to build the whole session around it. Setup, in case you want it crisp: a soldier is dropped blind into a swampy area split 50/50 between Blue-held territory and Yellow-held territory, and the Blue half is itself split into two equal subregions, Blue-2 and Red-held, so going in she's 1/4, 1/4, 1/2 across the three zones. She gets the radio working, and HQ says they can't tell her where she is, but if she's in Blue territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that she's in Blue-2 rather than the Red-held subregion. So the message tells her nothing directly about Blue vs Yellow — and the puzzle is that it's surpisingly unclear what her new probabilities should be. Doing the obvious thing, conditioning on the message as though it were a plain proposition, does something that feels flat wrong to her Yellow probability; treating the message as only fixing Blue-2 vs Red-held gives 3/8 and 1/8 with Yellow left at 1/2; and the fancier machinery people have proposed since gives yet other answers — including one where her Yellow probability moves even though the message was supposedly only about the Blue side — with arguments for each that got genuinely heated.

Don't explain the problem to me — I've read enough for that, this is for the room. What I need is the plan for the session. What has to be in place first before the puzzle will even register as a puzzle (my guess is they need to feel how clean ordinary conditioning on a hard fact is, or the radio message will just sound like one more fact), what order to run the pieces in and what depends on what, where exactly the gut-punch moment should land and how to stage it on a whiteboard. The misconceptions I expect: half the room shrugging that obviously she's 3/8 on Blue-2 and what's the big deal; someone asking whether this is just base-rate neglect in a costume; someone else treating the radio message as the material conditional and conditioning on that like it's no different from being told a coordinate. Where in the sequence do I head off each one? Also, do I bring in a version with polling numbers — expert says candidate X wins IF the economy is the top issue, and the "if" part never gets tested, which is bread and butter for us — or does that dilute the original? What do I openly refuse to get into at this level (I assume whatever imaging is, and the triviality results, and the fights about minimum information principles) and how do I say "not today" without sounding like I'm dodging? And one question I can ask at the end to find out whether they caught the difference between learning a conditional and learning a fact — not whether they can follow the arithmetic, but whether they'd flinch on their own the next time a source hands them one.
