I am a high school student writing an extended essay for my Theory of Knowledge course, and I would appreciate some help thinking through my topic. My essay compares two philosophers who disagree about whether dreams are experiences at all: Norman Malcolm, whose book Dreaming (1959) argues that dreaming is not something that happens to a person while asleep, and Daniel Dennett, whose paper "Are Dreams Experiences?" (1976) offers a kind of middle position between Malcolm and the ordinary view. I understand the broad outlines — Malcolm thinks that being asleep and being conscious of anything are mutually exclusive, and that our dream reports are best understood as things we sincerely say upon waking, not reports of experiences we had during the night; Dennett instead suggests that the dream narrative may be composed at wake-up and then inserted into memory with, so to speak, a backdated timestamp, so that we have apparent memories of experiences that were never lived through. What I am struggling with is the following comparison.

First, it seems to me that Malcolm and Dennett agree on something surprising: that dream reports, all by themselves, are very weak evidence for what was going on in the sleeper's mind during sleep. Is that fair, or does Malcolm's conceptual (or perhaps linguistic) claim that we simply cannot say anything about the mind of a sleeping person differ in kind from Dennett's merely empirical suspicion that we usually don't know? If they differ, how does that difference matter for my essay — for instance, when it comes to what each of them counts as good evidence?

Second, I have read that the existence of lucid dreams — dreams in which the dreamer knows, while still dreaming, that they are dreaming — would create trouble for these views, since a lucid dreamer can supposedly arrange in advance to give a signal (like a distinctive pattern of eye movements) that only someone currently having an experience could give. Please explain why this is supposed to be a threat, how strong the threat is to each of the two positions, and whether Dennett has any way of remaining agnostic or skeptical here. (It occurs to me, for instance, that someone who claimed a form of lucidity merely at the moment of waking, misremembered as lucidity during the dream, could produce the same reports — but I am not sure whether that response only proves that the evidence is weaker than it looks, rather than that the received view is wrong.)

Third, here is my own worry, which I would like you to evaluate rather than simply endorse. If Dennett can explain away my apparent memories of last night's dream by saying they were generated this morning, then could the same trick be pulled with my apparent memories of yesterday afternoon? What exactly stops his story about the dreams from generalizing into a story about all experience — that is, into the view that consciousness is less a stream we are actually in and more a narrative the brain writes up and backdates afterwards? I know Dennett is famous for biting larger bullets than most people would want to, so I would like you to say both whether the generalization is something his position genuinely commits him to, and how a defender of the ordinary, common-sense view of dreams (which my teacher tells me was defended later by people reacting against Malcolm and Dennett) would argue that dreams, unlike waking life, are a special case where the backdating story is plausible.

Finally, please tell me which of the two — Malcolm or Dennett — you think holds up better today, and why, in terms I can defend to my TOK examiner, who values precision about whether a claim is conceptual, empirical, or methodological. I would prefer a reasoned position over a bland verdict that both have something to offer. Please keep in mind that I cannot consult any sources beyond what you tell me, so please do not answer with reading suggestions — I need the substance itself.
