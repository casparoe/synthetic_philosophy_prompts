I'm grading a final exam for an upper-level metaethics seminar and would like a careful assessment of one student's answer. The exam question read as follows:

"Geach's challenge to expressivism is sometimes divided into two problems. The embedding problem concerns how an expressivist can account for the validity of arguments such as: lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; therefore, getting your little brother to lie is wrong — where the moral sentence occurs unasserted in the conditional's antecedent. The negation problem concerns which state a negated moral sentence, such as 'lying is not wrong,' could express, given that it cannot simply be the absence of the state expressed by the unnegated sentence. Explain why the embedding problem afflicts expressivism even once expressivism is carefully distinguished from simple subjectivism; present Blackburn's quasi-realist response in terms of higher-order attitudes; and explain why negation is widely thought to remain a difficulty for expressivism even if the embedding problem is solved."

The student's answer reads:

"Geach's point was that subjectivism fails because it cannot keep the meaning of a moral sentence constant across embedded and unembedded occurrences. In the argument above, if 'lying is wrong' just means that I disapprove of lying, then the antecedent of the conditional would be about my attitudes rather than about lying, and the argument could not be valid in the way modus ponens is. This is supposed to be a problem for any expressivist view. But Blackburn's expressivism is more sophisticated: unlike the subjectivist, he does not say that moral sentences report the speaker's attitudes; they merely express them. So when a moral sentence occurs in an antecedent, no attitude is expressed at all, and the sentence keeps whatever content it has. Since Geach's argument was aimed at subjectivism, which holds that moral sentences report attitudes, it does not really apply to Blackburn.

Blackburn's positive proposal is that to accept the conditional is to have a higher-order attitude: disapproval of the combination of disapproving of lying while failing to disapprove of getting your little brother to lie. Validity is then explained by inconsistency among the attitudes involved: someone who has the attitudes expressed by the premises cannot coherently lack the attitude expressed by the conclusion.

Once this is in place, negation poses no special problem. 'Lying is not wrong' simply expresses the absence of disapproval toward lying. If I disapprove of lying, I am inconsistent with anyone who lacks that disapproval, and inconsistency is all the expressivist needs to explain validity. One might worry that a speaker could fail to disapprove of lying while still judging that lying is wrong, but this only shows that attitudes can come apart from judgments, which the expressivist never denied. Blackburn's apparatus also generalizes: belief contexts, as in attributions of the belief that lying is wrong, raise the same structural issue, and the quasi-realist can treat them in parallel."
