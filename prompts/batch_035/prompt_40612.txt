I'm a researcher at an AI safety organization preparing a grant application for a twelve-month project, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a coherent proposal. The deliverable I'm asking for here is the proposal itself, roughly two pages when written out: the question stated precisely, why the existing positions leave it open, the method, a realistic timeline, and failure criteria. Please don't include a bibliography or cite specific papers by name — I'll handle references myself, and you should feel free to refer to positions and arguments by description.

The idea concerns the standing dispute between worst-case and average-case conceptions of safety guarantees for AI systems. The worst-case camp, exemplified by adversarial red-teaming and interpretability work aimed at hidden failure modes, insists that a system is not safe until it cannot be broken at all; the average-case camp, exemplified by aggregate benchmark evaluation and statistical estimates of harm across deployments, treats that standard as unachievable and prefers to bound expected harm. My suspicion is that the dispute has lasted this long partly because both sides work with a dichotomy: either you quantify over all inputs and environments, or you quantify over a probability distribution over them. Meanwhile, in the analysis of algorithms, there is a third option, usually called smoothed analysis, which was introduced to explain a well-known embarrassment: the simplex method for linear programming has provably terrible worst-case complexity, yet it runs quickly on essentially all practical instances. The trick is to analyze performance not on adversarial inputs and not on uniformly random inputs, but on adversarial inputs subjected to small random perturbations — and it turns out that this intermediate class is small enough for good bounds to exist and large enough to contain everything that actually shows up in practice.

My half-formed thought is that AI deployment environments have the same shape. Real deployments are neither IID samples from a benign distribution nor the outputs of unbounded adversaries; they are ordinary deployment conditions plus bounded, roughly resourced perturbers — a jailbreak-hunting user, a distribution shift, a moderately capable attacker. If one could define a class of deployment environments of the form "ordinary conditions perturbed by adversaries of bounded capability and budget," then safety guarantees over that class might be simultaneously achievable enough to be certifiable and inclusive enough to be meaningful to regulators and the public. The core difficulty is that in algorithm analysis the perturbation is a mathematical operation with clean properties, whereas here the "perturbation" is a strategic agent, and the "ordinary conditions" are not a formal object at all.

Turn this into the proposal. Among the things it should address: (1) a precise statement of the research question, which I take to be something like: can we give a formal characterization of a class of deployment environments strictly between average-case and worst-case such that safety guarantees over that class are both achievable by current methods and relevant to actual deployment, and does such a class retain its meaning when the perturbing agent is itself adaptive and potentially aware of the characterization?; (2) why the existing positions leave this open — in particular, why the worst-case side's implicit assumption that any retreat from universal quantification is a retreat into complacency, and the average-case side's implicit assumption that the reference distribution is given rather than chosen, both avoid the question of which intermediate quantifier is the right target; (3) the method — this is a formal and philosophical project, not an empirical scaling one, so say what the actual work is: defining candidate environment classes, proving or proving-out separations and inclusions between them, testing the definitions against real deployment failure modes as case studies, and asking which definitions a regulator could actually use; (4) a month-by-month plan for one year that honestly allocates time to the likelihood that the first definitions fail; and (5) what would count as the project failing — for example, if every intermediate class that is weak enough to certify turns out to exclude the failure modes people actually worry about, that is a substantive negative result and I want the proposal to say so plainly rather than bury it.

Two additional things the proposal should handle, because the funder will ask. First, an obvious objection: the perturbation in smoothed analysis is noise, but the perturbation in deployment is an adversary who can learn what counts as a small perturbation; if the class is defined by a budget, the adversary spends to the budget, and we may just have rebuilt the worst case with extra steps. The proposal should say whether this objection is fatal, whether it can be blunted (for instance, by making the bound a property of the certifier's knowledge rather than the adversary's), and how the project would find out. Second, why a foundation should fund a definitional project rather than more red-teaming: say what the deliverable is for people who have to decide, today, what standard of evidence to demand before deployment. Keep the tone measured and precise; the funder is sophisticated but is not itself embedded in the alignment literature, so define terms as you go.
