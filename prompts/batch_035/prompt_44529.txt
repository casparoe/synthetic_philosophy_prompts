I do computer vision research, mostly multimodal stuff — separate image and text encoders trained contrastively so a photo of a dog ends up near the string "a photo of a dog" in a shared embedding space. My philosophy background is one undergrad course I mostly slept through, so bear with me. Lately I've gotten kind of obsessed with Molyneux's question: take someone blind from birth who knows a cube and a sphere perfectly well by touch, and suppose they suddenly gain sight — can they tell which is which by sight alone, before touching either? Locke said no, Berkeley also said no but for reasons I never quite got straight, and there's this whole history afterwards of surgeons reporting that their newly-sighted patients were totally disoriented, plus more recent attempts to actually run the experiment with people who gained sight late in life after congenital cataract surgeries. My partner (who does developmental cog sci and thinks about infants all day) says the whole debate secretly depends on how much "pretraining" the visual system gets in the dark, from spontaneous retinal waves and whatever evolution baked in, which is exactly the kind of thing I have opinions about.

So here's the ask: translate the whole Molyneux debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can to what the philosophers were actually fighting about. I want you to use the words I use daily — modality-specific encoders, learned cross-modal alignment vs. a shared backbone, zero-shot transfer, distribution shift, the idea that alignment might come "for free" from two encoders that both have to model the same world, a randomly initialized vs. pretrained visual pathway as the confound my partner flagged, haptic feature statistics vs. early visual features like edges and junctions. Where a philosopher's position makes a claim, tell me what claim about representations and alignment it corresponds to, and where you have to fudge to make it fit, flag the fudge. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For instance — does the notion of the *same object* persisting across touch and sight, which seems to be doing real work in the original debate, track anything embedding-space talk can express, or is identity-over-modality just something my framework can't say? Same question for Berkeley's worry (I think it's his) that it's not enough to explain how visual ideas get *associated* with tactile ones — he wants them to share a common feel, and I genuinely don't know whether "the modalities converge on the same latent structure" is the same demand or a different one.

And then be honest with me about the residue: when something won't translate, is it because my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers saw, or because their disagreement was less substantive than it looked from the inside? Like — once you're allowed to talk in terms of what's learned before vs. after the modality gap gets bridged, is there anything left for Locke and Berkeley to actually disagree about, or is it a dispute about the training curriculum? I'd also take it as a bonus if you can tell me whether Condillac's statue thing (the one where the statue gets senses added one at a time — my partner brought it up and I didn't follow) is the same problem in a costume or a genuinely different one. Don't hold back on the philosophy side; I'd rather have to look up names than get a watered down version.
