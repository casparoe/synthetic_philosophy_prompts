I'm a first-year PhD student in machine learning with a philosophy background, trying to develop my first serious research direction in AI safety. I've been struck by something about the "control" approach to safety — the idea that instead of assuming a model is aligned, you design deployment protocols (monitors, tripwires, restricted channels, human sign-off at critical steps) such that even a misaligned model cannot do much harm. What bothers me is an epistemic gap I haven't seen addressed head-on: nearly everything we believe about whether a model is aligned comes from observing its behavior, but under a control regime we mostly observe the model when it is, in effect, under watch and constrained. That looks like a selection effect. A prisoner's good conduct under constant surveillance is very weak evidence about how they would behave unsupervised; and if the model is strategic, the behavior we sample under control is precisely the behavior most shaped by the control measures themselves, so it is evidence about the measures rather than about the model. So the question I want to pose is this: what can behavior observed under control protocols legitimately tell us about a model's alignment, and can we design control deployments that are also informative — deployments that double as experiments?

Please take the role of a senior AI safety researcher who has thought hard both about the control-versus-alignment distinction and about older work on inference from biased samples — selection effects, the way policy changes can invalidate the relationships estimated from past data, the way arguments can be honest at every step and still misleading in aggregate. Begin with some careful brainstorming: enumerate the distinct inferential routes from controlled behavior to claims about alignment (for example, transfer from tasks that are easy to monitor to tasks that are hard; the model's behavior during periods when control is secretly relaxed; adversarial probes deliberately embedded in deployment), and for each route, state explicitly what assumption it would need to hold. Then, using that analysis, write a two-page research proposal on my behalf: state the question precisely; explain why the existing paradigms — alignment evaluation on the one hand, control evaluations on the other — leave it open; lay out a concrete method, including what a pilot experiment would look like, what a formal or semi-formal model of the inference would add, and in what order I should attempt the pieces; and specify honestly what would count as the project failing, or as showing the question is ill-posed.

A few constraints on the write-up: keep the scope realistic for the first year of a PhD, with a plausible month-by-month shape; do not include a bibliography, since I will add references myself; and do not anchor the proposal to any specific paper by name — describe the relevant ideas and positions in your own words instead. Above all, avoid the temptation to resolve the tension by stipulation (e.g., by simply assuming the model cannot model its monitors); the interesting work is in saying which assumptions are cheap, which are expensive, and how the proposal would test the expensive ones.
