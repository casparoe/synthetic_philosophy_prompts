I'm a novelist working on a book set in a small river town in the Midwest, and I've hit a chapter I can't make myself write until I actually know what I think. The plot point: my protagonist, a retired schoolteacher who's just joined the city council, has to vote on whether to rename Halloran Street. Halloran founded the town's mill in the 1890s, built the library, endowed the hospital — but he also did things my research turned up for the fictional version: he wrote the restrictive covenants that kept Black families out of the north side for seventy years, and when workers tried to unionize in the 1930s he had the ringleaders beaten and run out of town. He's been dead eighty years. About a fifth of the town lives on the street, mostly people who like the name and don't know much about him, and an old woman whose family was run out under him has been coming to council meetings for two years asking for the change.

I've been sitting in on real council meetings for texture, and what I notice is that everyone argues past each other, and my protagonist is supposed to be the one person who argues well. The problem is I don't know what arguing well looks like here. Her brother-in-law in my draft keeps saying that if you start renaming things for moral failures, nothing survives, because every rich man of that era had blood somewhere on him, and tearing down the street name is really just a way for people today to feel righteous about a past they didn't participate in. A younger character says the opposite — that the name isn't history, it's an ongoing honor, and keeping it is an ongoing choice that happens to spit on the descendants of the families Halloran hurt. And I can see both of them being half right, which is honestly why the chapter is stuck: my protagonist can't just pick the sympathetic one.

So what I need from you, as research for her big scene: what actually separates these positions, if anything? Is there a principled line between remembering a town founder and continuing to honor him, or is that a distinction without a difference? And does the "everyone from that era was complicit, so the standard proves too much" argument really hold — or does it prove too much in the other direction, the way it would if you applied it to, say, a street named for someone alive within living memory? If you can give me the strongest version of each side plus where each one bends or breaks, that's the chapter. My protagonist ends up having to vote, so I also need to know what a defensible vote looks like — maybe a compromise vote, maybe not. Sorry for the length, this has been eating at me for weeks and my editor thinks I'm procrastinating (I'm a little bit procrastinating).
