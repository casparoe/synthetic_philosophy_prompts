I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, though I read a fair amount of ML research and I've spent a couple of years on interpretability-adjacent work at my company (mostly probing classifiers and trying to understand why our models fail on certain inputs). I want you to help me understand a debate that I keep bumping into but that never seems to resolve, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right.

Here is the debate as I understand it, from the inside of the field. Mechanistic interpretability researchers spend their time reverse-engineering neural networks: they find attention heads that implement copy-and-continue behavior, directions in activation space that seem to correspond to something like "sentiment" or "code versus prose," and, more recently, dictionaries of features extracted via sparse autoencoders that they claim decompose the network into meaningful units. Many of these people talk in a robustly realist way: the features are *there*, waiting to be discovered, and progress consists in finding the correct description of what the network is actually computing. Then there are skeptics — some of them senior researchers, some of them basically me after a bad week — who say that a neural network is a stack of matrix multiplications and that everything above that is a story we tell ourselves. On this view, "features" and "circuits" are convenient compressions for humans, like design patterns in a codebase: useful for communication and for organizing our own thinking, but not part of the ontology of the system. Between the two, there are people who concede the features exist but deny they're *natural* — there may be indefinitely many equally good decompositions, so talk of "the" representation is ill-posed. This debate has been running for years, both sides keep publishing, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed anyone's mind.

My working theory, which I want you to take seriously but also stress-test: I suspect the word "real" is doing two different jobs and nobody has separated them. One question is about causal structure — if you ablate this direction or amplify that feature, does the model's behavior change in the way the interpretation predicts, robustly and across contexts? That question seems answerable by experiment, and interpretability papers answer small versions of it all the time. The other question is about canonicity or uniqueness — is there one correct decomposition of the network into features, or many equally licensed ones? — and that seems like a different kind of question entirely, one that maybe doesn't have the same kind of answer. The realists strike me as answering the first question and the skeptics the second, while both use the sentence "the network really has representations." But I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and I may be reinventing some distinction badly or missing a third option — for instance, that the disagreement is really about what interpretation is *for* (building trust and safety guarantees versus building scientific knowledge), and each side's standard of "real" is calibrated to its own purpose.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of science who also knows the ML literature well enough to know what an induction head or a sparse autoencoder feature is. Then commit to the diagnosis you find best supported, argue for it concretely by pointing to what each side actually says and does (for example, note whether skeptics are moved by causal intervention results, and what that tells you), and be honest about whether anything — a new class of experiments, a formal result about decomposition uniqueness, a conceptual distinction, or just time — could actually end the argument. If you think my causal-structure-versus-canonicity split is wrong or incomplete, say so directly and explain what it misses.
