I teach political philosophy and we're doing a little departmental symposium on the "taxation is theft" thing, and I want a consistency check on my own package before I put it in writing and my colleagues go at it. Here's roughly where I stand:

(1) Theft is taking what rightfully belongs to another, so whether taxation is theft turns entirely on whether your pre-tax income was already rightfully yours.
(2) There is no fact of the matter about what is "rightfully yours" prior to some system of property rules — ownership is partly constituted by legal convention, so a moral claim to one's pre-tax income, considered apart from any legal scheme, is confused. (I take this to be roughly the Murphy and Nagel line, for whatever its worth.)
(3) Given that, "taxation is theft" is a bad argument in every version I've seen.
(4) But I also think there are substantive moral limits on what may be taxed — a duly enacted 100% tax on income above subsistence would be a serious wrong, not just bad policy.
(5) And the limits track labor: taxing wages is harder to justify than taxing bequests, land rents, or windfalls, because people have a special claim to the fruits of their own effort that they lack with respect to unearned gains.
(6) Finally, legitimacy is mainly procedural — democratic authorization is what makes a state's rules binding, not the content of those rules.

Go through the pairs and tell me which of these are really in tension, which only look like it, and rank the real ones by severity rather than just listing them. For each real one, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere. I honestly can't tell whether (4) and (5) are doing work that (2) has quietly made impossible, or whether (3) already comits me to shrugging at (4), so just tell me straight.
