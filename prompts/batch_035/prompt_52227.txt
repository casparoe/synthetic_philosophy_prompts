I'm a high school senior writing a term paper for a philosophy elective on Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness is a myth, and rather than arguing about his thesis in the abstract, I'd like to test it against a single real episode and see what it can and cannot show. Here is the episode and the facts as I understand them, so we're working from the same material. In December 1973, the board of trustees of the American Psychiatric Association voted to remove homosexuality from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, and in 1974 a referendum of the full membership upheld that decision by a modest majority. The seventh printing of the DSM-II then replaced the entry with a new category reserved for people distressed by or in conflict with their own sexual orientation, and the accompanying text specified that homosexuality by itself was not being classified as a disorder. Some version of a distress-based category of this kind stayed in the manual in various forms for decades afterward.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of Szasz's theory. I can see why people treat it as his vindication: a diagnosis entered the manual by one set of professional judgments and left it by a vote, under pressure from activists, without anyone discovering a biological test or lesion that settled the matter, which looks very much like his claim that psychiatric classification is a system of social conventions presented in the language of medicine. But I don't want the easy version of this argument. I'd like you to be precise about how much the episode actually establishes, and I have three places where I suspect the standard reading moves too fast.

First, does the plebiscite support Szasz's strong thesis, that mental illness is a literal myth and a category mistake, or only a weaker claim that psychiatric categories are partly conventional? And if it's only the weaker claim, is that even distinctive, given that classifications elsewhere in medicine are also negotiated by committees and revised without new pathology being discovered? Second, the removal was justified largely by research showing that gay people who were not patients were as well adjusted as anyone else, and the profession treated that as evidence; does the fact that psychiatry could be moved by evidence in this way count against the claim that its classifications change only through politics? Third, consider the compromise itself: the treatable condition became the distress and conflict about one's orientation rather than the orientation. Is that a point for Szasz, on the grounds that the normative judgment was merely relocated into a new clinical form, or a point against him, or a case his theory simply never anticipated and has nothing to say about?

Where you believe Szasz's theory is silent, or has to be stretched to cover some feature of the episode, please say so plainly rather than making it speak. I'd rather have an honest account of what the theory explains, what it misdescribes, and what the episode in turn does to the theory than a tidy confirmation of either side. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would compose a long letter.
