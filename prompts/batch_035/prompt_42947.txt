So, quick background: I sit on the grant-making committee of a mid-sized animal welfare foundation. Historically we fund farmed animal work — corporate campaigns, cage-free stuff, investigations. But at next month's meeting we have to settle a proposal that's split the program staff right down the middle, and since I drew the short straw, I'm the one who has to write the decision memo that gets circulated before hand. The proposal: our city asked for help, and a local wildlife rescue group wants to run a two-year pilot treating sarcoptic mange in the urban fox population — baiting/darting foxes with antiparasitics, plus some follow-up tracking. If you don't know mange, it's genuinely horrific: the animal spends months getting itchier and weaker, losing its fur, until it starves or gets hit by a car. The pilot would maybe reach a few hundred foxes a year.

There are basically three factions on the committee. One says fund it, obviously — suffering is suffering, and the fact that it's in a fox rather than a hen is morally irrelevant; also it's the kind of concrete intervention donors can see and get behind. The second faction says no, but for a wilder reason: the total amount of wild animal suffering out there is so astronomically bigger than a few hundred foxes — orders of magnitude more beings than we could ever treat — that the only rational use of the money is welfare research, figuring out what even helps before we do anything. The third faction says wild animals are mission creep, full stop: we were founded to work on farmed animals, and a whole different theory of change shouldn't get smuggled in through a grant memo. And one of the no-voters keeps going on about how mange outbreaks might be doing population regulation — that if you treat foxes now you get denser populations and worse outbreaks down the line, so we'd be medicalizing a system we don't actually understand. I honestly don't know which of these I find convincing, which I gather is exactly my problem.

Draft the memo for me: a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and it's your call which, but you have to pick one. Give reasons each faction could actually respond to (not the strawman version of their view), and give the strongest argument on the losing side it's due. If you go with conditions, make them concrete enough that a program officer could act on them, not vague exhortations about "further study". Keep it short enough that people will actually read it before the meeting, say 600ish words.
