You are a specialist in New Testament studies and the methodology of historical Jesus research, deeply versed in the criteria-of-authenticity tradition — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence — and in its critics. I need you to reconstruct a scholar's implicit method from her track record and predict her next ruling.

My doctoral supervisor is an old-school historical Jesus scholar. She has never written down her method; she distrusts methodological manifestos and insists the criteria are tools to be applied case by case. But over two years of seminars she has handed down rulings on a running list of sayings and incidents, and I have kept notes. Next month I present a chapter on the Last Supper tradition, and I want to walk into her office knowing where she will come down on the words over the bread and the cup — the institution narrative — before she tells me.

Her rulings so far, all final:

She judges AUTHENTIC:
1. The absolute prohibition of divorce and remarriage — attested in Mark, again in the double tradition, and again in Paul, who introduces it as a command of the Lord while visibly struggling to square it with his own pastoral concessions. She smiles whenever she mentions Paul's discomfort.
2. The disruption of the temple, which she accepts on the grounds that Mark and John report it independently.
3. The cry of abandonment from the cross, in the form Mark gives — noting that Luke and John each replace it with a more composed dying utterance, and taking the retention of the harder version in the earlier stream as telling.
4. The beatitude on the poor in its bluntly economic Lucan form, against Matthew's spiritualized version, with a parallel in the Gospel of Thomas.
5. The demand to a would-be disciple to let the dead bury their dead — attested only in the double tradition, and harsh enough to cost the early church something.

She judges INAUTHENTIC:
6. The Johannine pronouncements of pre-existent identity, such as the claim to have existed before Abraham — the evangelist's own theology, she says, placed on Jesus' lips.
7. The commission to make disciples of all nations at the close of Matthew — the risen Christ of Matthean community practice, she says.
8. The crowd's acceptance of responsibility for Jesus' blood, found only in Matthew — she rejects it even though it later became a burden to the church, because, she says, it served the polemical aims of the evangelist who wrote it.

The new case: the words of institution over the bread and cup. The facts she will have in view: Paul cites them as a tradition he received and handed on, writing before any surviving gospel; Mark gives a version close to Paul's; Luke's form shows, on many accounts, contact with the same Pauline-shaped stream; and Matthew follows Mark. So the material has the strongest independent attestation of anything on the list. Yet these are also precisely the words a eucharist-celebrating community had every reason to put in Jesus' mouth, and they carry the church's own theology of his death.

Work through the following in order. First, map the candidate decision rules that fit her entire track record — there is more than one; note which obvious rules her rulings have already eliminated (a pure embarrassment criterion cannot survive ruling 8, for instance, so say exactly what refinement rescues it). Second, state what each surviving rule predicts for the institution words. Third, commit to a prediction rather than hedging: given how she treated Pauline attestation in the divorce ruling, and how she has weighed attestation against convenience elsewhere, what will she say, and why? Finally, tell me which further case — a specific passage or kind of material — would best discriminate between the surviving rules; and if her track record genuinely underdetermines the prediction, say what methodological commitment she would be making with either answer.

Take your time and be rigorous: the reconstruction of the rules matters more to me than the final verdict.
