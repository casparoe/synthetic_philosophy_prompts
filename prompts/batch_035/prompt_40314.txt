I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a large transplant hospital, and I believe our accumulated precedents on when a patient is dead are about to be tested by a proposal unlike anything we have ruled on before. Our track record, in brief: (1) a man with a massive intracranial hemorrhage — no brainstem reflexes, a conclusively positive apnea test, a confirmatory study showing no intracranial blood flow, heart still beating on the ventilator — we ruled dead; (2) a woman one year into a vegetative state after anoxic injury, breathing without assistance, brainstem reflexes intact — we ruled not dead; (3) a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death case in which, after withdrawal of the ventilator and two minutes of confirmed asystole, we declared death and the organs were recovered immediately, with no dissent on the committee; (4) a brain-dead pregnant woman maintained on a ventilator for fourteen weeks so that the fetus could reach viability, where we reaffirmed at each monthly review that she remained dead throughout. Those four cases are everything we have decided; we have never ruled on anything else.

The new proposal is another controlled donation after circulatory death, with a twist. The surgeons ask that, after the same two minutes of asystole and the same form of death declaration, the patient be cannulated and placed on extracorporeal circulation to restore pulsatile blood flow to the body — with a clamp guaranteeing that no blood reaches the brain — because warm, continuously perfused abdominal organs, livers especially, fare far better in transplant than cold-flushed ones. Half the committee thinks this is merely organ preservation with extra steps; the other half thinks that if what carried our two-minute precedent was the permanent stoppage of circulation, then mechanically restarting circulation through most of the body — even with the brain excluded — reinstates the very condition whose absence defined the patient as dead, and that the only position that survives is one on which our two-minute rule was really a brain rule all along.

Before we vote, I want the analysis structured honestly. First, tell me which principles are actually consistent with all four precedents — I am confident there is more than one, and I would like the rejected candidates named as well, along with the specific precedent that eliminates each. Second, say what each surviving principle predicts about this proposal. Third, identify the closest analog among our precedents and be candid about whether it genuinely settles the question or only seems to. Fourth, give your best guess at our ruling, with the one line of reasoning you would enter in the minutes. Finally, describe the further case — of a kind our track record has never touched — that would most cleanly distinguish the surviving principles from one another. Please put the full analysis first, and then state the predicted decision as the very last line, in the form \answer{...}, containing exactly one of approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions.
