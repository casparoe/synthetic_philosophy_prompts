I'm a second-year master's student serving as the graduate representative on the steering committee of our university's Center for Advanced AI Systems, and I'm taking a seminar this term on the governance and ethics of machine learning, so I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting on a question that's split the committee. The Center has been invited to become an institutional signatory to the Frontier Compute Accord, a voluntary pledge for organizations that train or deploy models above a certain scale. Signatories commit to three things: publishing complete safety-evaluation summaries for every model above a fixed compute threshold; obtaining independent third-party audits of dangerous-capability assessments before deployment; and publicly declining contracts that involve autonomous operation in safety-critical domains. The Accord as drafted contains no conditional clauses — it binds unconditionally once signed. In our seminar we've been discussing what our instructor calls the competitiveness constraint: roughly, the claim that any safety commitment which raises costs will be outcompeted by actors who don't adopt it, so voluntary measures are self-undermining unless everyone moves together. The Center's own spin-off companies compete for commercial contracts, which makes this worry less abstract for us than it might be for a purely academic body.

One committee member has proposed signing with an amendment: a rider specifying that the Center's obligations activate only once organizations holding roughly seventy percent of relevant frontier compute are themselves bound, converting the pledge into a threshold-triggered commitment. A second member argues the rider guts the point entirely — it converts a unilateral commitment into cheap talk, and a university's signature is precisely the kind of low-cost signal that slowly builds the norm that others eventually internalize. A third member raises a wrinkle from our seminar that complicates the framing of the whole debate: some of the Accord's audit and evaluation requirements plausibly accelerate capability work rather than slow it, and the safety-capability overlap means it isn't even clear that everything in the pledge imposes a "tax" in the relevant sense — some provisions may be costless or even competitively beneficial, which matters for whether the competitiveness objection applies to the Accord as a whole or only to parts of it.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate, with the following sections in this order and roughly these lengths: (1) Recommendation, about 120 words, stating a concrete bottom line — sign as drafted, sign with the threshold rider, decline while publishing our reasons, or abstain pending renegotiation with the Accord's drafters; (2) The Competitiveness Objection, about 250 words, giving the strongest honest version of the worry, including the position of our spin-offs; (3) The Rider and the Overlap, about 250 words, assessing whether the mutual-assurance amendment answers the objection or undermines the signaling function our second member cares about, and how the safety-capability overlap reshapes the cost-benefit picture; (4) The Strongest Case for the Losing Side, about 150 words, presented so its proponents could recognize their view in it; and (5) Conditions and Next Steps, about 100 words, stating what we would ask of the Accord's drafters or fellow signatories if we proceed. I'd like the memo to be one the committee could vote on directly, not a survey of positions.
