A bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent 37 years as an experimental physical chemist and only got serious about philosophy after retiring a couple of years back, so I've been working through decision theory the way I'd work through the literature of a new field — theorems first, squabbles later. And I've hit a wall with the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation, and I want your help diagnosing why the debate is so stuck.

Here's how it looks to a newcomer. On one side you've got the classic machinery: Dutch book arguments, money pumps for cyclic preferences, representation theorems showing that if your preferences satisfy a pile of axioms, you behave as if you're maximising expected utility. On the other side you've got critics saying none of this shows you *ought* to maximise expected utility — the theorems only show that axiom-satisfying preferences can be represented in a certain way, which is a mathematical fact and not a norm; and the money pumps only bite if you let yourself be pumped, since an agent with non-EU preferences can just refuse the second bet (or so the story goes, with cleverer and cleverer pump constructions chasing cleverer and cleverer refusals — backward induction versions, ones where you pay to avoid the whole situation, etc). The defenders respond that refusing the second bet looks less like rational choice and more like paying to protect a commitment, and round it goes. As far as I can tell everybody knows everybody else's moves and nobody has shifted an inch in decades.

My working hypothesis, coming from the science side: when a theorem and people's intuitions clash in physics, the fight is almost never about the theorem — it's about which system you're applying it to or what the terms mean. So my guess is this dispute is really about whether "rationality" here means internal consistency amongst your own preferences or something more like responsiveness to reasons in a risky world — two seperate projects wearing one word. But I'm honestly not confident, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of the positions. Is it a verbal dispute? A genuinely substantive normative one that's just hard? Are the two camps answering different questions (what can be proven about agents vs what agents should be) without noticing it? Or is my scientist's framing itself the mistake, because there's no fact of the matter here of the sort that modelling disputes presuppose?

And crucially: given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A new distinction, a new theorem, empirical work on how real people handle sequential choices, or nothing at all — and why? Don't hold back on the technical side, by the way. I've read enough to follow the Allais stuff and the standard pump constructions, so assume I know the lay of the land and tell me what the land actually is. I'd rather be corrected than accomodated.
