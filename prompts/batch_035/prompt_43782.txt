I'm preparing discussion material for an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of logic, built around Lewis Carroll's 1895 Mind paper in which a tortoise accepts both a premise and a conditional yet still refuses the conclusion, forcing its interlocutor to append ever-higher-level conditionals as further premises without ever closing the gap. My students have grasped the standard reading — that inference rules cannot be replaced by additional premises — but the seminar has stalled on what that moral actually amounts to, and I'd like to dramatize the disagreement by having two philosophers fight it out. Please write a dialogue for me.

The setting: two colleagues — one a conventionalist in the spirit of Carnap, the other a Quinean revisabilist (name them as you like) — lingering over coffee after a departmental talk on Carroll's paper. The conventionalist holds that a rule like modus ponens is constitutive of the meaning of "if": someone who accepts the premises but withholds the conclusion, like the tortoise, has not raised a doubt but changed the subject — their "if" is not ours — so the regress exposes a kind of logical illiteracy rather than an epistemic gap, and there is nothing left for a justification of deduction to do. The revisabilist counters, drawing on Quine's holism, that justification always runs out somewhere and that the stopping point is practice rather than meaning: rules are entrenched habits of inference, and habits are revisable in principle — as when a logician, impressed by the quantum-mechanical picture of propositions, proposed revising the classical laws, or as paraconsistent logicians deliberately tolerate contradictions in inconsistent databases rather than letting them explode into triviality. On that view the tortoise's refusal is unintelligent only in degree; it is a first sketch of a rival practice. The two should genuinely engage with each other's points rather than talk past each other. Does "revising logic" mean discovering that we were wrong, or redefining our vocabulary — and can the revisabilist herself keep that distinction, given her attack on the analytic/synthetic line? If the conventionalist says deviant logicians merely speak another language, what does she say to the deviant logician who insists he is disagreeing with us rather than translating, and what becomes of the disputed word "if" across the "languages"? The revisabilist, for her part, should be pressed on whether the quantum and database cases are really revisions of logic itself or just local engineering choices made inside an unrevised classical core, and on why, if logic is just habit, anyone should feel the tortoise is making a mistake rather than merely being unusual.

Aim for roughly eight to twelve exchanges, let each character land at least one point that the other has to work to answer, and don't manufacture a tidy resolution — an honest stalemate or a mutual concession is better than a winner. Both should be at the top of their game; neither should be a strawman, and they should be allowed to concede small points without capitulating. Finally, a request at the meta-level: if you think I've misdescribed either position, or that the dialectic as I've set it up cannot sustain genuine disagreement — for instance, because the two sides are really talking past each other in a way neither character could repair from inside the conversation — tell me so plainly at the start rather than quietly complying. I'd rather fix my framing than hand my students a counterfeit exchange.
