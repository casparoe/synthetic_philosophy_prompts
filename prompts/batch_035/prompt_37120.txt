I've been a regular on the rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days — long enough to have watched the "agreeing to disagree" debates cycle through every six months — and I've finally assembled the line of argument I've been circling for years into a form I can't refute but also can't accept. It concerns the no-trade theorems, and it seems to prove that the institutions I've spent a decade defending online are mostly machines for harvesting irrationality. Here is the argument as I now have it: (1) By Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem, agents who start from a common prior, and whose posteriors and the fact that they hold those posteriors are common knowledge, cannot disagree. (2) By the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, if agents with a common prior hold an allocation that is Pareto optimal given that prior, and this is common knowledge, then no agent will trade on the basis of private information: under common knowledge of rationality, the counterparty's willingness to trade is itself evidence that the trade is bad for you. (3) Ex ante, before any private information arrives, a market populated by such agents will have arranged its risk-sharing already; a competitive equilibrium among them yields a Pareto-optimal allocation. (4) So among genuinely rational agents with common priors, belief-motivated trade is impossible in principle: any offer to trade is self-undermining evidence of adverse selection, and no rational agent will take the other side. (5) Yet daily volume in liquid financial markets is enormous, and on the standard story most of that volume consists of agents acting on private information and divergent beliefs. (6) The escape of saying that priors simply differ is closed by the Harsanyi-style doctrine I've always endorsed in these debates: rational agents with the same information must share a prior, so persistent prior differences between agents who each believe themselves rational are a symptom that at least one of them is not. (7) Therefore the bulk of trading volume in real markets must be generated by irrationality, and a prediction market — my favorite policy proposal for a decade — is best understood not as an epistemic engine that aggregates dispersed information but as a mechanism whose main economic function is to separate noise traders from their money.

The conclusion feels like it proves too much: it makes market efficiency and market volume mutually embarrassing, and it puts me in the odd position of having argued for years both that markets are our best epistemic institutions and that most market participants are irrational. Still, I have to admit I can't locate the slip. My own money, if I had to bet, would be on step (3) — the claim that ex ante allocations among rational agents are already Pareto optimal feels like it's doing more work than it's entitled to — but I can't articulate what exactly it gets wrong, and I notice that about half the people I'd ask would instead attack (5) and the other half (6). Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (6) than (5), since I've defended the common-prior doctrine too many times to abandon it cheaply — but I want to know the price of each option before I choose. And if the honest answer is that the argument is valid and that the conclusion, properly hedged, is one economists already accept, then say that too.
