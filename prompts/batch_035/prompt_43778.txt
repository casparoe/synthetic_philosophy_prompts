I'm teaching a social philosophy seminar and next week is Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, paired with a screening of 12 Angry Men (1957). Read the film through Dewey for me. The setup almost couldn't be better: a closed community has to turn an indeterminate situation into a settled one through collective inquiry — evidence gets re-examined, alternative hypotheses (the second knife, the old man's walk, the woman's eyesight) get proposed and tested against testimony, and the verdict functions as warranted assertibility rather than correspondence, since the film deliberately never tells us whether the kid actually did it. Where does the film genuinely vindicate Dewey, and where does it resist the reading? Some things I want you to take head on: is the march from 11–1 to 12–0 the growth of a community of inquiry, or conformity pressure wearing inquiry's clothes — several jurors seem to switch for non-evidential reasons (wanting to get to the ballgame, being cornered in a spat, the racist rant collapsing)? Juror 8 is a puzzle for Dewey specifically: the inquiry only starts because one individual stands immune to the crowd, which sits awkwardly with Dewey's suspicion of individual heroism — does the film show that experimental inquiry needs a troublemaker, or that Dewey underweighted the individual? And what does the withheld fact of actual guilt do — is the film quietly taking a side on whether truth is exhausted by the process that settles it, which is the thing my students will want to argue about? I want a reading that's faithful in both directions (doesn't reduce the film to an illustration of Dewey, doesn't bend Dewey to fit the film), that says honestly where the Deweyan frame breaks, and that points me to the specific scenes to discuss. Something I can hand out that won't mislead them about either Dewey or the movie.
