Quick bit of context: I run a philosophy club at a sixth form college, about 16 students in year 12 and 13, and we get one 50-minute session a fortnight. Next half term the theme is minds and machines, and I want to open with an activity rather than a lecture. Here's the idea I want you to design in full.

I want the students to essentially *be* Searle's Chinese Room without anyone telling them that's what they're doing until afterwards. So: the group is arranged somehow so that, as a collective system, they take in written questions in a language none of them speaks (let's assume Chinese, since I can probably borrow a Chinese-speaking teacher from the languages department to help with prep) and send back written replies that, to a person outside the room, look like fluent answers from someone who understands what they're saying. The students inside only ever handle symbols and rules. I don't care whether you have them sit in a literal circle behind a screen or whatever — that's your call — but I want the mechanics concrete: what the rulebook actually contains, how it's built before the session (I can get the bilingual teacher to prep materials but she's not free on the day), what gets passed in and out, who the outside interlocutor is, and how we keep the outside person genuinely ignorant of the inside setup so the "conversation" stays honest.

Crucially I want them to run into the philosophical problem before it gets named. During the debrief — and only after they've each been asked separately whether *they* understood anything, and whether the *room* did, and been made to notice those can come apart — that's when the actual argument should get laid out, and that's when they can hear terms like the systems reply and the robot reply. Ideally the debrief maps specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the literature: e.g. if one student starts improvising instead of following the rules, or if the group spontaneously divides the rulebook into departments, that's gold and I want the debrief to say exactly what it's gold for. I'd also like some pre-scripted outside questions that are designed to bait the room into breaking character, if you see what I mean — questions where the rule-following answer is subtly weird.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — bored students, someone peeking at meanings, the rulebook being too slow, outside kids telling inside kids what the symbols mean, whatever — and how to handle each without wrecking the point. Second, and this is the part I most want you to be honest about: the real argument needs the room to be *genuinely good* at Chinese, and a classroom version can't deliver that, so the students might conclude the room failed because it was a cheap fake rather than because rule-following as such lacks understanding. Which parts of the thought experiment can the activity actually reproduce, and which parts do I have to just stipulate from the front of the room at the end?

Practicalities: assume a normal classroom, card, envelopes, marker pens, a screen or partition if needed, no laptops. Whole plan — rules, materials, prep, minute-by-minute timeline, debrief script, failure modes — under 700 words please, and keep it tight rather than exhaustive. British English, and don't spell seperate sections with headers if they're just getting in the way of the word count — running prose with a few bolded stage names is fine.
