So last night me and my friend Dev had one of our marathon arguments — went till 2am, four cups of tea — about whether his plan to quit his job and go freelance as a video editor will actually work out. And it ended the way these always end for us: we each said a number (I gave it a 40% chance of working out within two years, he said 75), and then, because that's our ritual, he offered to bet at the midpoint odds, I refused the bet, and per our rule whoever refuses a bet at their own stated odds has to nudge there number toward the other person's. So I ended at 48% and he ended at 68%, and we called it a night.

The thing is, we've run this ritual for years on any factual-ish dispute — his cousin's startup, whether the landlord would renew, whether our team would get relegated. But for arguments about values (whether it's okay to keep flying with that cheap airline given their safety record, how much you owe your parents) we never bet and never update; we just agree to disagree. And last night, after we'd done the numbers, Dev said something that's been bugging me since: the whole ritual only makes sense if we each think the other is roughly as good at judging this stuff as we are ourselves — otherwise why would my refusing a bet mean anything to him? Which made me realise I don't actually know what our practice is presupposing.

I gather there's some result in decision theory — Aumann's agreement theorem, I think? a friend of a friend mentioned it once — that two rational people who share the same evidence can't agree to disagree. So my question is: what does our little ritual actually presuppose? Is it basically an informal version of that theorem, or is it presupposing something stronger, or weaker? Does the fact that we never apply it to value disagreements presuppose that value disputes can't be moved by evidence, or only that we don't want them moved? Is the practice even consistent — the same me who moves 8 points because I balked at a bet also won't budge an inch on the ethics stuff; can both of those be right, or is one of us implicitly treating one domain as evidence all the way down and the other as not? And where is the ritual quietly doing work I haven't noticed — e.g. does it presuppose that whenever we disagree, at least one of us must be overconfident, thereby ruling out that we just want different things from the prediction?

Please reconstruct the implicit epistemology behind the practice, tell me which parts of it the ritual strictly needs and which it could drop and still work, and say what would have to change if we took the agreement-theorem thinking fully seriously (would we even be allowed our value-disagreement exception?). Write the whole answer in British English, please.
