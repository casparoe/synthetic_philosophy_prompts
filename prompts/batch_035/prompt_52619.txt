I teach a seminar on Hume's Essays, Moral and Political, and last week we spent two sessions on the economic essays of the Political Discourses. A student ended the second session with an argument that has been troubling me since, because if it is sound, it turns Hume into a champion of exactly the kind of easy-money policy he detested. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis of it.

First, the background from Hume himself, as the student laid it out. In the essay Of Money, Hume argues that money is not itself wealth: the real riches of a nation consist in its goods and the industry of its people, and where the quantity of money is greater, prices are proportionally higher, so that the amount of money circulating matters for the price level and, in the long run, for little else. Yet in the same essay he allows a famous exception: in the interval between the arrival of an increased quantity of gold and silver and the corresponding rise of prices, the new money does have a real effect, because people find themselves with more to spend before anything has become dearer, and this animation of spirits stimulates industry, indeed he says the interval is the only period during which an increase of money is favorable to industry. And in the essay Of the Balance of Trade, Hume argues that trade between nations automatically redistributes specie until prices across them equalize, so that money flows out of a country whose prices are high and into one whose prices are low, without any state needing to hoard or ration its coin. In the same essay he also warns that a nation which multiplies paper credit beyond the stock of metal drains its metal away and exposes itself to grave dangers.

Now the argument itself:

(1) For Hume, an increase in the quantity of money stimulates industry during, and only during, the interval between the arrival of new money and the rise of prices.

(2) The interval ends when prices adjust; prices adjust when sellers and workers come to anticipate the new level; so the interval lasts exactly as long as the increase remains, in the relevant sense, unanticipated.

(3) A government can therefore arrange a long sequence of small injections of money, each individually too small to move anyone's expectations, so that at any given moment the economy has recently received an injection whose price effects have not yet been absorbed.

(4) If the economy has always recently received an unabsorbed injection, then it is always inside an interval of the kind described in (1), and so the stimulus Hume attributes to that interval is always operative: industry is perpetually being spurred by money that has arrived but not yet been priced in.

(5) Therefore, on Hume's own premises, a state that manages its paper credit with sufficient delicacy can keep its population permanently in the condition of heightened industry that, on his account, an accidental windfall of treasure produces only briefly.

(6) Since, on Hume's account, this stimulation of industry is the only real benefit money can confer, such a state has reason to pursue the policy indefinitely: continuous, calibrated inflation is a standing engine of prosperity.

(7) But Hume regards the multiplication of paper credit as a danger that banishes the precious metals and invites ruin, and he holds public debt and projectors of easy schemes in contempt; he would have regarded (6) with horror. So either Hume's monetary theory commits him to a policy he condemned, or some step in the argument fails.

Tell me which step you reject, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: explain why the step you reject looked irresistible in the first place, and then say what must be given up or qualified if that step is abandoned, both as a matter of Hume interpretation and as a matter of the economics itself. If you think the argument is in fact valid, and that Hume simply failed to draw the consequence of his own theory, defend that view against the most obvious objections. I will confess my own attachments: I would very much like to keep (1), since the interval claim is the heart of what makes the essay Of Money more than a dry statement of a quantity theory, and I am drawn to (2) as well. I am aware that this may leave me paying too much elsewhere, which is precisely why I want an independent adjudication.
