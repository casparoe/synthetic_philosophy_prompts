I've been reading about the contrast between Norway's Halden Prison and the American system of mass incarceration, and I keep running into what looks like a philosophical fault line rather than just a policy disagreement. In the Norwegian model, the official framing is that the loss of liberty is itself the entire punishment: everything inside the prison is supposed to be as close to ordinary life as security permits, guards are trained as mentors as much as custodians, most sentences are capped at twenty-one years, and release is keyed to assessed risk of reoffending rather than to a debt fully served. The American system, by contrast, calls its departments "corrections" and its institutions "correctional facilities," yet maintains life without parole, three-strikes laws, solitary confinement, and in some states felony disenfranchisement that outlives the sentence itself. What I want to know is what each of these patterns presupposes about the point of punishment. Does the Norwegian setup really presuppose a purely forward-looking, consequentialist theory of punishment, or could a retributivist also endorse it on the ground that confinement, however humane, already gives the offender their just deserts? And does the American pattern presuppose that desert is a fixed quantum fixed at sentencing by the seriousness of the crime, or something stranger?

Here's the wrinkle that confuses me most. The American system also has sentence-reduction mechanisms — "good time" credits for institutional conduct, and parole boards that can release someone early — and these seem to presuppose that desert is not a fixed quantum at all, but a running ledger that can be paid down through behavior after sentencing. That sits awkwardly next to life without parole and the death penalty in the same systems, where the underlying judgment seems to be that some deserts can never be paid down no matter how the person changes. Meanwhile Norway has its own anomaly: a preventive detention mechanism by which someone judged still dangerous can be held past the twenty-one-year cap, which looks like an admission that desert runs out before dangerousness does — two separate grounds for confinement sitting in one system. So my questions are: what implicit theory of desert, responsibility, and human change does each system's package of practices actually require; which parts of each package could be reinterpreted under the rival theory, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than committed; and are there internal inconsistencies — say, between good-conduct credits and LWOP, or between the twenty-one-year cap and preventive detention — that no single theory of punishment could license all at once? I'd also like to know what the two systems presuppose about the malleability of the self: whether someone who committed a crime at twenty-five is, for purposes of desert, the same person who stands before the parole board at fifty, and whether the two systems give different implicit answers to that.

Please write the answer in American English, and be disciplined about separating what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I'm less interested in which system is better than in what each one's underlying picture of desert, deterrence, and personal change would have to be for its pieces to hang together.
