I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the past few months I've been keeping a running document of my positions on the ethics of harming versus not helping — basically a spec sheet for my own intuitions, so I can check that it passes its own test cases. I'd like an audit of whether the set hangs together. Here's what I've committed to, with the reasoning that sold me on each:

1. There is a real moral difference between killing someone and letting them die. The clearest case: a surgeon must not kill one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying ones, even though refusing to operate means letting five people die.

2. I would divert a runaway trolley away from five people and onto a side track where it will kill one, and I would not push a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path to stop it, even though the arithmetic is the same in both cases.

3. The difference between the two trolley cases is explained by something like the doctrine of double effect: harm you intend as a means to your end is categorically worse than harm you merely foresee as a side effect.

4. What ultimately grounds the killing/letting-die distinction is causal responsibility. Killing is worse because you bring the death about — you make the world contain a death it otherwise would not have contained — whereas letting die is a failure to make the world better. I take this to be why the surgeon is worse than the person who declines to donate a kidney to a stranger: the surgeon causes a death; the non-donor merely fails to prevent one.

5. Turning off a ventilator at the request of a competent, terminally ill patient is not killing. It's allowing the patient's disease to kill them, and it's permissible in a way that administering a lethal injection is not.

6. There is a demanding duty of rescue. Walking past a pond where a child is drowning, when you could wade in at modest cost to yourself, is nearly as wrong as drowning the child yourself — and how wrong it is to stand by scales with the stakes, so failing to save many is worse than failing to save one.

Each of these still seems right to me on its own, the way each function in a module can pass its unit tests while the integration tests still fail. So I'm not asking you to argue me out of any single position. What I want is a consistency review: which pairs or triples of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and, for each genuine conflict, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me a flat list, and tell me explicitly which single change would clean up the most.
