My local effective altruism group spent a whole meeting arguing about where to direct our pooled donation this year, and I was drafted to summarize the dispute, but I honestly can't referee it myself. The candidate cause was research on welfare in farmed insects — specifically the black soldier fly larvae raised in industrial facilities for animal feed and pet food, which now involves staggering numbers of individuals globally. Maya argued for switching most of the pool to this. Her reasoning: even if each larva has only a small chance of being sentient, and even if it would count for a tiny fraction of a vertebrate's suffering, the arithmetic runs away from everything else — trillions of individuals times even a one percent credence in sentience times even a thousandth of a chicken's moral weight still swamps the broiler welfare campaigns we've funded in the past, where the expected lives improved per dollar is much smaller. Dev pushed back that this proves too much: the same move licenses donating to plant-sentience research, or to genuinely fringe possibilities with negligible probabilities, since anything times a vast enough number looks overwhelming; he called it a Pascalian trap where tiny probabilities get laundered into huge expected values. Maya replied that the credences aren't arbitrary numbers you dial up until the cause wins — they're graded by evidence, and insects sit on the evidence gradient in a way plants don't: nociceptors, opioid-like receptors, central nervous integration, motivational trade-offs between avoiding harm and pursuing rewards, even experiments where bumblebees repeatedly rolled small objects for no apparent reward, which some read as play. Dev's last word was that her numbers are doing all the work while being uncalibratable: whether the right credence is 0.1% or 10% flips the entire priority ranking, no amount of behavioral evidence discriminates between those two numbers, and so the expected-value calculation is just a formal costume worn by an intuition — and that at least the broiler campaigns rest on probabilities we can actually defend.

Then say who is more right overall, and flag anything both of them seem to be missing — for instance whether the choice between funding research versus funding direct interventions changes the way the uncertainty should be handled.
