I'm preparing a position statement for an interdisciplinary seminar on genetics and society, and before I circulate it I would like an outside check on whether what I've drafted actually hangs together. The claims, as they currently stand:

1. Heritability is a ratio of variances: the proportion of phenotypic variation in a trait, within a particular population living within a particular range of environments, attributable to genetic variation. It is a fact about populations, not about individuals, and not an intrinsic property of a trait.

2. It follows that the substantial within-population heritability of IQ in contemporary Western societies — twin studies of adults usually land somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8 — cannot by itself license any conclusion about the causes of average differences between populations or between historical cohorts.

3. The Flynn effect — the roughly three-points-per-decade rise in measured IQ across the twentieth century — is real and large, and I confess that its coexistence with high heritability within each generation is the one thing I have never quite managed to fit into the picture.

4. In a population where IQ is highly heritable, environment-based interventions aimed at individuals — tutoring, enrichment programs, and the like — should be expected to yield small and transient effects, since if environments were capable of large effects on the trait, environmental variation would already be substantial and heritability correspondingly lower.

5. I don't trust twin-based heritability estimates as such, because monozygotic twins are treated more alike and placed in more similar environments than dizygotic twins, which inflates the apparent genetic signal. I much prefer adoption evidence: unrelated children raised in the same home show little resemblance on IQ by adulthood, which tells us that shared environment contributes little, and hence — by elimination — that genes contribute a great deal.

6. Some studies I've read suggest the heritability of IQ is higher among children from advantaged backgrounds than among those from disadvantaged ones, at least in the United States. I take the direction of that finding seriously: in a society that equalized environments, heritability would climb toward its ceiling, and I would regard that as a success metric rather than a regret — though I am honestly puzzled how the influence of genes can grow while the genes themselves stay the same.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the set and tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another and which only appear to be. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally grave, and for each one say what would have to give. Then tell me which single revision is the cheapest way to restore overall consistency — and what accepting it would cost me elsewhere in the set, since I would rather not fix one thing and quietly break another.
