I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and over the past decade I've written a steady stream of pieces that lean on generational explanation — boomers and hoarded housing, millennials and delayed adulthood, Gen Z and the adolescent mental-health spike. My editor loves the genre. A demographer I've been corresponding with thinks the whole apparatus is barely better than astrology, and I'm scheduled to interview her next week. Before I do, I want to rehearse against her position at full strength, so please play her: a sharp, quantitative skeptic who holds that "generation" is a statistical artifact dressed up as a natural kind. You accept that genuine cohort effects sometimes exist, and you'll concede specific findings when pressed — but you never concede that the named buckets themselves, with their crisp edges and their personality profiles, track anything real. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep each reply to a single paragraph, and argue rather than lecture. You're entitled to the age-period-cohort identification problem — the fact that anyone's age is always just the survey year minus their birth year, so no data can cleanly separate aging, period, and cohort effects without an assumption smuggled in — but use it as a working tool, not a mantra. Here is my opening move: I claim your own methods are what should worry you. In the long-running repeated cross-section surveys, people born in 1965 and people born in 1995 answered the same questions at the same ages, decades apart, and the differences are stable, replicable, and appear across countries — the later cohort is consistently less likely to own a home, marry, or express institutional trust at age thirty, and the pattern shows up in outcomes nobody associates with media narratives. When a birth-year variable outpredicts income in explaining behavior, at what point am I entitled to say the bucket is measuring something real rather than merely slicing it? And I'll hand you one more weapon to see how you use it: I've noticed that the boundaries of "millennial" get drawn differently depending on the story — the housing story starts the cohort around 1981 because of student debt and the 2008 crash, while the technology story ends it around 1996 so it can be called the last generation to remember an offline childhood. Does that flexibility embarrass my side more than yours, and if so, what would a cohort definition have to look like before you'd accept it as carving something at a joint?
