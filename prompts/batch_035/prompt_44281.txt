I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, writing a dissertation chapter on the distinction between mechanical rigidity and behavioral flexibility, and I've been asked to give a guest lecture for an undergraduate film-and-philosophy course. The concept I want to build the lecture around is what Douglas Hofstadter dubbed sphexishness: the digger wasp that, before stowing prey in her burrow, leaves it at the threshold while she inspects the inside; if an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she's within, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and re-runs the inspection — for as many iterations as the experimenter cares to repeat. From outside the loop the behavior looks intelligent; inside, it turns out to be a closed subroutine, triggered and terminated by cues in the world rather than by any monitoring of her own goal. I'd like to read Groundhog Day as a film about this spectrum, and I want help stress-testing the reading before I present it.

Here is the reading in outline. Phil Connors begins the film as a kind of sphex: within the loop he runs fixed routines keyed to cues — the same greeting to Ned Ryerson on the corner, the same rehearsed lines on Rita — and he seems to believe that running the correct sequence of moves will mechanically produce the output he wants, so that Rita functions for him the way the threshold functions for the wasp: a trigger in the world that opens and closes his loop. The townspeople, meanwhile, are literal sphexes, running the same script every day, and only Phil, positioned outside the loop, is in a position to notice. The film then stages a theory of what it takes to stop being sphexish, and the striking claim is that it is not more cleverness: Phil is enormously clever inside the loop, and cleverness only makes him a more elaborate sphex — even his suicide attempts have the form of submitting the same output and expecting the environment to accept it. What finally breaks the pattern is a change in what he wants, so that flexibility turns out to be motivational rather than cognitive. And the loop raises the free will question in an odd form: with tomorrow's consequences abolished, Phil has maximal options and minimal freedom, and his escape reads like a compatibilist parable — freedom as the capacity to revise one's own program rather than exemption from determinism.

Where I need your help: (1) Does the reading survive the places the film resists it? The loop is externally imposed, so Phil's early rigidity looks like a fact about his situation rather than his psychology, whereas the wasp's rigidity is internal and her world is normal — is that a fatal inversion, or can the reading accommodate it, and is the film really about sphexishness or only about repetition, which is a different thing? (2) Which specific scenes would you anchor the lecture to, and where does the film itself seem to know the difference between failing because one's routine is closed and failing because one has bad information? (3) What does the film add to the rigidity/flexibility debate that argument alone doesn't — and where is the analogy doing work it shouldn't? I want the lecture to be faithful in both directions: a real reading of the film rather than an illustration with a movie attached, and a real treatment of the sphexishness idea rather than a stretch. Please be candid about the parts of my outline that don't hold.
