My philosophy reading group has spent the last few weeks on the internal vs external reasons debate, and I've noticed something annoying about basically every example in the literature: whenever someone wants an external reason to exist where no internal reason does, they make the agent defective in some way. Depressed, or brainwashed, or ignorant, or compulsive, or holding some obviously false belief about where their interests lie. That hands the internalist an easy out, because they can say the external reason would convert into an internal one once the defect is fixed through sound deliberation. So what I want is a case engineered the other way: an agent who is already everything the internalist's idealization machinery demands — fully informed, free of compulsion, coherent, imaginative, has genuinely reflected for years — and who still cannot arrive, by any sound deliberative route, at the reason the externalist insists she has. And crucially, don't let the agent be estranged from the value at stake: she should recognize and even love the value in question, just not for herself. Something like (but you can do better) a conservatory-trained pianist who knows exactly what her talent could produce, still goes to concerts, champions other performers, and after mature deliberation prefers teaching middle-school band in a small town — and keeps preferring it on every re-examination. The external reason I have in mind is that her talent and the greatness of the music that would exist supposedly ground a reason for her to perform that no amount of correct deliberation will ever move her to act on.

So construct that scenario for me, one paragraph, ordinary and realistic — no sci-fi, no exotic psychology, the agent should be someone you'd actually admire. Then walk through what the internal reasons theorist says about her and what the external reasons theorist says, and show the case meets each of my constraints rather than stipulating that it does. After that I want the honest part: which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the dispute if you remove that feature (say, if she were merely ambivalent or underinformed). Then take up the externalist's best counterattack — the claim that a deliberative route from her existing motivations to performing still exists somewhere in the abstract — and tell me whether that reply is admissible or whether it quietly smuggles the external reason in as a premise. Finish with your own view on which side comes out of this case better, and be upfront about how confident you are in each claim along the way, singling out the point you're least sure of. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold, just like a long email to the group.
