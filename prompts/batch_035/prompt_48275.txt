My roommate, who is finishing a statistics PhD, walked me through an argument at dinner last week about referendum voting, and I've been unable to shake it since — every premise sounded fine in isolation, but the conclusion seems to prove far too much. Here is how she laid it out. (1) The Condorcet jury theorem says that when each member of a group judging between two alternatives is independently more likely than not to be correct, the probability that the majority picks the right answer approaches certainty as the group grows. (2) With a national electorate of a hundred million voters, even a very weak individual edge — each voter being right, say, 51 percent of the time — compounds so that the majority is right with a probability indistinguishable from one. (3) Almost nobody is systematically worse than a coin flip on any question they bother to form an opinion about, so the competence assumption is satisfied for practically any binary question with a fact of the matter. (4) Therefore, on any such question put to a national vote — whether a statistical claim in a ballot pamphlet is accurate, whether a given policy caused inflation, whether a scientific consensus is correct — the majority position is right as a matter of near-mathematical necessity. (5) So the familiar complaint that voters are too ignorant or misinformed to be trusted with referendums is not a political judgment at all but a straightforward arithmetic error, and whenever experts disagree with a referendum outcome, the referendum is right and the experts are wrong.

I'm taking a course on democratic theory this term, and my instructor keeps gesturing at epistemic arguments for majority rule, so at first the argument felt like exactly what I'd been looking for. But the conclusion strikes me as absurd: it would mean that a hundred million people all exposed to the same misinformation, the same cable channels, the same viral posts are collectively infallible in a way that a committee of a thousand experts could never be, and that we should settle essentially every contested factual question by plebiscite. Yet every time I try to locate the flaw, the individual premises look fine to me again. My vague suspicion is that something goes wrong between (2) and (4) — something about how the theorem is being carried over from the setting where it was proved to the setting she's applying it in — but I can't name it, and I also can't tell whether the deeper problem is instead that (3) quietly turns every political question into the kind of question the theorem is about. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd most like to keep (1), since the theorem itself seems like plain probability, but I want to understand exactly what I'm buying if I keep it — and whether keeping it leaves me any residual epistemic argument for majorities at all, once the step I give up is gone.
