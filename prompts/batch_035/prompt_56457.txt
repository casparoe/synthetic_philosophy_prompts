I've been on my town's historic preservation commission for a couple of years now (small city, mostly volunteers), and I'm starting to feel like our practices around the past don't add up to any single coherent thing. Some background: I took a philosophy elective ages ago where we read an essay Nietzsche published in 1874, usually translated as something like On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life. He basically says history is only justified by what it does for present-day life, and he sketches three ways of relating to the past: monumental history, where you mine the past for great deeds and heroes to inspire present action; antiquarian history, where you preserve and revere the local and the old because it's where you come from; and critical history, where you drag the past to the bench and condemn it, tearing down what you can't live with. He thinks each one is needed but also that each becomes toxic or distorting if it dominates — monumental history falsifies by smoothing over context, antiquarian history turns into worshipful hoarding, critical history can't condemn the past without standing on ground the past itself produced.

Here's the thing: I'm pretty sure our commission, and our city government more broadly, is doing all three at once, and I can't figure out if that's coherent or if we're quietly contradicting ourselves. Concretely: we blocked the demolition of a 1920s movie theater (pure antiquarian reverence, honestly — the thing is a fire trap and nobody can afford to renovate it); the city council just approved a fancy plaza downtown celebrating our founders with heroic bronze statues and inspirational quotes about the pioneer spirit (that feels monumental in Nietzsche's sense, and it's weirdly selective about which parts of the founding era get celebrated); and last year we voted to strip a eugenicist doctor's name off a middle school and move a monument he funded into storage (which looks like critical history doing its thing). Meanwhile a developer wants to turn a decomissioned power plant into housing, and half the commission wants to save the smokestacks as heritage while the other half asks what on earth they ever did for anyone's life.

So my question: what view of the past and its value do these municipal practices, taken together, actually presuppose? Is the mixture philosophically incoherent, or is it exactly what you'd expect if Nietzsche is right that all three modes serve life in different ways? Which of these practices would Nietzsche say only makes sense under one specific mode and which are underdetermined — could, say, the school renaming be justified on antiquarian or even monumental grounds too? And if we tried to run preservation policy on just one of the three modes consistently, what would change about what gets saved, torn down, or celebrated around here? Please answer in American English, and feel free to push back on my framing if you think I'm misreading the essay — I read it once, years ago, in a translation I no longer own.
