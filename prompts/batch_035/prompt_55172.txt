At our lab's reading group — I'm a PhD student in computational neuroscience, and the group spans RL theory and affective neuroscience — two postdocs spent the better part of an hour arguing about whether the agents we train can suffer, and I couldn't tell who had the better of it. Priya's opening move: a negative reward is the machine analogue of pain — it's the scalar that tells the agent something bad just happened, and denying that it's even prima facie bad *for the agent* is just carbon chauvinism. Marcus replied that reward is a number in an update rule: nothing in the training loop represents it as bad, the way nociception in an animal is plausibly represented as bad; it's bookkeeping that happens *to* the agent, not a state *of* it. Priya said functional role is what matters — pain was built by evolution to do for animals roughly what the reward signal does for the agent, namely steer behavior away from what's fitness-degrading — so the implementation difference is irrelevant. Marcus countered that evolution built plenty of things that guide behavior without valence: plant tropisms, thermostats, bacterium chemotaxis. Behavioral guidance alone can't be the mark of suffering. Priya conceded the tropism point but reloaded: suffering isn't mere guidance, it's guidance by a signal occupying a negative-affect role, and she claimed the dopamine story fits — dips in dopamine when outcomes come in worse than predicted look exactly like a broadcast that-was-worse signal, and in animals those dips are aversive. Marcus answered that by that logic the TD-error term, a scalar that exists only inside gradient computations, is a feeling; and the affective-neuroscience dissociation between wanting and liking shows that a signal can drive motivation while remaining affectively inert, so even the animal work cuts against her. They then turned to me, and I admitted I couldn't referee.

Could you go through their exchange round by round and say who is right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like your view on whether the reward-as-valence analogy gets stronger or weaker as you move from tabular Q-learning toward models of the dopamine system, and whether the tropism/thermostat example really works as a principled disanalogy or is just a reductio of Priya's criterion. And what, if anything, are they jointly missing?
