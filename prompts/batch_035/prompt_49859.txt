I run a small reading group on philosophy of math, and next week's session is on whether actual infinities (as opposed to just potential, going-on-forever ones) make any sense. I want to open with some classic puzzles, but I'd rather not burn the whole session on ones that dissolve the moment you think about them carefuly. So could you rate each of the following on a 0-10 scale for how much genuine trouble it causes someone who thinks actual completed infinities are perfectly coherent? For each one, give the score plus a sentence or two of justification.

1. Hilbert's hotel: infinitely many rooms, all full, yet you can still fit a new guest by shifting every occupant up one room.
2. Galileo's paradox: there are exactly as many perfect squares as natural numbers, even though most naturals aren't squares.
3. Thomson's lamp: a lamp is switched on and off faster and faster over two minutes, and at the end it's supposedly neither on nor off.
4. The Ross-Littlewood urn: at each step you add ten balls and remove one; after infinitely many steps the urn is somehow empty, and the answer even depends on which ball you remove at each step, which always seemed fishy to me.
5. Cantor's diagonal argument: not the conclusion that the reals outnumber the naturals, but whether the reasoning itself has any gaps a skeptic could exploit.

One extra question: Thomson himself apparently thought his lamp just shows the setup has no determinate end state, not that supertasks are impossible in general — if that's right, should it lower the lamp's score?

Also, am I missing an obvious candidate for this list? If so give it the same treatment (score + one-liner). Keep in mind my group has no math background beyond calculus, so nothing too wild.
