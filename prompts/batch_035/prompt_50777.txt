As a novelist I've built two decades of characters on a philosophical conviction I want stress-tested: that people have stable character traits — courage, callousness, generosity — which are genuine dispositions in the metaphysician's sense, captured roughly by a conditional analysis (a person is brave if they would act bravely were a serious threat to arise), and that these traits, not situations, carry most of the explanatory weight in why people do what they do. I know this sits badly with situationist social psychology — the obedience and bystander experiments — and I have a ready reply that I half suspect is too cheap: that the experiments show situations finking or masking standing dispositions, the way an electro-fink stops the fragile glass from breaking and packing foam stops it from shattering, rather than showing that the traits were never there. What would genuinely count against my view? Sort the possible defeaters — longitudinal or cross-situational consistency results, formal problems for the conditional analysis itself, cases where the fink/mask reply becomes an unfalsifiable charm against any evidence, the worry that traits are evaluating rather than explaining — by how decisive they would be, and tell me which of them a serious defender of trait realism would concede as counting against the position and which they would fight, distinguishing evidence that would be fatal from evidence that is merely awkward. Be honest about whether my situationist reply has insulated the position from every possible outcome, and whether that insulation is a defect for a view whose whole job is to explain behavior. Flag how confident you are in each major claim, and name the single point in your answer you are least sure of. Conclude with a final line of the form "Verdict: X", where X is exactly one of: exposed, exposed but defensible, insulated but legitimately so, insulated and vacuous.
