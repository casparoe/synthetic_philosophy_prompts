i'm a PhD student in epistemology (mostly on skepticism, so early modern is adjacent rather than home turf), and I keep going back and forth on Berkeley's master argument. You know the one: Philonous asks Hylas to conceive of a tree or house existing unperceived, Hylas says he can do it easily, and Philonous replies that this is impossible -- the moment you try, you're conceiving it *to yourself*, so what you're actually conceiving is a tree-with-a-conceiver, or at best you're doing something that doesn't count as conceiving the tree per se. Hylas half-concedes ("I do not only see it, but I perceive that I see it" is roughly how he puts it, iirc) and Berkeley declares victory.

My advisor thinks the argument is a nonstarter because it conflates the act of conceiving with the content: you can think about a tree existing unperceived without thereby making the tree perceived, just like you can think about a number that nobody has ever thought of without contradicting yourself. That's basically the standard de re / de dicto complaint. But another grad student in my program, who's more Berkeley-sympathetic, argues the text doesn't deserve the flatfooted reading: on his view Philonous's point is better understood as the thought *that I am conceiving it* being inescapably part of what Hylas has in mind, so the purported conception is a counterfeit, and that if the objector says "then I conceive the tree per se," the reply is that such a thought would be in principle indistinguishable in content from one about nothing at all -- the wise/foolish thinker distinction from elsewhere in the Dialogues is supposed to do some work here. I honestly can't tell how much of that is actually in the text versus charitable reconstruction.
