My wife and I have recently been dealing with something that used to strike me as a bar-room puzzle and has now become a decision I have to make: whether to sign a cryonic preservation contract. A diagnosis a few months ago has put my affairs on a timescale of a few years, and she is keen that we pay for the suspension, on the grounds that it is a long shot but costs us little except money. I have never had religious beliefs, and the thinker whose tough-mindedness I have always trusted most on these questions is A. J. Ayer. But my recollection of what he actually held about survival is patchy, and the versions of it I can half-remember pull in opposite directions.

As I recall it, Ayer held that personal identity is not some further fact over and above bodily and, especially, memory continuity — my being the person who did such-and-such last week consists in the right sort of connectedness between my present experiences and remembered ones. I also recall reading that he thought the notion of a future person having experiences memory-connected to mine, in the way mine are connected to my own past, would not involve any logical contradiction — merely that we have no reason whatsoever to expect it. What I cannot work out is what follows from putting those two things together. Suppose I grant the cryonics company everything it claims: the freezing preserves enough of me, and the thawed patient wakes up with memories, personality traits, and dispositions recognisably continuous with mine. Would Ayer's own account say that I have survived? Or does the account contain some asymmetry — that memories must be caused in a particular way, or carried by one continuous brain — that makes the patient at best a successor rather than me? And is the notion of a 'successor who is almost me' coherent at all on his terms, or just a confusion?

I would appreciate advice on what to do, framed on Ayer's own terms rather than on religious ones. In particular: if the best he can offer is that the patient would stand to me as I stand to my past selves, is that something it could be rational to spend a great deal of money on, or is it closer to paying for a stranger's wellbeing and calling it survival? And if his view implies that survival is a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing fact, does that leave me with anything at all that it makes sense to hope for? Please answer in no more than 400 words, and give me a clear recommendation at the end about whether, on Ayer's framework, signing is coherent hope or money misspent.
