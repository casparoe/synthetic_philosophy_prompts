My first real paycheck hit my account last week and I stared at the gap between gross and net for a good ten minutes, and then this weekend at my roommate's birthday dinner that staring turned into a two hour arguement I got to watch from the middle seat. Dan (works in crypto, very online) said taxation is just theft with extra steps: the government takes your money without asking you individually, and if a private party did the same thing -- even while leaving a polite note about roads and schools -- we'd all call it robbery. Maya, who's in law school, said that's confused from the get-go, because "your money" before tax isn't a well-defined thing: what you're entitled to is determined by the legal system, and the legal system is funded by taxes, so Dan is measuring the government's take against a baseline the government itself creates. Her analogy was that it's like complaining the referee's whistle took points off the board when the scoreboard only exists because there's a referee at all. Dan had two comebacks: first, that "you consented by staying" style reasoning proves too much, because then the mafia has your consent too as long as it extorts you slightly less violently than the alternative would; and second, that the baseline point would mean the government literally cannot steal from anyone ever, since it gets to define what counts as yours in the first place -- which is exactly the conclusion an actual thief would love to reach. Maya said he was deliberatly misreading her: her claim wasn't that anything a government labels a tax is automatically fine, it was that "theft" means taking what you're *entitled* to, entitlement is a normative question, and you can't settle it by pointing at the transfer and shouting "mugger" -- the slogan smuggles a whole theory of justice in disguised as a neutral description. Dan pounced on that: by her standard no burglary is ever theft either, since the burglar presumably also has a theory of why he deserved the TV; theft obviously doesn't require the thief to agree he's stealing, so why would it require the government's agreement? Maya's last word was that there's a difference between an entitlement claim being *contested* and there being no fact of the matter, and that whatever settles the question has to do with the process the taking went through -- who had standing to contest it, whether it was authorized under rules applying to everyone -- not whether the taker showed up in a ski mask or at an office building.

By the end they both turned to me, apparently because I'd been nodding at each of them in turn and they wanted a tiebreaker. I do not have a tiebreaker. Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who was right about which specific point, where each of them actually went wrong (if they did), and whether there's a move neither of them ever made that would have cut through the whole thing.
