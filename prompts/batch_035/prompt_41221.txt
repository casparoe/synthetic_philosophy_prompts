Exam question: "The gardener's failure to water the flowers caused them to die. Explain why causation by omission creates problems for (a) process-based theories of causation, such as Salmon's and Dowe's, and (b) counterfactual dependence theories, such as Lewis's. Then discuss what you take to be the most promising response, and defend it."

Student's answer: "An omission is the absence of some event, and absences are not themselves events: they lack spatiotemporal location and are not part of the causal order in the way events are. Process theories like Salmon's and Dowe's require a spatiotemporally continuous causal process, transmitting marks or conserved quantities, running from cause to effect. Plainly there is no such process running from the gardener to the dead flowers, so the case looks bad for them. However, the omission itself can be treated as a pseudo-process connecting gardener and flowers: pseudo-processes are processes too, and as long as the pseudo-process is spatiotemporally continuous, Dowe's theory can accommodate it, so process theories can actually handle the case after all.

Counterfactual theories fare differently. It is true that had the gardener watered the flowers, they would not have died; equally, had the Queen of England watered them, they would not have died. Since so many absences stand in the same counterfactual relation to the death, counterfactual dependence simply fails to hold in omission cases, and this shows that the counterfactual analysis is extensionally inadequate when it comes to omissions.

A further observation: the case is really one of prevention, since watering the flowers would have prevented their death, and prevention is just causation by omission under another name. So once we recognize that omissions really function as preventions, causation by omission turns out to pose no additional problem beyond the problem of prevention.

As for the most promising response: which omission gets cited as the cause is fixed by norms and expectations — the gardener's failure rather than the Queen's, because the gardener was supposed to water. Since pragmatic salience of this kind is what determines which omission is the cause, causation by omission is not a genuine metaphysical phenomenon at all; claims about omissions causing things are simply a loose, pragmatic way of talking about the underlying event-causal facts."
