Got into a weird argument about tort law and now I can't let it go. In most places parents can sue a doctor for "wrongful birth" — say the genetic screening was botched, the parents lost the chance to terminate, and they recover the extra costs of raising a child with a severe condition. But the child themself basically can't bring a "wrongful life" suit, claiming they'd have been better off never being born — courts tend to say comparing impaired existence to nonexistence is impossible, or not something a court should touch. What view about the value of coming into existence does this pair of rulings presuppose? Is it basically a rejection of Benatar's asymmetry, or can you get to the same result another way — e.g. a person-affecting story where there's nobody who's worse off if the child is never conceived? And is the combination even coherent, since the parents' claim seems to treat the child's existence as a big cost while the child's claim is denied precisely because existence can't be weighed? If you think the courts are smuggling in a substantive verdict about life rather than staying neutral, say which parts of the reasoning are doing the smuggling, and tell me what would have to change if a legislature took Benatar's asymmetry seriously.
