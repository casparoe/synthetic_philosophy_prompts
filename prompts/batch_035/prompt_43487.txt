I've drifted into holding the view that the standard ~40-hour week is basically a cultural fossil: the Protestant work ethic locked in a norm of continuous full-time employment, and there's no deep economic or technological reason for it anymore, so the right fix is something like a 20-hour norm at proportionally similar pay, phased in, with people spending the recovered time on actual leisure rather than second gigs. The French 35-hour experiment kind of fizzling, and Japan ending up with karoshi at one extreme, both look to me like evidence that hours norms are culture all the way down rather than responses to some economic constraint.

What would genuinely count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, not a laundry list: the empirical stuff (evidence on whether cutting hours vs cutting headcount actually preserves output, what happened with France beyond the headlines), why Keynes's prediction of a 15-hour week failed and whether that failure generalizes to my view, positional/competitive consumption arms races that make shorter hours unstable, the risk that "leisure" just turns into precarity for anyone who can't afford to work 20 hours, and the meaning-and-structure worry that people collapse without full-time work. For each, tell me whether a smart defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or wave it away, and be honest if the view has already been qualified into something like "20 hours for the right people in the right jobs" -- and whether that's a real problem or just common sense. Don't give me a both-says rundown: commit to which defeater is the strongest and tell me whether you think the view actually survives it.
