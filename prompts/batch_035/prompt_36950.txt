I'm on the safety evaluation team at a mid-sized AI lab — not a frontier player, but large enough that our deployment decisions affect millions of downstream users, and we're always racing someone to release. Every release cycle we face the same decision: a proposed safety intervention (a refusal-training revision, a monitoring layer, an interpretability-based pre-deployment screen, a stricter data filter) carries a measurable cost — benchmark performance, latency, compute, iteration speed — and we have to recommend adopting it anyway, watering it down, or rejecting it, with sign-off required from the VP of Research within about a week. Right now this is decided ad hoc, and the argument always collapses into someone invoking the competitiveness constraint — that safety work only gets adopted if it's roughly free, or at least cheap enough not to lose the race — as though that settled which interventions pass. I want that slogan turned into an actual procedure.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run on any proposal in under an hour. It should be grounded in the real conceptual content of the alignment tax idea, and it should preserve the distinctions that matter rather than flattening them: the difference between the constraint as an empirical prediction about what will be adopted in a competitive market and as a normative guideline for research prioritization; the difference between costs borne by the deploying actor and costs borne by users or the world at large; the difference between a capability tax, a resource tax (latency, price, compute), and a development-speed tax; and the fact that "competitive" is only defined relative to a counterfactual competitor who may not exist or may be running the same intervention anyway. The procedure should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a clear recommendation, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run it on three proposals of the kind we actually see:

1. A refusal-training revision that cuts the rate at which our model assists with clearly harmful requests by roughly half, but measurably raises over-refusals on benign requests and drops scores on a standard benchmark suite by about 2%.
2. An interpretability-based screening pass before each release that doesn't touch model weights at all but adds a three-week delay to every release cycle and consumes a senior researcher's time.
3. A monitoring technique that is completely free — no capability cost, no latency — but only works reliably below a certain capability threshold, and we are already training models near that threshold.

For each, give the procedure's verdict and the reasoning behind it. At least one verdict should be one my team will hate — we are, for instance, quite attached to proposal 2 — and you should explain why the procedure nevertheless yields it. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire: which simplifications are most likely to produce a wrong recommendation (treating capability as a single scalar, assuming the benchmark drop is proportional to the real-world capability drop, ignoring the second-order effects whereby making a model safer can make it more useful and vice versa), and what early-warning signs would tell us a misfire has already occurred.
