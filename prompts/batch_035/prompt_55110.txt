I chair the tenure committee in a mid-size department, five voting members, I only vote to break ties (never happened), and our bylaws have just stopped being helpful. They say tenure is granted when the committee finds the record adequate in research, teaching, and service — all three — but they're silent on whether "finds" means we vote on each of those separately or on the tenure question as a whole. We've never had to care, because it's never mattered. Now it does.

Our precedents (voters are A–E, premise votes then the final straw poll):

- Dr. Reyes: research 5-0, teaching 4-1, service 4-1; final 4-1 for. Granted.
- Dr. Silva: research 3-2, teaching 3-2, service 3-2, the same three yes's each time; final 3-2 for. Granted.
- Dr. Tam: research 5-0, teaching 5-0, service 2-3; final 2-3 against. Denied.

Monday's candidate, Dr. Kwon: research 3-2 for (A, B, C), teaching 3-2 for (B, C, D), service 3-2 for (B, C, E) — but on the final question only B and C are for, 2-3 against. Nobody's voting strategically, everyone accepts the all-three rule for their own vote, and everyone's being honest about the record.

So what do we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I can see the argument that every premise passed, exactly like Silva's, so Kwon is entitled to what Silva got — and B and C are already saying the two records look identical on paper. I can also see that we've always decided by the final question and there's no precedent for ignoring one. What rule(s) are we actually running here, do our precedents genuinely settle it or just happen to agree, and what case that we've never had would have settled it for us? And if you tell me to grant, give me something to say to A, D and E, who will point out that a majority of the committee thinks she shouldn't be tenured — right now all I've got is that they should've voted no on teaching or service if they wanted that to count.
