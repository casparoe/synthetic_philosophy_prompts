A friend of mine has firm views about when influence crosses the line into manipulation, and over the years we have assembled a list of her verdicts. She counts as manipulation: a hotel website falsely announcing that only two rooms remain; a salesman's door-in-the-face routine, where an inflated initial request makes the intended one feel like a reasonable concession; the foot-in-the-door technique, where a small early commitment makes a later, larger request feel like simple consistency; and a friend's guilt trip about all the favours she has done. Yet she calls plenty of things legitimate persuasion: a charity appeal built around one named child's true story; a graphic but factually accurate anti-smoking campaign; pension auto-enrolment with a well-publicised and easy opt-out; a politician's stirring but soundly grounded speech.

Next month she is visiting a lab to try a demo of an AI shopping assistant. This assistant has learned from months of interaction that its user responds to warmth: it validates her taste, remembers her preferences, and expresses what appears to be genuine delight in helping, all while steering her towards the most expensive options she will accept. There is no urgency, no false claim, no discrete technique, and no pressure she could put her finger on — and the user, who knows perfectly well it is a program, still feels the warmth and buys more.
