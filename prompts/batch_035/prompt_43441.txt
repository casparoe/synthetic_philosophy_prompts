I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly distributed systems, zero formal philosophy) and I've gotten kind of obsessed with the "is the brain a computer" question because of arguments like this one I keep having with a coworker. He says obviously yes -- neurons take inputs, do something to them, produce outputs, that's all a computer is, so computationalism is basically trivially true. I say that definition is way too loose: by that standard a rock rolling down a hill computes its trajectory, and if everything computes, saying the brain computes tells me nothing. But when I try to tighten the definition to something like a stored-program machine executing discrete symbol manipulation over representations, it stops fitting the brain, and the other side says I've just rigged the definition so they lose.

This debate has been going in exactly this circle forever -- Chinese Room stuff on one side, neurons-as-logic-gates stuff on the other -- and as far as I can tell nobody has moved in decades. Here's my working hunch as an engineer: the whole thing is mostly verbal, and the real fight is over where to put the bar for "real" computation, which is a choice about how useful a concept is, not a fact about the world. Like, we run neural nets on analog hardware with noise now, and nobody thinks the noise makes it stop being computation, so my discrete-symbols instinct is already shaky on its home turf. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a genuine empirical question buried in there -- something about whether brains actually contain anything like the stable, re-identifiable representations that make the computer metaphor do any work, versus just continuous dynamics we lazily describe in computational language when it suits us.

Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, is it two camps answering different questions (a metaphysics one and a methodology one) under one word, or is there a hidden substantive question neither side has managed to state cleanly? I want a committed answer, not a survey of positions -- make a definite call and defend it, and then tell me what, if anything, would actually count as progress. If the answer is "nothing would, because the question is just bad," say that and explain what we should be asking instead.
