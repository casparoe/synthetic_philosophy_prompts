I've been reading my way through classical pragmatism and have arrived at a set of positions on truth that I'd like checked for consistency. Here they are: (1) truth is the opinion that inquiry, pursued long enough and by a community of investigators large and varied enough, would eventually settle on and never afterwards revise — this is my understanding of Peirce; (2) a belief's being true consists, at least in part, in its working — in successfully guiding action and being confirmed in experience — so that a belief which could never make any practical difference to anyone has nothing that makes it true or false; this is my understanding of James; (3) it was true, hundreds of millions of years before any inquirers existed, that there was no life on Earth; (4) present consensus is no guarantee of truth: a belief held unanimously today can still be false; (5) some questions inquiry will never settle nonetheless have determinate answers — I take it there is a fact about what a particular untranslated script from a dead language meant, even if no one ever deciphers it.

Which of these stand in genuine tension, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest consistent revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and for each genuine one say what would have to give and what that costs. If you think the set is consistent as it stands, say so; and if you think one of the claims is not really a claim about truth at all, and so cannot conflict with the others, say that directly rather than reconciling things that shouldn't be reconciled. Finally, if you think I've misdescribed Peirce or James anywhere, flag it rather than working silently with my gloss.
