I'm taking a graduate seminar in philosophy of science this term, and we've just finished three weeks on thought experiments — Galileo's falling bodies, Stevin's chain of spheres on the inclines, Einstein's elevator, Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat. The seminar has essentially polarized into two camps that keep talking past each other: the empiricists, who follow John Norton in holding that a thought experiment is nothing over and above an argument, with every premise ultimately sourced from mundane experience, and the Platonists, who follow James Robert Brown in holding that at least some thought experiments — paradigmatically Galileo's argument that heavy and light bodies fall at the same speed — involve something like direct a priori perception of a law of nature. My final project is to simulate an adversarial collaboration between one committed member of each camp, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement they should produce.

Concretely, I want four sections. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what thought experiments demonstrably accomplish in the history of science (for instance, that Galileo's tied-stones argument undermined the Aristotelian proportionality of fall speed to weight, that Einstein's elevator did real work in motivating what became the equivalence principle, that the demon and its later analyses exposed the statistical character of the second law), phrased so both sides can sign without flinching. Second, a disagreements table with the precise formulations of where they part ways — each one worded so that its own side would accept it, e.g., something like an empiricist formulation that the falling-bodies case is a reductio whose premises Galileo could have learned from ordinary handling of weights, alongside a Platonist formulation that the argument's force comes from seeing the law in the imagined scenario rather than from the premises alone. Third, the cruxes: candidates that came up in seminar include whether any thought experiment contains an epistemic ingredient beyond deduction from previously accumulated experience; whether the felt 'aha, I see that it must fall together' is explicable as recognition of familiar regularities or needs a stronger explanation; and whether there exists even one historical case of a thought experiment overturning well-confirmed empirical belief without eventual new empirical input. For each crux, give a test, argument, or historical case both parties would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Fourth, a short closing note identifying any part of the residual dispute you think is merely verbal — for instance, whether the two camps are using 'a priori' or 'experience' in incompatible ways — with a sentence of justification.

Please keep the tone of a document two real collaborators could put their names to: neither vacuous (don't hide behind 'thought experiments are interesting and context matters') nor slanted toward either side, and don't resolve the dispute in the wording of the agreed claims. If you think a candidate crux is actually a distraction, say so in a brief editorial aside rather than silently dropping it.
