I'm preparing a dissertation prospectus in philosophy of physics, and my advisor's only comment on my draft chapter was that it "re-litigates Newton versus Leibniz, which nobody needs." I believe the chapter does something else, but I haven't managed to say what in a paragraph, and the prospectus is due in six weeks. I'd like you to turn my half-formed idea into a proper chapter proposal.

The idea, such as it is: Newton's bucket is standardly presented as an experiment that decides between substantivalism (space is a thing in its own right) and relationism (there are only distances and configurations of bodies). The water climbs the sides when it rotates, and since the concave surface cannot be explained by the water's motion relative to the bucket itself, the story goes, it must be explained by motion relative to absolute space. But this can't be right as a decision procedure, because a Machian relationalist can accept everything Newton observed: the water is rotating relative to the total distribution of matter, and that is a fact expressible entirely in relations. The performable experiment seems to underdetermine the ontology, and I suspect the real work the bucket does is much narrower than either side allows: it shows only that rotation is not reducible to change of distance and orientation between the water and its immediate surroundings — something even a relationalist could concede without conceding that space is a substance.

There is a second piece I keep circling. Newton himself ran a variant of the experiment — two globes joined by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty universe, with the tension in the cord revealing their rotation. The bucket is the wrong experiment for the Machian, since a Machian needs distant masses for the rotation to be relative to; the globes are the right experiment, and it is unperformable. So the dialectic has a strange structure: the experiment we can run doesn't settle the dispute, and the experiment that would settle it cannot be run. A chapter that made this structure precise — rather than taking a side — might be a real contribution. What I need to know is whether that is a chapter or a whole dissertation.

Please write the proposal for the chapter. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the usual framing, substance versus relations, leaves that question open; say what I would actually do, month by month over one semester; lay out the possible conclusions and what considerations would decide among them; specify what would count as the chapter failing; and give an honest assessment of whether the project collapses into a survey of familiar positions — in which case my advisor would be vindicated and I would want to know that now. Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will fill those in myself.
