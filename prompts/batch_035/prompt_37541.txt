My lab has been arguing about the unilateralist's curse for weeks and I think one of us has accidentally derived that whistleblowers are almost always in the wrong. Setup for anyone who needs it: there's a paper by Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg (2016, Social Epistemology) about a principle of conformity. A piece of research is net-harmful to publish. N equally competent researchers each make their own noisy, unbiased estimate of whether publishing is a good idea, and the paper gets out if even one of them says yes — that's the unilateralist structure, any single yes is a yes. If each researcher has a 10% chance of erring on the optimistic side, then with 20 researchers the harmful result gets published with probability 1 - 0.9^20, roughly 88%. The action happens way more often than any individual's error rate, and it gets worse the more people are in the pool. Their remedy is a principle of conformity: when an action is unilateralist in this way and the others in your reference class have each declined it, you should decline too, unless you have reason to think your judgment is better than the group average.

Here's the argument my labmate built on top of that, which we can't agree on how to break:

(1) Principle of conformity (PC): if an action is unilateralist-structured and your epistemic peers have declined it, don't act unless you have reason to believe your judgment is better than the group average.

(2) "Reason to believe your judgment is better" cannot include how confident you feel. The whole engine of the curse is that individual confidence doesn't track the group-level error rate — the researcher who errs optimistically feels exactly as right as the one who judges correctly. If felt certainty counted as a qualification, the curse argument would never get started, since every overoptimist would exempt themselves. So the exemption has to be externally checkable: superior track record, genuinely private information, demonstrably better training.

(3) Now take the cases we actually use to teach moral courage: a whistleblower who is one of forty employees who all saw the same documents and all stayed quiet; a scientist whose warning got dismissed by equally qualified colleagues; the first member of an unjust institution to resign publicly. In these cases the dissenter typically has nothing that satisfies (2) — average credentials, shared information, no special track record. Their evidence that they're right is just that they've thought it over and it seems obvious, which is precisely what (2) rules out.

(4) The peers' silence in those cases is exactly the "declined to act" that triggers PC. Model the other thirty-nine employees as making noisy unbiased estimates of whether whistleblowing is net-good, and PC applies with full force: don't blow the whistle.

(5) So PC condemns every lone dissenter without external marks of superiority — which is nearly every dissenter we celebrate.

(6) Therefore either our moral exemplars were behaving irrationally (and we should tell students the whistleblower did wrong unless she happened to have elite credentials), or something in the derivation of PC doesn't survive contact with the whistleblower case.

She takes (6) to mean the curse argument proves too much and there's a hidden asymmetry assumption in there somewhere, but she can't name it and honestly neither can I. For what it's worth, I want to keep PC in some form — for decisions like publishing dangerous results or deploying AI systems it feels exactly right, and the math is genuinely scary — but I also really don't want to bite the whistleblower bullet.
