I teach a graduate seminar on social ontology, and this semester a student derailed my tidy unit on social kinds with a question I could not answer cleanly: do generational labels — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — pick out real kinds, or are they just marketing shorthand that we have mistaken for a taxonomy? I have since concluded that I don't have a settled view, and I'd like yours in a specific form. For the purposes of the question, take "real kind" in roughly Richard Boyd's homeostatic property cluster sense: a grouping whose members reliably share a cluster of properties because some underlying mechanism or set of mechanisms maintains the clustering, so that the grouping supports induction and explanation better than an arbitrary partition would. Contrast this with a merely nominal cohort partition, where nothing but the arbitrary slicing of a continuous demographic variable — year of birth — holds the group together. So, precisely: what is your credence that "Millennials" (say, those born 1981 to 1996, per one widely used polling definition) names a real kind in that sense rather than a nominal one?

I want a number with a decomposition, not a hedge. At minimum I'd expect terms for the following: (1) that there are genuine cohort effects at all, given the age-period-cohort identification problem — whether shared formative experiences can leave detectable population-level marks that are not just age effects or period effects in disguise; (2) that some homeostatic mechanism, such as a shared media environment, common conditions of economic entry, or intergenerational cultural transmission, could actually maintain a property cluster across a span as wide as two decades; (3) that between-cohort differences are large enough relative to within-cohort variance for the grouping to carry inductive weight — it has been claimed to me that year of birth predicts less about a person's attitudes than income or education does, and I suspect that is right; (4) that vagueness of boundaries is compatible with kindhood, as it appears to be for species; and (5) whether, following Ian Hacking, you think social kinds must be interactive — labels that alter the people labeled — for the clustering to count as real rather than imposed, and whether generations plausibly pass that test. Assign each sub-question a rough probability with one line of justification, show how the terms combine into your final number, and identify which term that number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: my own credence sits around 0.3, while the colleague with whom I team-teach the seminar insists on 0.7, on the grounds that generations are at least as real as psychiatric diagnoses or monetary units, which nobody in our field dismisses as unreal. If you can, tell me which of the two of us your decomposition suggests is making the error: am I demanding a standard of kindhood that no social kind could meet, or is she mistaking mere causal relevance (birth year does matter for some things) for kindhood? Finally, tell me what would move your number most — a better theory of social kinds, some kind of longitudinal evidence, or something about the mechanisms themselves — and in which direction it would move it.
