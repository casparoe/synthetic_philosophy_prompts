quick context: I'm a third-year taking a course on social choice and democratic theory, and we just hit futarchy — Robin Hanson's thing where you vote on values but bet on beliefs. My TA runs a weekly applied section of about 18 students and lets one of us run the session each week; mine's in two weeks and I want to run a live prediction market on the section itself. Design the whole thing for me.

Constraints: 90 minutes, ~18 students, seminar room with movable desks, no real money allowed — and extra credit is off the table too, dept rules, so it has to be play money with candy/prizes/bragging rights as the payoff. I can print stuff beforehand.

What I want the game to do, roughly in this order:

1. A round where markets on things the students themselves will do — attendance at the optional session, average on next week's quiz, which movie we'll vote to watch at end of term — visibly aggregate private information. I want some students holding info cards the others don't have, so when the price moves it's actually information flowing and not just vibes.
2. The values/beliefs split made concrete: the class votes on something (values) while a separate market runs on what the vote will decide (beliefs), so they can literally see the two channels Hanson separates. And I want at least one moment where holding shares in an outcome changes how people campaign or vote — the self-fulfilling prophecy thing — so we can talk about endogeneity in policy markets.
3. A manipulation round: give one student a war chest and a mission to push some price somewhere for reasons unrelated to belief. I've read that Hanson thinks manipulators mostly end up subsidizing honest traders, and I want the class to discover for themselves whether soaking up the manipulation subsidy is as easy as it sounds in a market with 18 participants.

Then the debrief: map each moment onto the actual futarchy machinery — which part was voting on values, which was betting on beliefs, where the market worked as an epistemic instrument vs. where it was just gambling — and be honest about the failure modes I should expect: thin markets, everyone herding on the first quoted price, a couple of sharks doing all the trading while the rest free-ride. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to patch it — do I need to act as a market maker with a bid/ask spread, or run call auctions a few times per round, or force minimum trading activity?

And finally the honest note: what can a 90-minute market with 18 participants genuinely not show about futarchy — I'm guessing scale, liquidity, long horizons, people actually specializing as forecasters — and what I'd have to just assert in the debrief rather than demonstrate. I don't want the game to leave people thinking futarchy is more robust than it is just because the toy version worked, if that makes sense.

also if you have ideas for handling the student who refuses to trade at all or treats the whole thing as a joke, that'd help. theres always one.
