My cousin, who took a political philosophy course years ago and has been holding forth on it at family dinners ever since, gave me his summary of civil disobedience over the holidays. Since I don't know the literature well enough to push back, I'd like your help auditing it. He said, roughly: "Civil disobedience is just the philosophers' term for breaking the law on principle — anything from blocking a road to leaking classified documents counts as long as your conscience is really in it. Rawls basically invented the concept: he defined it as public, nonviolent, conscientious lawbreaking aimed at getting an unjust law changed, and only after you've exhausted the normal legal channels. He also insisted on accepting your punishment — submitting to the legal consequences is what proves you still respect the system as a whole, which is what separates civil disobedience from plain criminality. That's also why it has to be the unjust law itself you break; if you break a law you think is perfectly fine, you're just an ordinary criminal. But real protesters never meet his conditions: whole movements weren't trying to change this or that law but the entire political order, and plenty of them weren't interested in going meekly to jail either. So Rawls's definition ends up being less of a definition and more of a machine for approving the protests he happened to like, and the concept should just be dropped."

I have a feeling several things are subtly off here — I half remember that Rawls's account was tailored to a particular kind of society rather than offered as a universal definition, and that the "accepting your punishment" condition is more contested than my cousin lets on — but I can't say exactly where he goes wrong, and I'll admit his point about real movements not fitting the mold struck me as pointing at something genuine. Could you go through the summary claim by claim? Tell me what a Rawlsian would accept as a fair gloss, what is a subtle distortion (for instance, where he's conflated civil disobedience with things Rawls deliberately kept separate, or overstated a modest thesis), and whether his conclusion — that the concept is nothing but a vehicle for approving favored protests — actually follows from the parts he got right. If some of his worries survive the corrections, I'd like to know that too.
