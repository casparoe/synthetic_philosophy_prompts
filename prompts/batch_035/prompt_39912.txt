Quick bit of background: I'm a novelist (four books out, the kind that get nice reviews and modest sales), and this fall a friend is letting me guest-run one session of her undergraduate philosophy seminar — 18 students, 75 minutes, and my own philosophy training is basically just a lot of anxious reading. The reason she asked me at all is a pattern I keep hitting in reader feedback on my own work, which apparantly has a name in your world. In one draft, a factory-owner character of mine expands operations knowing it will wreck a river valley; he doesn't care either way, and my readers uniformly wrote that he knew what he was doing, intended it even. In a later version, same character, same knowledge, same indifference, but the side effect happens to help the valley instead (tourism money, whatever), and suddenly the same readers call it incidental — accidental, not really his doing. Same head, same information, opposite verdict, and the verdict flips with whether the outcome is bad or good. My friend says this is basically the Knobe effect and there's a whole fight about what it shows: something about the concept of intentionality itself, or about how we process blame, or just an artifact of how these questions get asked.

So design the 75 minutes for me. I want the students to run into the asymmetry themselves before anyone names it — no lecture up front, and please don't have me present my own book as the example, I'd rather stay out of it. Use fresh vignettes, a couple in non-corporate settings so it doesn't feel like the one famous case in a disguise (I'm told students have seen that one). Rules, materials, and some mechanism where each student commits to a judgment in writing before seeing anyone else's — people reverse themselves once the room gets a vibe, I've watched it happen in workshops. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the live explanations of the effect rather than onto my script. And if you can work in a second layer, I'd love it: somewhere near the end I want the students judging not just whether a character intended a side effect but whether the author did, since readers do this to me constantly — they decide I meant something deliberately purely because it landed badly, and I've never known whether to be flattered. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I've taught enough writing workshops to know something always breaks), and be honest about which part of the underlying debate no classroom game can actually reproduce, so I know what I'll just have to assert in the debrief.
