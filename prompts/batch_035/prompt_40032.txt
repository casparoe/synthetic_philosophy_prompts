In a graduate workshop on scholarly writing that our department runs for PhD students, one of the faculty facilitators has taken to invoking Orwell's line from Politics and the English Language -- roughly, that insincerity is the great enemy of clear language -- and using it to do fairly aggressive work. The setup, as I recall the passage, is that a writer whose real aims diverge from his declared ones will instinctively reach for long words and worn-out idioms the way a cuttlefish releases ink. Our facilitator used it twice last term: once, plausibly, against political euphemism (a government department calling an evacuation a "managed withdrawal" while promising it wasn't one); and once, much more contentiously, against a chapter draft by a student working on post-structuralist feminist theory, where he claimed the prose's opacity was itself evidence that the author did not believe what she was arguing. Several of us thought the second use was a stretch, but none of us could say precisely why, and the facilitator's response was essentially that we were defending jargon because we were invested in it.

I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I want to know what the slogan actually commits me to before I use it or argue against it again. As I see it, the sentence could be making several distinct claims that differ enormously in strength, and I'd like them laid out as a menu I can bring to the next session. There is what looks like a causal-psychological claim: that a gap between one's real and one's declared aims tends to produce verbal fog, via some mechanism like motivated evasion. There is a much stronger evidential claim: that obscurity is evidence of insincerity, which is the reading my facilitator needed for the second use, since he was inferring bad faith from the prose alone. There is a near-converse claim about the direction of fit, since Orwell also suggests that the corruption runs the other way too, from language back into thought, which would complicate the inference from bad prose to bad faith. There is possibly a sufficiency claim, that sincerity plus ordinary effort yields clarity, which strikes me as obviously false -- people are sincerely and hardheadedly muddled all the time, and some things are just difficult to say -- and maybe a claim restricted to political language specifically, which would explain why the managed-withdrawal example feels apt while the transfer to academic theory does not. For each reading, I'd like a verdict: trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but wrong, or too vague to evaluate. And then the practical question: which readings, if any, actually license what my facilitator did -- treating an individual scholar's difficult prose as a symptom of insincerity rather than of, say, suboptimal writing habits, imported technical vocabulary, or genuine difficulty in the material?

One caveat and one request. The caveat: I'm quoting from memory and my copy of the essay is in storage, so don't hold me to Orwell's exact wording; if the precise sentence matters for separating the readings, flag that. The request: if you think the question rests on a confused premise -- say, that the slogan was never meant to survive outside its original target of political propaganda, or that disambiguating one-liners like this is the wrong way to read an essayist -- then tell me that plainly rather than manufacturing readings to fill out a list. I'd rather leave the workshop knowing the slogan should be retired from that use altogether than arrive with a ten-item menu that sounds thorough but doesn't hold up.
