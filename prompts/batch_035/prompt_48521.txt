hey, i'm a phd student in political theory and i volunteer with this group that runs civic-ed workshops for first-year undergrads (mostly not phil majors). every year we do a session on basically "should everyone's vote count the same" and it always devolves into people just yelling their priors. so this year i want to hand out a sorting quiz, like "which theory of political authority do you *actually* hold?", buzzfeed format but serious under the hood.

the positions i want as outcomes: estlund-style epistemic proceduralism (democratic procedures have real epistemic value but the authority comes from qualified acceptability / fairness, not raw accuracy), brennan-style epistocracy of the knowledgeable, rights-based pure proceduralism in the waldron neighborhood, and lottocracy/demarchy (the burnheim / van reybrouck lineage, where selection by lot rather than elections is the fix). if some answer pattern really maps to something else — mill's plural voting, or an unnamed combo — fine, name it or say it's unnamed.

the part i need actual help with is the diagnostic questions. i don't want anything that gives the game away. no "do you think ignorant voters should be allowed to vote" — everyone either says no or feels gross saying yes, and it sorts by label instead of by belief. i want concrete cases where the four views genuinely come apart. some i've been drafting, feel free to improve or replace:

- a referendum where 60% of voters believe a straightforwardly false factual claim about the policy's effects (every study says it does X, they're sure it does Y), and it fails because of that. is anything wrong with that outcome *beyond* the disappointed preferences of the losing side?
- imagine a political-knowledge test that is fair and valid, but it happens to screen out a much higher share of one ethnic group, not by design, just correlation. does your answer change if the correlation is small vs large?
- an oracle tells you the full electorate picks the "right" answer 51% of the time and a council of the well-informed picks it 70% of the time. does that gap justify the council? does the same argument justify a benevolent dictator at 90%? (this is supposed to be the estlund stopper, i want it in the quiz somewhere, probably not phrased as an oracle though, up to you)
- a citizens' assembly chosen by lot gets a policy right that the elected parliament botched. what, if anything, does that single data point tell you about which institution *should* have decided?

requirements: 8-10 questions total, each one a case with 2-3 plain-language answer options (no jargon — audience is 19 year olds), then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the four positions plus whatever stragglers. the questions themselves have to be answerable by someone who's never heard of any of the names; the key can name names since they get a handout afterwards.

two specific things i want flagged rather than papered over: (1) if two of the views answer every one of my cases identically — i suspect waldron and the estlundians might, unless one of you can find a case that splits them — say so and either merge them or add a question that actually seperates them, if such a question exists; (2) note any answer pattern that corresponds to a real but basically unoccupied position, and say whether you think it's unoccupied because it's incoherent or just because nobody's bothered defending it lately. i'd rather the quiz honestly say "your views are weird and historically homeless" than shove everyone into a bucket.
