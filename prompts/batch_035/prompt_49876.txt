My seminar has been arguing about the two-envelope paradox, and a friend now claims to have built a version in which the standard escape is unavailable. His game: two sealed envelopes, one containing twice the other; the pair {2^n, 2^{n+1}} dollars is chosen with probability 2^n/3^{n+1}, for n = 0, 1, 2, ...; a fair coin then determines which envelope holds the smaller amount; I receive one envelope at random, knowing all of this. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Suppose I open my envelope and see 2^n with n ≥ 1. That value is consistent with the pair {2^n, 2^{n+1}} (probability 2^n/3^{n+1}) or with the pair {2^{n-1}, 2^n} (probability 2^{n-1}/3^n), so the odds that my envelope holds the smaller amount are 2:3, a probability of 2/5.
(2) The expected gain from switching is therefore (2/5)(2^n) − (3/5)(2^{n-1}) = 2^{n-1}/5 > 0; and if I see 1, switching certainly gains a dollar.
(3) So conditional on every observation I could make, switching has positive expected gain.
(4) If an action has positive conditional expected gain given every possible observation, then it has positive expected gain simpliciter.
(5) Therefore I should switch even before opening; yet the identical computation, run from the perspective of the other envelope, shows that switching back also has positive expected gain, so each envelope is strictly better than the other — which the symmetry of the setup forbids.
(6) He concludes that expected-utility reasoning undermines itself, and that the usual dismissal of the two-envelope argument as a blunder about conditioning was too quick: here the conditional expectations are exactly right, and switch-dominance follows anyway.

He is even willing to charge a fee per switch, insisting that whatever fee he names, steps (2) and (4) tell me to keep switching — even though two consecutive switches return me to my original envelope minus two fees. I have checked the arithmetic and cannot fault it; the absurdity surfaces at (5), but (5) looks like a straightforward consequence of the symmetry, so I assume the failure is earlier and I cannot locate it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
