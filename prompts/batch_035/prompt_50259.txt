I am a novelist planning a story in which the protagonist is not an individual ant but the colony itself, in the spirit of the Aunt Hillary material in Hofstadter's "Prelude... on Ant Fugue" from Gödel, Escher, Bach: the ants are treated as dumb neurons, and the colony as the thinking agent whose ideas outlive the particular workers that carry them. Draft the story in exactly five scenes. In the first, introduce the colony as a character with habits, moods, and something like long-term memory, even though no individual ant lives more than a season or two. In the second, have a couple of scout ants return with conflicting reports about a new nest site, and let the colony-level "decision" emerge slowly from pheromone dynamics rather than from any ant's understanding. In the third, show a myrmecologist character arguing with a philosopher friend about whether anything is literally experiencing these events, or whether our protagonist is only a metaphor the reader is invited to indulge. In the fourth, give the colony something like a setback — a flood, a rival colony, a parasite that turns workers into zombies — that tests whether its "personality" survives when large parts of it are destroyed and rebuilt. In the final scene, resolve the question deliberately ambiguously: leave the reader unable to say whether the colony is a mind that suffers and plans, or a process that merely looks narrative-shaped from outside. Each scene should be at most two paragraphs, and the whole story should not end with an explicit answer to the title question.
