I've spent years in the rationalist blogosphere — the LessWrong decision-theory sequences, Slate Star Codex at its peak, the adjacent forums where people argue about exploration budgets and value of information the way others argue about sports. That culture trained me to treat inherited claims as low-prior hypotheses that must pay rent in anticipated experience before I act on them, and to be suspicious of any rule that can't explain itself. The single licensed exception is Chesterton's fence: don't remove a constraint until you understand why it was put there. It recently dawned on me that this exception is doing a enormous amount of quiet work — that the way my community deploys it is essentially a rationalist skin stretched over Burkean conservatism, and that I've never actually examined what's underneath the skin. So here is my request.

Restate the Burkean case for inherited institutions — prescription, the accumulated wisdom embedded in practices nobody can fully articulate, skepticism of sweeping rational redesign — in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. That vocabulary includes: priors over institutions as compressed evidence from many agents across many generations; value of information and the decision-theoretic case for acquiring it before acting; exploration budgets and the tradeoff between exploiting known-good policies and testing alternatives; option value and the asymmetry between reversible and irreversible moves; model misspecification, i.e., acting under the assumption that your world-model is wrong in ways you can't enumerate; and the engineer's heuristic of not deleting configuration lines whose purpose you can't reconstruct. Translate the position into those terms and only those terms.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which Burkean claims survive as clean, precise statements in my vocabulary? Which survive only with distortion — and what exactly is distorted? And which have no expression at all? For that last category I want your verdict on each item: is the residue telling me my vocabulary is missing something the conservatives could see, or is it telling me that the Burkean claim was less clear than it looked and only sounded deep?

I'll be upfront about my prior. My instinct is that the whole conservative epistemology collapses into a modest decision-theoretic point — be humble about your world-model before you take irreversible actions, because institutions encode evidence you haven't personally observed — which is just expected utility maximization with a warning label attached, and which I can accept without adopting anything distinctively conservative. If that collapse is illegitimate, tell me exactly where the translation failed and what got lost. In particular, I suspect the part I've never been able to make sense of is the claim that deference to tradition is somehow constitutive of good reasoning rather than merely instrumentally justified by it — that there is something about the stance of reverence toward inherited practice that matters even in cases where the instrumental argument runs out. If you think that's a real residue my framework cannot state, say so plainly and explain what my vocabulary would need to add to say it. If you think it's mood-affiliation dressed up as epistemology, say that plainly too. I'm not looking for a both-sides summary; I want to know whether my instinct that conservatism is decision theory plus caution is right, or whether it's a translation error.
