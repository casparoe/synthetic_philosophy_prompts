I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper, and an increasing share of my beat consists of expert advisory bodies: vaccine safety committees, dietary guideline panels, drug advisory boards, and the occasional multinational consortium issuing a consensus statement. The recurring problem is how to characterize, in a two-hundred-word brief filed on deadline, what a panel actually decided — because what a panel collectively concludes is often not what a majority of its members individually believe. The judgment-aggregation literature has a name for the cleanest version of this, the discursive dilemma, along with a family of distinctions I only half understand: premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, agenda-sensitivity, oligarchic rules, the possibility that a group's official position is one no member holds. What I need is a procedure a working reporter can actually run.

Please write that procedure: an ordered sequence of steps for deciding, given a committee's vote record or published annex, whether to lead with the collective conclusion as issued, whether to report the premise-level votes instead, when the word "split" is honest and when it misleads, and when the aggregation method itself is the story. For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when the record is incomplete or the aggregation rule is undisclosed. The procedure must run in the ninety minutes between a press release landing and my deadline; it must be usable by a reporter who can read a technical annex but has never studied formal logic; and it must mark the points at which it stops and hands the question to a human — for instance, whether a committee's chosen aggregation rule is normatively legitimate is not something a reporter can adjudicate on deadline, but whether the rule was disclosed is a reportable fact.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a nine-member vaccine advisory committee where the proposition that an adverse event is causally linked to a vaccine passes five to four, the proposition that the link is serious enough to outweigh the benefit in a given subgroup passes five to four on the strength of a different majority — one member in the overlap — so that the recommended contraindication fails eight to one, and the committee announces it found no basis for restricting use. Second: a dietary panel that is unanimous, nine to zero, on each factual premise, but splits five to four on the normative conclusion about whether the risk warrants new guidance. Third: a panel whose votes are weighted and whose chair holds a tie-break, so that the aggregation rule can be only partly reverse-engineered from the published annex. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like — for instance, that the honest brief states the committee's official conclusion flatly, without the premise-level splits that would make a better story — and close with a candid account of where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle.
