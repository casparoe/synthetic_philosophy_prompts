I'm a PhD student in epistemology gearing up to write my dissertation, and I've decided to focus my second chapter on the value problem for knowledge — the puzzle Plato raises in the Meno when Socrates asks why one should prefer knowledge of the road to Larissa to a merely true belief about it, given that a lucky guess will get you to Larissa just as reliably. My advisor keeps telling me that the literature (revived in earnest by Kvanvig's 2003 book, and sharpened since by what people call the swamping problem) tends to leap from "here's an intuition" straight to "here's my theory," without ever pausing to say what would actually count as an adequate solution. Before I commit to defending any particular view, I want to get the specification straight.

Please act as a senior epistemologist with a taste for decision-theoretic and value-theoretic precision, and think carefully before you write — I'd like you to brainstorm internally first and then commit to a list. Write me the desiderata: the full set of requirements that any adequate solution to the value problem must meet, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. To make sure we're not just rehashing the standard summary, here are the specific things I want the list to reckon with, and I'd like you to decide for each whether it belongs on the list as a genuine desideratum or is a prejudice of one camp:

(1) the standard trichotomy between the primary question (why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief?), the secondary question (why is it more valuable than any proper subset of its components — true belief plus justification, plus reliability?), and the tertiary question about whether knowledge's value exceeds the sum of its parts — and whether a solution must answer all three or only some; (2) the swamping worry, e.g. the old analogy of good coffee: if the coffee tastes good, we don't value it more for having come from a reliable machine, so if truth is what we care about, why does a reliable route to truth add value to a belief that is already true; (3) the fact that knowledge of necessary truths seems to have no nearby worlds where the belief goes wrong, so any solution that locates knowledge's extra value in avoiding error across modal space may have trouble explaining why mathematical knowledge is better than a lucky true mathematical guess; (4) cases like fake-barn county, where a true belief is safe or otherwise epistemically well-placed in some respects yet intuitively lacks the value we want — should the desiderata be sensitive to cases where the standard analyses of knowledge disagree about whether knowledge is even present; (5) the observation that some knowledge seems utterly worthless for any practical purpose (knowing the exact number of blades of grass on some lawn), which pressures any solution that ties knowledge's value to its role in action or assertion; and (6) whether the solution must make knowledge's value robust across all knowers and stakes, or can allow that knowledge is more valuable for agents in certain decision-theoretic situations only.

For each desideratum, tell me whether it's non-negotiable or a preference, and — this is the part I care most about — flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension if so. Then close with a brief scorecard: how process reliabilism, the virtue-theoretic credit view, knowledge-first approaches, and the route through knowledge's role as the norm of practical reasoning do against the list, and which single desideratum does the most work in knocking contenders out. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I only care about the reasoning, not the citations.
