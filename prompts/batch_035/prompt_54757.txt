Last night a friend of mine and I had a two-hour argument about negative utilitarianism that ended without either of us conceding anything, and I've been turning over her closing summary since. I want to know how much of it I should actually accept, but I don't know the literature well enough to tell the accurate parts from the rhetorical ones. Here is her summary, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

"Negative utilitarianism is the view that suffering is the only thing that carries moral weight — happiness and everything else we call good counts for exactly nothing. It goes back to Karl Popper, who argued that since we can relieve misery but can never guarantee anyone's happiness, the first obligation of ethics is to minimize suffering. Follow the view through and you reach the ugly parts: a negative utilitarian has to be indifferent about whether a happy life continues, since its happiness contributes nothing; and if you offered her a button that painlessly ended all life in an instant, she'd have to press it, because a world with no sufferers is strictly better by her lights. Nobody actually accepts that, and the view also forbids ever trading a small amount of suffering for a large amount of happiness, so between the two it's pretty much refuted. The only people still taking it seriously are the effective-altruist crowd, and they only do because suffering is easier to measure than happiness."

Please go through this claim by claim. For each one, tell me whether a careful negative utilitarian would accept it as stated, whether it's a defensible simplification of something more qualified, or whether it quietly substitutes a stronger thesis for a weaker one — in particular, I suspect she's running together the strict view that positive states have zero weight with the broader family of suffering-focused positions, and I don't know where Popper himself actually stood or how much of the priority claim she attributes to him was about what we can know rather than about what counts. Then assess her two refutations: does the painless-extinction argument show what she thinks it shows, and is "nobody actually accepts that" doing any argumentative work a philosopher would respect? Finally, tell me whether her last sentence, about why people in effective altruism focus on suffering, gets the motivation right. I came out of the argument thinking the view was crazy but her summary felt suspiciously tidy, so I'd like the audit to tell me which of those reactions to keep.
