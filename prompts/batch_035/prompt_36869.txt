I teach a seminar that touches on philosophy of economics, and something has been nagging at me all term. I want the implicit epistemology of a practice dug out, not a survey of the case law.

The practice: how securities law treats information. Trading on your own deep research is fine — celebrated, even. The analyst who counts cars in retailer parking lots from satellite images gets held up as a hero of efficient markets. But trade on the same fact about earnings because you learned it in a board meeting, and you're a felon — even where you never signed anything promising silence, and even though the market as a whole would have absorbed that information in minutes anyway. Now the background is strange too. By the Aumann agreement line of thought and the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result, fully rational agents with common priors wouldn't trade at all, so the very existence of liquid markets presupposes that traders disagree — private, unequally distributed information is the fuel, not a bug. So what does the insider trading doctrine actually presuppose about knowledge and its flow through prices?

I can see three candidate theories and can't tell which the practice needs. (a) A property view: the information belongs to the firm, like a trade secret, and the insider is fencing stolen goods. (b) A fairness view: everyone should come to the market with the same epistemic starting line, and insider trading is wrong because of the asymmetry itself. (c) A tainted-channel view: knowledge aquired through a fiduciary role is somehow dirty in origin, regardless of who ends up trading on it or how unequally it's distributed afterwards. And here's the wrinkle I keep tripping over: the actual cases tend to punish breach of duty, not the asymmetry, which points at (c), while all the surrounding rhetoric is about level playing fields, which is (b). Can these be made consistent, or does the doctrine secretly require one and fail without it? Then tell me: if we took the Hayekian picture fully seriously — prices as aggregators of exactly the kind of dispersed private information the law keeps quarantined — what would have to change? Would the line between heroic research and criminal trading need to be redrawn somewhere else entirely, and where? I'd also like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. which of these theories it merely fits comfortably with rather than presupposes, since I suspect that's most of the story and my students keep assuming its the reverse.
