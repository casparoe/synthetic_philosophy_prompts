I have a set of verdicts about the moral significance of brain size, and I would like help working out whether any single principle captures them, or whether I must give some of them up. My verdicts are these. An adult with microcephaly whose brain is far below average size but who lives a rich social and emotional life has exactly the same moral status as anyone else. A blue whale, despite possessing a brain several times the size of mine, does not thereby matter more than I do, so raw size cannot be the measure. Yet when I consider a mouse lemur, which has a higher brain-to-body ratio than a chimpanzee, I still think the chimpanzee matters considerably more, so the ratio cannot be the measure either. Neanderthals, whose brains were on average slightly larger than those of modern humans, clearly had moral status equal to ours. A crow, with a brain smaller than a walnut, nonetheless seems to matter more than a larger-brained but simpler animal, in line with its causal and social intelligence. A hypothetical person whose brain mass were doubled by some intervention, with no change whatsoever in their capacities, would gain no additional moral standing. Yet a hypothetical being whose brain were twice human size and who consequently had vastly greater understanding, memory, and self-awareness than we do, seems to me to matter more than we do. Finally, the case I find hardest: an anencephalic infant with almost no functioning cortex, and a healthy pig with a modest brain but evident sentience and sociality — my verdict that the infant matters more feels unavoidable, but it is hard to reconcile with everything above.

So my question is this. Is there a principled account — perhaps one on which brain size is only ever evidence of the capacities that ground moral status, rather than itself the ground — that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? If so, please state the principle precisely, say which verdict each clause is there to capture, and apply it to cases I have not listed, such as an elephant (large brain, few cortical neurons), a corvid-like alien with a tiny brain and human-level cognition, and an extraterrestrial whose cognition is distributed across several modest organs rather than one large brain. If no single principle accommodates everything, please say candidly which of my verdicts must be abandoned and why, and whether the survivors are better served by one unified principle or by a small set of them. I would also like to know whether the distinction between what grounds moral status and what merely indicates it leaves room for brain size to matter epistemically — for instance, in our judgements about long-extinct species whose behaviour we can no longer observe. Please write your answer in British English.
