My friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker — the case where you're stranded in the desert, a driver will take you to town only if she predicts you'll pay her once you're safe, and after you're rescued paying is no longer in your interest — and I've come away genuinely unsettled rather than convinced. My friend's position is that the case proves rationality is self-defeating: the perfectly rational hitchhiker dies in the desert, because the driver foresees he won't pay once paying no longer serves him, while the hitchhiker who is disposed to pay — who behaves irrationally from the standpoint of the moment after being rescued — gets to live. Her conclusion is that a rational agent should therefore deliberately cultivate an irrational disposition in advance, so that he can later collect the rewards that irrationality will bring. She adds that this can't be dismissed as a fringe case, since it suggests decision theory recommends dismantling itself whenever prediction of one's character is at stake. I can see that something is off in her reasoning, but I can't locate exactly where. Is deliberately making yourself irrational in this way a rational act, an irrational one, or is the question malformed — and if it's malformed, what is the framing getting wrong about what the case does and doesn't show? I'd like to understand both what the standard decision-theoretic responses would say about the hitchhiker's predicament and where my friend's chain of reasoning first goes astray.
