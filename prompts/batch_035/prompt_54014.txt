I'm a third-year student in a seminar on the law of atrocity, spending most of the term on Raphael Lemkin — how he built the concept out of earlier vocabulary like "denationalization" and "barbarism," how his original conception covered the destruction of a group's national pattern through means well beyond killing (displacing people, destroying institutions, suppressing language, settling the oppressor's population onto the land), and how the 1948 Convention then narrowed it: only national, ethnic, racial, and religious groups count, political groups were dropped under Soviet pressure, cultural destruction largely fell out, and the whole thing hinges on the special intent to destroy the group "as such." On the side, I volunteer as a research intern at a small NGO that writes monthly situation reports on ongoing mass atrocities. Our drafting has been ad hoc: one report calls a situation "genocide," the next calls the same situation "ethnic cleansing," and a reviewer once softened a draft to "widespread abuses" for reasons nobody recorded. My supervisor has asked me to build a rubric the drafting team can actually apply, so that the label follows from stated criteria rather than from whoever happens to be writing.

Please turn this into a usable decision procedure for our situation reports. It should pose its questions in a fixed order — roughly: which protected-group question, which acts question (the Convention's enumerated acts go beyond killing to include infliction of serious harm, conditions of life calculated to destroy, prevention of births, forcible transfer of children), and above all the specific-intent question — and for each it should say what counts as a sufficient answer, given that perpetrators rarely announce their intent and we often have to infer it from patterns (systematic exclusivity of targeting, language of dehumanization in official channels, refusal to allow survival options). It must be explicit about the distinction between the legal label and looser moral uses, and about the fallback hierarchy: when the evidence won't support "genocide," what do we write instead — crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, persecution — and what each fallback commits us to. It should also be honest about the politics baked into naming: governments avoid the word because of the duty to prevent it triggers and the diplomatic costs it imposes, and euphemisms get coined precisely to avoid it, so our rubric has to say why we are not simply mirroring that avoidance. Mark clearly the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgment call has to be recorded with its reasons.

Then run the procedure on three hypotheticals: first, a Khmer Rouge-style campaign in which a regime kills a vast proportion of a political-economic class — enormous death toll, but the victims are defined by class and political alignment rather than by any of the four protected grounds; second, a Myanmar-Rohingya-style case with mass killing, systematic destruction of villages, and prevention of births, where the pattern evidence is strong but there is no smoking-gun statement of intent; third, a case of mass forced displacement and the systematic dismantling of a group's language, religious institutions, and children's transfer into the oppressor's families, but with comparatively few killings — the situation where Lemkin's own broader concept and the Convention's definition pull apart most visibly. At least one of these should come out in a way I will find uncomfortable; please pick which one honestly. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do — the cases it will misfire on and the questions it cannot settle. Keep the whole thing under 750 words.
