I have no academic background in political philosophy, but I've spent a couple of years arguing about this on my own, mostly in online forums, and I've noticed something that bothers me more than the question itself: the argument about whether some form of rule by the better-informed would be better than universal democracy never goes anywhere. The pattern is always the same. Someone points out that most voters know shockingly little about the policies they vote on, that they vote based on loyalties and resentments rather than reasoning, and that we would never let an ignorant person perform surgery or fly a plane, so why should they help steer a country? The other side answers that political knowledge is not like surgical skill, that giving more political weight to the credentialed means handing permanent power to the people who write and grade the exams, that the whole point of everyone having a vote was to protect the people the powerful would otherwise ignore, and that in practice every regime that tested citizens for political competence ended up rigging the test against whoever the rulers disliked. And then it just stops. Both sides keep saying the same things, everyone seems satisfied that their side has obviously won, and no one, including me, has changed their mind even slightly in years.

Here's my attempt to describe the disagreement so you can diagnose it. As far as I can tell, both sides actually agree that the average voter is poorly informed and that this produces some bad outcomes. Both sides agree that an electorate of genuinely well-informed and public-spirited citizens would make better decisions than ours does. Both even agree that competence should matter somehow, since the democratic side also wants educated representatives, free information, deliberation before voting, and so on. So the fight isn't about whether knowledge matters. It seems to be about whether political power should be conditional on knowledge, where the democratic side says the equal vote is not a means to good decisions but a protection against domination, and the epistocratic side says that a protection which predictably produces terrible decisions is not much of a protection. That looks to me like two different questions being answered under one heading: one side is answering "which arrangement produces the best decisions?" and the other is answering "which arrangement can be justified to everyone who must live under it?" — and each side treats the other's question as a dodge.

But I'm not sure. It could also be a hidden empirical disagreement in disguise: if the ignorant vote essentially at random, then their votes cancel out and democracy's epistemic handicap is tiny, so the whole argument about voter incompetence would lose its force while remaining true — and if instead ignorance is systematically biased in some direction, the handicap is real and large. Each side seems to quietly assume whichever version supports its conclusion, and nobody I've argued with has ever been able to say which one is true. Or maybe it comes down to bedrock intuitions about equality that neither side can argue for, so that all the talk about competence and protection is just decoration.

So here is what I'm asking for. Don't adjudicate the dispute and don't survey the arguments; I've read the arguments. Diagnose the disagreement itself. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions under one name? Which parts of what I've described do the real work, and which are noise? Is there a hidden empirical question in there that could actually be settled, and if so, what would settling it change? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything — a distinction someone hasn't drawn yet, a piece of evidence, a better way of posing the question — could move people like the ones I argue with, or whether I should expect this particular argument to stay exactly where it is forever, and why.
