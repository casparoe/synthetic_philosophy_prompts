My ethics seminar has been stuck for a month on the "man of his time" defence — whether it works for Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus — and something about the stall seems diagnostic to me. Nobody disagrees about the facts: we all accept abolitionists existed in Jefferson's day (he himself wrote that he knew slavery was wrong), Las Casas condemned Columbus's atrocities from within his own lifetime, and Churchill's contemporaries criticized the Bengal famine response. And yet half the group thinks the defence is basically fine and half thinks its special pleading, and no argument budges anyone. One wrinkle: the two sides also seem to mean different things by "defence" — excusing the man himself, adjusting how much credit he deserves, or deciding what honoring him now endorses. My hypothesis is that underneath it all is a hidden disagreement about what blame is even for: one side treats it retrospectively (tracking what the agent could reasonably have known), the other treats it as forward-looking and expressive. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive-but-empirical, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress.
