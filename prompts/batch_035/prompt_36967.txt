I'm an intensivist on the ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital. Last month we had a donation-after-circulatory-death case in which the family asked me, point blank, whether their relative was dead when the recovery team began, and I found I could not give an answer I actually believed. I have been asked to draft the committee's position statement, and I've decided to argue something that will not be popular: that our institutional criteria for death quietly encode a policy choice rather than record a biological fact. My argument, in outline: the circulatory criterion rests on a no-touch interval that is set by how long we are willing to wait, not by evidence about when auto-resuscitation becomes impossible; the whole-brain criterion is in practice a bedside exam probing a handful of brainstem pathways rather than the whole brain; and the families who press us on these cases are, in a sense, right that something normative is being smuggled in — so the honest protocol would state what we are actually doing and why we think it justified, rather than insisting it is pure biology. My audience at the committee meeting: two transplant surgeons who see the dead-donor rule as the only firewall between transplantation and killing; the hospital counsel, who will resist anything that admits daylight between our protocol and the Uniform Determination of Death Act; a neurologist who defends the brain-death exam as clinically principled; and a family-liaison nurse who believes any candor about gray zones will shatter public trust in organ donation. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections each of them will actually raise, phrased in their own vocabulary and ordered roughly by how much damage they would do; what I should concede up front; a two-minute reply to each, with a note on where the reply is soft; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
