Next month I defend my colloquium paper on the Death in Damascus problem — the case, familiar from the causal decision theory literature, in which a fugitive encounters Death in Damascus, gathers that Death expects him the next day in Aleppo, and reasons that he should stay put in Damascus; yet wherever he decides to be, his very decision is evidence that Death has already anticipated it, so causal decision theory's recommendation flips back on itself in an infinite regress. My paper argues that the standard diagnoses miss the real lesson. The usual attempts to stabilize the case — randomizing between the cities, or adopting a ratificationist constraint under which an option must look acceptable once chosen — treat the trouble as a defect of the decision rule. I argue instead that the trouble lies in the agent's epistemic relation to her own future action: when the predictor is reliable enough, deliberation and action become evidentially inseparable, and the regress is simply what it looks like from the inside when an agent can no longer treat her own choice as an intervention while also being unable to stop treating it as evidence. On my view, coin-flipping helps only if the predictor cannot predict the coin, in which case the randomness is doing the causal work and not the decision theory; and ratificationism quietly helps itself to evidential reasoning that causal decision theory officially renounces. The constructive part of the paper is a distinction between two stances a deliberator can take toward her own prospective act — the interventional stance and the evidential stance — and the claim that Death in Damascus is precisely the scenario in which neither stance is coherently available to a single agent at a single time.
