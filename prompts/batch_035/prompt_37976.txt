i'm taking a seminar this term on comparative memory politics -- germany after 1945, japan, turkey and the armenian genocide, the american south and the lost cause stuff. one of the other students, who spent a year in berlin and is basically our resident germany person, keeps using this framing she picked up from a lecture over there: a country confronting its past is like a patient in therapy. germany is the one who actually went and did the work -- showed up to the sessions, stopped repressing, eventually got healthy enough to have normal relationships with its neighbors. japan is the avoidant patient who minimizes and changes the subject when the textbooks come up. turkey hasn't even admitted it has a problem. and the american south is like a family that retells a flattering story about the conflict until the grandkids beleive it. she used this to argue that the real difference between these cases is psychological, and that the prescription for everyone is the german one: more confrontation, more memorials, more guilt actually worked through, and that countries that resist this are doing what an avoidant patient does.

our GSI sort of waved at this being too neat but never actually took it apart, and it keeps coming back in discussion. can you audit it properly? i want the mapping made explicit -- who or what is the patient, who's the therapist, what plays the role of repression, of a session, of recovery or discharge -- and then where the mapping breaks. but please also sort the disanalogies: which ones actually matter for the conclusion she wants (everyone should do what germany did) and which are cosmetic. like, does it matter that a patient is one continuous person while a nation's "self" gets replaced generation by generation, or that therapy is private while national memory work is performed for an audience, often the victims' descendants or domestic voters? does it matter that in therapy there's supposed to be an endpoint, while people who study vergangenheitsbewältigung seem to describe it as permanent? i'd also want to know what the analogy quietly imports -- the assumption that confronting the past is what makes you healthy, for instance, rather than something a strong state can afford to do after it's already secure.

and please actually take a position instead of giving me the balanced both-sides version. if you think the analogy is basically sound and she's right, say so and say why the disanalogies don't sink it. if you think the therapy picture is doing sneaky work -- e.g. making a contested political choice (who remembers, on whose terms, funded by whom) look like an obvious clinical necessity -- then say that instead. and if there's a better analogy for the same phenomenon, i want to hear it, not just the autopsy of the old one.
