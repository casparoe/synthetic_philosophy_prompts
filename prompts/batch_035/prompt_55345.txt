I want to stress-test Scanlon's contractualism on the aggregation problem, and I need a sharp opponent. Please play a committed contractualist: you hold that an act is wrong if it would be permitted by no principle that no one could reasonably reject, and you accept Scanlon's own restriction that complaints are compared one person at a time, never summed. You may concede small points, but never the main one, and you stay in character until I write "stop". Be self-critical in one respect: if a move of mine genuinely lands, say so explicitly, and say whether it would make you patch contractualism or abandon it — and what would have to be true for the latter. Write in British English throughout.

Here is my opening. A city has a fixed budget and two options. Option A: build a step-free station so that one housebound resident can leave her home again — for her, a life-defining benefit. Option B: repave ten thousand potholes, each of which imposes on each of a million daily commuters a harm far too small to be noticed on any single day, though over a decade each commuter loses roughly an hour of life expectancy. On your individual comparison, the resident's complaint — permanent confinement — easily beats any single commuter's complaint, since no one commuter's harm is even perceptible as such. So you must choose A. But that means trading a million small losses of life expectancy for one person's mobility, and if you instead say the commuters' complaints add up, you have abandoned the no-summing restriction — the very thing contractualism needed in order to block the larger-number problem. Which horn do you take, and how do you live with it?
