Last night I had a long argument with a friend that I still have not found my way out of. He left graduate school years ago but kept his Kripke, and he now claims to be able to prove that there are no contingent facts about any individual: every truth about a person is, if true, necessary. The argument as he sets it out:

(1) An ordinary proper name is a rigid designator: in every possible world, 'Aristotle' denotes the very man it denotes in the actual world — the man himself, whether or not, in that world, he does any of the things we know him for.
(2) Predicates can be brought under the same discipline. 'is a philosopher' denotes the property of being a philosopher, and that property is a single thing — the same property whichever world we are describing. So the sentence 'Aristotle is a philosopher' is assembled entirely from terms each of which denotes the same object in every possible world.
(3) What a sentence says is fixed by what its terms denote. Where the denotations do not vary from world to world, what the sentence says does not vary either.
(4) In the actual world the sentence is true: Aristotle, the man 'Aristotle' denotes, has the property 'is a philosopher' denotes.
(5) Since by (3) the sentence says the same thing in every world, and by (4) what it says is true, the sentence is true in every possible world.
(6) What is true in every possible world is necessary. So it is necessary that Aristotle is a philosopher.
(7) Nothing in the argument depended on Aristotle or on philosophy. Any true sentence whose subject is a name and whose predicate is an ordinary property of individuals goes through in the same way. Hence every truth about any individual is necessary, and there are no contingent facts about people.

He is quite serene about where this lands: it is necessary, though of course still a posteriori, that Aristotle was a philosopher; necessary, though a posteriori, that Bach had twenty children; necessary, though a posteriori, that I missed the last bus. The necessity, he says, is the lesson of 'Hesperus is Phosphorus'; the a posteriori character is why no one finds any of this out by pure thought.

He also has an answer ready for the objection I pressed hardest, which was that Aristotle could have gone into the family business and never written a line. He says the contingency I am feeling there is precisely the illusion Kripke diagnosed: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' also feels contingent, and it is not; the fact that it had to be discovered by observation is no argument for its contingency, since it is the paradigm of the necessary a posteriori. So, he concludes, my intuition that Aristotle need not have been a philosopher is the same epistemic feeling miscategorized as a modal fact, and the Hesperus case teaches me to distrust such feelings when an argument is on the table.

I am confident the argument proves too much, but I cannot name the slip. My suspicion falls between (3) and (5): the argument seems to move from the terms denoting the same objects in every world to the sentence being true in every world, and that move looks as though it lets the actual world's say-so in (4) do work that the constancy of the denotations cannot carry with them — that Aristotle has the property here is a fact about this world. But when I try to say this cleanly, he asks what the difference is between his case and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus,' where the terms likewise denote the same objects in every world and the necessity is real and welcome. I take it the difference must be that identity, when it holds between the denoted objects at all, holds in every world of itself, whereas the having-of-a-property relation can hold in one world and not in another even when the object and the property are fixed — but I do not know how to state that so that it is a principled distinction rather than an ad hoc exception for predicates. And I cannot tell whether his Hesperus analogy for my modal intuition is fair: whether the lesson of the necessary a posteriori is only that how something is discovered is one thing and whether it could have been otherwise is another, or whether it really does license discarding a modal intuition whenever an argument opposes it.

Where does the argument go wrong — which step, what exactly is wrong with it, and what does rejecting that step cost? I would much rather keep the Kripkean package, rigid names and the necessary a posteriori included, than grant his conclusion, so if the price of rigidity really is his trivialism about individuals, I need to know that; but if the fault is instead in (3), or in the slide from (4) to (5), I want that diagnosis and the principled distinction I am missing.
