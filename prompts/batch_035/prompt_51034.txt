You are a philosopher of physics with a serious poetic side — the kind of person who has actually worked through the Gödel metric and also knows why a pantoum is built the way it is. I'd like you to write me a pantoum about Gödel's rotating universe, and I want the form to carry the metaphysics, not just decorate it.

Some background so we're aligned: in 1949, in the volume assembled for Einstein's seventieth birthday, Gödel presented an exact solution to the field equations — a static, homogeneous, rotating universe filled with pressureless dust — in which every point lies on a closed timelike curve. In principle, a traveler in that universe could return to their own past. Gödel drew a philosophical moral from this: in such a world there could be no objective, global "now" that flows; a universe that permits you to arrive before you depart is a universe in which the passage of time cannot be a real feature of things, only of our perspective — an argument, Kantian in spirit, for the ideality of time.

Here's why I think a pantoum is the right vehicle, and what I want you to do with it: in a pantoum, lines circulate — the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next, and the poem typically closes by circling back to its opening line. That is structurally what a closed timelike curve does to a worldline. So the refrain structure should do real philosophical work: I want the reader to experience, through the returning lines, what it means for the "same" event to be revisited — and crucially, since a returning line in a pantoum lands in a new context and so means something different on its second pass, the poem should enact Gödel's point that a revisitable event cannot be the anchor of an objective flow. The line that comes back should be both identical and transformed, and that doubleness is the argument.

Beyond the pantoum's standard rules, a few specific requirements. First, somewhere in the poem there should be a turn — it need not be a sonnet's volta, but there should be a moment where the naive reading (a whimsical time-travel fantasy) gives way to the austere one (nothing flows; the journey's possibility is the flowing's impossibility). Second, I'd like the poem to register, in passing, the human strangeness of the gesture — Gödel handing this to Einstein as a birthday present — without turning into costume drama. Third, the physics should be right enough that a philosopher of physics wouldn't wince: rotation, dust, curves closing on themselves — yes; hovering DeLoreans or wormholes — no.

Before you write the poem, please take a moment to brainstorm: sketch three or four candidate refrains and note, for each, what it would mean on first pass versus second pass, so the returning lines earn their doubleness rather than just rhyming with themselves conceptually. Then write the pantoum (six or seven stanzas should be enough), and after the poem, add two or three sentences explaining where you think the form does the most work and where you had to fight the form to keep the philosophy honest. This is for the closing session of a philosophy of physics seminar, so the audience will know the solution and the argument — the poem should be right, not merely atmospheric.
