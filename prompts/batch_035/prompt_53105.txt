I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and next term I'm leading a seminar session on Daniel Dennett's 1991 paper "Real Patterns" and the debate it opened up around the three stances: whether the intentional stance tracks something real, and if so in what sense of "real." For the session I want a diagnostic decision tree to hand out, but I want it built in the right order: derive the logical space from the questions first, and only afterwards locate the named positions inside it. Every branch should be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it delivers; nobody should be able to reach a leaf just by knowing the labels.

The root of the tree: the reader accepts that the physical description of the world is causally complete, with no extra metaphysics — no dualism, no extra fundamental properties. So every leaf must be some form of physicalism about the special sciences in general and about belief attribution in particular, and the tree sorts leaves by the status they assign to higher-level patterns.

The branch questions should each be a concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart. I want the following cases somewhere on the tree, because our seminar discussion keeps circling back to them:

1. A glider in the Game of Life. Given the complete cell-by-cell description, is the glider a real thing in the world, or a convenience of ours? Is there a fact of the matter here at all, or does the answer depend on who is asking and for what?

2. Dennett's test for patternhood. On his way of making "real pattern" precise, a pattern is real when tracking it buys predictive leverage over a null hypothesis — roughly, the hypothesis that the data are random — at an acceptable informational cost. Is the choice of null objective, so that whether something is a real pattern is observer-independent? Or is it interest-relative in a way that makes patternhood at least partly our contribution? If the latter, does anything survive of the claim that the pattern was there before we noticed it?

3. False but predictive attribution. Consider a case of the rain-dance shape: an observer predicts a group's behavior reliably by attributing beliefs the observer herself regards as flatly false, and the prediction succeeds because it targets the behavior, not the world. Does the success of the intentional stance require that the attributed beliefs be by and large true, or can predictive usefulness float free of truth altogether? Where someone answers this question should do a lot of work in sorting them.

4. Marginal agents. A chess engine, a crow, a human infant, a patient in a persistent vegetative state. Is there a determinate boundary where belief stops, a penumbra of vague cases, or no fact of the matter until an attribution is made?

5. The design stance, which everyone forgets. Are function attributions — the heart is for pumping, this enzyme is for copying — observer-independent facts, or are they in the same boat as belief attributions? This question should be on the tree precisely because most taxonomies of this debate ignore the middle stance, and I want to see whether ignoring it collapses two leaves that should be distinct.

6. Patternhood all the way down. Is the physical description itself just another level of pattern — real relative to a scale and a set of interests like every other — or is there a level of fact about the world as it is, independent of all our taxonomies, which anchors the reality of everything above it?

7. Fodor's worry, which I'd like on the tree as a late-stage test: suppose cognitive science eventually yields local generalizations about the attitudes that predict well in our parochil corner but do not carve nature at anything worth calling a joint — the way a taxonomy of stars might serve navigation wonderfully while being no guide to what stars fundamentally are. Does belief talk survive that result intact, survive with downgraded status, or die?

The leaves must include: Dennett's own pattern realism; hard instrumentalism, on which belief talk is a predictive tool whose attributions are not even apt for truth; eliminativism about the attitudes; a Fodor-style realism on which beliefs are natural kinds, so that folk psychology is an ordinary empirical theory, vindicable or refutable; and rainforest realism, on which every scientifically serviceable pattern is real in a scale-relative way, the physical patterns having no more exalted a status than the rest. If the logical space contains further coherent combinations, mark them as unnamed and diagnose their vacancy in a sentence each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate — that is, some combination of answers that two of the positions are officially supposed to disagree about but in practice give the same response to. I am particularly suspicious that the tree will not cleanly separate Dennett's pattern realism from instrumentalism on the one side and from rainforest realism on the other, and if that suspicion is right I want it said plainly rather than papered over with rhetoric about "mild realism."

Format: begin with the tree itself, rendered as an indented outline, followed by the notes on unoccupied combinations and on inseparable leaves. No preamble, no restating what I have asked for.
