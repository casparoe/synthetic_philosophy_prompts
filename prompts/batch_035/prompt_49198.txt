I've been trying to work out, from my gut reactions to concrete cases, when a competitive dynamic is a genuine race to the bottom — a multipolar trap, Moloch proper — and when it's just ordinary competition whose whole point is to grind someone's profits away. My verdicts: an airline fare war that drives prices down to marginal cost is fine, even good, though it annihilates every airline's profits. A doping race among cyclists, each doping because the others do, is a paradigm trap. But a training race, each rider adding hours because the rivals train more, feels fine — even admirable — though structurally it looks the same: relative rankings barely move, and everyone pays real costs. Local governments bidding escalating tax abatements to win a battery factory: a trap, even though the money isn't burned but simply transferred to the factory's owners. Firms in an advertising war where each campaign exists only to neutralize the rival's last one: a trap, pure dissipation. Yet peacock tails and host–parasite co-evolution, which are structurally indistinguishable from arms races, I don't count as Moloch at all — possibly because there is no one who could ever have coordinated, but I'm not sure that's a principled reason. My tentative principle — a race is a trap when the competitors' efforts cancel out while their costs are real — fits the advertising and doping cases but cannot explain why the training race feels fine, and it misclassifies the fare war, where profits are annihilated and the effort really is wasted from the airlines' point of view. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the best single principle capturing as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely; (2) which verdicts it captures and why; (3) which verdict, if any, it should overturn, and why that one rather than another; (4) its most serious rival principle and the verdict that forces a choice between the two; (5) what your favored principle predicts about the case I keep flip-flopping on — two coffee shops on the same street extending their opening hours night after night to match each other, paying real staff costs, capturing no new customers, but serving night owls who are quietly grateful.
