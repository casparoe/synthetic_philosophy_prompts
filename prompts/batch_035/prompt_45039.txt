I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the keynote at the fortieth-anniversary dinner of the low-residency MFA program I graduated from, which is also the retirement dinner for its founder, who taught me twenty years ago. Here is my complication. My most recent novel was partly drafted with a large language model: I fed it scenes, it returned variants, I kept a handful of its phrases verbatim, rewrote most of the rest, and discarded its suggestions far more often than I accepted them. The founder considers this a betrayal of the discipline she built. Roughly half the graduating students chose the program precisely because it was advertised as a machine-free room; the other half use the tools daily and will be listening to hear whether I grovel. The novel itself was well reviewed, and no reviewer raised the question of how it was made — a fact I keep turning over, since it suggests the reading public either cannot tell or does not care, and each possibility reflects on me differently.

Write the keynote. I want it to stand on a genuine philosophical account of creativity — the kind that distinguishes a product's merely being new from a work's counting as creative, and that can say something non-obvious about where the line falls when generation and selection come apart in two different hands. It must be neither confession nor defense. It has to give the founder something true about what she actually taught me, give the machine-skeptical students something they didn't already believe, and leave the machine-enthusiastic students carrying a heavier burden than they walked in with. Assume twenty minutes, an audience of working writers, and a room that will punish both evasion and grandstanding. You may not resolve the tension by declaring the question undecidable; that would be the one speech every faction would forgive and none would remember.
