So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm writing a book in which one character insists the people around her are just patterns she's detected — and I want to actually get the philosophy right instead of hand-waving it. I've been reading Dennett's paper "Real Patterns" (the 1991 one from the Journal of Philosophy) and there's a part I can't quite unpack on my own. He describes a sequence of frames that begin as a simple bar-code pattern and then get progresively more random, and at some point, he says, there just isn't a pattern there anymore. His test seems to turn on efficiency: a pattern is real when it's more economical to describe something as the pattern plus a list of exceptions than to inventory it item by item — the way it takes way fewer bits to say the frame is mostly bar code with a quarter of the pixels scrambled than to spell out every pixel.

Here's what I want to know: if that's what makes a belief real, does the same hold for fictional characters? My protagonist exists as a pattern in my readers' heads, she supports predictions about what she'll do next chapter, she compresses a whole manuscript into a name you can grip. Is she real the way Dennett says beliefs are real — just noisier? And if realness comes in degrees like that, where exactly does that leave the line between my characters and my family? Please walk me through the passage like I'm smart but untrained, and then tell me straight whether I'm stretching his point past what it can bear.
