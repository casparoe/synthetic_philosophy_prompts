Hi, quick background: I'm a philosopher (mostly epistemology, did some philosophy of history ages ago) and I'm the token non-historian on our university's Core Curriculum Committee. We're redesigning the required freshman survey, currently titled "Origins of the Modern World," and the historians have split into three camps and then punted the whole thing to a subcommittee, which of course includes me. Camp one wants to keep the classic throughline -- rise of the West, Renaissance to industrialization -- and "add" non-Western material (two weeks on the Ottoman and Mughal empires, one on Ming China) so the course covers everyone. Camp two wants to restructure around global comparison: keep the big questions (why did some regions come to dominate trade and technology by 1800?) but ask them about everywhere at once, with no region cast as the protagonist. Camp three, mostly younger faculty, says any single narrative smuggles in someone's vantage point, so the honest move is to abandon the throughline entirely and have students take several seperate provincial histories -- an Islamic-world course, a Latin America course, and so on -- with no pretense of synthesis.

I've been tasked with drafting the written recommendation the subcomittee will circulate to the full committee before the April vote, since I'm supposedly neutral. The thing I keep circling back to, and which I'd love the memo to actually confront rather than paper over, is whether "telling the past from everywhere at once" is even coherent. Camp two's global comparison still needs a yardstick -- development, state capacity, integration into world markets -- and maybe the yardstick is the Eurocentrism, just relocated rather than removed. But camp three's pluralism looks like it gives up on explanation altogether, as if every historical question should be answered with "depends where you're standing," which is a strange thing to teach eighteen-year-olds whose one required history course this is. And camp one's additive fix feels like it concedes the critics' point while changing almost nothing.

So: draft the memo. It needs a clear recommendation (one of the three structures, or a hybrid if you can make it concrete rather than a dodge), reasons the historians could actually engage with, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on behalf of whichever option loses. Don't let it read like a philosophy paper -- the committee wants something they can vote on, with actual curricular specifics (weeks, modules, what gets cut to make room), not a meditation on standpoint with no bottom line. If you think there's a principled way to be partial to a perspective while flagging the partiality, or that comparison can proceed without a privileged yardstick, make the case in operational terms, since that's what the vote will turn on.
