Background: I got into this after writing a piece on the 2011 OPERA dust-up, when the Gran Sasso folks clocked neutrinos from CERN as arriving early — faster than light — and it eventually turned out to be a loose fiber connection to their master clock. My physicist source's takeaway was basically Popper's: relativity is science precisely because it sticks its neck out. It forbids something. If superluminal neutrinos had been real, relativity would have had to go. Astrology, by contrast, forbids nothing, so nothing could ever count against it. That felt right to me. Then a friend of mine (sociology grad student, loves this stuff) walked me through the following, and now I'm stuck.

(1) A theory is scientific only if it is falsifiable: there has to be some possible observation which, if it turned up, would show the theory false.
(2) But a theory never forbids an observation all by itself. Relativity forbids superluminal neutrinos only when you tack on auxiliaries — the timing gear works, the cable is snug, the software is doing what everyone thinks its doing. That's just Duhem's old point, and OPERA illustrates it nicely: the reading was only ever in conflict with the package, never with relativity alone.
(3) Here's the kicker, though: for any observation whatsoever, you can cook up some auxiliary story that keeps the theory and swallows the observation. Made-up data, contradictory data, anything — there is always some consistent package if you're willing to rewrite enough of the background.
(4) So no observation, however wild, would ever force anyone to abandon any theory. And since (2) is completely general, this goes for every theory, not just relativity.
(5) To be falsifiable, per (1), is for there to exist an observation that would show the theory false — one that could not be squared with it.
(6) Therefore no theory is falsifiable, and Popper's criterion, taken seriously, rules all of science unscientific. Which is nuts: the criterion was supposed to separate physics from astrology, and instead it can't even separate physics from nothing.

My friend claims this means falsificationism refutes itself and demarcation is a lost cause. I don't want to buy that. Something feels like its slipping between (3) and (5) but I can't put my finger on it, and honestly I'd rather understand the trick than win the argument.

So: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine to me at first, and what does rejecting it cost? And once the dust settles, does anything survive for Popper, or is my friend right that demarcation dies here?
