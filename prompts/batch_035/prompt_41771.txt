My roommates have been going at it for a week about whether the American "empire" is due for decline, and they finally asked me to referee, except I'm compeltely out of my depth. Can you go through their argument move by move and tell me who's right about what?

It started when Dan read some old essay (by a British historian, Glubb I think) claiming that empires across all of recorded history last roughly 250 years before decline sets in, and that the US is right around that age now. He said that no matter what the pundits say about American resilience, the base rate is the base rate: the smart money bets the way history already went.

Maya said that's cargo-cult statistics. The whole thing turns on what reference class you put the US in, and that choice is arbitrary. If the class is "empires," you get one number; if it's "constitutional democracies" or "nuclear powers" or "large federal states with peaceful transfers of power," you get others, and nothing in the world tells you which class is the right one. Picking "empires" just smuggles in the conclusion that the US is one.

Dan came back with what he called the outside view. He said psychologists have shown that people making long-range forecasts obsess over the details of their own case — the inside view — and ignore how similar projects have actually turned out, and that switching to base rates beat the actual domain experts. So the pundits insisting "this time is different" are precisely the people the research predicts will be wrong.

Maya wasn't done though. She said the outside-view success stories are all cases where the cases really are comparable — estimating how long a software project will take by looking at other projects by the same team, that kind of thing. Empires aren't an exchangeable pile: Rome fell for reasons (overextension, plagues, succession crises) that mostly don't apply to a country with ICBMs and a service economy. And she said the best forecasters in those forecasting tournaments didn't just take the base rate and stop either — they started from it and then adjusted for the specifics, which is exactly what she's telling Dan to do, so the research cuts her way, not his.

Dan's last word was that "adjusting for the specifics" is a blank check for motivated reasoning — everybody thinks their empire is special — and the base rate at least has the virtue of being hard to fudge.

Who's right about what here? Please rate each of the five moves from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who comes out ahead overall, and tell me if there's something both of them are missing.
