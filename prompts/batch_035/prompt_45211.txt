my epistemology reading group keeps arguing about testimony and one of the guys always reaches for the same analogy: believing what people tell you is like believing your own senses. when you look around the room you don't first prove your eyes are working and then beleive there's a table, you just believe it until something goes wrong. he says testimony deserves the same deal: trust is the default and the burden is on the sceptic to give a reason to doubt. and he uses it for a strong practical conclusion too: basically everything i "know" — history, science, where my grandmother was born — arrived via other people saying so, so a policy of checking the sources yourself would leave me knowing next to nothing, meaning default-trust isn't just permissible, it's the only rational option. i gather there's an actual divide in the literature here, with one side treating testimony as a basic source of justification, perception-style, and the hume-ish side treating it as secondhand info that needs independent backing. so audit the analogy for me: what's supposed to play the role of the percept, the sense organ, the enabling background conditions (lighting, my eyes actually functioning), illusion and hallucination, and the possibility of cross-checking? where does the mapping break — speakers have beliefs and interests and can lie, the causal chain to the original fact is long and mediated, and there's no equivalent of covering one eye to see if the table stays put. which of the disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are fine to ignore? and then the part i really care about: he's lately taken to saying that asking an AI assistant is "just more testimony," so the same default-trust should carry over — does the perceptual model, or whatever survives of it, stretch to that case, and if so, what plays the role of the reliable faculty that's supposed to be functioning properly?
