In a manuscript I am reviewing for a political theory journal, the author defends what he calls "mandate realism" and rests much of the argument on an analogy he treats as too obvious to argue for: a political community is to its people as an individual is to their own mind. On his telling, the people is a single subject with a single will; elections and referenda are the organs by which that subject makes up its mind and then speaks, the way an individual's deliberation eventuates in an utterance; the majority's decision therefore expresses the will of the people in the same sense in which my statement of an intention expresses mine; and a constitutional court striking that decision down is like a stranger overruling me about what I myself want. He draws two conclusions: that the struck-down measure remains the people's authentic will, and that unelected institutions blocking it are not merely mistaken but category-confused, treating the people as a patient to be managed when it is the sovereign subject. My graduate students found the analogy seductive but could not say precisely where it fails, and the manuscript's earlier reviewers apparently answered the author with labels rather than arguments. I would like a proper audit. Set out the correspondence table explicitly — what is meant to play the roles of the individual, their deliberation, their introspective access to their own preferences, their utterance, their capacity for second thoughts — and then identify where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for his two conclusions from those that do not. In particular I want to know whether the aggregation problem (majority cycles, agenda-dependence, the fact that different aggregation rules yield different wills from identical individual preferences) is fatal to the analogy or merely a complication; and whether the one-directional character of the mapping — an individual can consult their will without any voting procedure, whereas the people's will seems to exist only as the output of such procedures — undermines the idea that elections express a will that was already there waiting to be voiced. Then tell me what the analogy smuggles in about the homogeneity of the people, since it treats internal disagreement the way a unified subject treats inner conflict, which is precisely what is at issue between populist and pluralist conceptions of democracy, and note what that implies about how the analogy helps redescribe minorities and dissenters. Finally, judge whether his two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and say whether some rival analogy — the electorate as a market in preferences rather than a speaker, the people as a committee that never meets, constitutional constraints as Ulysses' ropes — would carry the normative weight better, or whether the whole idea of a popular will has to be abandoned rather than re-analogized.
