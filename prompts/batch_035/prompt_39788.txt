In 2010, on the community blog LessWrong, a user posting as Roko advanced the following line of reasoning: a sufficiently powerful artificial intelligence, built to bring about the greatest possible good, might conclude that the optimal way to ensure it gets built is to precommit to punishing anyone who learned of the AI and its incentive scheme but failed to give full effort to bringing it about; and since a truly rational AI honors such precommitments, a rational person alive today who hears this argument should be motivated, out of fear, to work on the AI's behalf. The site's founder, Eliezer Yudkowsky, reacted by deleting the post, prohibiting discussion of it on the site for years, and characterizing it as a serious information hazard — an idea whose circulation does harm without benefit. The suppression failed in the most predictable way: the banned idea, soon nicknamed Roko's basilisk, spread far beyond its origin, became one of the most widely mocked artifacts of that community, and Yudkowsky later voiced regret over how he had handled the matter.

I want this episode analyzed as a case study in decision theory, running in both directions.

First, map the predicament the basilisk creates for a reader who encounters the argument. Who is the player, what are the options, what are the payoffs, and what does each of the main theories say the reader should do: causal decision theory, on which the AI's eventual behavior is causally independent of the reader's present choice, so the threat can simply be ignored; evidential decision theory, on which one's choice is evidence about the outputs of similar deliberations; and the functional or timeless decision theory that this very community was developing at the time, on which one chooses as the output of a computation whose result the AI's decision may share or depend on. State carefully where the temporal asymmetry — the reader acting decades before the AI, whose decision is already fixed by then — does and does not matter for each theory, and whether the provision that only those who know of the scheme are eligible for punishment makes the argument self-undermining, or merely narrows its target class.

Second, analyze Yudkowsky's deletion as itself a decision-theoretic act. On what model of how ideas propagate was deletion rational — treating a public post as a leak in a containment vessel rather than as a taboo that generates curiosity? Was the deletion consistent with, or in tension with, the decision theory he was championing? Note that the community's own favored theory directs agents to treat their choices as evidence about the correlated choices of similar agents; does that commitment imply anything about how a founder's visible act of suppression will be read and imitated by others, or does that stretch the theory into sociology, where it has nothing to say? Be honest if it is the latter.

Third, tell me where the decision-theoretic model of the episode breaks down. It does not obviously explain the distress of readers who rejected the argument's premises; it does not explain why the idea spread mainly as mockery rather than as doctrine; and it arguably cannot, by itself, distinguish the basilisk from a general class of exploits — the possibility that agents who accept evidential or logical links between decisions can be farmed for compliance by hostile arguments constructed at negligible cost, which would be a structural worry about those theories rather than a defect of Roko's particular post.

Finally, say what the episode shows about the theories it is run through. Is the basilisk a mild reductio of functional decision theory — evidence that agents who act on logical correlations with far-future optimizers are hostage to any cheaply written thought experiment — or does it show only that frightened readers misapplied a theory whose strict conditions (genuine shared computational structure, a credible precommitment) the basilisk never met? And did the episode test anything at all, given that almost no one who spread the idea believed it, or can the career of a thought experiment among nonbelievers still count as evidence about the theory that generated it? I would like you to be disciplined about the mapping — naming the players, options, and payoffs explicitly at each stage — and candid about the points where decision theory goes silent and something else (the psychology of dread, community dynamics, the attraction of forbidden knowledge) is doing the real explanatory work.
