So I've been in a grad reading group on higher-order evidence for basically a year and a half now, and I've slowly realized the argument about epistemic akrasia is never going anywhere, and I'd like a diagnosis of why.

The target case, quickly: an agent believes p while also believing that her own total evidence doesn't support p. One camp says this combination is always irrational — it's the epistemic version of weakness of will, and in forming the first-order belief the agent is in effect stepping on her own evidence, since her second-order belief undercuts the very basis she'd cite for the first-order one. The other camp keeps building cases where the akratic agent looks okay or even better off: your first-order evidence supports p, then you get misleading but strong evidence that this first-order evidence is unreliable; forming the second-order belief seems clearly right, and this camp says that given what actually does support p, hanging onto the first-order belief is fine too, or at least not automatically crazy. Everyone agrees on all the facts in every case. They just flip on whether the akratic agent is rational, and flip in opposite directions, forever.

Here's a thing that makes me suspect the fight isn't really about the cases. A while back the field more or less settled on a taxonomy — reasons you have because of what your evidence supports, versus reasons you have just because of the state you're currently in — and I would've thought that distinction would cut the dispute cleanly, like ok, now we can see which claim each side is actually making. Instead both sides just absorbed the taxonomy and kept arguing exactly as before. Nobody has budged in what, a decade?

So my actual question. What kind of disagreement is this? My leading hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: it's at least partly verbal, and the two camps are each using 'rational' to evaluate something different — one evaluating the belief-state against how things actually are, the other evaluating it against rules the agent could follow from the inside without godlike knowledge of which of her beliefs are true. But maybe that's too convenient, since each camp does seem to think the other is wrong about something, not just answering a different question. So alternatively: is it a genuine substantive disagreement that's just hard? Is it bedrock intuition — some people just have the reflex that believing against your own lights is like believing at random, others don't, and there's nothing either side can argue from? Use what each side actually says to decide, not just my framing of it.

And then, whatever your diagnosis is, tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a new kind of case, some formal result about accuracy, an agreed stipulation about which question we're asking — or whether the right answer is that no progress should be expected, and why.
