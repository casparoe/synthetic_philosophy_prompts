My two oldest friends got into it at a barbecue a couple weekends back and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning by the end. Some background: our hometown had a mining disaster back in the 1920s where a bunch of guys died because the company ignored safety warnings, and this year the town put up a small memorial and the local paper digitized its whole archive. Dana has been pushing for the schools to make it a whole unit — field trips, oral history project, names on a wall. Marcus thinks this is a terrible idea, and it turned into a whole thing in front of everyone.

Roughly how it went. (1) Dana said the dead are owed it — those men got erased at the time, the paper called it an unfortunate accident for eighty years, and if the living drop it now they're just finishing the job the company started. Forgetting is never neutral, somebody always benefits from it. (2) Marcus said the dead can't be owed anything — being owed something requires being around for it to get settled, and anyway Dana doesn't actually remember these men, she's inheriting a wound. Nobody alive signed up to carry a hundred-year-old grievance, and the kids who'd be made to carry it did nothing wrong. (3) Dana said that's exactly why it needs to be structured remembering and not a grudge — facts, names, causes, timelines — which is the opposite of a grudge, and a grudge is what you get when people remember vaguely and angrily instead of properly. (4) Marcus said no institution gets to control how memory lands, every "never forget" exhibit gets experienced as us-versus-them anyway, and look how well a century of everybody remembering every atrocity has turned out — half the conflicts in the news are basically grievance archives being recited out loud. He also said the law itself eventually says enough, that's what statutes of limitations are, so memory can't be some unconditional duty that never expires. (5) Dana's last word was that the statute of limitations thing proves too much — by that logic there's some year when the Holocaust stops mattering, and if your principle can't explain why that's wrong, it's the wrong principle. Then they both turned to me because I'd stupidly mentioned taking an ethics course ten years ago.

Can you go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? Like where Dana is actually onto something, where Marcus is, and whether they're even disagreeing about the same thing anymore by the end — it felt like somewhere around step 3 they were talking about two different kinds of remembering and neither of them noticed. And is there something both of them are missing entirely? I keep going back and forth and would love a proper referee's call.
