I spent forty years as an endocrinologist, most of them on the regulation of blood glucose, and I have recently retired into a second education in the philosophy of mind. I have been reading about the symbol grounding problem, and I find that my old professional instincts have produced in me a proposal I have not seen stated quite this way, so I want to test it against a credence — yours, decomposed.

Here is the proposal. In the body, a hormone like insulin does not represent glucose the way a barometer reading represents pressure: it does not merely covary with a state of the world and sit there awaiting interpretation. It participates in regulation. Its meaning, if that word applies, is exhausted by its role in keeping a controlled variable inside a viability range — a set-point, a deviation signal, a corrective action. By analogy, I want to propose that a symbol is grounded in a system only if the system can use that symbol to regulate something: an internal variable, an ongoing interaction with the environment, something that can drift out of range and be corrected. Call this the homeostatic criterion. A colleague tells me this bears a family resemblance to teleosemantics, though I confess I have only begun that literature.

Now the question I want your credence on, stated so it has a truth value: a large language model trained solely on human-produced text cannot satisfy the homeostatic criterion for its words, and therefore cannot genuinely understand them. Note the second clause is conditional on the first; if the criterion is wrong, the conclusion does not follow, whatever else is true of these systems.

Please give me a number and the derivation, not a survey of positions. At minimum I would expect terms for: the probability that genuine understanding requires some non-derivative grounding at all (rather than, say, understanding being a matter of functional role alone); the probability that regulation of the kind I describe is the right form such grounding must take, as against perceptual or causal-covariational forms; the probability that training on text is genuinely "purely linguistic" given that the text was produced by organisms who were themselves grounded and may transmit something through it; and any further terms you think belong, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification. Then combine them, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what evidence would move that term — including, if you can, an experimental or engineering result that would count either way.

For reference, my own credence sits at about 0.55, uncomfortably close to the fence, which is itself informative: I suspect my number is dominated by my uncertainty about the criterion rather than about the machines. Tell me whether your decomposition agrees.
