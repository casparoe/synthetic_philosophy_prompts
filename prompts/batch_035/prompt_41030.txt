In criminal trials across common-law jurisdictions, an unspoken division of labour governs who may say what. Witnesses testify to what happened; expert witnesses are qualified to explain matters calling for specialised knowledge, from ballistics to psychiatry; but no witness, however credentialed, is permitted to tell the jury that the defendant's conduct was wicked, that he deserved what he got, or that his excuse was adequate. That judgement is reserved to the jury, and jurors are effectively told that culpability is a question their own consciences must answer. Yet the same legal systems are far from consistent about excluding second-hand moral judgement. Research ethics committees issue binding verdicts on the permissibility of experiments, largely through deferral to a small cadre of experienced members; professional disciplinary panels pronounce on whether a doctor's or lawyer's conduct fell below acceptable standards, guided by expert opinion; and in tort law, judges themselves determine, as a matter of law, whether conduct was sufficiently outrageous to ground a claim for emotional harm. What view of moral knowledge does the courtroom exclusion of moral expertise presuppose, if any? Does the trial practice genuinely rest on moral testimony pessimism — the thought that one cannot come to know that an act was wrong merely by being told so — or only on a claim about democratic legitimacy, or on doubts about whether moral expertise is the kind of thing anyone can have? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the exclusion, say whether it can be reconciled with the deference practised elsewhere in the same systems, and tell me what would have to change in courtroom practice if we accepted instead that testimony can transmit moral knowledge in something like the way it transmits facts.
