Our Nietzsche reading group is finishing up a whole semester and somehow I've been assigned to bring a poem to the last session instead of a response paper (our facilitator thinks he's funny). We spent the final month on eternal recurrence, and the thing that split the group most wasn't the cosmological version — whether time loops and the same events literally repeat — but the practical test in The Gay Science: a demon sits down beside you, tells you this exact life, every pain and every joy in the same order, will return innumerable times, and your reaction to hearing that is supposed to function as a verdict on how you've actually lived. Half the group read it as a thought experiment about affirmation (amor fati, loving fate, all that) and the other half kept insisting Nietzsche believed the physical doctrine too, and honestly both readings sat in the room the whole time and neither would leave.

Write me a sestina on this. Here's the part I actually care about: the form has to do the philosophical work, not just decorate it. The six end-words come back every stanza in fixed rotation but never in the same grammatical role or sense — "again" as adverb, as noun, as part of a compound; "will" as verb and as noun; "yes" as something you can bite down on. So the same words return identically and yet read differently each time, which is basicly the whole point: the events come back the same, the meaning doens't, and whether the return reads as curse or benediction is decided by the one living it, not the wheel. Use these six end words: again, same, moment, will, light, yes. If you can get "light" to do double duty as "not heavy" and as illumination somewhere in there, our resident Zarathustra person owes me a coffee.

The envoi should compress the demon's visit into three lines — question, then answer — using all six words, and it has to land on the "yes" without sounding cheap or triumphant, since the whole problem with the doctrine is that a fake ecstatic ending is exactly what it's supposed to be a test against. Please don't smuggle in the cosmological argument (the bit about finite arrangements of force cycling through infinite time) — the poem should work as the thought experiment, the test, not as physics, and someone who knows the doctrine cold should be able to see why a sestina rather than some other repeating form, while someone who's never read a page of Nietzsche should just get a good poem about a life that comes back. Standard sestina length, all six stanzas plus envoi, don't get cute and cut it short.
