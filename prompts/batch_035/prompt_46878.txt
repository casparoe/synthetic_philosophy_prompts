I run a weekly evening philosophy class at a community center: about fifteen adults, no philosophical background, ninety minutes, and a whiteboard with nothing else. Next week I have to teach Molyneux's problem, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself: the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what, the one example that carries the whole session (and what it must have, and lack), the misconceptions the class will predictably form — in the order they will form them — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, a closing question that would reveal whether they have grasped the idea rather than the history, and what I should openly decline to cover at this level.

One circumstance shapes the whole plan. One of my regulars, a woman who lost her sight at nineteen and has attended for two years, asked for this session by name, and also asked that the evening not be built around her. I want the plan to take her presence seriously without turning her into either an exhibit or an oracle — she will not, simply by being blind, know the answer to a question about people blind from birth who gain sight. And in fact her case is not Molyneux's case: she went blind late and retains visual memories, whereas Molyneux's subject is blind from birth. I'd like the plan to say whether that distinction should be drawn by me early, left for her to raise if she chooses, or used as the hinge into the modern material — since the patients in the recent sight-restoration studies, who came closest to actually running Molyneux's experiment, were like his imagined man and unlike her: blind from birth, and operated on well after infancy.

Beyond that, I want the plan to hold apart three questions the class will run together from the first minute: whether the newly sighted person can see at all; whether, before touching anything, they can tell a sphere from a cube by sight alone; and whether the answer, whichever way it goes, is a fact about what experience teaches rather than what reason supplies — which is the question Locke took himself to be asking. And when the surgical studies finally come up, I don't want the class walking out believing an experiment simply settled a three-hundred-year-old philosophical dispute: tell me where in the sequence that expectation should be defused, and what exactly such studies can and cannot show.
