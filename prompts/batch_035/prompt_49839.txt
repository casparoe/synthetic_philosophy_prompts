I've read Dennett's hierarchy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures as describing four genuinely different kinds of cognitive architecture rather than four points on a single continuum. On my reading, the transitions between them are transitions in kind, not in degree: a Popperian creature is not merely a faster, better-fitted Skinnerian one but something structurally different, because the locus of trial and error has moved — the costly testing of candidate behaviors happens in an internal model of the environment rather than in the world, where mistakes are fatal or expensive. (I take Dennett to have gotten this framing from Popper's remark, often paraphrased as the observation that critical reasoning lets our hypotheses die in our place.) I hold, further, that this is a claim about mechanisms: what makes something Popperian is the presence of a detachable, revisable inner model in which variations can be tried out and most of them discarded, not raw learning speed or the volume of trials.

I want you to act as a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist — imagine someone who has spent equal time on evolutionary theory, comparative cognition, and the philosophy of Dennett — and give me a rigorous map of what would count against this "kinds, not degrees" reading. Before writing your answer, brainstorm internally: generate the strongest objections you can, then prune and rank them. Then present the defeaters organized along the following lines:

1. Empirical defeaters. What discoveries in comparative cognition, neuroscience, or animal behavior would undermine the idea of genuine kind boundaries? Consider, for instance, evidence of graded transitions across closely related species, or mechanisms like hippocampal offline replay, in which sleeping animals appear to run candidate trajectories that they never enacted — does simulation during sleep make a creature Popperian, and if so, does that threaten my mechanistic claim that the distinction is architectural rather than a matter of how the trials happen to be scheduled?

2. Conceptual counterexamples. Here is one that worries me, and I would like you to develop it fully and evaluate it: a large language model trained by gradient descent. During training, an enormous number of candidate hypotheses about the next word are "tried" and most of them eliminated — but the trials happen in weight space, over many passes through stored data, with the errors being prediction failures rather than fitness costs. A deployed model with frozen weights then never learns again at all. Is this creature Skinnerian (learning is entirely trial-and-error driven by an error signal), Popperian (its "hypotheses" were all pre-tested before deployment, so its behavior is offloaded inner variation), or neither, and does the existence of such a case show that my insistence on "kinds" forces me to count things by incidental properties? Consider also edge cases of your own: slime mold habituation, a chess engine doing look-ahead over a tree of possible futures, or an immune system that generates antibodies by blind variation and selection entirely inside the organism's body.

3. Insulation and honest self-assessment. Dennett himself presented the hierarchy with disclaimers about its being a heuristic taxonomy rather than a rigorous carve of nature at its joints. If I keep the "kinds, not degrees" thesis but quietly retreat to "kinds of heuristic idealization" whenever a boundary case appears, has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability — and for a taxonomy of this sort, would that even be a defect, or is it the wrong kind of claim to be falsifiable in the first place? Give me your honest view of how exposed the thesis actually is, and be willing to conclude that it is well protected and that this is or is not a problem for a view of its type.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of the position would concede it as genuine damage or dismiss it as a misdescription — and where you can, tell me what the position predicts that its degree-continuum rival does not, since predictive divergence is the cleanest test I can think of. Take your time and be precise: I am more interested in a carefully hedged map of the terrain than a tidy verdict.
