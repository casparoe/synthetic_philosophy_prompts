I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I fell down a rabbit hole a few years ago on evolutionary debunking arguments in ethics and I've been following the back-and-forth ever since. Here's the debate as I understand it, and I want to say upfront my question is not "who's right" but "why has this gone nowhere in ~15 years, and what kind of disagreement is it actually".

The setup, briefly: Sharon Street argued in 2006ish that if you're a moral realist (you think moral facts exist independently of our attitudes), you face a dilemma about how our evolutionary history relates to those facts. Either evolutionary forces influenced our moral judgments heavily, in which case it looks like a massive coincidence if the judgments we ended up with also happen to track independent moral truth (evolution selected for fitness, not for truth, so why would fitness-tracking and truth-tracking line up?), or evolution didn't influence them much, which contradicts everything we know about how our evaluative tendencies actually formed. Richard Joyce made a similar-ish point around the same time. So the realist looks stuck.

Then the responses came in. Some realists say evolution wasn't aimed exclusively at fitness in the relevant sense, or that moral truths coincided with fitness-enhancing beliefs because roughly "don't murder your neighbors" is both good for survival and actually morally right. Others go the "third factor" route (I think David Enoch is the big name here): something like survival itself being good does the explaining, so it's not a coincidence that our survival-promoting judgments land on moral truth. And Street's reply to that, as far as I can tell, is basically "you can't help yourself to the claim that survival is good, that's a moral claim, the debunker is allowed to be skeptical of exactly that" — and then the debate just... loops. Every paper I read after 2010 or so feels like a restatement of a move that was already on the table in 2008. Street says third-factor replies beg the question, the third-factor people say no, the demand to justify without any normative premises is itself a substantive normative demand that the antirealist is just assuming, and nobody concedes anything.

So here's my question, and I have a working hypothesis I want you to take seriously but also push back on where it's wrong.

My hypothesis: this isn't a factual disagreement at all. Both sides agree on the evolutionary science, on the phenomenology of moral judgment, all of it. What they actually disagree about is a epistemological standard — specifically, what a non-question-begging justification is allowed to use as premises. The debunker thinks citing normative facts in defense of trusting normative faculties is circular in a disqualifying way; the realist thinks that standard, if applied consistently, would debunk perception or induction too, and we don't accept that result there, so why accept it here. If I'm right, then the whole literature is two camps talking past each other under the heading "does evolution debunk morality" when the real fight is about whether "epistemic conservatism" (trusting your faculties until given reason not to) applies to evaluative faculties the same way it applies to perceptual ones. And if that's the structure, then no new empirical discovery or thought experiment could possibly move the needle, and the stall is baked in.

But maybe I'm wrong. Maybe there's a substantive disagreement hiding in there — like whether the coincidence is really improbable (some people argue the correlation isn't a coincidence at all because moral truths are about things like flourishing, and evolution shaped us to care about flourishing, so the alignment is explained rather than lucky). Or maybe the two sides are answering different questions, one about metaphysical status of moral facts and one about whether *we specifically* are justified in our moral beliefs, and the literature conflates them.

What I want from you: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, argued from what the sides actually say (you know the standard moves — the coincidence objection, third factors, the begging-the-question exchange), not a survey. Then tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress here — a distinction someone hasn't drawn yet, a way of framing it that would force one side to concede something, or an argument that the stall is permanent and we should just say so. If you think my hypothesis is partially right but missing something, say what.
