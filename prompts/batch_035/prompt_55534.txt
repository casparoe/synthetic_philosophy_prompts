I'm putting together a term paper for a course on democratic theory, and I want to work through the Irish Citizens' Assembly (2016–2018) as a test case for the theory of sortition and its neighbors — term limits, rotation in office, and the critique of career politicians. In case the details matter: the Assembly was 99 citizens randomly selected from the electoral register (stratified by gender, age, region, and social class), chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge, Mary Laffoy. Members received expenses but no pay, served for the life of the body rather than seeking any ongoing role, met over a series of weekend sessions at a hotel outside Dublin with expert presentations and roundtable deliberation, and produced recommendations on five questions the government had assigned to it — the Eighth Amendment on abortion, fixed-term parliaments, how referendums are conducted, an ageing population, and climate change. Its abortion recommendations were taken up by the Oireachtas and led to the 2018 referendum that repealed the Eighth Amendment, though the government was formally required only to respond to each report, not to adopt anything. Two wrinkles I find interesting: about a fifth of the original members withdrew over the course of the process and were replaced, and at one point seven replacement members had to be removed when it turned out they had been recruited through a polling company employee's acquaintances rather than by lot.

What I'd like is a careful mapping of this episode onto sortition theory in both directions. One direction: what the theory predicts and explains. The standard argument, going back to claims that election was historically the aristocratic mode of selection while the lot was the democratic one, is that random selection gives you descriptive representativeness without dependence on wealth, rhetorical skill, or the desire to hold office — and that it structurally prevents the emergence of a political class, since nobody can campaign for the seat, nobody keeps it, and nobody's career depends on pleasing anyone. Map each piece onto the Assembly: who effectively did the "selecting" and by what mechanism, what the term limits were and how they differed from the statutory term limits usually debated, what replaced electoral accountability given that members faced no voters, and where the theory genuinely earns its keep — for instance, in explaining why a body that legally could have been ignored still exerted the authority it did, or how the non-random replacement scandal bears on the theory's claim that the legitimacy of a lotteried body rests entirely on the integrity of the lottery.

The other direction: where the theory is silent or misdescribes what happened. Politicians chose the Assembly's agenda and could add or withhold topics; its recommendations were advisory; its deliberations were structured by expert testimony and a judicial chair in ways a body of co-equal citizens might resist describing; members had no constituency, no continuity, and no capacity to follow through on implementation. The classic worries about sortition — incompetence, capture by whoever frames the questions, the absence of accountability — need to be assessed honestly rather than waved away. And then the part I most want to get right: what the episode shows about term limits and career politicians specifically. Term limit arguments usually try to get the ordinary citizen back into politics by evicting incumbents ex post; sortition gets the same population in ex ante and keeps anyone from ever occupying a seat they could treat as a career. Does the Assembly's relative success suggest term limits are a crude proxy for what random selection achieves directly, or does it show the two are after different things — since term limits still select for those who want office, while the Assembly had, by construction, nobody who did? Please be disciplined about what the theory actually says versus what you'd have to admit it doesn't.
