Last Passover my cousin Ruth, who designs typefaces for a living, and my roommate Dev, a first-year cog-sci PhD student, got into a long argument at dinner about what makes a lowercase 'a' an 'a' — like, all of them, across every font and every handwriting, despite the fact that they share basically zero concrete shapes. I mostly watched but it went back and forth enough that they finally asked me to referee, and honestly I couldnt follow the later rounds. Here's the exchange as best as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Ruth started by saying the whole question is confused: you can't give a feature list that all 'a's share. The double-story 'a' in a serif book and the single-story 'a' in a lot of fonts and children's handwriting barely overlap structurally; strip serifs, squash it, rotate it, and it stays an 'a'. So there's no essence, just family resemblance plus the brute fact that readers call it one. (2) Dev said that's too quick — he'd been reading about Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project, which tried to get a computer to render all 26 lowercase letters in new coherent styles, and the interesting bit was that sameness runs in two directions: same letter across wildly different styles, and same style shared across all the letters of a font. Dev said that shows Ruth is looking for the wrong kind of essence: no concrete shape can be shared, but there can be an abstract architecture — something like a set of roles (a bowl-ish part, a stem-ish part, an enclosed counter) whose relations are preserved under all the variation, the way a melody stays the same melody when transposed into any key. (3) Ruth answered that roles are reader-relative, not form-intrinsic: a bowl is only a "bowl" relative to someone already trained on Roman script, and that training is a historical accident — someone literate only in, say, Chinese would carve those same marks up completely differently, so the so-called architecture is downstream of convention, which makes Dev's answer circular. (4) Dev brought up categorical perception: you can show people smoothly morphed letterforms and their perception snaps into discrete categories, with sharp boundaries and poor discrimination *within* a category — which he said demonstrates there's a real structured category in the mind, not just an arbitrary label. (5) Ruth's comeback was that the snapping is learned and plastic: kids famously confuse b and d for years, category boudaries can shift with training, and type designers themselves keep pushing how far an 'a' can drift before readers balk — so the "category" is just wherever a community of readers currently draws the line, a moving target, not a discovery about the letterform itself.

They want a verdict and I promised I'd find someone to give one. Can you adjudicate move by move — who's right about which specific point, whether any of the moves equivocate or talk past each other, and whether some residue of the fight is just verbal (e.g., depending on what "essence" is even supposed to mean here)? And please say if there's something both of them are missing entirely. Ruth bets you'll side with her, Dev bets you won't, so maybe also say which of them ends up more right overall.
