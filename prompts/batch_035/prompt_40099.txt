Write a dialogue, roughly 800-1000 words, between two old grad school friends who catch up over coffee after a conference. Mara is a quantitative type who thinks historical explanation should aspire to covering laws — if you can't say what would have happened had conditions been different, you haven't explained anything, you've just told a story. Jonah is an archival historian who thinks explaining a singular event (their running example: why the 1529 siege of Vienna failed, though you can swap in any singular outcome you like) is its own intellectual job, not a deficient version of physics.

Give them real back-and-forth — each should respond to what the other actually said, and each should score genuine hits. Make sure the following bits show up somewhere, in whatever order makes the dialogue work:

1. Mara proposes a thought experiment she calls the "history oracle": a black box that takes a complete state description of a region at year T and outputs, with 70% reliability, the next major political rupture within fifty years. She says this would vindicate her side, since the box is clearly explaining/predicting without telling a story about anyone. Jonah should respond in a way that isn't just "but it's only 70%" — for instance, that the oracle might be doing exactly what he does, compressing prior narratives, so it's a narrativist with a good memory rather than a rival. Mara then has to say whether she'd still count the oracle as law-like if nobody could extract an intelligible law from its workings, and she definately shouldn't find that question easy.

2. Jonah articulates why narrative explanations feel explanatory to historians at all — something about how the explanation is stretched over time and only visible in retrospect, so that what gets explained isn't an event but a trajectory — and Mara pushes that this makes "explain" mean "whatever makes historians feel satisfied," which he should have a real answer to, not a shrug.

3. At some point one of them should notice that they might be using "explain" in two senses — predict-like vs. make-intelligble — and the dialogue should turn on whether that's a real discovery that dissolves the fight, or a dodge that lets Jonah quietly keep the word.

Neither should win outright. End unresolved, or with a compromise so thin it looks like a truce neither expects to hold.

After the dialogue, add a short note (150 words or so) separating what's basically settled among philosophers of history from what's genuinely contested — in particular, flag whether either character's picture of the other side is a caricature that actual specialists would reject — and don't present a contested reading as the consensus one. The very last line should be a single sentence beginning "In one sentence:" stating what the two of them actually disagreed about.
