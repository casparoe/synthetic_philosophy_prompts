Sooo I'm in a bit of a funny position. I'm a second-year philosophy major taking a seminar on utilitarianism and its critics, and ever since we covered it I've been kind of obsessed with negative utilitarianism, specifically the version with a lexical threshold. The way I understand it from the course: below some threshold of intensity and duration, suffering and happiness can be traded off against each other like normal consequentialism says, but above that threshold, serious suffering can't be compensated by ANY amount of extra happiness — you can't make up for one person's agony with a hundred people's great weekend. Regular total utilitarianism would let a very happy majority outweigh a miserable minority, and the lexical threshold view just refuses to do that arithmetic. I find this super compelling on paper.

The catch is I also sit on my university's student hardship fund commitee, which gives out small emergency grants (usually £100-600 each, roughly £12,000 a year total, we meet weekly and spend maybe 30 minutes per case, three of us including a welfare officer who has to sign off on anything). Right now our method is basically vibes — whoever's story hits us hardest gets the money, which I suspect is exactly the bias-filled intuitional mess the theory I like is supposed to replace. So what I want is for you to turn lexical threshold negative utilitarianism into an actual decision procedure the committee could run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a no, and — this is important to me — where it has to hand the decision back to a human because it's a values question the theory can't settle (like where the threshold actually sits, or whether duration stacks with intensity). Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on three real-ish cases we've had: a student who can't afford an emergency dental procedure and is on painkillers to sleep, a student two months behind on rent facing eviction, and a request to co-fund a wellbeing week with free massages and a puppy petting session. One thing I specifically want you to check: my suspicion is that a strict version of the procedure would say we should basically never fund anything like the wellbeing week, since every pound spent on massages could have gone to hardship, and the rest of the committee is going to hate that answer — does that conclusion really follow from the theory, or is there a principled way to block it that doesn't just smuggle standard utilitarianism back in? Also please say where the procedure misfires in practice, especially the case where two applications are both above the threshold but we can only fund one.
