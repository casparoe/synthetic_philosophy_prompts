I'm taking an introductory course in philosophy of mind, and we recently covered the philosophical zombie thought experiment: a being molecule-for-molecule identical to me, behaving exactly as I do, but with no conscious experience "inside." As I understand the conceivability argument, the fact that we can coherently imagine such a being is supposed to show that consciousness doesn't logically supervene on the physical, which would push us toward property dualism. Setting up the thought experiment this way made me wonder, though, why everyone treats it as a purely hypothetical exercise.

Over the summer I read some popular neuroscience describing conditions like blindsight, in which patients insist they cannot see anything in part of their visual field yet can still point at objects located there with surprising accuracy, as well as sleepwalking, in which people navigate their homes and even cook while later reporting no awareness of doing so. These patients seem to lack the relevant awareness while their outward behavior remains remarkably intact. So my question is this: hasn't neuroscience in effect already discovered philosophical zombies in the clinic, and doesn't that settle the debate in favor of dualism? It seems to me that researchers should stop arguing about whether zombies are conceivable and move on to the more concrete project of identifying the extra, non-physical ingredient that these "zombie-like" patients are missing.

My study partner thinks I'm missing something about how the thought experiment is set up, but she couldn't articulate exactly what, and our teaching assistant just laughed and said we'd get to it later in the semester. Could you explain where my reasoning goes wrong — or, if you think it doesn't, how the clinical evidence I mentioned should reshape the standard framing of the conceivability argument?
