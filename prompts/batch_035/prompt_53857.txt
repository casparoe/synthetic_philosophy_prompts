My best friend and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about whether self-deception is even possible, and by the end I realized I could state her position only as a series of verdicts on examples, never as a principle. I would like your help reconstructing what rule she is implicitly running. Please take the role of a professional philosopher working in philosophy of mind and action theory, think carefully about each verdict before generalizing, and begin your answer with a short brainstorm of candidate principles before committing to any of them. Feel free to connect her rule to familiar positions in the literature, but remember that her verdicts, not the literature, are what you are trying to predict.

The background: she insists self-deception is possible and common; I leaned toward the view that the very idea is confused, since deceiving oneself seems to require being both the deceiver and the deceived. The examples below are ones she ruled on during the argument.

Her verdicts, in the order they came up:

1. The husband who says he has no doubts at all about his wife's fidelity, yet steers conversations away from the topic, never checks the phone bill, and flinches whenever her name comes up in a certain tone — she said: yes, self-deception.
2. The smoker who tells herself she is not addicted and could quit whenever she wanted, and who refuses to keep count of how much she actually smokes — yes.
3. The woman who deliberately leaves the envelope with her genetic test results unopened for two years and says, when asked, that she would simply rather not know — no; my friend called that evasion, not deception of oneself.
4. The founder who daydreams constantly about her startup's success but, asked point blank, says the odds are long — no; fantasy is not belief, my friend said.
5. The man who consciously resolves to believe he loves running, repeats affirmations daily, and eventually does believe it — no; that is deliberate belief cultivation, and nothing is hidden from him.

She left before we reached two further cases, and I want to predict her verdicts on them:

Case A: a man who, in one low moment, admits to a friend that of course he knows the marriage is over — he has even quietly consulted a lawyer — yet in every ordinary context asserts, with what appears to be complete sincerity, that things between them are good, and seems genuinely puzzled when others treat the marriage as troubled.

Case B: a woman with no wish to be ill who, after a twinge in her chest, spends her nights seeking out and crediting the most alarming medical pages, dismisses the reassuring ones as superficial, and has come to sincerely believe she has a serious heart condition despite one clean test after another.

My questions, in order: Which principles fit her first five verdicts equally well? What does each of those principles predict about Case A and Case B? Which prediction do you find most plausible for her, and why? Which further case, if she were to rule on it, would most cleanly separate the candidate principles from one another? And finally: taken together, do her five verdicts and your predicted ones amount to an answer to the question we actually started with — whether self-deception is possible at all, or whether every case dissolves into evasion, fantasy, or ordinary bias once described precisely?
