A friend of mine in cognitive science has been pressing an argument that computationalism — the thesis that the brain is a computer and that cognition is computation — destroys itself, and I can't locate the flaw, though his conclusion seems far too strong. Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it from our conversations:

(1) For a physical system to implement a computation is for there to be a mapping from the system's physical states onto the computation's formal states such that, as the system evolves, the mapped states stand in the relations the computation specifies.
(2) Nothing in this definition requires that the mapping be simple, natural, or otherwise privileged; it need only exist.
(3) Any physical system with a large enough repertoire of states — a wall, a bucket of water, the weather over a continent — will, over a long enough interval, pass through states that can be mapped onto the state-transitions of any finite computation, provided we are free to gerrymander both how we individuate the physical states and how we align them with the computational ones.
(4) So the wall implements the very computation my brain implements, and in fact every computation.
(5) If implementing the right computation suffices for the mental life associated with it, then the wall has that mental life — which no one accepts.
(6) Therefore either computationalism is false, or implementation must be constrained by something more than the bare existence of a mapping.

My friend takes himself to have refuted computationalism outright, but (6) doesn't read like a refutation; it reads like a research question about what the constraints on implementation are. What I want from you is a committed diagnosis, not a survey. First, tell me where the argument actually breaks, and take a definite position on whether it is invalid or sound-if-we-grant-its-premises. If some step equivocates — for instance, sliding between "a mapping exists" and "the system is running the computation" — name the step and lay out the two readings explicitly. If the argument is valid as it stands, then pick the premise you would reject, defend that choice against the obvious alternatives, and tell me what the rejection costs: I understand some philosophers respond by requiring that the mapping respect causal or counterfactual structure among the physical states, and others simply accept that gerrymandered implementations are harmless so long as ordinary attributions of computation use non-gerrymandered ones, and I want to know what your choice commits you to, not what the options are. For my part, I keep wanting to reject (2) and demand that the mapping be natural in some sense, but I worry that naturalness or simplicity is itself relative to how we describe the system, which would just relocate the problem rather than solve it — so I'd like you to tell me whether that worry is fatal, and whether the position you're recommending actually escapes it.
