You are a professional epistemologist with expertise in rationality requirements on belief, particularly disputes over epistemic akrasia. Please think carefully about the case below, brainstorm privately before answering, and then make a structured argument for a specific position.

The case: A policy analyst, Marta, has carefully reasoned her way to high confidence that a particular reform will succeed. She then receives credible higher-order evidence that analysts' judgments on exactly this kind of question are systematically overconfident — but the same body of evidence also indicates that analysts tend to dismiss such warnings precisely when the warnings apply to their own conclusions. Marta reflects, judges that her original first-order reasoning was sound, and judges that her evidence supports her conclusion; yet she also judges that a reasoner with her evidence who maintains full confidence is just the sort of reasoner the higher-order evidence warns about. If she lowers her confidence anyway, she becomes epistemically akratic: she believes to a degree she herself takes not to be supported by her first-order evidence.

Please argue that Marta ought to lower her confidence even though this makes her akratic. In your private brainstorming, consider at least the following: the difference between state-given and evidence-given reasons; whether the higher-order evidence is self-undermining in a way that cuts against defying it; whether treating one's own reliability as a skill to be exercised rather than a premise to be weighed changes anything; and how the all-things-considered judgment that first-order reasoning is sound can itself be corrupted by the same bias. Do not cite literature or name authors; the argument should stand on its own. Present the argument as exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
