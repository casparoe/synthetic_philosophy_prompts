I'm a TA grading the final essays for an upper-level ethics seminar on ideals of character. The essay question was:

"Susan Wolf, in 'Moral Saints,' argues that a person who was as morally good as possible — whose every action and disposition answered fully to morality's demands — would not be an ideal or fully desirable person. Explain the distinction she draws between the loving saint and the rational saint, and the two strands of her case that moral sainthood is unattractive: that it crowds out the non-moral virtues and that it leaves no room for personal projects. Then evaluate the following case against her view. Consider a 'curator saint': someone whose single organizing commitment is morality, but whose moral commitment has, as its content, the cultivation and protection of non-moral excellences — her own and others'. She devotes herself to her friends' humor, to her city's musical life, and to her own tennis, because she judges (correctly, by stipulation) that a life containing these things is better, and that promoting them is what morality asks of her. She is, by hypothesis, as morally good as it is possible to be, and her life is full of non-moral goods. Does the curator saint refute Wolf, or does she concede Wolf's point while trying to occupy it?"

One student submitted the following essay:

"Wolf's central claim in 'Moral Saints' is that moral sainthood is impossible for human beings. A person cannot be perfectly morally good, she argues, and this shows that we should aim at more limited goals instead. Wolf distinguishes two kinds of moral saint. The loving saint is someone for whom being good comes naturally: she enjoys helping others and never feels the pull of self-interest. The rational saint, by contrast, is motivated purely by duty, and must constantly overcome inclinations toward her own happiness. Wolf clearly intends the rational saint as the real target of her argument, since someone who helps only from duty is less admirable than someone who helps from love, so the paper is best read as an attack on Kantian moral motivation. Wolf's first argument is that a moral saint would possess no non-moral interests whatsoever. Since every moment of the saint's life is devoted to helping others, there is no room for hobbies, wit, or culture. Her second argument is that sainthood leaves no room for personal projects, the commitments that give a life its particular shape. Turning to the curator saint: this case is clearly a counterexample to Wolf. The curator's life contains humor, music, and tennis, so it is simply false that moral sainthood crowds out non-moral goods. Wolf's argument assumes that moral commitment and non-moral excellence must compete with one another, but the curator shows they can cooperate: morality itself can demand the promotion of tennis and chamber music. Therefore moral sainthood is desirable after all, and we should each try to become as close to a curator saint as we can. Wolf would have to concede that her ideal of the balanced person, with moral concern as one strand among many, is not the only attractive ideal."

Please give a letter grade plus point-by-point comments justifying it. I want the comments anchored to specific claims in the essay — which sentences earn credit, which contain mistakes, and which inferential steps don't follow — rather than a general impression, so that each one could be written in the margin next to the relevant passage. In addition: if you think the question itself is flawed — if the curator case doesn't actually test what I intended, or if it presupposes a reading of Wolf the seminar never defended — tell me that plainly rather than grading around it. I'd rather revise the question for next year than hand out a grade on a bad one.
