In my department's theory seminar we spent an evening on the free energy principle, and two of the regulars, Marcus and Ilse, went at it for the better part of an hour. Since I couldn't referee it, I'll recount the exchange as it happened and ask you to adjudicate.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the principle is empty: it asserts that any system that persists over time must minimize free energy, but that holds a priori — no observation could ever conflict with it, since any system that failed to minimize free energy wouldn't be around to be observed. So whatever it is, it isn't a scientific theory, and calling it one dresses up a tautology in thermodynamic language.

(2) Ilse replied that this demands the wrong thing of it: the free energy principle isn't a falsifiable theory but a framework, and its job is to spawn process theories — concrete, testable models of perception, learning, and action — which are then judged by experiment. Frameworks, she said, are evaluated on fruitfulness, not falsifiability, the way natural selection was before population genetics made it precise.

(3) Marcus said that concession gives the game away: if the principle itself makes no predictions, then it does no explanatory work — like explaining that opium puts people to sleep because of its dormitive virtue. Whatever gets confirmed gets confirmed because of the process theories, so crediting the principle for their successes is parasitic bookkeeping.

(4) Ilse denied the parasitism charge. The principle, she argued, does real unifying work: it says perception and action are two routes to the same quantity — change beliefs to fit the world, or change the world to fit your beliefs — and it dissolves the classic dark-room objection in passing: nothing that expects to find itself in a stream seeks a dark room, since staying in a dark room would maximize surprise for a fish.

(5) Marcus countered that the dark-room reply just pushes the question back a level. Where does the fish's generative model come from? Selection: creatures whose models didn't encode their niches aren't here. But then the principle's prediction that organisms behave adaptively is guaranteed by the selection argument, so what we have is natural selection re-derived with variational calculus, and everything that looks like new content is inherited content.

(6) Ilse answered that the math isn't decoration. Because one and the same quantity is minimized in perception, learning, and action, measurements in one domain put quantitative constraints on the others — saccade timing, placebo responding, synaptic gain changes — and a framework that licenses cross-domain derivations is earning its keep even if no single experiment could refute the framework as a whole.

(7) Marcus closed with a dilemma: the claim that persisting systems are at non-equilibrium steady state and therefore admit Markov blankets is either trivially true of any self-organizing dynamical system, in which case it carries no distinctive content, or it is a substantive empirical claim, in which case it is not a priori and needs defending — the principle cannot be both a framework-level truism and a discovery.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons, and then say who is more right overall. I'd also like to hear what, if anything, both of them are missing about what would settle the question of whether the free energy principle is an empty formalism or a genuine scientific contribution.
