I work on EU governance policy, and there is a structural feature of the Union's democracy-protection machinery that I keep turning over without settling. The EU treaty lists democracy and the rule of law among its founding values, declares that all member states share them, and provides a two-stage procedure for dealing with a member state that violates them: in the first stage, a large supermajority of the other member states (four fifths of the Council, with the European Parliament's consent) may formally determine that a member state presents a clear risk of a serious breach of those values; in the second stage, suspending the offending state's treaty rights, including its vote in the Council, requires unanimity of the remaining member states. In its entire history, the procedure has been opened against two member states and has never reached the sanctions stage; the Union eventually moved against one of them not through this procedure at all, but by attaching conditions to the disbursement of EU funds. A defender of the government in question always has the same ready rejoinder: the government was elected, often repeatedly, and enjoys the support of a large share of the national electorate, so it is the Union — unelected in the eyes of that electorate, or at least not elected by it — that is acting undemocratically by overriding a national majority.

My question is what philosophical commitments this arrangement presupposes, and whether they are mutually consistent.

As I read it, the practice seems to presuppose at least three things at once, and I can't tell if they can all be held together. First, it presupposes some substantive, non-majoritarian conception of democracy: the whole point of the procedure is that a government can keep winning elections and still count as a threat to the values the Union exists to protect, which suggests the Union is working with a view on which democracy is constituted by things like judicial independence, media pluralism, and constraints on executive power — not merely by whoever wins the vote-count. Second, it presupposes that this conception is one the Union is entitled to enforce against a member state's own constitutional self-understanding — that legitimacy does not stop at the popular sovereignty of the member states. Third, though, the enforcement mechanism itself seems to presuppose the opposite: the procedure requires the unanimous consent of the member states' governments, treating them as sovereign peers whose agreement is the source of the Union's authority to act, rather than as subjects of a supranational standard. The punishment is hostage to the consent of exactly the class of actors — incumbent governments — with the strongest precedent-based reasons not to authorize it.

So: what theory of democratic legitimacy, if any, does this practice actually rest on? Is the unanimity requirement better read as presupposing intergovernmentalism about legitimacy, or merely as a prudential brake that any well-designed constitution might include, in which case the practice is philosophically underdetermined between several views? Can the three commitments I listed be reconciled — for instance, by distinguishing the ground of the Union's authority from the conditions of its exercise — or does the arrangement embody a genuine contradiction, which would explain why the sanctions stage has never been reached? I'd also like your view on the contrast case: what does it say about the Union's implicit theory of its own legitimacy that the effective instrument against a backsliding member turned out to be not the democracy-protection procedure but the spending power — money made conditional rather than rights suspended? And finally, if we replaced the presupposed conception with a straightforward popular-sovereignty view, under which the national electorate's choice is the final word on what counts as democracy there, what exactly would have to change in the arrangement — and would the resulting institution still be doing the work the founders of the procedure wanted it to do?
