I convene a graduate seminar in the philosophy of language, and this term we are working through Hofstadter's chapter on the location of meaning, together with his distinction between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. My co-convenor, Dr Fielding, has firm verdicts about where meaning resides, delivered over several years of seminar discussion, and they appear to me to be consistent — though I have begun to suspect they are consistent with more than one underlying principle. Her record so far, across five cases:

1. The undeciphered Linear A tablets: meaningful. There is a fact of the matter about what the scribes said, whether or not anyone ever recovers it.
2. A sandstone face on which wind erosion has, by sheer chance, produced something that reads as a line of English verse: meaningless, however legible, since nothing with a mind fixed that content.
3. A Victorian spirit-medium's automatic writing: meaningless — the medium's hand moved, but no determinate content was ever meant.
4. A radio signal from an extinct extraterrestrial civilisation, plainly artificial and dense with internal structure and repetition, but undeciphered after fifty years of effort: meaningful. She is untroubled that no one has yet cracked it.
5. A recording of a Bach suite, played to listeners with no exposure whatsoever to Western music: meaningful. Their deafness is our loss, not the record's.

Next week we reach a case I have been saving. A wartime signals officer encodes his final dispatch with a one-time pad; minutes afterwards, his headquarters burns, destroying the pad and every copy of the plaintext. The ciphertext alone survives, in a drawer. The point of a one-time pad is that nothing in the ciphertext's own structure — no frequency, no pattern, no redundancy whatsoever — could ever allow a decoder, however patient or gifted, to extract the dispatch. The only routes to the content were the pad and the plaintext, and both are ash.

Will Dr Fielding judge the surviving ciphertext meaningful or not? Begin your response with your prediction, stated in a single sentence, and then give your reasoning. In particular, I should like you to do four things: identify the principles under which her five verdicts are jointly consistent — I can see at least two candidates, one that locates meaning in what a minded author fixed, and one, closer to Hofstadter, that requires the artefact itself to carry, in its own structure, a route by which some possible decoder could recover the inner message, though there may be others; say what each principle predicts about the ciphertext; give your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it; and propose the further case that would most cleanly tell the principles apart. No preamble and no restatement of the question, please.
