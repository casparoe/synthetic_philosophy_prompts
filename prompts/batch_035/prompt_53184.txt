The exam question was: "Thomson's violinist case and Marquis's future-like-ours argument are standardly taken to aim at different targets. Explain what each argument is trying to establish, and assess whether either argument, if successful, would undermine the other." The course covered both in some detail, including the follow-up discussion of Thomson's Samaritan distinction, so the students had the resources to do better than a summary.

Here's the student's answer, copied as written:

"Thomson's argument in the violinist case is meant to establish that the fetus does not have a right to life. She asks us to imagine waking up attached to a famous violinist who needs the use of your kidneys for nine months. Since you are permitted to unplug yourself, she concludes that a woman is permitted to end a pregnancy, because the fetus, like the violinist, has no right to the use of someone else's body. From this it follows that abortion is morally permissible in all cases, since the right to life never includes a claim on another person's body. Marquis's future-like-ours argument is a direct response to Thomson. He argues that what makes killing wrong is that it deprives the victim of a future like ours, full of experiences, projects, and so on, and a fetus has such a future, so killing it is wrong in the same way killing an innocent adult is. And since Marquis succeeds in showing that abortion deprives the fetus of its future, his argument defeats Thomson's: even if the fetus has no right to the woman's body, depriving it of a future of value is still seriously wrong. So in the end the two arguments don't really engage each other — Thomson's is about rights over one's own body and Marquis's is about what makes killing wrong, and both claims could be true at once."
