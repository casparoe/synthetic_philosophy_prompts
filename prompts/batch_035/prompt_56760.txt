I've spent much of my career as a science and ideas journalist writing about technology, and in covering the debate set off by Clark and Chalmers's old Otto-and-Inga thought experiment, I've come to realize I have firm gut verdicts about particular cases — and that they may not all hang together. For what it's worth, I accept the textbook verdict on Otto: his notebook counts as part of his memory, because he consults it the way Inga consults her biological memory. But here are my own cases. A mathematician I once profiled worked everything out in a battered notebook, and when he lost it, I agreed without hesitation that he had lost part of his memory rather than an aid to it. I say the same about my own interview recordings and transcripts: when I misplace one, it feels less like losing a document than like losing a memory. Yet when I notice I can no longer find my way across my own city without GPS, my gut says the opposite — the app hasn't become part of my mind, it has replaced my mind's spatial competence, and that is dependence, not extension. My newsroom's spell checker, which silently touches every sentence I publish, I don't count as part of my mind at all. And a colleague who drafts with a large language model I count even less, though he consults it as automatically as the mathematician consulted his notebook. I have a tentative principle of my own: a tool is part of my mind when losing it would feel like losing a cognitive capacity rather than losing an aid. The GPS case seems to be trouble for it, since by that test the app plainly qualifies, and yet I deny that it does. So my question is this: is there a principle that captures all five verdicts, or something close, and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: first, state the principle precisely; second, name the case that motivates each clause of it; third, name the verdict of mine it overturns, if any, and why; fourth, apply it to a case I have not considered — a novelist who dictates her chapters as voice memos and revises them only by replaying them while walking, never rereading; and fifth, state the strongest rival principle and why it loses to yours.
