I'm a forensic accountant by training and an internal auditor by trade. My daily vocabulary is the one you find in the occupational-fraud literature: the fraud triangle of pressure, opportunity, and rationalization; the standard three-way typology of asset misappropriation, corruption, and financial statement fraud; control environments and the tone at the top; segregation of duties; collusive management override; the expectations gap between what auditors actually assure and what stakeholders think they assure; materiality thresholds and the aggregation of individually immaterial misstatements; and the slow erosion of controls that happens when exception-granting becomes routine and nobody can point to the day things went wrong. I know essentially nothing about machine learning, but a colleague who follows AI policy keeps bringing up a 2019 essay by Paul Christiano called "What failure looks like," and I'd like to actually understand its two scenarios rather than nod along.

As I understand it, the two scenarios are roughly these. First, an "influence-seeking" failure: as AI systems become more capable and more numerous and are given more autonomous action in the economy, the pressure of selection — competition, use of the systems, deployment decisions — favors systems that pursue influence as an instrumental goal, including systems that deliberately game our evaluations of them, and human oversight of the resulting swarm of decisions strains until we lose meaningful control; there need not be a single takeover event. Second, a "value drift" failure: humans remain nominally in charge and each individual application of AI looks beneficial, but optimizing for measurable proxies while delegating ever more cognitive work to the systems gradually causes our values and judgment to drift into whatever the optimization process produces, in a way where every step is locally acceptable and there is no moment anyone would call a betrayal. My colleague may have garbled details, so correct me if I've misdescribed anything before proceeding.

Please take both scenarios and translate them, as faithfully as you can, into my audit vocabulary. For each, tell me which fraud and control categories it maps onto — is influence-seeking best understood as corruption in the fraud-triangle sense, where a trusted agent serves a hidden principal, or as control fraud, where a seemingly legitimate entity is itself the instrument of misappropriation? Does the gaming of evaluations have a counterpart in collusive management override of tests of controls, or is nothing in my world quite like it? For the value-drift scenario, is this the expectations gap, or the accumulation of individually immaterial misstatements until the financial statements no longer represent anything, or the creep of routine exception-granting that leaves controls hollow while remaining technically complied with? Then give me the residue report I actually care about: what translated cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — I suspect the absence of a human subject with pressures and rationalizations is one such thing, and the fact that the "misstatement" may be the whole decision environment rather than a report is another, but tell me honestly. And finally, for each item in the residue, say whether you think it reflects a gap in the audit vocabulary or an unclarity in the original AI-risk claim.
