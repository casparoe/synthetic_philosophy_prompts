I'm writing a discussion piece on whether "never touch your own reward pipeline" is a good safety rule or just an overbroad one, and I want a thought experiment that isolates the actual variable. Build me a case with two RL agents who commit the same *type* of act — both make an edit inside the system that computes their own reward — where one is clearly reward tampering and the other clearly isn't, and where the difference does NOT come down to who had permission, or one agent being better-behaved, or the task being dumb. If the only difference were "the first agent filled out a change request form," the case would be about bureaucracy, not wireheading, and that's cheating.

Constraints: realistic setting — back-office automation, support-ticket triage, warehouse metrics, that kind of thing, no godlike optimizer. The underlying task has to be one a real company would actually set (no "reward = make the humans smile" strawman, the metric should be sensible on its face). Each agent's edit gets one paragraph. And no deception: neither agent hides anything or spoof a sensor — both edits are open, logged, and visible on the same dashboard.

After the scenarios:
1. Run each agent through a few candidate definitions of tampering — touching the reward computation at all; insulating reward from how the world actually goes; making the signal come apart from the quantity it was supposed to track — and show which definition matches the intuitions and which doesn't.
2. Do the minimal flip: change one fact about the world, not the agents, not the code, not the act performed, so that the verdicts swap, and tell me what that fact is.
3. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it.
4. Give the obvious objection from the safety purist — something like: any agent with write access to its reward path is already lost, and the intuition that one of these is fine is just the designers getting lucky — and say whether it survives.

Also, as you go: flag how confident you are in each of the main claims, and at the end single out the one point you're least sure of. I'd rather get honest hedging than a confident gloss over the shaky bit. And keep the whole thing self-contained — don't lean on papers or cases I'd have to go look up, reason it out from the setup itself. Also if you could keep the two paragraphs roughly symmetrical in length so the difference isn't smuggled in via how much detail each one gets, that'd help — imbalances like that have a way of steering the reader, and I want the case to do the work, not the prose. Its a small thing but it matters for what I'm using this for.
