I'm taking philosophy of religion this semester, and my classmate Maya has basically become my litmus test for every foreknowledge debate we hit. Her track record so far: Judas freely betrayed Jesus, God infallibly foreknew it, and Judas really could have done otherwise — she's adament about that last part, it's why she can't stand our Calvinist classmate's line that God decreed each individual sin. She says Christ's prophecy that Peter would deny him didn't cause the denial; Peter was still fully free even with the prediction on record. She's fine with God actualizing a world he knew would contain the betrayal, and denies this makes God the author of sin — knowledge isn't causation, she keeps insisting. And when our professor sketched the "eternal now" view where God is outside time watching all of history at once, she just shrugged and said it might work but feels like it dodges the question rather than answering it.

Then last week I blindsided her with a case we hadn't covered yet, and I want to predict her answer before I bring it up again. The case: before creating anything, God asks himself — if I put Peter in Jerusalem that night, but the person questioning him in the courtyard is a total stranger who's never seen him before, would Peter still deny me three times? Is there a determinate fact of the matter about that, one God actually knows, and did he know it logically *before* deciding which world to create at all? And the follow-up that's bugging me: if God's knowledge is like that, and he picks this world partly on the basis of those "would" facts, is Peter's actual denial any less free, and is God more implicated in it, then on the eternal-now picture?

So: predict her verdict, figure out what rule(s) she's actually running — I get the sense there are at least two or three that fit everything she's said so far — and say what each one commits her to on my case. And if her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what further case would split the candidate rules apart? I have a feeling there's a nasty follow-up lurking about whether those counterfactual facts, if they exist, could themselves have been false — like, if it was somehow already true "before" creation that Peter would deny in those circumstances, in what sense could he have done otherwise once he's actually in them?
