I am grading midterm exams for an upper-level undergraduate course in metaphysics and would like a careful second read of one answer before I assign it a mark. The exam question was as follows:

"David Lewis argued that the phenomenon of intrinsic change — an object having a property at one time and the incompatible property at another — creates a special problem for endurantism, sometimes called the problem of temporary intrinsics. Explain the argument, present the endurantist reply you find strongest, and assess whether it succeeds. Then explain whether and how perdurantism bears on the puzzle of material coincidence: the statue and the piece of clay that constitute it, which share all their matter but seem to differ in what will happen to them."

The student's answer reads, in full:

"Lewis's argument starts from a simple case: I was bent at noon and straight at midnight. If I endure — if I am wholly present at each time I exist — then one and the same thing has both bentness and straightness, and these are intrinsic properties, properties things have just in themselves, not in virtue of any relation to anything else. Lewis argues the endurantist has only two ways out, and neither works.

The first is to say the properties are had relative to times: I am bent-at-noon and straight-at-midnight. Lewis objects that this makes shape into a relation to a time, which spoils its intrinsicality — having a shape is not standing in a relation to anything. The second is the adverbialist solution, which Lewis himself endorses: the object is not simply bent but bent-ly, it has its shape in a temporally modified way, just as, on some views of perception, we sense redly rather than standing in a relation to a red sense-datum. This preserves the letter of intrinsicness, since the manner of instantiation is not a relation to anything external. But it does so by multiplying manners of having properties, and Lewis concedes it is uneconomical. The natural conclusion is that the argument is really a dilemma forcing us toward perdurantism.

On perdurantism, strictly speaking nothing ordinary is bent; only a momentary stage is bent, and what I call myself at noon is just that stage — the persisting thing is a mere logical construction out of such stages. The stage has its shape simpliciter, so intrinsics are saved, and since an endurantist who refuses this picture must insist that wholly present things exist only now, and so is committed to presentism, perdurantism also fits better with the eternalism that relativity makes natural.

Perdurantism also dissolves the puzzle of the statue and the clay for free. Since the statue and the clay share all their temporal parts, perdurantism entails that they are identical, and what looked like two objects in one place is just one four-dimensional object. The apparent differences between them — that the clay could survive being squashed while the statue could not — are then just differences in how we refer to it, as with Hesperus and Phosphorus."
