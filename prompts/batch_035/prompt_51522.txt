I lead a monthly discussion group at my church — about a dozen adults, most of them without a college background, no philosophy among them except one retired engineer who argues about everything. Next month it's my turn to pick the topic and run the session, and I want to take on something I've been chewing over privately for a couple of years: Augustine's claim that lying is never permitted — not to save a life, not to protect an innocent person, not even, if I've read him right, in jest. What hooked me is a case he considers: if a murderer asks you where his intended victim is hiding, you may refuse to answer, and you may even try to throw him off without saying anything false, but you may not lie. I've slowly come to think the whole position stands on a distinction he draws between lying and concealment — the idea that there is a wide territory of withholding, deflecting, and staying silent that is not lying at all — and that most people who find him monstrous have simply never watched that distinction do its work.

I don't need you to write the session's content for me — I've read enough summaries to know what I think. What I need is a plan for how to explain it. Specifically: first, the order the pieces have to come in — what has to be established before the murderer case can even be introduced without the room erupting, and why that order and not another; second, the single example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must not have; third, the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence each one needs to be headed off. I can predict some of them myself: that "never lie" is just ordinary religious teaching and Augustine is stating the obvious; that a lie simply means saying something false, so jokes and honest mistakes would count as lies; and the opposite worry, that once concealment is allowed the position quietly collapses into "deceive whenever you like, just pick your words carefully." There are surely others I'm not seeing, and I'd like to know where exactly in the arc each belongs. Fourth, one question I can put to the group at the end that would show me whether they've actually grasped the distinction rather than merely been shocked or soothed into agreement. And fifth, what I should openly decline to get into — I expect the modern arguments about white lies to children and doctors softening diagnoses to come up fast, and I have opinions of my own, but I would rather know what to table for another month than how to settle those arguments on the spot.

One constraint: the session is an hour, in the parish hall, no slides and no whiteboard I can count on — just talk and at most one photocopied page. Please write the plan in American English.
