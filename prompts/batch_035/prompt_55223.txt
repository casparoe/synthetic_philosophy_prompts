I'm putting together a one-page position statement on whether we can acquire moral knowledge from other people for a reading group on testimony, and before I submit it I'd like someone to audit it for consistency, because I've convinced myself over several drafts that all of these are true and I can now no longer tell which ones are doing the work. Here is the set:

(a) In general, a reliable informant's say-so is enough for knowledge: when a trustworthy friend tells me the pharmacy closes at seven, I know it closes at seven without seeing any of the evidence myself. (b) Moral testimony is somehow different: if I ask a friend whether it's wrong to skip my grandmother's ninetieth birthday for a concert, and she says it's wrong, and I simply believe her and act accordingly, something has gone wrong — even if she is completely right and even if I get things right more often this way than by reasoning it out myself. (c) My explanation for this is that moral knowledge requires moral understanding: you have to grasp the reasons yourself, and while testimony can hand you the conclusion, it cannot hand you the grasp. (d) Even so, I'm confident that young children genuinely know that hitting their siblings is wrong, and that this knowledge comes almost entirely on their parents' say-so, long before they can articulate any reasons. (e) Meanwhile I believe Gödel proved that arithmetic is incomplete purely on the authority of mathematicians, having never worked through the proof, and I don't feel that this belief is defective in the way the birthday case is. (f) And finally, when it comes to genuinely messy situations — how to handle a relative's addiction, say — I would defer to my grandmother's judgment over my own without hesitation, and I don't regard that deference as defective in the way (b) suggests, even though I'm taking her verdict on trust.

Please go through the pairs and tell me which are in genuine tension, which merely look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, tell me what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set. And if there's a single revision that would clean up the most, I'd like to know what it is — I honestly can't tell whether the problem starts with (c), or whether (c) is fine and the trouble is that (b) was never really an epistemic complaint in the first place.
