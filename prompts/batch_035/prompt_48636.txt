I am a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years of fitting kinetic models to spectroscopic data — who has lately taken up philosophy of probability in retirement, mostly to find out whether the philosophers believe the things my colleagues and I quietly did at the bench without thinking about them. One puzzle has been needling me: I gather it is known as the Judy Benjamin problem, after the soldier in the original example, and that it is usually taken to show that plain Bayesian conditioning, and even Jeffrey's generalisation of it, cannot handle a certain ordinary sort of evidence. I should like your considered credence on it, and I want the reasoning laid out, not just a number.

The setup, as I'll fix it. A soldier on night manoeuvres becomes lost in a region divided into two territories: friendly Blue territory, two thirds of the region, and enemy Red territory, one third. Red territory is itself split evenly between a headquarters sector and a rear-support sector. Being lost, her credences presumably follow the areas: two thirds for Blue, one sixth each for the two Red sectors. She then finds an official sign, which she has every reason to trust, telling her in effect that if she is in Red territory, the odds are three to one that she is in the headquarters sector rather than the support sector. Nothing on the sign bears on Blue versus Red as such. The question I want adjudicated: what should her credence in friendly Blue territory be after reading the sign?

As I understand the best-known answer, one demands that her belief state change as little as possible, in the sense of relative entropy, from her prior, subject to the constraint the sign imposes — and on the numbers above this leaves her credence in Blue territory at roughly 0.695, up from two thirds. The sign told her something exclusively about enemy territory, and it made her more confident she is in the region it said nothing about. In spectroscopy we used minimum cross-entropy as a tool for reconstructing spectra from noisy data, and a fine tool it was; but I never mistook our tools for canons of rationality, and I never saw the method push probability towards a cell it had no reason to touch.

Here is how I should like the answer set out, please, in American English.

1. Bottom line (roughly 75 words). Your credence — an actual number, no hedging — that the soldier's credence in Blue territory should remain exactly two thirds after reading the sign, which is where my own suspicion lies.
2. The decomposition (roughly 300 words). Set out the sub-questions your number rests on, with a rough probability for each. I would expect at least these: what exactly the sign constrains — does it fix only her conditional credence that she is in the headquarters sector given that she is in Red territory, or does it also bear on how probable Red territory itself is? Then, given the constraint, whether minimum relative entropy is a requirement of rationality that uniquely determines the posterior, or merely one useful convention among several; and whether the rise in Blue credence is a genuine defect of the rule or a counterintuitive consequence we ought to swallow.
3. Combining the terms (roughly 150 words). Show me how the sub-question probabilities combine into your bottom-line number, and say which sub-question the final figure is most sensitive to.
4. What would move me (roughly 150 words). Tell me what argument or consideration would most shift your number, and in which direction. Please reason it through yourself rather than deferring to whichever resolution the literature has settled on — if it has settled anything.
