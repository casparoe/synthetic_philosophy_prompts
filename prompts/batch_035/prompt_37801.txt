I've spent the semester in a philosophy of mind reading group where we keep circling self-deception without landing anywhere, and I've realized part of the problem is that we've been arguing about theories before settling what a theory of self-deception is a theory *for*. The candidates on the table divide cleanly: the intentionist line, where self-deception mirrors interpersonal deception with the agent somehow playing both roles; the divided-mind line, where the self is partitioned and one part keeps things from another; and the deflationary line, where the phenomenon is just motivationally biased belief formation, with no intention, no partitions, and no need for anyone to believe both p and not-p. My own anchor case, if you want one, is a colleague who is certain her marriage is solid while cataloguing, for her friends, evidence that would convince her instantly in anyone else's case; whether that certainty is self-deception, mere wishful bias, or something else seems to me exactly what the spec should decide, not presuppose.

So before I read another word of this literature, I want the desiderata: what any adequate theory of self-deception has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Some candidate requirements I'd want you to evaluate rather than assume, since I suspect a few are genuine and a few are one camp's prejudices smuggled in as standards: the theory must distinguish self-deception from its nearest neighbors (ordinary error, wishful thinking, akrasia, confabulation, and therapeutic forgetting) rather than collapsing them; it must say something principled about the static puzzle (does the self-deceived person somehow hold both p and not-p, and if not, what explains the contrast with lying?) and the dynamic one (how could a plan to deceive oneself succeed when knowing of the plan would seem to defeat it); it should account for the signature texture of the phenomenon — evidence gathered selectively, the same data weighed differently for oneself than for others, the belief conveniently tending toward what spares the believer distress, and the way the belief is *maintained* over months through habits of attention rather than formed in one act; it must be consistent with what the experiments show (choice blindness, split-brain confabulation, dissonance reduction, motivated reasoning) without over-reading them as direct observations of self-deception; and, a requirement I keep adding and doubting, it should mark off self-deception proper from cases where the believer merely signs on to a comforting story someone else engineered, like accepting flattery one knows was designed to flatter. Tell me which of these are real requirements, which are negotiable or optional, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether demanding that the agent be somehow responsible or culpable can be met by a fully non-intentional process, and if not, whether that unsatisfiability is itself the result of the field — and which single requirement does the most work in eliminating contenders. Then, and only briefly, run the intentionist, divided-mind, and deflationary pictures against the list, since I care much more about whether the standard is right than about who passes it.

One request about form: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no numbered lists — just continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter. The requirements and their motivations should be clear from the sentences themselves.
