I've been landed with a 45-minute session at our local philosophy evening class on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness. The audience is sharp adults, mostly retired professionals, who've done a year of informal philosophy reading but no neuroscience at all, and they will all arrive carrying the pop version they half-remember: cut the bundle connecting the hemispheres, and out come two people. I don't want a script for the session — I want a plan for it, and I want your help thinking through the structure.

The angle I want to bring, and you should tell me if you think it's a mistake, is that the interesting question isn't "one mind or two?" but what "unity of consciousness" was even supposed to mean before the surgeons got involved. My hunch is that people run together at least three different things: (a) a single stream of experience, (b) a single point of view or field in which things show up, and (c) the capacity of the whole system to integrate information for report and deliberate action. My guess is the surgery only ever disturbs the third, and that the cases are philosophically valuable precisely because it's so hard to tell, case by case, which of the three the experiment is actually probing. What I care about is that by the end the audience can apply that distinction to the material themselves, not recite it back at me.

Concretely, I'd like you to cover:

- The order things have to go in. Something has to be in place before the cases can be presented as evidence rather than as a circus act, and I suspect that something is the distinction above — but check whether that's right, and if so whether it needs to come fully formed at the start or can be built up alongside the cases.

- The one experiment to build the session around. My candidates are the chicken-claw/snow-scene one, where the patient matches a chicken claw and a snow scene, picks a shovel for the snow, and the speaking hemisphere explains the shovel as for cleaning out the chicken shed; and the anecdote where a patient starts wandering across the room and, asked why, says he's going to get a Coke, the command having been shown to the other hemisphere. I lean towards the Coke one because the confabulation is already inside it, but it may be too loose to carry the weight. Advise me, and say what the winning example must show and what it must not be allowed to suggest.

- The misconceptions, and where in the sequence to head each off: that the surgery proved there were two of them all along; that the right hemisphere is the "silent self" or the real one; that the left hemisphere's confabulating shows the self is just a liar; and all the lateralisation folklore (creative right brain, logical left brain). Also, one of the regulars is a retired doctor who will insist that since the corpus callosum is what makes anyone one person, cutting it must by definition split the mind — I want a better response to him than "no it doesn't."

- The bit most pop accounts omit, which I think is load-bearing: the dramatic dissociations essentially only show up under tight lab conditions — brief lateralised presentation, fixed gaze — because in ordinary life the patients move their eyes and heads, so both hemispheres mostly receive the same input, and patients typically report feeling no different after the operation. If the split only becomes visible when the experimenter holds the world still, what exactly has been shown about consciousness, and what about the experiment? I'd like this woven in rather than tacked on the end, but tell me where it fits best.

- One closing question I could put to them that would reveal whether they've actually grasped the difference between access and experience, rather than just enjoyed the freak show.

And two honesty checks: first, say plainly what I should refuse to get into at this level — I'm not teaching the neuroanatomy of the visual pathways beyond the bare minimum, and I suspect I should also refuse to settle whether the patient is two persons, though tell me if you think declining that is a cop-out rather than good judgement. Second, tell me if any part of my framing above is wrong or unstable — I'd much rather you corrected me now than after.
