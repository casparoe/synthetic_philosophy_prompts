My oldest friend, Maya, has been ruling on hypothetical promise cases since we were graduate students together; she treats them the way other people treat crossword puzzles. Her track record, in the order she decided them:

1. A promise extracted at gunpoint (drive the getaway car or else): not binding.
2. A promise made during a blackout caused by a drug interaction — sincere in tone, entirely unremembered, and the promisee had no reason to suspect anything was off: not binding.
3. A promise made because the promisee fabricated an emergency that didn't exist: not binding.
4. A promise to look after a friend's cat while the friend traveled, later expressly released — the friend said, in front of witnesses, that she was free of it and shouldn't feel bound at all, while still quietly hoping she'd do it: not binding.
5. A promise made to her father, while he was dying and fully lucid, to keep the family house in the family — performed years after his death: binding.
6. A vow spoken at the graveside, addressed to her already-dead father, no promise ever having been made while he was alive: not binding.
7. A promise to a four-year-old to attend the school play — the child too young to grasp the very idea of promising, but old enough to wait by the window all afternoon: binding.
8. A silent, never-communicated vow to pay for a friend's surgery, existing only in her own head and her diary: not binding.

Next month's case: a field linguist who has spent years with an isolated community that has no institution of promising — no vows, no oaths, no concept of a binding commitment. Their nearest practice is announcing one's intentions, which they treat as forecasts that may or may not come true. In their language she tells them, truthfully and with full seriousness, that she will bring medicine next month. They understand her as forecasting her own behavior, weigh the forecast accordingly, and stake a great deal on it. Maya will be asked whether the commitment binds.

I want a careful extrapolation from her record, not a survey of the literature:

- Which accounts of why promises bind actually survive all eight verdicts — assurance or expectation-based views, social-practice views, consent- or will-based views, and anything else worth taking seriously — and which are already eliminated by a specific ruling (say which ruling kills which). Note the variants each survivor needs to absorb cases like the dead father or the four-year-old.
- What each surviving rule predicts for the medicine case, and whether any rule is forced into an uncomfortable distinction between a promise's bindingness and some nearby ground of obligation (like a duty to make good on induced reliance).
- Your best guess at Maya's verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her: which of the eight rulings does the most work, and is any of them close enough to tension with your guess that you'd want to ask her about it first?
- The single further case — other than the medicine case itself — whose verdict would best divide the surviving rules, and what each rule would say about it.

End with a final line, exactly of the form \answer{BINDING} or \answer{NOT BINDING}, giving your prediction for the medicine case.
