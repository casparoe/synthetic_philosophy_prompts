my day job is supply chain analytics, so I spend my life around linear programming solvers, and last month at a family thing my cousin (very online libertarian) and my housemate (Marxist, has actually read Capital) got into it about whether socialist planning could ever work, and I've been in the weeds of the old socialist calculation debate ever since. Here's what's bugging me: the actual exchange between Mises, Lange, and Hayek has been sitting there basically unchanged since the 1930s-40s, every move on both sides is known, and yet people who clearly know the material still can't agree even about what the disagreement is about. Mises says no rational calculation without genuine market prices for capital goods; Lange comes back with the trial-and-error planning board that basically simulates the market; Hayek then says everyone's been answering the wrong question because the real issue is dispersed, tacit, constantly changing knowledge that no board could ever collect, and Lange (as I understand it) more or less waves this off as an engineering problem of computation. And now every couple years someone declares computers have settled it, and someone else says that completely misses the point, and we're back where we started.

My working theory, offered as just a theory, is that at least three different questions have been running under one heading: (1) whether calculation is possible in principle, which sounds like a formal question about whether an economy can even be specified as a solvable system; (2) whether the information could ever be gathered and kept current, which looks empirical but keeps sliding into something about what knowledge even is; and (3) whether it's all really an incentives problem in disguise, about whether anyone would actually bother to report their local conditions honestly to a planner. When my cousin argues he's answering (3) while quoting stuff aimed at (1), and when my housemate cites the "computers solved it" stuff he's answering (1) against a claim that was maybe never about (1) at all. But I don't trust my own reading of the literature enough to know whether this three-way split is fair, or whether I've invented a clean distinction the actual debate doesn't respect.

So what I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this stalled debate rather than a verdict on who was right: is the core disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of the sides answering different questions under one name? Which of my three questions is really doing the work, are some of them not genuinely separable, and is there a hidden empirical question (about computation, about how firms actually work, about how much tacit knowledge matters) buried in there? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here -- a distinction, a model, some result -- or is this one of those debates that can't be moved by anything and if so why? One more ask: please keep straight for me which parts of this are actually settled among people who know the topic and which are still genuinely contested, because I keep getting confident-sounding answers from both of them that I suspect are passing off contested claims as consensus.
