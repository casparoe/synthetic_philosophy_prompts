For the final session of my early modern philosophy seminar — a unit that has just worked through Leibniz on theodicy, the principle of sufficient reason, compossibility, pre-established harmony, and the predicate-in-subject doctrine — I run a satire workshop where students pair Voltaire's response to optimism with modern genre parodies. This year I want to hand out a worked example of satire whose humor comes from getting the Leibniz right rather than name-dropping him, and I'd like a draft I can annotate with the class.

Write a corporate press release, headline and subheadline plus roughly 550 words, announcing version 2.0 of Theodicy, a project-scheduling suite from a startup called Monad Labs. The release straight-facedly announces that the new optimizer has found the best of all possible sprints, and every joke should be a real feature of Leibniz's system carried into the corporate register: a changelog entry rejecting a feature request on the grounds that the requested feature, while good in itself, fails compossibility with the actual world; telemetry that quietly harvests minute perceptions the users never notice having; integration handled by pre-established harmony, so that frontend and backend never communicate and yet always ship in sync; a security policy explaining that monads are windowless and therefore cannot leak data; an HR paragraph invoking the distinction between reasons that necessitate and reasons that merely incline to explain why the algorithm did not have to lay off the QA team but did anyway; a bug taxonomy dividing defects into metaphysical, physical, and moral evil, with metaphysical evil shipped as intended since finitude is not patchable; and a developer-concept clause on the model of the complete individual concept, under which any missed deadline is revealed to have been contained in the junior developer's concept from eternity, making retrospectives purely commemorative. Include at least one quote from a concerned outside reviewer — a freelance Spinozist consultant who grumbles that a schedule following with geometric necessity from the nature of reality needs no optimizer and that the company is charging a subscription fee for the order of things — and a closing quote from the CEO responding to a disgruntled former contractor who was dismissed for circulating a satirical poem about an earthquake, with the CEO patiently explaining that a tiny evil harmonizing into a greater good is the whole business model.

The irony should stay deadpan and legible: a reader who has never touched the Leibniz material should still find it a plausible, slightly sinister press release, while a specialist should laugh rather than wince at any of the machinery. Don't name any real philosopher or cite any real work — the Spinozist, the pamphleteer, and the CEO are invented characters, and the philosophy should arrive only through the product's own vocabulary. End on a beat that leaves the reader with the genuine problem rather than a punchline: something like a customer complaint being logged as already composible with the best of all possible worlds, so that the reader senses how the doctrine threatens to absorb every objection as evidence for itself — the question of whether the best-of-all-possible-worlds claim is refutable at all, or whether it was only ever optimizing for its own defense.
