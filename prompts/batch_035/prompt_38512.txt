I've been writing up my views on logical omniscience — the old puzzle that if knowledge or belief is closed under logical consequence, then anyone who knows the axioms of arithmetic knows every theorem of arithmetic, which no finite agent does — and before I circulate the draft I'd like a consistency audit of the package I've arrived at. Here are the commitments as I'd state them:

(1) Epistemic closure, in one specific form: if S knows p, and S knows that p entails q, then S knows q. I want this without any rider requiring that S has actually performed the derivation; riders of that sort strike me as gutting the principle, since they make knowing q a matter of grinding through a proof rather than of what follows from what S already has. Note that I have deliberately built the "knows that p entails q" clause into the principle.

(2) Competent adults genuinely know the axioms of arithmetic and the basic principles of first-order logic — full-strength, explicit knowledge, not some attenuated "implicit" or "core" sense of knowing.

(3) Knowledge is not dispositional. When someone says an agent "implicitly knows" a consequence of what she knows, that is at best a prediction that she could come to know it, not the attribution of a state she is already in; being able to come to know q is not already knowing q.

(4) Real agents' beliefs are not closed under logical consequence, and this failure is not a benign idealization of the frictionless-plane kind. When the phenomenon under study is bounded reasoning itself, a model that builds in closure has idealized away the very thing it was supposed to illuminate, so I'm suspicious of buying omniscience back as harmless modeling shorthand.

(5) The Hintikka-style possible-worlds semantics for knowledge and belief is basically right — the right starting point for epistemic logic, which I take to be the right general framework for reasoning about agents, their information, and their protocols.

(6) A case verdict I find myself firmly holding: a student knows the axioms, has never once thought about a certain forty-line theorem, and would produce a valid proof of it if she set to work for an afternoon. I want to say she knew the theorem before she proved it, and that proving it is how she finds out that she knew it — not how she comes to know it.

(7) Nobody currently knows whether Goldbach's conjecture is true, including the many competent people who know the axioms and could follow any given proof of it, or of its negation, line by line.

Please tell me which subsets of these stand in genuine tension, which pairs merely look as though they conflict and why the conflict dissolves on inspection, and which of my two "modeling" commitments, (4) and (5), can be jointly kept and at what price. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging; for each genuine one, give the cheapest repair and what that repair costs elsewhere in the package; and identify the single revision that would clean up the most overall. I should add that I honestly can't locate the trouble: the textbook derivation of omniscience from closure feels like it has to be the culprit, but every time I try to run it I find I need an extra premise I'm not sure I hold, and yet the package still strikes me as unstable.
