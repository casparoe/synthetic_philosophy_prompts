My cousin is an engineer on a team designing a spinning space habitat — the kind where you rotate the whole station so the crew gets pressed against the outer wall, fake gravity via centrifugal effect. She was staying with me last week, and her and my housemate Ren got into a proper argument about what the spinning is *relative to*, and it got heated enough that they made me promise to "look into it." I'm out of my depth, so I need you.

Before the argument, a tiny bit of background on Newton's bucket, since it came up: Newton noted that if you hang a bucket of water and spin the bucket, at first the water stays flat and only the bucket moves, but gradually the water starts rotating too and climbs up the sides into a concave shape. The fun part is the very beginning, when the bucket is spinning fastest relative to the water and the water surface is still flat, and later, when water and bucket are momentarily at rest relative to each other and the surface is maximally curved. So the curvature tracks rotation relative to something that isn't the bucket.

Here's the exchange as best as I remember it:

(1) Ren said this is the classic argument that rotation is relative to absolute space itself, not to other objects. The water curves even when there's no relative motion to point to, so there has to be some background thing the rotation is relative to, and space is that thing. He said this is why it's obvious the habitat's spin works even though "nothing around it moves."

(2) My cousin said that's way too fast — the water is rotating relative to the lab, the Earth, the distant stars, etc., and that's plenty. She added Leibniz's point: if you imagine shifting the entire universe ten feet to the left in absolute space, nothing would be any different, so absolute space does no work and isn't really a thing.

(3) Ren said shift arguments only cover uniform motion, and that's exactly the distinction the bucket draws — velocity is relative, but rotation/accelation is not, so Leibniz's argument misses the target. He then asked: suppose the habitat were the only object in the entire universe. Would the crew still get pressed to the wall when it spins? He said obviously yes, which means rotation is relative to space itself, not to stars.

(4) My cousin brought up Mach, who apparently said inertia itself (including centrifugal effects) is caused by the mass of the distant stars, so no stars, no wall-pressing, and the empty-universe question is rigged. She also said she'd read that even Einstein noted you could rotate the whole rest of the universe around a stationary bucket and get the same curved water surface, which vindicates the relationist, and that general relativity was built partly to capture this.

(5) Ren replied that GR famously did NOT come out Machian — the metric field is a real dynamical thing that exists even where there's no matter, and gravitational waves ripple through empty space carrying energy. So there is spacetime structure over and above relations between bodies, and Newton was basically right with a small edit: replace absolute space with the metric field. He said that means my cousin's empty-universe intuition is wrong and the crew does get pressed to the wall.

My cousin's closing move was that for the actual engineering none of this matters — spin relative to the stars, spin relative to the local metric, same numbers on the spec sheet — and that worrying about empty universes is physicists' fan fiction. Ren said it matters exactly there, because it decides whether artificial gravity is a fact about the habitat or a fact about the universe it sits in.

Can you adjudicate this? Please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a little first about what each side is actually committed to before you score anything. Then go move by move through (1)-(5) and say who's right about what, including where someone is right for sloppy reasons or wrong while gesturing at something true. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And finally, give me your honest verdict on the empty-universe habitat question: alone in the cosmos, does the crew stick to the wall or not — or is the question somehow broken, and if so, in whose favor does that break? You're a philosopher of physics who also knows enough GR to be dangerous. Take a side where you need to; don't just split the difference.
