I returned to graduate school in economics after several years in product analytics at a large e-commerce marketplace, and a design decision my old team argued about has followed me into my dissertation. On our platform, buyers could see how many previous buyers had chosen each option but never why; I watched a mediocre product snowball on the strength of its early sales while a better one stalled, and I have come to suspect we were running a live information-cascade experiment. My half-formed idea is this: in the canonical herding models, cascades form because agents observe predecessors' actions but not their private signals, which suggests the phenomenon depends on a coarse communication channel rather than on any deep fact about rational inference. If so, it should be possible to say precisely how much richer the channel has to become — action alone, then action plus a stated confidence level, then a stated reason, then the raw signal — before rational herding collapses. And conversely, if disclosure is costly or voluntary, early movers may rationally reveal only their action, precisely so that followers herd behind them: richer channels get built and then go unused.
