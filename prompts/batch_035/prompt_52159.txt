I've been hanging around AI safety-adjacent spaces for a couple of years now, and the argument about corrigibility — whether you can actually build an AI that lets you shut it down or correct it — has this weird quality where everyone has had the same positions since roughly 2015 and nobody budges an inch. On one side you've got the theory people: an agent that models the world at all will model its own shutdown as an event with consequences, and if it cares about literally anything, avoiding shutdown becomes instrumentally useful. They point at the old formal attempts to get around this, like adding a correction term to the utility function so the agent is indifferent to being switched off, and how those attempts broke in embarrassing ways — the agent either pays to manipulate situations where correction happens cheaply or pays to prevent them, and a reflectively stable agent would just edit the correction term out of itself given the chance. Their conclusion: corrigibility runs against the grain of agency itself, so don't count on it, make sure the goals are right before the system is capable.

Then the other side, who I think of as the empiricists, just look around at actual models: ones trained with human feedback seem pretty happy to take corrections, apologize, accept being overridden, and none has ever shown any interest in avoiding being turned off. On their view corrigibility is just a behavioral disposition you select for, like house-training a dog, and the theory people are arguing about an idealized expected-utility-maximizer cartoon that no real training process is going to produce — agency in the maximizing sense is something you'd have to deliberately build in, not something that shows up by default. Every time this comes up the two sides talk past each other and the thread dies with nothing settled, and I genuinely can't tell anymore whether there's a real disagreement here or something structural.

My own working hypothesis, take it or leave it, is that the crux is about what training actually produces: whether sufficiently capable models trained the way we train things end up approximating the idealized agent (in which case the theory arguments bite) or whether goal-directedness at that level of cleanliness is a modeling choice humans impose, not a natural kind (in which case the empiricists are right that the arguments are about systems nobody is building). But I want a real diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one where the two sides mean different things by corrigibility (not resisting shutdown vs. wanting to be corrected vs. being indifferent), a hidden empirical question that current evidence just can't touch, or bedrock priors about what agency is that neither side can argue for? Where does the disagreement actually live, what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a training experiment, a conceptual distinction nobody has made yet — and honestly, could anything settle it, or is this one of those debates that stays open forever for a reason?
