I teach a practical course in animal behavior, and a pattern has been nagging at me all term — partly from watching the rats and partly from watching my students. In evolution, the experimenting happens out in the open and the bill is paid in bodies: a lineage only finds out that a shell is too heavy in the currency of the individuals who carried it and left fewer offspring. A rat in one of my students' mazes finds the dead end the ordinary way, by running into it — the same individual now generates the variation and absorbs the cost, so the trial happens within a lifetime instead of across generations, but the mistake is still a real one, paid in time and effort. Yet at the choice point my rats do something else: they pause, sway, and sample one arm with their heads before committing their feet, and I honestly can't tell whose currency that smaller mistake is paid in. And then there are my students and my chess-playing brother. He would sooner resign than touch a piece before playing it, because his errors happen on a board in his head, where being wrong costs a few seconds; and my best students, when a problem resists thinking, don't think harder — they reach for a spreadsheet or a quick plot, and the notation seems to do part of the testing for them.

As best I can state it, the pattern is this: it is the same loop every time — produce variants, let some fail, keep the survivors — but the place where the failure happens migrates. First out in the world, where being wrong is fatal; then inside the animal, where it is expensive but survivable; then in imagination, where it is nearly free; and finally out again, into instruments and formalisms designed by other people, which return verdicts on our ideas without our risking anything of our own. A friend with a philosophy background said this reminded her of Popper, who somewhere remarks to the effect that science is precious because our conjectures can be shot down in our place. But that remark, as I understand it, is about one rung only: Popper wasn't talking about gene pools or about checklists and wind tunnels, and I don't get the impression he saw the whole gradient as a single thing with a history.

So my questions: is there an established name or literature for this migration of the cost of error, or for the ladder of creatures at different points along it? I'm not after a bare label. I want to know whether the field treats the rungs as genuinely cumulative (does the chess player still run the rat's mechanisms underneath, or has something replaced them?), whether the top rung — the one with the tools — is supposed to be a difference of degree or of kind, and where the edges are: my rats pausing at the choice point sit awkwardly between rungs, and I can't decide whether a pilot running a checklist is doing the same thing as my brother visualizing a move or something importantly different. If no settled concept covers it, I'd take a well-drawn one: a name and a definition that say what belongs on the ladder, what doesn't, and why.
