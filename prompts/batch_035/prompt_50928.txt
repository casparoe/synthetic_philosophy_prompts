I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of probability — about eighteen students, one 75-minute session, no prerequisites beyond one semester of logic. We're coming to the St. Petersburg paradox, and I don't want them to meet it as a bullet point on the standard list of embarrassments for expected-value maximization; I want them to feel the gap between what the mathematics licenses and what they will actually pay. Design an in-class game in which students play repeated St. Petersburg lotteries with real (token) payouts governed by a capped house bankroll — payouts doubling with each consecutive heads, but the game ending when the house's fund is exhausted — followed by a second phase in which the right to play a fresh lottery is auctioned off in pairs, so that willingness-to-pay is revealed by what students bid rather than by what they say when asked hypothetically. If you can also work in a correlated variant — two lotteries sharing the same sequence of coin flips, so the class can see for themselves why pooling correlated plays does nothing to tame the tail — I would want that too; a colleague swears by it, but I have never seen it run and don't know whether it is more trouble than it is worth.

Two constraints. First, no real money: the payoff scheme should use tokens convertible at the end of term into something trivial (candy, a rounding courtesy on participation, bragging rights), and the debrief should address head-on whether the smallness of the stakes undermines the lesson — specifically, whether the students' caution reflects risk aversion proper or mere indifference to pennies, and how a facilitator could tell the two apart from the bidding data. Second, the point is not for students to "discover" any particular resolution — bounded utility, diminishing marginal returns, appeals to the finitude of any actual bankroll, or a flat refusal to grant the expectation normative force — but for them to run into the clash before it is named, and then for the debrief to map specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the formal setup and onto the standard lines of response.

Give me the design as exactly five numbered items — the setup and materials; the rules of the base game including the cap and how the coin flips are administered; the auction phase and how bids are recorded; the debrief, mapping moments in the activity onto components of the paradox and its candidate resolutions; and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with how to handle each failure — each item a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. I have been told that activity writeups longer than about three hundred words never get read by anyone but their author, so keep each item tight even where it hurts.
