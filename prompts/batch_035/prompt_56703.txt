Something I keep chewing on: in pretty much every american city, when a developer wants to put apartments on a lot, there's a public hearing, and the only people with a seat at the table are the ones who already live nearby. The people who would live in the building — future tenants, folks currently priced out of the city — get no hearing at all. They dont exist yet, as far as the process is concerned. And yet the same country treats freedom of movement as a basic right: anyone can move to the city whenever they want, no permission needed... but only if housing exists to move into, and whether that housing exists is decided by the neighbors. So the right to arrive is unconditional while the right to somewhere to arrive *to* is subject to neighbor veto.

What theory of who has a right to the city does this setup actually presuppose? Is it something like a club model — current residents are members, newcomers are applicants, and admission is up to the membership? Or a stakeholder model, where neighbors get standing because they bear concentrated, visible costs (traffic, shadows, noise) while the would-be residents' benefits are diffuse and hypothetical? Or maybe something else entirely — reconstruct whatever implicit view makes the whole thing coherent, if any does.

Some sub-questions:

1. Distinguish what the hearing process *strictly* presupposes from what it's merely compatible with. Could several rival theories of urban political standing all license the same hearing format, or does the format actually pin down a view?

2. Here's the part that puzzles me. The same legal systems have fair housing rules saying neighbors can't block a building because of *who* would live there, but they're fine with neighbors blocking density itself. So you can veto a building because it's four stories, but not because it's for poor people — even though the practical effect can be identical. Is there a principled line there, or is that distinction doing suspicious work? What view of discrimination does the line presuppose, if any?

3. Suppose you swapped the implicit theory for an open-city view — everyone affected by a decision about whether they can live somewhere gets standing in it. What would have to change in practice, and how would you even operationalize it? The future residents are unknown, don't know they'd be affected, and arguably don't exist yet. Is that a real obstacle or an excuse?

4. Is it consistent with itself? The same cities also give renters a full vote in elections and pass anti-displacement protections, which seems to presuppose that residence, not ownership, grounds standing — yet the hearing process in practice weights the people who show up, and the people who show up are the ones with time on weekday afternoons and a stake in their property values. Maybe the practice doesn't have one implicit theory at all but several that pull in different directions.

Be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, and steelman the defender who says this is all just externalities management — that neighbors aren't gatekeepers of the city, they're just the people who'd be hit by the costs, and the process is an (imperfect) way of making them whole, not a membership vote. If that's right, what would the process look like if it took its own story seriously? Compensate the neighbors and build anyway?
