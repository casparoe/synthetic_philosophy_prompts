I'm taking a seminar on Mill, and this week we were assigned the parts of Considerations on Representative Government dealing with India, along with A Few Words on Non-Intervention. Afterwards, two students in the class kept arguing and eventually asked the rest of us to weigh in. One of them, Daniel, claimed that Mill's position on India doesn't so much embarrass his liberalism as expose a principled exclusion built into it: the harm principle and representative ideals were only ever meant for societies Mill regarded as civilized, so his endorsement of benevolent despotism for India is consistent, however repugnant. The other, Priya, replied that this makes things worse rather than better — hypocrisy would at least be a failure of nerve, whereas Daniel's reading makes the theory structurally imperial, since the distinction between civilized and backward peoples does the justifying. Daniel then argued that Mill had a kind of escape clause: despotism over India was meant only as a temporary tutelage toward eventual self-government, and in A Few Words on Non-Intervention Mill actually opposed conquest and settler domination, so his heart was closer to anti-imperialism than his critics admit. Priya answered that a developmental alibi is exactly what an empire wants to hear — that the ruled are being prepared for freedom indefinitely later — and that Mill spent his entire working life employed by the East India Company, so the tutelage story was too convenient for him ever to scrutinize honestly. Daniel's last word was that this is an ad hominem: Mill also defended Company rule against transfer to the Crown on the grounds that Company administrators were insulated expertise rather than parliamentary patronage, which sounds less like rationalizing domination and more like ordinary institutional reasoning. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about each specific point, whether anyone is arguing at cross purposes, and who comes out more right overall?
