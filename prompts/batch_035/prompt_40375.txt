I teach an upper-level seminar on the Phenomenology of Spirit, and at the end of term, as a relief from exegesis, I set a satirical writing exercise: students must produce a parody that would convince me they have actually understood the lord–bondsman dialectic rather than merely memorised its vocabulary. This year's assignment is a corporate newsletter article, and I would like to draft a model answer myself — partly to calibrate my marking, partly because I rather enjoy it.

Imagine a mid-sized management consultancy that has just rolled out a mandatory peer-recognition platform — call it MutualRecog™ — on which employees award one another points for being recognised, the points being redeemable only for further recognition. Write the announcement article for the internal newsletter (headline plus roughly 600 words) in the dulcet voice of corporate communications, signed by an invented Chief Recognition Officer. The satire must be carried by the dialectic itself rather than by mere mockery of LinkedIn prose: the central joke about the recognition economy should turn out to be Hegel's own joke — that recognition from someone one refuses to recognise in return is worthless; that the official recognising class ends up the dependent party, having outsourced its selfhood to subordinates it cannot value; and that those doing the actual work come to independence precisely through that work and through their fear of the lord, while the lord, needing recognition he has made impossible, quietly stagnates. Please include a sidebar FAQ in which an employee asks what happens if one simply stops wanting recognition at all, and is gently managed away from the question, plus at least one pull-quote from an engagement consultant whose enthusiasm is indistinguishable from dependence. The tone should be deadpan throughout — legible irony, no winking at the reader — and a Hegel scholar should laugh in recognition rather than wince at a botched paraphrase. If you can smuggle in a hint that stoicism is the next shape consciousness will take, so much the better, but do not force it.

Please write in British English throughout ('recognise' rather than 'recognize', and so on), and keep the piece fair: I want my students to feel the dialectic's real bite, not merely to enjoy a pastiche of corporate culture.
