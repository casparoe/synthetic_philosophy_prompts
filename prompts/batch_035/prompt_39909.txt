After a long-running argument with a close friend — she holds that the meaning of a person's words is fixed entirely by what's in her head, while I've come to think that something about the world, and about the linguistic community, partly fixes it — we've decided to stop talking past each other and write up our dispute properly. We want you to draft the joint statement we should be able to sign. Use the Twin Earth setup as our test case: a planet molecule-for-molecule identical to ours around 1750, except that the clear, drinkable liquid in its rivers and oceans is some other chemical compound XYZ, not H₂O; and assume that in 1750 nobody on either planet could tell the difference chemically. The two positions to represent are hers (all content relevant to meaning is determined by intrinsic features of the individual speaker) and mine (what "water" refers to depends partly on the actual liquid in the speaker's environment, and on facts about the community's usage, that no one in 1750 knew). The document should have: a numbered section of claims both of us can accept, stated without begging the question either way (we can both agree, for instance, that in 1750 no speaker on either planet could distinguish the two liquids, and that our twin-speakers were internally alike — we may well disagree about what follows from that); a table of precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as fair, covering at least what the 1750 Earthling meant by "water," what the Twin Earthling meant, and whether the two meant the same thing; then the cruxes — we suspect one is whether sameness of all internal states can coexist with difference of meaning, and another is what role the community rather than the individual plays — and for each crux, one concrete case or argument we both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for example: a case where a single speaker is switched between planets without noticing, or where a substance is discovered mid-life to have a different microstructure than the whole community assumed). If any part of our dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. Don't include any reading list or scholarly apparatus; the document should stand on its own.
