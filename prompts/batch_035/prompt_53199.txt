I'm putting together some cases for a phil-mind discussion group and I want one built to spec about higher-order theories of consciousness. Here's the design.

The case: a subject, a moment, and two theories — Rosenthal-style higher-order thought theory on one side, Tye-style first-order representationalism on the other. Both theories have to agree the subject is conscious (no unconsciousness dispute, no blindsight, no subliminal anything — those are all overused). But they have to disagree about what the subject is conscious of, and in a specific direction: the first-order theory has to say the subject is conscious of more fine-grained properties than the HOT theory does. The divergence is about grain, not about presence or absence of experience.

Constraints:

- Everyday, realistic scenario, one paragraph. Cooking, listening to music, walking through a park — whatever works. No lesions, no gadgets, no exotic psychological subjects.
- No error anywhere in the system. The first-order percept has to be accurate, and the HOT must not simply misdescribe the percept. I'm banning the confabulation/misrepresentation move — the standard objection to higher-order theories is that the higher-order state can get the first-order one wrong, and I don't want that doing the work here. The subject should be a normally attentive person, not lazy and not weirdly hyper-precise.

After the scenario:

1. Show the verdicts actually follow from the theories — spell out what the first-order state represents, what the HOT is about, and why each theory assigns the conscious contents it does. This is the key thing: the case is meant to test whether higher-order theories can recover phenomenology from the higher-order side, so if the scenario just stipulates "the subject consciously notices X," that's cheating — the verdicts have to be generated by the theories, not written into the setup.
2. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work. I suspect it's a grain mismatch between the two levels of representation, but tell me if its actually something else.
3. Play out the escape routes. First, the move of letting HOTs have purely demonstrative contents — thoughts that just point at the experience and inherit all its determinate features without re-describing any of them. Does that rescue the grain? Is it cheap, or does the first-order theorist face a symmetrical problem about how many determinates her theory puts into consciousness? Second: does my ban on misrepresentation actually do any work? Would the case survive if I let confabulation back in, or does it just collapse into the standard misrepresentation objection?
4. Be honest about whether this ends up as a real problem for higher-order theories or just the overflow debate (rich experience vs. what you can report) in a differnt costume. If it's the latter, tell me what modification would make it genuinely distinct.
