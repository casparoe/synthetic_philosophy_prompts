Write a satirical government pamphlet, maybe 600-700 words, from a fictional Ministry of Moral Efficiency in a country that has decided, as a matter of public policy, to switch from a guilt-based culture to a shame-based one. The pamphlet is addressed to ordinary citizens, telling them how to stop feeling guilty (an "internal tax on the self," as the ministry calls it) and start feeling shame instead, which the ministry considers more efficient because enforcement can be outsourced to the neighbors. It should be formatted with sections like a real government brochure: an FAQ, a "what has changed" box, some testimonial quotes from citizens whose names are suspiciously similar, and a troubleshooting section for people who "experience residual guilt after the transition period" (they should be told this is a known side effect that will clear up).

The humor should come from getting the actual guilt/shame distinction right, not from mocking it: guilt is about the act and works even when no one is watching, shame is about the whole self and needs an audience — so the pamphlet's problems should be the real ones. E.g. the ministry keeps having to deal with the fact that shame doesn't work on strangers, so they need citizens to maintain "a stable witnessing community" and discourage anonymity; that shame punishes being-caught rather than doing-wrong, so the FAQ has to awkwardly explain why it's fine that no one feels bad about things nobody saw; and that you can feel shame about things that aren't your fault at all, which the ministry spins as a feature ("comprehensive coverage"). Also the awkward moment where they realize that if everyone is watching everyone, everyone has something on everyone, which they announce as having "achieved social trust."

Please add a short note at the end (outside the pamphlet, maybe 100 words) saying how confident you are in how you portrayed the guilt/shame distinction, flagging anywhere you leaned on the old "shame culture vs guilt culture" anthropology that a lot of people now consider oversimplified, and singling out the one bit you're least sure about — for me the trickiest thing is whether guilt really is more "private" than shame in the way the pamphlet needs it to be, but you pick whatever you genuinely are least sure of.
