The setup, since you'll need it to judge what I'm about to defend: Parfit-style depletion case. A community chooses a depleting policy, and because of the policy the people who exist a couple centuries later are *different* people than would have existed otherwise, and their lives are worth living, just badly worse than the lives the alternative policy's people would've had. Nobody is worse off than they would have been, since under the alternative they wouldn't have existed at all. So the standard person-affecting claim ("wrong because someone is worse off") can't get a grip. Parfit concluded we need impersonal principles to say what's wrong here, and then the whole population ethics mess (repugnant conclusion etc.) comes rushing in.

My friend's move, which I now sort of believe but only sort of, was to attack the fragility premise. His line was that identities are much less sensitive to policy than the thought experiment pretends -- the sperm-and-egg story makes it seem like any change in the timeline swaps everyone out, but real depletion-vs-conservation policies plausibly leave the same large majority of future people existing, just worse off, so the nonidentity problem is mostly a philosopher's artifact and person-affecting views survive fine. We argued about whether that's empirically true (I think over centuries it's just not, but he had intuitions about convergent life paths, whatever that means), and then about whether it even matters -- his deeper point, which actually landed on me, was that if your verdict about the wrongness of depletion flips depending on an obscure empirical fact about who ends up existing, your moral theory is hostage to metaphysics of personhood in a way it shouldn't be.

So here's the thesis I've ended up with, stitched together from both halves of the fight: (1) my friend is right that the standard nonidentity setup overstates how much turns on identity fragility, but wrong that this rescues the person-affecting approach -- the deep point is that a theory whose verdicts on depletion track *which* people exist has the wrong shape, so the fragility debate is mostly a distraction; (2) the right lesson is that some choices are wrong without being wrongings of anyone in particular -- you need impersonal or at least mixed principles; but (3) I want to get this without swallowing the repugnant conclusion, and I hand-wave at that part, which Dev kept pointing out, loudly.
