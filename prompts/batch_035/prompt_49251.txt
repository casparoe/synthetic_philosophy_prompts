I'm a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on metaontology, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a settled name for. The standard story we've been given, going back to Carnap, divides disputes into substantive ones and merely verbal ones: two parties who agree about all the facts but talk past each other because their words carry different meanings. Our professor's stock examples are things like whether Pluto is a planet, or whether a lawn is "really" thousands of grass plants or one thing. What I've noticed, though, and what none of the assigned readings addresses directly, is that verbal disputes behave in two very different ways once they are exposed as verbal. Some collapse instantly: once you see that the word could go either way and nothing turns on it, the disagreement evaporates, and nobody walks away feeling that they lost. But others persist stubbornly. Take a nominalist and a Platonist who both accept that the question whether numbers exist is settled by the linguistic framework one adopts, who agree that either framework can be adopted, and who agree that the choice between them is pragmatic rather than factual. By the lights of our course, they have nothing left to disagree about. Yet, in the seminar room and in the literature, each keeps insisting that the other is wrong about something — the Platonist feels the nominalist is doing violence to mathematical practice, the nominalist feels the Platonist has posited something absurd — even while explicitly granting that no fact is in dispute.

The phenomenon I want a name for, then, is a dispute that both parties acknowledge to be merely verbal and that nonetheless does not dissolve: the residual sense of disagreement that survives its own classification as verbal. It's not quite Carnap's internal/external distinction, because the parties already accept the Carnapian picture and the residue remains anyway. And it doesn't seem to be what people call metalinguistic negotiation, because neither side is trying to win control of the word — they agree on how it should be used going forward and still feel the other is mistaken. Does this post-verbal residue have an established name, or a literature attached to it? If it does, I'd like to know how that literature distinguishes genuine residual disagreement from a merely psychological inability to let go of a fight. And if it doesn't, I'd be grateful if you could coin a term for it, give a definition, and locate its edges — for instance, whether the standoff between compatibilists and incompatibilists, once both sides concede that "free will" is a term of art each may define as they like, belongs to the same category, and whether a dispute can count as residual if only one party feels the pull.
