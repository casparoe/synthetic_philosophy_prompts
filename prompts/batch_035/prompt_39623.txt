So here's my situation: I'm a political theory PhD student TA-ing an undergrad course on democratic theory, and the unit I got assigned is the whole "who votes" cluster — compulsory voting, lowering the voting age to 16, and votes for non-citizens (resident aliens, plus the people who argue for diaspora voting or full all-affected-interests enfranchisement). The way it's taught now is as three seperate debates with three seperate literatures, but my hunch is that under the hood they're all one fight about the franchise: what actually makes someone entitled to a vote — being affected by decisions, being subject to the coercive authority that issues them, some kind of reciprocal membership, or a competence-based license? What I want you to write is a decision-tree handout that treats all three debates as points in a single logical space, rather than three topics.

The tree should start from a commitment everyone in the room already has (call it "the franchise has to be justified, not just inherited from whoever historically had it") and then branch on questions a second-year could answer without knowing any position names. Branch labels are up to you, but the dimensions I care about are roughly: (a) is the ground of the vote affectedness, subjection, membership, or competence; (b) is low turnout itself a wrong or merely a bad outcome, and does forcing people to show up (compulsory attendance vs. compulsory preference disclosure vs. nothing) make sense given your answer; (c) is there a competence threshold below which voting can permissibly be withheld, and what kind of thing clears it — age, knowledge, being subject to the law; (d) stakes and duration — does thirty years of residence without citizenship differ from three, does a 15-year-old governed by school policies differ from one who isn't, does being affected across borders count for anything. Then locate the named positions as leaves: the all-subjected principle view, all-affected-interests, epistocratic restrictionism, the civic-republican duty-to-vote view that endorses compulsory voting, the liberal rights view that wants a very wide but entirely voluntary franchise, the status quo age-based view, and whatever else falls out. Aim for maybe 8–12 leaves, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment — e.g. does the all-affected view really enfranchise the whole planet on climate policy, does the epistocrat have any story about who administers the competence test and why they're trustworthy.

Two things beyond the tree itself. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't genuinely separate — my guess is the compulsory-voting republicans and the wide-franchise rights liberals get tangled, since both can endorse compulsory attendance for different reasons, but you tell me whether that's a real failure of the tree or just me not seeing the right discriminating question. Second, note the unoccupied corners: combinations of branch answers nobody seems to defend, with a sentence each on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Don't structure any of it around "here's what Rawls says, here's what Brennan says" — build from the logical space and slot the named positions in afterwards, with glosses if you name anyone at all. Audience is second/third-years, it's a two-page handout, and no citations please, I'll add the reading myself later once I know what I think the tree should look like.
