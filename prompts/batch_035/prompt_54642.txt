I'm in a philosophy of law seminar this term, and we've just reached intellectual property. The professor keeps pushing on the distinction between owning objects and owning ideas, and I've realized my own gut verdicts on idea-taking are a mess, so I want to run a reflective-equilibrium exercise on them. Here are my cases and verdicts. A friend reads her unpublished short story at a workshop and I later publish it under my own name: obviously wrong. A bartender invents a cocktail, posts the recipe with her name on it, and I make it at home for friends without mentioning her: fine. A typeface designer releases a font under a paid license and I use it in a logo I sell: wrong. An indie designer shows a dress silhouette on a runway and I produce near-identical dresses for my shop: legal, and I lean toward fine, though it feels slightly sleazy. I independently reprove a theorem that turned out to be already proved and publish my proof: fine, indeed praiseworthy. I buy a magic trick, figure out the method, and post it to a public forum: wrong, even though the method is "just an idea" and I discovered the secret partly through my own analysis. A friend spends months finding a speedrunning route for a game we both play and I post it first claiming discovery: wrong, but the wrongness seems to be all about credit, not about my using the route. A friend coins a slang term in our group chat that then spreads across the internet: fine, nobody thinks each repeat owes him attribution. A friend tells me a business idea in confidence and I launch it: wrong. And finally, taking the bare premise of a published novel: fine; taking its premise plus chapter structure plus the web of character relationships: not fine.

My tentative principle is something like this: an idea is appropriable when its value to its originator depends on control over its circulation, and not appropriable when its value depends on its spreading. The short story, the font, the trick, the business idea, and the game route are things whose worth to the creator collapses if others can use them freely; the cocktail and the slang term gain value for their originators precisely by circulating. But the principle strains almost immediately. The dress loses value to the designer when copied just as surely as the font does, yet I acquit the dress and convict the font. Publishing a proof and exposing a trick are both revelations of method, and one seems noble while the other seems like vandalism. And my slang-term verdict is hard to square with the fact that a coiner's identity and in-group standing really are diluted as the term spreads — some value from control there too, yet no duty of credit arises.

So my questions. Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts — perhaps one that adds dimensions like independent versus copied derivation, the creator's labor investment, whether the use deprives the originator of expected benefit, or something about the credit norms internal to a practice — or do I need a disjunctive, patchwork account? Please state your best candidate principle (or small set of candidates) precisely, motivating each clause with the specific case that demands it. Then tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed: a chef's signature dish copied dish-for-dish by a rival restaurant; a professor's lecture notes passed around without permission among students at other universities; a hobbyist cartographer's freely published hiking maps bundled into a paid app; and an illustrator whose distinctive style is used as the reference for a commercially sold image generator's outputs. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save: I suspect the font-versus-dress pair or the proof-versus-trick pair has to give, and I'd like to know which one you'd overturn and what that costs me.
