My roommate is a physics grad student who's a committed Everettian, and last weekend she ambushed me with an argument she claims shows her own interpretation can't make sense of probability. It's been bugging me since. It goes like this. (1) In many-worlds, the Schrödinger equation is all there is: the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically, no collapse, nothing chancy anywhere in the ontology. (2) So when I put a qubit through a Stern-Gerlach device, both outcomes happen — one successor of me registers spin-up, another registers spin-down — and I can know this in advance with certainty, because it follows from the deterministic dynamics. (3) Probability, for an agent, is a measure of uncertainty about how things will turn out — credence about which of several possible futures will be actual. (4) But if I'm certain in advance that a future me will see spin-up AND a future me will see spin-down, then for each outcome I already know it occurs, so there's nothing left for me to be uncertain about. (5) Hence in many-worlds the only coherent credences about measurement outcomes are 0 and 1, and the Born rule's 25/75 (or whatever the amplitudes dictate) can't be anyone's credences. (6) Yet the whole selling point of Everett is that it's empirically equivalent to collapse theories, whose predictions just are the Born statistics. (7) So many-worlds is self-undermining: it can't coherently say the things it needs to say to match what we observe.

I have a suspicion step (4) is where something sneaky happens — it feels like it slides between knowing that every outcome gets seen by someone, and knowing what *I* will see, whatever "I" means after branching — but when I pushed on this she had a comeback ready. She said: fine, suppose you insist there's still uncertainty about "which successor I'll turn out to be." Both successors are equally me, and there are exactly two of them, so any sensible measure over them gives 1/2 and 1/2. But the Born rule doesn't generally assign equal weights — it weights by amplitude-squared — so either you get the wrong probabilities (1/2 and 1/2 when the experiment says otherwise) or you sneak the weighting in by hand, at which point you've stopped deriving the Born rule and just postulated it. She thinks this follow-up closes the door on the self-locating-uncertainty escape route.

I'd like to keep the empirical equivalence claim (6-adjacent stuff) if I can, since I find many-worlds attractive for Ockham-style reasons, but I want to understand what I'm actually signing up for. Which step of her argument should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? In particular, does the move I'm gesturing at (whatever it is) actually buy back non-trivial probabilities, and if so does it buy back *Born-rule* probabilities, or does her branch-counting follow-up show it only buys back some probabilities that then need a separate story? And is that separate story — I gather people have tried decision-theoretic things and anthropic-ish things — a real derivation or a promissory note? Please be concrete about where the argument bends or breaks.
