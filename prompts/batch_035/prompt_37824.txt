I spent the better part of four decades in pain pharmacology before retiring, and I have recently been reading my way into the philosophy of mind on the side. I find that the phenomenon I know best clinically — pain asymbolia — sits precisely where two philosophical accounts of pain's badness come apart, and I would like your help thinking through it probabilistically rather than dialectically, as I am more accustomed to confidence intervals than to thegive-and-take of arguments.

The background, stated plainly so we are working from the same page: some patients with lesions (typically in the medial pain system, e.g., the insula or cingulate) report that they feel pain, can rate its intensity and location, and yet display no distress, no withdrawal, and are perfectly content to endure it. In ordinary cases, by contrast, pain seems to involve both a sensory-discriminative component (where, how intense, what quality) and an affective-motivational one (that it is awful, that one must make it stop). One philosophical camp holds that the badness of pain is exhausted by this second, distress-like component: pain is bad because, and only insofar as, the subject dislikes it or is disposed to want it gone. Another camp holds that the badness is intrinsic to the painful experience itself, so that a patient who genuinely feels the pain but feels no distress would still have something bad happening to them — they are simply not registering its badness, the way a lesion might blunt the registering of anything else.

Here is the proposition I want a number on: it is possible for pain to be bad for a person while that person is entirely indifferent to it. My own credence sits somewhere around 0.6, though I distrust it — I suspect it is contaminated by my clinical instinct that the asymbolic patients are, in some hard-to-define sense, worse off than they report being. What I want from you is the decomposition. Specifically, how much of the answer turns on: (a) whether the affective-motivational component is a genuinely separable part of the pain experience rather than just the sensory component plus a disposition to react; (b) whether the actual clinical data support the interpretation that asymbolics have preserved sensation with abolished affect, rather than, say, diminished sensation with preserved affect; (c) whether a state can be bad for someone without any pro-attitude toward its absence — which, as I understand it, is the old dispute between experiential theories of welfare and desire-based ones; and (d) whether indifference in these patients is best read as a rational revaluation (they have correctly discovered the pain is not so bad after all) or as a local failure of access to its badness. Assign a rough probability to each of these sub-questions, give me one line of justification for each, tell me how they combine into your overall number, and then tell me which single term the final credence is most sensitive to. If you think I have mis-specified the decomposition and an important term is missing, say so and weight it too.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most as a former experimentalist: what evidence would move your number most? I am imagining interventions I could in principle have run in my own lab — pharmacological dissociation of the two components, or reports from patients under analgesics that selectively blunt affect while sparing sensation — but I would like your view on which observable, in principle or in practice, would be the strongest discriminating evidence between the two camps.
