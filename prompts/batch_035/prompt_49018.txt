In a philosophy of physics reading group, we discussed Wigner's 1960 essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, which covers the kinds of examples everyone knows: mathematics developed for its own sake later turning out indispensable in physics, the appearance of pi in the Gaussian distribution used for measurement error, group theory and complex numbers migrating into quantum mechanics. At the end, one participant leaned on the title slogan to shut down a disagreement between two others about whether mathematics is discovered or invented, arguing that the effectiveness of mathematics shows the physical world genuinely has mathematical structure and therefore that mathematical objects exist independently of us — Platonism, delivered by the title of the essay alone. I suspect that is far more than the slogan licenses, but I also suspect that some defensible readings of it are more than platitudes, and I would like to be able to say precisely what is being claimed and what is being smuggled in. Could you lay out, as a numbered list I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from weak descriptive claims about which bits of mathematics happened to be useful, through claims about selection effects and survivorship bias that might make the phenomenon less surprising than it looks, to strong modal claims about physics being impossible without abstract mathematics, to full-blown metaphysical theses about the world's mathematical nature? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, substantive and probably false, or hopelessly vague, and — crucially — whether it would actually support the Platonist conclusion it was used to reach in our session. Conclude by saying which reading you think best captures what people who invoke the slogan mid-argument most often intend, and how someone pressing the selection-effect response should go about targeting the stronger readings rather than merely restating that we do physics with the mathematics that works for physics.
