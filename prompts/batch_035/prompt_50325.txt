So here's a thing I keep going back and forth on with a friend. Cryonics, the industry where a company fills your body (mostly your head) with antifreeze and stores you in liquid nitrogen after you die, hoping future medicine can fix whatever killed you and reboot you. The legal setup is what gets me. Basically everywhere, they can't begin until a doctor has pronounced you legally dead. But the whole pitch rests on the idea that the person who was just pronounced dead isn't really dead in the relevant sense — cryonicists like to say that clinical death is a process, not an event, and that the real death is information-theoretic death, the point at which the structure in your brain gets scrambled past any possible recovery, which happens some while after your heart stops. So the state's definition of death and the practice's definition of death have to both apply to you at the same moment, which seems like a strange thing for an institution to run on.

What I want is a reconstruction of the philosophy this whole arrangement quietly presupposes. Like: what theory of personal identity do you need for the paperwork to make sense? If you are basically the information pattern stored in your brain, then a future machine that rebuilds a person from that pattern brings back you, and signing up is just very long-term ambulance transport. But if identity is something else — biological continuity, an animal that keeps living, whatever — then the future machine builds a convincing copy, and you still died back there. Does the practice strictly require the pattern view, or could a continuity theorist still rationally sign up (maybe the chance that it's you beats the certainty of nothing)?

And then death's badness. The Epicurean move would be that being frozen and being cremated are equally nothing to you, so why pay a hundred grand? Is the answer just the deprivation account — death is bad because it takes away future goods, and being frozen means the goods are still there, waiting? Related thing that bugs me: the practice seems to have to say the frozen interval itself doesn't deprive you of anything, since you're not conscious and not accumulating lost experiences the way a corpse would — but that sounds uncomfortably close to saying a gap in your life is free, which I suspect a lot of people would deny in other contexts. Where's the line?

Also tell me where cryonics is philosophically underdetermined — which bits does it strictly need to be true versus which theories merely sit comfortably with it. And if the law ever did move to an information-theoretic standard of death instead of whole-brain death, what else in our institutions would have to give — organ donation timing, murder charges, life insurance paying out to fund your own revival (that loop already seems funny to me, you being dead enough to collect but not too dead to come back)?

Sorry if this is rambly — the core question is: what exactly has to be true about identity and about death's badness for this practice to be coherent rather than just an expensive funeral?
