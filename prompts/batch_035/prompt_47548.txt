Background: I'm a retired physicist, 35 years mostly spent on control systems and dynamical modeling for a national lab, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy — badly and enthusiastically, as my wife puts it. Right now I'm on Marx. The base/superstructure business: the economic base (who owns the means of production, how work is organized) shapes or determines the superstructure (law, politics, religion, ideology), with Engels later backing off to say the superstructure "reacts back" on the base. And I keep having the same reaction: this reads like a claim about feedback, but an unfamiliar one. In my world the plant and the controller are separable, and the controller is designed — often by people standing outside the loop. Marx seems to be saying something stranger: the controller grows out of the plant, and nobody designed it.

So here's what I want from you. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of social science who is also genuinely fluent in dynamical systems — don't just sprinkle the word "feedback" and call it a day, and please don't hand me a reading list either, I want this in your own words. Think carefully, maybe do some brainstorming on paper first before committing to the translation. Then:

Restate historical materialism in the vocabulary I actually use: forcing and response, slow and fast state variables, gain, stability and instability, multiple equilibria, timescale separation, hysteresis, path dependence. Cover the strong version (base determines superstructure more or less one-way) and the weaker Engels-style version (mutual influence with the base dominant "in the last instance"), and be honest about which parts of the translation are faithful and which you had to fudge. A few spots where I especially want to know if my mapping holds or breaks:

1. Property law. Marxians count law as superstructure, but property law looks to me like part of the plant itself — you can't describe the production relations without it. Is the boundary between base and superstructure even a well-defined state-space cut, or is it more like an arbitrary coordinate choice?
2. Ideology. The claim that people hold beliefs because the beliefs stabilize a system those people are inside — is there ANYTHING in control theory or dynamical systems that captures a controller element that doesn't know it's part of the loop? This one keeps me up at night.
3. The transition question. "Why did capitalism emerge here and not there" — is that an initial-conditions claim, a bifurcation claim, or something my vocabulary genuinely can't express?

Then the part I care about most: the residue report. What in the original claim survives translation cleanly, what survives only distorted, and what can't be said in my vocabulary at all? And give me your honest read on which side the residue indicts — is my vocabulary missing something real, or was historical materialism never as precise as it sounds to a physicist's ear? I've been told the modern philosophy-of-social-science versions involve things like functional explanation vs. causal explanation — if you can sketch those distinctions in plain words and tell me whether the modern versions get closer to something I could actually model, or whether the whole idea sort of dissolves when you demand the precision I spent my career demanding, I'd appreciate it. My gut says it's mostly a slow-variable-dominates-fast-variable claim dressed up in 19th century German, and I want to know if that gut is right or if it's the Dunning-Kruger talking. Don't flatter me.
