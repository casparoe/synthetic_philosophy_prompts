I'm a PhD student in comparative literature writing a chapter on translation under formal constraint, and I'd like to open it with a commissioned poem rather than an epigraph. The chapter's organizing claim, drawn from Hofstadter's treatment of translating a short fixed-form French poem in Le Ton beau de Marot, is that a poem's soul resides partly in its formal skeleton — its meter, its rhymes, its games — so that a rendering which preserves only propositional content is a kind of autopsy: informative about what the poem said, silent about what the poem was. Rather than assert that claim, I want the poem to enact it. Specifically: write a villanelle about the act of translating a short rhymed lyric, but with a deliberate twist on the form. The two refrains should stand for the two rival creeds of translation — the one that says save the meaning and let the music go, the one that says save the music and trust the meaning to follow — and each time a refrain returns, as the villanelle requires it to, it must come back as a re-translation of itself: the same metrical shape and the same end rhyme, but entirely different words. The poem's single most structural feature, the verbatim refrain, is thus the one thing it cannot supply, and the reader should feel exactly what is preserved and what slips away in that repeated failure. Please don't name Hofstadter, Marot, or any translation theorist inside the poem itself; someone who knows the villanelle's rules should be able to see that the violation is intentional, and someone who knows translation studies should recognize what is at stake without a signpost.
