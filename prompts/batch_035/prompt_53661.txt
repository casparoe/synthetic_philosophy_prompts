i keep getting dragged into arguments about whether viruses are alive, and they always end the same way: everyone lists features, the lists conflict, and nobody can say what would even settle it. before i have this fight again i want the spec first. write the requirements any adequate defintion of life has to meet, keeping in mind the theory needs to be good enough for two separate jobs — ruling on borderline cases here on earth, and recognizing the thing if we ever find it somewhere else, possibly in a chemistry nothing like ours.

borderline cases it should have something to say about: viruses obviously, and the big ones like mimivirus that carry their own metabolic genes; prions; dormant spores and frozen embryos, where nothing is happening metabolically for centuries but we still want to call them alive; the mule, which is alive but can't do the whole lineage thing; HeLa cells, which outlived the person they came from and arguably became their own thing; a dead tree still standing; and a growing crystal or a self-copying computer program in RAM, which are the cases that are supposed to not count. my labmate also insists on asking about a simulated chemistry where little moving patterns consume a resource and reproduce, and i can't tell if that's alive or if i just described a screensaver.

distinctions the theory can't collapse, at least if it wants to be taken seriously: alive vs merely self-organizing (stars, hurricanes, convection cells), alive vs merely self-replicating (crystal growth, von neumann probes), alive vs once-alive. results from biology it can't contradict — e.g. it can't imply every gene or every enzyme is alive, or that a sterile worker bee isn't. and a requirement i suspect matters more than people think: whatever the definition is, it can't just mean "descended from our last common ancestor," because then the astrobiology job is unanswerable in principle, and it can't be a naked list of features our particular branch of life happens to have. also it should either explain why the gray zone exists or explain why the gray zone is a defect of our concepts rather than of the world — one of those two.

for each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of particular camps — the metabolism-first people will care about some, the darwinian-evolution-first people about others, and i want to know which demands are real and which are camp shibboleths. most importantly, flag any pair of requirements that can't be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that "decides the borderline cases cleanly" and "general enough for alien chemistries" pull against each other, but i want to see whether that's real or just my lack of imagination.

then, briefly, a scorecard: metabolism-first views, evolution-first views (the NASA astrobiology working defintion about a self-sustaining chemical system capable of darwinian evolution), and the autopoiesis / self-maintaining-organization crowd. a table is fine but the criteria are the part i actually care about — don't spend all your words on the scoring.
