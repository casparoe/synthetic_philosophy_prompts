A colleague and I are drafting a paper on Goodhart's law, and we have noticed that we always gloss it as a mechanism about incentives: the measure goes bad because it becomes someone's target, and someone starts gaming it. I want a test case that puts pressure on that gloss, built to the following specifications. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph, set in an ordinary workplace — a hospital ward, a school, or an engineering team would do — in which the people being measured never learn that the metric exists and never change their behavior at all. The harm must arrive entirely on the evaluator's side: what the metric degrades is what the evaluator can perceive, not what anyone is motivated to do. The evaluator must be conscientious and good at the job, not a cynic ticking boxes; there is to be no gamed measure, no falsified number, and no one on the measured side doing anything they would not otherwise have done. After the scenario, show that it meets each specification; then state whether it still counts as Goodhart's law, and if you think it does not, say which kind of failure it is instead; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work — I suspect it is the sealing-off of the information channel from the incentive channel, but you tell me — and finally tell me what happens to the case if that seal is broken, so that the evaluator's metric-shaped perceptions begin to flow back into the agents' incentives: does it collapse back into the standard story about targets and gaming? The case is meant to probe whether Goodhart's law is fundamentally a law about incentives or a law about information, so a case that smuggles the harm in through anyone's changed behavior on the measured side fails the brief.
