Two of my friends have been going at it for a week over something that happened in a video game and it's gotten weirdly heated, so I want an outside referee. Context: my friend Dana plays this old MMO, and last month a botched server migration rolled the whole game world back about three weeks. The rollback ate, among other things, a cosmetic hat that was no longer obtainable — you had to be online during a one-time event in 2019 and she was, so hers was basically unique on the server. Her friend Marcus thinks her reaction (she filed a formal complaint with the game company and posted a very angry thread) is embarrassing. I've tried to reconstruct the argument in order, this is pretty much how it went:

(1) Dana said the rollback was theft: the company destroyed an item she owned, an item with real market value (people sold these hats for real money on third-party sites, whether or not that was allowed).

(2) Marcus said nothing was stolen because there's nothing there to steal — the "hat" is a config setting on the company's own servers, and per the terms of service she never owned it anyway, she had a license to display a graphical effect, revocable at will. Deleting your own database entry isn't theft, it's just Tuesday.

(3) Dana said that's a weird criterion for realness — by that logic her emails aren't real either, since they're "just magnetic patterns on someone's drive," but nobody says an email is fake. If an email can be real and be destroyed, why can't the hat be real and be destroyed? She says it's an honest-to-god digital object with a history, provenance, uniqueness.

(4) Marcus said emails are different because they carry information with consequences outside the email system — they get you fired, hired, they're evidence in court — while the hat has no existence outside the game, a closed system the company can rewrite at will. Tomorrow they could patch the hat to look like a bucket, or make everyone have one, and no fact outside the game would have changed. Try doing that to your grandmother's emails. So its "value" is a costume-party value, and losing it is like losing the costume contest — a disappointment, sure, but not a harm to a real interest.

(5) Dana said fine, forget the hat-as-object, the harm question is easier than he's making it: the 600 hours she spent earning it were real hours of her actual life, the rollback erased the product of those hours, and that's no different from a landlord burning a painting you made while renting from him. The hours are the interest that got set back.

(6) Marcus said this is the sunk-cost confusion again: the hours were consumed as entertainment when they happened, like a movie ticket — you paid, you got the fun, the transaction is complete. You can't "lose" hours you already spent and already enjoyed. She's double counting: trying to keep the enjoyment of the 600 hours AND get the hat back.

(7) Dana's last word was that by his logic nothing you make can ever be destroyed in a way that matters unless the raw materials were yours, which is insane — and also, she half-remembers an actual case in China where a guy went to prison over a virtual sword thing, so the law seems to think this stuff is real too. Marcus said the law thinks lots of dumb things.

They've agreed to let me get a verdict from someone who doesn't know either of them. Please go through moves (1) through (7) one at a time and say who's right about what — I don't want a mushy "both have a point," I want you to actually take sides on each move and justify it. Then say who comes out ahead overall and what (if anything) both of them are missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the very first sentence, no preamble, no restating what happened back to me. Also fair warning, Marcus is going to read whatever you write, so if you're going to declare my friend's stuff not real, at least be nice about it.
