Years of reading LessWrong-adjacent posts have left me with a fairly deflationary picture of pain: it's a kludgy alarm signal, and pain asymbolia — the condition where the sensation arrives but the subject simply doesn't mind it — is the standard exhibit for the claim that the sting, not the signal, is what's bad. But that argument only runs in one direction, and I'd like to test it from the other side. Construct a thought experiment in which the sensory-discriminative component of pain is absent or nearly so — nothing burning, aching, stabbing, or localisable — while everything we pre-theoretically count as the badness of pain is fully present: the aversion, the urgency, the commandeering of attention, the crowding-out of every other project, the begging for relief. Constraints: the case should be realistic enough that a chronic pain clinic could plausibly misfile it; the dissociation must arise from ordinary psychological materials rather than an authorial decree; and you may not simply stipulate the verdicts — the case has to generate them from the theories. Afterwards, walk through what each of three views says about the case: (a) the view that pain's badness lies in its felt unpleasantness, (b) Tye-style representationalism about pain, and (c) Klein-style imperativism, on which pain is essentially a command to protect the body. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if you dial that feature down, and how the defender of whichever view fares worst should attack the case. I'd also like your honest assessment of whether the case is constructible at all, or whether suffering without any pain-feeling covertly fails to be a case of pain — and whether that failure would itself tell us something about where the badness actually lives. This matters to me because it bears on the rationalist dream of editing pain out of our own architecture — keeping the signal, deleting the sting — and whether that project is aimed at the right target.
