I'm a software test engineer with no formal philosophy training. My working vocabulary is the vocabulary of verification: test suites, oracles, mutation testing, coverage, vacuous passes, flaky tests. A test "establishes" a property, in my world, only if the test would fail when the property is violated — a test that passes regardless of whether the property holds tells me nothing, and we actively hunt those down with mutation testing, where we deliberately break the code and check whether any test notices. I have been reading some epistemology in the evenings and have run into the debate over epistemic closure — the principle that if you know p, and you know that p entails q, then you know q — and over Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, which accepts that knowledge requires your belief to track the fact (roughly: if the fact were otherwise, you wouldn't believe it, and in nearby situations where the fact holds, you do believe it) and, famously, gives up closure rather than the tracking condition. Before I decide whether giving up closure is a genuine discovery or a philosopher's artifact, I want the whole thing restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can.

Here is my first pass at the translation, which you should correct, extend, or overturn as needed. Knowing that p, on the tracking view, looks like deploying a probe that is sensitive to the failure mode ¬p: the probe would have gone off had p been false, and it goes off in the actual and nearby situations where p is true. Closure then says that if my probe would catch violations of p, and I can see on the spec that p entails q, my suite thereby establishes q. And the tracking theorist's refusal of closure looks like this: my probes were chosen and tuned against the failure modes of p, not of q; there is a mutant — a possible situation — in which p holds just as my probes expect, but q fails anyway, and since none of my probes was ever aimed at that mutant, none of them detects it. Sensitivity, on this reading, is a property of the probe and the fault class it was built against, not of the logical relations among the properties, so entailment in the spec cannot transfer sensitivity from p's probes to q. That is why the tracking theorist can say, in the cases philosophers cite, that you know you have hands while not knowing that you are not a handless spirit deceived into seeming to have hands: the belief-forming methods you actually use would have gone off if you lacked hands in ordinary ways, but nothing in your cognitive repertoire was ever tested against the exotic fault class in which the world systematically lies to you.

Now give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' debate survive this translation cleanly, which had to be distorted to fit, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all — for instance, the complaint that the theory licenses asserting conjunctions everyone finds abominable, of the form "I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a brain in a vat," and the epistemologists' talk of what one is entitled to take for granted; do these have counterparts in how testers talk about vacuous passes and unprobed fault classes, or not? And the question I actually care about: when the residue is laid out, does it show that my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers can see, or does it show that their debate is less substantive than it looks — that the "failure of closure" is mostly an artifact of individuating knowledge by the probe rather than by the content, the way a mutation score is an artifact of which mutants happen to be in the corpus? Be honest about which side of that line each piece of residue falls on; I would rather learn that my vocabulary is missing something than be flattered that the philosophers are confused, and vice versa.
