I'm a novelist working through my third book, and I keep running into the same craft problem: how to render a character's emotion on the page. Broadly my options are to write it as a thought or appraisal (she understood, with sudden clarity, that she despised him), as a bodily sensation (her throat tightened, her palms went cold), or as a raw feeling described from the inside (a gray, spreading dread, unattached to any object). My impression is that philosophers genuinely disagree about what an emotion is — some treat emotions as judgments or appraisals of a situation, some as perceptions, whether of bodily change or of the world as carrying a certain significance, and some as feelings that can't be reduced to either — and I suspect the disagreement isn't idle for my purposes: which account is closer to the truth should bear on which rendering is faithful rather than merely effective. What I want is a procedure I can actually run at my desk, mid-draft, in the ten minutes before I commit to a paragraph. It should be a set of ordered questions that tells me which rendering to use for a given emotional beat, what counts as a sufficient reason to prefer one, and — this matters most — where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to me because the choice between the underlying theories is itself the artistic choice, not something a checklist can settle. For context on frequency and stakes: I write roughly six of these beats a week, I can't stop to reacquaint myself with the literature each time, and my editor will push back on any passage where the emotion reads as pasted on rather than lived, so the procedure needs to produce defensible choices quickly.

Please run the procedure on three beats from my current draft. First, a woman feels fear when a car skids toward hers on an icy road, before she has any thought she could put into words. Second, a man is told by his brother of a betrayal, fully grasps what he's been told, and reports feeling nothing at all for a day. Third, a character grieves someone she also resented, and her feeling seems to shift its object depending on which room of the house she's standing in. I'd especially like to know whether the procedure, on the second beat, supports my instinct that the emptiness itself, not any felt quality, is the emotion — or whether that instinct cheats the theories rather than following any one of them — and I want you to be honest if the procedure ever comes back with a rendering I'm plainly inclined not to write.

Since you'll have to lean on some view of what emotions are to build this at all, tell me plainly where your reasoning is most likely to misfire: which of the three beats the procedure handles worst, what a fourth case would have to look like for the procedure to give a different rendering than it gives on mine, and what consideration — some kind of case, or some argument — would move you to build the procedure on a different theory altogether.
