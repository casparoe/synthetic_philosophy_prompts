I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about well-being by going with my gut on cases, and I'd like someone to tell me if there's a principle underneath my verdicts or whether they're just a mess. Some background: I'm auditing a philosophy course where we read Nozick's experience machine stuff, and afterwards a few of us argued about it for hours without getting anywhere, so I wrote down my gut reactions. One: I wouldn't plug into the experience machine, and I can't say a plugged-in life is going well for the person even if it feels wonderful from the inside. Two: the husband whose wife has been having an affair for a decade while he believes the marriage is wonderful -- badly off, his life is going much worse than he thinks. Three: but a dying friend who is told their estranged son called and forgave them, when he didn't -- I just can't say that made their last days worse, even though it's structurally the same kind of deception about something they deeply care about. Four: a woman whose dearest wish is that a stranger she once helped on the other side of the world is doing okay; the stranger is in fact doing fine, but she never learns this and her life is otherwise drab -- I don't feel her well-being went up one bit because of it. Five: someone who spends a decade on a scientific project that succeeds exactly as she hoped, but she dies the week before the results come in -- her life still went well, that seems obvious. Six: a fully immersive VR holiday that the person knows is fake going in and can't distinguish from the real thing -- completely fine by me, no complaint, she knew what she was buying and got it.

My tentative principle is something like: your life goes well insofar as the things you care about actually are the way you want them to be, not merely seem that way from the inside. That handles one, two and five nicely, but it stumbles on three and four, which is exactly the pair my friends and I couldn't stop fighting about. In three, the friend is deceived about something they care enormously about and yet I don't count it as a harm; in four, the desire is genuinely satisfied out in the world and yet I don't count it as a benefit. And case six is awkward in a different way: she takes the holiday to be fake and it is fake, so there's no illusion, but my principle seems to imply you can never do well by valuing fake things, which feels preachy rather than true.

So: is there one principle, or a family of them, that captures all six verdicts or close to it? If some verdict has to go, which one and why -- is it three (should I just accept the dying friend was harmed?) or four (should I accept the stranger's flourishing did add to her well-being?)? State the principle as precisely as you can, clause by clause if needed, and tell me which clause each case motivates. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't made up my mind about: (a) the memory edit -- you live your ordinary flawed life, but in your final year all the bad memories are smoothed over so that retrospectively it all seems to have gone beautifully; (b) posthumous desire -- if I want my kids to flourish after I'm gone and they do, is my well-being higher while I'm dead? I'd also like you to be honest about whether my verdicts are actually reconcilable at all, or whether I'm clinging to a complexity that a cleaner theory would just overrule. Please answer in British English, and don't hold back -- my friends certainly didn't. (Apologies if any of this is half-baked btw, I'm very much not a philosopher.)
