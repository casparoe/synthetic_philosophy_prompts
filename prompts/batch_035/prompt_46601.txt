Quick context so you know what this is for: I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a column about the Ship of Theseus — not the boring "is it the same ship?" version but the bit nobody writes about, which is that our intuitions seem to care about *which* parts get replaced, not just how many. A hospital can turn over its entire staff over thirty years and still be "the same hospital," but swap out one keel and suddenly people say she's not the same boat. I want to test whether that's a real asymmetry in how we think about persistence or just vibes, and I need a case sharp enough to put in print.

So here's what I want you to build: a pair of Ship-of-Theseus cases that are identical except for one variable — the *distribution* of the replaced material, not the amount. Two ships, same age, same type, same total percentage of original timber replaced over the same number of refits. But in ship A the replacements are all in the consumable, wear-item bits: deck planking, rail caps, fastenings, rigging, hatch covers. In ship B the same proportion comes out of the structural core: the keel, the stem, the frames. No sci-fi, no minds, no souls — these are working wooden boats (fishing boats, or a sail-training tall ship, your pick, just keep it plausible). One paragraph per scenario.

Then I need you to show the cases actually work, because I'll get letters otherwise. Walk me through what a "the original matter is what matters" view of persistence has to say about each ship, and what a continuity-of-function or continuity-of-history view says. Here's the design target: the material view should be forced to say A and B are the same amount "original" — same percentage of old timber — while ordinary intuition says B is somehow more of a different boat than A is. Then tell me which feature of the pair is doing the work: is it the structural role of the parts, their causal role in holding the whole thing together, or something about how we think "the boat" is organized around a skeleton? And tell me what happens to the intuion if I flatten that feature — say, if B's keel replacement happens all at once in an emergency refit after a grounding instead of piecemeal over twenty years. Does the asymmetry survive?

Bonus if you can fold in the old Hobbes move — B's removed keel timbers get saved and eventually reassembled into a second boat — but then tell me whether the pull toward calling that second boat "the original" tracks the matter or the form it's arranged into. I suspect it's the form, that nobody would care about a pile of old keel timber in a shed, and I'd like to know if I'm wrong.

Last thing, and be honest here: how would someone who thinks spatiotemporal continuity settles the whole question attack the pair as question-begging, and is the pair itself cheating — am I engineering cases to elicit the exact intuition I already wanted for the column? I'd rather hear that from you than from a philosopher on twitter after the piece runs.
