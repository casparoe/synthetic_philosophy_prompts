Okay so last night my friend and I had this massive argument about the doctrine of double effect. Her position was basically that it's a dignified-sounding name for whatever our gut already decided — people who want to flip the switch write down "foreseen but not intended" and feel principled. My position was that the intended/foreseen distinction actually does work, that you can feel it doing work, but I had to admit that when I teach it, students just nod along like it's a vocabulary item. So I want to make them feel the distinction the way I felt it in the argument: by having to legislate it themselves and then getting wrecked by their own rule.

The class: intro ethics, 18 students, one 80-minute session. Design a game where they act as some kind of precedent-setting tribunal — the setup should be futuristic or otherwise weird enough that they don't recognize "trolley problem" and reach for canned answers (so no actual trolleys; make it drones, or rescue robots, something like that). They should adjudicate a series of cases where an agent diverted a harm, ruling permissible/impermissible, and after every couple of cases each team has to write down a one-sentence rule that decides ALL such cases going forward. They get points for every future case their rule calls the way the majority calls it, minus points for every additional clause they bolt on later — so there's real pressure to find a genuinely general rule instead of patching, and the patches they do reach for should be where the double effect machinery (intended vs. foreseen, harm as means vs. side-effect) shows up as the thing that saves them. The case list should escalate: start with obvious ones, then the version where the five victims are out of danger but the track loops back around and the agent had to know it, then a case where the harm and the good are so entangled that any rule they wrote licenses something they'll hate. I don't need real-money payoffs, just points and maybe a dumb prize.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the actual distinctions, including the closeness problem (the case where foreseen harm is so close to what the agent does that "foreseen" feels like a lie). Also tell me honestly which parts of the doctrine the game can't reproduce and I'll have to just assert in the debrief, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing the teams will try to smuggle words like "roughly" into their rules to dodge commitment, tell me how to handle that. And please don't have the debrief just vindicate the doctrine; if the game can equally support my friend's "it's all post-hoc" reading, I want the students to see that too. Sorry this got long, the argument is still kind of buzzing in my head.
