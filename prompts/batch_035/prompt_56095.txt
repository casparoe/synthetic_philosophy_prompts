I teach an undergraduate ethics course in which we spend three weeks on the care ethics tradition — Gilligan's objection to Kohlberg's justice-centered stages of moral development, Nel Noddings on engrossment and motivational displacement, Joan Tronto's phases of care and her distinction between concrete caring-for and the more diffuse caring-about, and Virginia Held on care as a practice with institutional reach rather than a private virtue. This term I've decided to pair the unit with a screening of Ozu's Tokyo Story (1953), but I've discovered that I keep teaching the film and the philosophy at cross purposes, so I'd like a proper reading that takes both seriously.

In case it's useful, the beats I want to work with are the following. The elderly couple, Shūkichi and Tomi, travel from Onomichi to visit their Tokyo children: the eldest son Kōichi, a busy suburban doctor, and the eldest daughter Shige, who runs a hair salon. Both are dutiful but inattentive, and eventually pay to park their parents at a hot-spring resort at Atami largely so their bedroom can be freed up for Shige's own use; the couple returns early, the nightlife having kept them from sleeping. Only Noriko, the widowed daughter-in-law of their son lost in the war, takes a day off from her office job to show them the city, and Tomi spends her one night of genuine connection sleeping at Noriko's small flat. After the parents return home, Tomi falls gravely ill and dies; Kōichi, Shige, and the youngest son Keizō rush down, arrive essentially in time for the funeral, and leave immediately afterward — Shige asking for Tomi's clothes as keepsakes before the mourning is even over — while only Noriko stays on. In the final scenes, the youngest daughter Kyōko rails at her siblings' selfishness, and Noriko gently answers that everyone gradually gets that way, that the drift between parents and children is inevitable, and that Kyōko will understand once she has her own life to protect. Left alone with Shūkichi, Noriko confesses that she is not the selfless person he credits her with being — she has long stopped thinking much about her dead husband, and she is lonely and wants a life of her own. He gives her Tomi's watch and sends her back to Tokyo; he remains behind, alone, resigned.

Here is what I'd like the reading to do. First, work through the film with the care ethics vocabulary: the children's arrangements look like role-scripted duty plus Tronto's caring-about without caring-for — offloading, scheduling, paying — while Noriko exemplifies the attentiveness to actual need that Noddings puts at the center, and the film's emphasis on her non-biological tie complicates any attempt to rest care on kinship obligation. Second, say where the film resists the reading, because I think it genuinely does: the parents themselves decline to make a claim on their children, even apologize for being burdens, and seem grateful for care a care ethicist would call deficient; Noriko's own care is partly sustained by an idealized relation she then confesses is false; and the film's ending, in which the drift between generations is presented as natural and more or less accepted, sits uneasily with a moral theory built around critique of exactly that kind of privileged irresponsibility. Is the film quietly indicting the children and asking us to do the moralizing it withholds, or is its equanimity a genuine counter-position to care ethics — an insistence that the chasm Noriko describes is a fact of the life cycle, not an injustice? Third, tell me what you think the film gives the care ethics debate that the arguments alone do not — I have a suspicion it has something to do with the perspective of the care-receivers, which the canonical texts handle badly, but I'd like a considered view rather than a hunch. I'll be grading the students on how well they can hold the film and the theory accountable to each other in both directions, so please model that rather than resolving everything in the theory's favor.
