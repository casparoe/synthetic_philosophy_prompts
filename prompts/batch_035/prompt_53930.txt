Last night my friend and I spent three hours arguing about Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and somehow ended up fighting about Frankenstein. She takes Winner to be claiming that an artifact carries politics only through deliberate design — his famous case is the low parkway bridges allegedly built to keep buses, and the people who ride them, away from the beaches — and she says the novel refutes him: Victor's creature is made with no political design whatsoever, and whatever politics it comes to have is simply projected onto it by the frightened humans who organize themselves around it, which makes the novel a victory for social shaping over technological determinism. I think she has Winner backwards: the creature seems to me to belong to Winner's second category, the inherently political artifact, like his nuclear plant — a technology whose very nature demands a particular surrounding social order, and the catastrophe of the novel is precisely that Victor builds the thing while refusing to build the order it requires. Read Frankenstein through Winner and tell me which of us the text supports. I want a reading that takes both the essay and the novel seriously: say whether the scenes my friend and I kept circling — the creature managing the De Lacey household from hiding, Justine's trial, the demand for a mate — fit her reading or mine, and where the novel resists the whole analogy, since Shelley insists the artifact is a person who can demand justice, not a bridge or a reactor. And if the analogy does hold anywhere, I'd like to know what the novel adds to the determinism debate that Winner's arguments alone don't.
