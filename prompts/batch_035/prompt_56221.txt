Next month I present to our economics department's theory workshop a paper defending a particular reading of the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. The setup, for context: two players alternate between taking and passing; each pass enlarges both pots; taking ends the game with the taker getting the larger share; and backward induction apparently proves that a rational player takes at the very first node, so that the game, however long, never gets going. My thesis is that the induction is valid but its premises are not available off the equilibrium path: it presupposes common knowledge of rationality, reaching any later node is itself public evidence against that common knowledge, and what it is rational to do at a reached node is therefore a question about belief revision after surprising evidence rather than a matter of theorem. I then argue that this reading fits the experimental record — subjects passing well into the tree — better than either of the standard dismissals of it, namely that the subjects are irrational or that they are altruists who care about the opponent's payoff. The audience is the hard part. Roughly a third are equilibrium theorists who regard subgame perfection as settled and will hear my claim as Binmore-style loose talk about off-path beliefs; one of them in particular is fond of saying that if you abandon backward induction in the centipede game you abandon sequential rationality in every finite sequential game, bargaining and hold-up models included. Another third are experimental and behavioral people who think the theory half of my paper is a distraction — for them the lab settled this decades ago, and they will want to know why I am laundering a descriptive finding back into a normative claim. The remainder are macro and industrial organization people who will immediately generalize whatever I say to reputational models: one of them has already told me that if reached nodes carry evidence against common knowledge, then every model of strategic patience in a finitely repeated game is in trouble, and she means it as a reductio.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet with the following sections, in this order. Ranking — the eight or so objections I will actually face from this particular room, ordered by how much damage each would do if it lands, with one line of rationale apiece (about 150 words). Objections and replies — each objection phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft (about 1,200 words). Concede up front — the two points I should grant before anyone asks, so my replies do not read as evasion (about 120 words). The question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that threatens the load-bearing step of my view, namely that arrival at a later node is genuine evidence about a player's type rather than noise; say why it is dangerous and give my best available answer (about 180 words). Cue card — five sentences to have memorized, in the workshop's own vocabulary rather than mine (about 100 words).
