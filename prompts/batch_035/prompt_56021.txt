I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the past two years I've been building, as a side project, a Monte Carlo framework that generates counterfactual histories: regions as coarse-grained agents with production and trade, plus exogenous event streams for climate, harvests, and epidemic introductions. Right now all the non-human inputs are just noise processes I sample and inject — a drought is a draw from a distribution, a pandemic is an arrival time. But I keep facing the same design fork: should germs, climate, and volcanic winters stay exogenous parameters, or be promoted into coupled dynamical variables that feed back into settlement, labor, and institutions (the way the Black Death's mortality reshaped wages and land tenure, or the way a decades-long volcanic downturn can cascade through harvests into unrest and state failure)? The popular history I read keeps calling these things "actors" — germs as conquerors' allies, the Little Ice Age as a protagonist — while the scholarly criticism I've encountered, mostly secondhand through responses to Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, warns that this slides into environmental determinism, explaining away political choices and human suffering as geography's inevitable output.

What worries me is that in my simulation, the attribution seems to fall out of my modeling choices rather than out of the run. If climate is exogenous, a collapse gets read as "drought-caused"; if I endogenize it, the same ensemble suddenly attributes the collapse to institutional rigidity, because the institutions failed to buffer a shock that now appears in the model's causal ancestry. Two calibrations, two stories, and I have no principled basis for preferring either — which looks suspiciously like the old determinism debate reborn as an architecture decision. There's also a counterfactual question I find genuinely confusing: as a programmer I recognize something like Pearl's do-operator as essentially dependency injection — I can re-run history with a mocked climate and read off exact probabilities. But historians seem to mean something looser and more contested by "climate caused the famine" or "the famine was a policy crime," and I can't tell whether my ability to compute exact counterfactuals answers their causal question, merely illustrates it, or quietly changes the subject.

So my ask is this: for someone in my position — building and interpreting such a model, with no philosophy background but reasonable tolerance for careful argument — what are the main open conceptual questions in this area (non-human causes in history, their relation to human agency, and the determinism worry), and which of them actually deserve my limited attention? Please rank them and defend the ranking, making explicit the criteria you're using, and separating three kinds of questions as you go: those whose answers would change my model architecture (exogenous vs. endogenous, where to draw system boundaries, how to represent pathogen or climate dynamics), those that only change how I should describe and interpret outputs (when "the volcano did it" is legitimate shorthand vs. anthropomorphism or a way of letting political actors off the hook), and those that are merely verbal — disputes about the word "cause" or "actor" that would dissolve under sharper definitions. For each question you rank highly, say what makes it tractable for a non-philosopher, and what would count as progress on it. I'd rather not have a reading list or a bibliography; what I want is a defended agenda that tells me which of these knots are worth my weekends and which I should stop feeling guilty about not untangling.
