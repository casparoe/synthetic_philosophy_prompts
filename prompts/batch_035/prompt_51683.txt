I run a decision-theory reading group for philosophy graduate students, and I want a one-page handout: a decision tree that takes a student from the bare statement of Newcomb's problem — a predictor who has already filled or left empty a closed box, and who was right in every observed case — to the position on the problem they actually hold, with the position named at the leaf.

The tree must be built from the logical space, not from a list of schools. Start by fixing the candidate splits, then hang the named positions off them, rather than working backwards from the labels. The splits I want in play (you may add or discard, but say why) are:

1. Does the fact that the closed box's contents correlate with your choice give you any reason about what to do, or only information about how rich you are about to be?
2. Suppose you learned that the predictor reached its prediction by simulating your deliberation in fine grain, so that what you are now doing is in a sense what it already saw: does that change what you should do, and if so, does it change it only for agents who know this, or also for agents who merely suspect it?
3. When you ask what you should do, are you asking how to get the most from this table now, or are you asking after the general policy whose adoption, in others like you facing such predictors, is what made the boxes what they are?
4. Is there any sense in which the 'you' whose act is being evaluated is the reasoning itself — the thing that gets copied into simulations and into the predictor's model — rather than the person standing at the table at 5:59?

Every branch node must be phrased so a student can answer it from their own convictions without knowing which label it leads to. So no node may use 'causal', 'evidential', 'dominance', 'expected utility', 'precommitment', 'functional', or any other term of art, and no node may ask whether the student 'is' a one-boxer or a causal decision theorist.

The leaves must include at least: the one-boxing position that turns on the correlation; the two-boxing position that turns on the boxes being already fixed; the one-boxing position that turns on identifying with one's reasoning as such; and one further position that is actually held by real people but seldom named in textbooks — I have in mind the person who one-boxes not on theory but on induction ('it worked every time before'), and who would take both boxes if the predictor's record were spotty. Also include at least one combination of answers to your nodes that nobody holds, and diagnose it: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Two side cases must be placed at the leaves, because plain Newcomb collapses positions that these prise apart: the medical variant (a gene that both disposes you to a behaviour and causes the bad outcome, where you cannot affect whether you have the gene), and the transparent variant (the closed box is glass; the predictor put the money in iff it predicted you would take only that box; you can see it is full). Show, for each, which leaf it lands in, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate even with these cases — I expect at least one such pair, and I would rather be told about it than have it papered over.

Keep the whole thing to one page: the tree, a sentence per leaf, the two placements, the unoccupied leaf, and the inseparable pair. No bibliography, no citations; assume the cases are as I described them. Close with a single line beginning 'Summary:' that states, in one sentence, which of your branch nodes does most of the sorting work and why.
