I run a ~40 person customer support team at a software company. No academic background, this is just something I've been chewing on for a couple years. Two years ago our COO rolled out dashboards for the whole team — average handle time, tickets resolved per day, a satisfaction score — and I've watched it slowly wreck things: agents bounce hard tickets between queues until they expire so the numbers stay clean, "resolve" stuff that isn't resolved, and our two best troubleshooters quit because they always looked bad on paper. Reading about Goodhart's law on my own time has got me to a position I can't shake: the gaming isn't a bug we can patch, it's what you get the moment a measure becomes a target, so adding more metrics to catch the gaming is just feeding the thing. And here's a distinction that's my own, tell me if its dumb: there's blatant gaming (marking tickets done when they aren't) and subtle gaming (honestly just never picking up the hard calls), and the subtle kind is worse because it's invisible, fully compliant, and gets promoted. I've definately seen more of the subtle kind since the dashboards went in.

Next week I pitch the exec team on cutting the metrics down to almost nothing and having managers actually listen to calls again. The room: the COO who built the dashboard system and is proud of it, the CEO who loves the what-gets-measured-gets-managed line, and a data analyst who I suspect thinks dashboards can fix themselves. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections these three will actually raise, roughly in order of how much damage they'd do, what I should concede up front, where my replies are soft, and especially an answer to the question I'm dreading — "if we can't measure performance, how do we decide raises?" Last thing: be honest with me about whether Goodhart's law actually buys me anything in that room, or whether the analyst is right that it's just a fancy name for "people respond to incentives," because I half suspect she is.
