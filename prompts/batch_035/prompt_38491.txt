I am a control engineer: I design control systems for chemical plants and aircraft, and I am fluent in that vocabulary — plants and controllers, observers and state estimation, feedback and feedforward, stability margins, model mismatch, adaptive and self-tuning control, cascaded loops, anti-windup. I have no background in philosophy or AI safety research.

A cluster of problems in AI safety, gathered under the label of embedded agency (a sequence written by Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski in 2018), starts from the thought that the standard theory of rational agency assumes a clean separation between the agent and the world it acts on, whereas real agents are physical parts of the environments they act in. The cluster has four strands. First, decision theory: an embedded agent cannot treat its own action as an exogenous input, because its choosing is itself a causal process inside the world it is modelling, so the counterfactuals of its own decision-making become murky. Second, embedded world-models: a small agent cannot contain a complete model of a world that includes itself, and must therefore act under logical as well as ordinary uncertainty. Third, self-reference: the difficulties an agent faces when trying to predict or reason about its own future reasoning. Fourth, subsystems: the worry that an agent built out of parts may contain sub-parts whose objectives diverge from those of the whole, or that exploit its blind spots.

Restate each of these four strands in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can — for instance, tell me where the good-regulator idea and the internal model principle belong, where an observer is and is not inside the loop it estimates, and what cascaded control and anti-windup do and do not capture. Then give me the residue report: which of the embedded-agency worries have exact counterparts in things control engineers already name and routinely handle; which translate only with distortion, and precisely what gets distorted in the translation; and which have no counterpart at all. For the ones with no counterpart, say whether that reflects something control theory genuinely lacks (and what it would have to add) or something muddled in the original worry. Keep the translation honest: where a control-engineering assumption is doing the work of making a problem look solved or familiar, flag it rather than presenting it as a translation finding.
