I am a final-year undergraduate taking a module in philosophy of psychiatry, and our two seminar leaders have spent the whole term arguing past each other about the ontological status of DSM categories. The first, whom the class has nicknamed the "kind-hunter", holds that conditions like schizophrenia are imperfectly operationalised markers for real psychopathological kinds — biologically grounded states that exist independently of our classifying them — and that the way to fix the DSM's notorious problems with comorbidity and heterogeneity is to let neurobiological research converge on nature's joints, in the spirit of the NIMH's Research Domain Criteria project. The second, a Hackingian, holds that psychiatric kinds are interactive rather than indifferent: classifying people changes them — their self-descriptions, their symptom reporting, their willingness to seek a diagnosis, the support communities they join — so the act of classification moves the very target it aims to describe, and no amount of biomarker research will reveal a fixed joint, because on her view there may be no fixed joint to reveal. The historical record seems to cut both ways: the near-disappearance of hysterical fugue and the eruption of dissociative identity disorder diagnoses in the 1980s look like looping effects in action, yet several once-speculative medical categories were eventually vindicated.

Please draft the joint statement these two should have produced instead of shouting across the seminar table. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign — about the looping effects themselves, about the distinction between indifferent and interactive kinds, and about what each agrees the historical episodes do and do not show; (2) a disagreements table stating precisely where they part ways, each row phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether looping effects are noise that a mature science eventually eliminates or a permanent structural feature of any classification of people, and another is whether a category can be partly self-fulfilling and yet still correspond to a genuine kind; (4) for each crux, a concrete case, observation, or study both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. As test cases, please consider the fate of cognitive disengagement syndrome, formerly known as sluggish cognitive tempo, which its proponents keep proposing for inclusion in the DSM, and the deliberate self-advocacy of the neurodiversity movement, which aims to steer the loop rather than escape it. Finally, state which parts of their residual disagreement, if any, are merely verbal — for instance, over what "real" or "natural kind" means in this setting — and which are genuinely substantive.
