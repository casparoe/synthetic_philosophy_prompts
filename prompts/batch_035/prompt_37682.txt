Retired chemist here — 35 years in a spectroscopy lab, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. Lately it's this business of higher-order evidence, evidence about your own evidence or about how reliable you yourself are. I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts on cases, on the theory that's how you're supposed to do this philosophy thing. Here's the list. I want to know whether there's one principle behind all of them, and I'd rather you tell me honestly that some verdict has to go than have you build me some monster principle with a bespoke clause per case.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. An old colleague ran a measurement he was confident in. Later that week a memo came out saying his instrument batch had a fault that biased readings, though nobody could say in which direction. My verdict: he should lower his confidence even though nothing about the reading itself changed. The instrument was fine before the memo and fine after, but the memo definately matters.

2. A friend of mine, smart guy, worked through some question while very drunk and arrived at a conclusion. Sober the next day, he can't find any flaw in the steps, and he remembers doing the work. I say he has to redo the whole thing sober before trusting it. "It still looks right to me" doesn't cut it when the marker of unreliability is that strong.

3. Now the one I keep going back and forth on. Same friend, sober this time, has thought through a question carefully, slowly, on paper. Then someone tells him: people with your background reliably get questions like this wrong in one particular direction, but we can't show you the trick. He goes back over his work and still can't see the flaw. Part of me says he should discount his conclusion anyway — that's just case 2 with a weaker marker. But another part of me says no: he did it slowly, he rechecked, and "you're probably wrong but we won't say how" isn't something he can do anything with. I find I trust this second instinct, though I can't for the life of me say why it doesn't also cover case 2.

4. A doctor examines a patient, concludes it's a rare condition her workup genuinely does support, then remembers that doctors who conclude this condition are wrong 90% of the time even when the workup looks clean. Verdict: she should mostly discount her own conclusion. Agreed to this one at a dinner party without hesitation.

5. My own case, early career. The senior people all told me my result was wrong, that I'd made some error I couldn't find. I checked and checked, found nothing, stuck with it. Turned out I was right, but I want to be clear the verdict doesn't depend on that — I'd say I was within my rights even if I'd been wrong, since they all shared the same background and would plausibly make the same mistake together. Their seniority was itself not great evidence against me.

6. Someone reasons to a conclusion, then is told: people who receive exactly this kind of warning tend to overcorrect and become too unsure of themselves. Verdict: they should neither ignore the warning nor fully apply it, something in between. This one makes my head hurt and I include it mostly to see what you do with it.

So: one principle that gets all six, or most? If saving all six costs you a clause per case, tell me which verdict(s) to throw out instead, and why those. And then tell me what the resulting principle says about the case I actually care about, which I deliberately didn't give a verdict on: what should I do when I notice, in the middle of an argument, that I've gotten defensive — knowing that defensive people systematically overrate their own side? Right then, in the moment: trust the uneasiness, discount the reasoning, or something else entirely?
