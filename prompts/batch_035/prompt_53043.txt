I never studied philosophy at school, but over the past year I've been reading old books I can find for free on the internet, and one of them has me stuck. It's the Pensées of Blaise Pascal, a seventeenth-century French mathematician who also wrote on religious matters, in an English translation from the early 1900s. The text is a heap of notes he never finished arranging, and much of it is hard going, but the fragment everyone calls the wager is the one I keep returning to. I thought I understood it at first — it gets summarized everywhere as a bet on God — but the more I reread it, the more I suspect the summary leaves out the parts that actually carry the weight, and I'd like help working through the original reasoning.

As far as I can make out, Pascal sets it up as a conversation with a doubter who protests that he would believe if he could, but cannot simply will himself into it. Pascal replies that either God exists or he does not, and that reason cannot decide between them; and yet the doubter is not free to sit out, since life forces a choice — he is already seated at the table, so to speak, and abstaining counts as one of the wagers. Pascal then asks him to weigh what he risks against what he can win: the stake is a finite stretch of one life lived a certain way, and the prize is an endless stretch of infinite happiness, so even a tiny chance of winning makes staking it worthwhile. He concludes that if you win, you win everything, and if you lose, you lose nothing, so you should wager without fear. And when the doubter repeats that he cannot command his own belief, Pascal does not tell him to try harder; instead he advises him to behave outwardly as believers do — to go through the motions, join in the observances — with the claim that this will, in time, make the belief arrive of its own accord.

There are three places where I feel the floor is missing beneath my feet. First, the claim that if you lose, you lose nothing: taken literally, that seems plainly wrong — living as religion prescribes does cost things — so I assume he means something narrower, and I'd like to know what. Second, the forcing move: is it really true that not betting is itself a bet, or does that trade on conflating declining an offer with actively placing a stake? I have a feeling the answer turns on the fact that time runs out regardless, but I can't quite make it explicit. Third, and strangest to me: if belief is not voluntary, then his remedy of going through the motions until belief follows looks like acquiring a belief by habituation rather than by evidence. Is that supposed to be a rational route to conviction, and is it even honest, given that Pascal elsewhere seems to hold that faith comes from the heart rather than from argument? I would be grateful for a patient, plain-language walkthrough of the reasoning, step by step, and especially for help seeing which of my three worries Pascal has an answer to and which he does not.
