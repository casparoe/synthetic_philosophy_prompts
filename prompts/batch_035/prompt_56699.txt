I've come to hold a position in debates about how highly capable AI systems should be allowed to talk to people, and I want it stress-tested: that truthfulness is the only binding constraint on persuasion. If every claim the system makes is accurate, and it discloses that it is an AI, then whatever influence it exerts counts as education rather than manipulation, and it would be paternalism to make it argue worse than it can — we already let brilliant teachers, trial lawyers, and fundraisers deploy their full persuasive skill on unwary audiences and call that legitimate. I'm aware of the stock rebuttal that superhuman persuasiveness changes everything, but I find it vague, and I want to know what specifically the view has to fear. What observations, cases, or arguments would count against it, ranked from near-decisive to merely awkward? I'm especially interested in whether "everything said is true" does the moral work I think it does when selection, framing, and emphasis are doing the persuading; whether there is an asymmetry between human and machine persuaders that isn't just sentimental attachment to our own species; and whether the view can be insulated against objections without cheating — for instance by narrowing "truthful" until the constraint becomes toothless. For each putative defeater, say whether a committed defender of the view should concede it or can reasonably dismiss it, and be willing to conclude that the view is genuinely well protected if it is.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my question.
