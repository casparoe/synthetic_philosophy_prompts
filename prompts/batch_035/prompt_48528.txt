I've been arguing with my brother-in-law, a fairly uncompromising utilitarian, about whether being as morally good as possible would actually be good for a person — or good for the rest of us. Rather than trade slogans, I've been collecting my honest gut verdicts on particular people, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. My cousin donated a kidney to a stranger last year, matter-of-factly, and otherwise left her life unchanged: I admire her without reservation and would be glad to be more like her. My oldest friend abandoned a career he loved in journalism for finance, which bores him, and now gives away 60% of his salary: I admire him too, but differently — I feel he has lost something, and I would not choose his life even though it contains more good than mine does. A third acquaintance gives 10%, plays the viola seriously, has an infectious sense of humor, and is generous in a hundred small ways: I think of him as living the best of these three lives, and that bothers me, because he is plainly the least morally good of the three. A man I read about declined to travel to his own mother's funeral so that he could donate the airfare instead: I find this close to repugnant, even though it is what strict impartiality demands and the world is slightly better for it. And a former colleague whose conscience never rests — who feels guilt over every dessert and cannot sit through a comedy without tallying the world's suffering — strikes me as damaged somehow, even though she does nothing wrong and an enormous amount of right.

My tentative principle was something like: moral goodness remains admirable but becomes undesirable — a defect rather than a further perfection — once it starts crowding out the non-moral goods that make a person whole, such as humor, curiosity, loves and partialities. But I can already see it doesn't fit. It saves the viola player and explains the guilt-ridden ex-colleague, but it can't handle the funeral case, where the loss is not in the man himself but in what he failed to feel; and it makes my admiration for my cousin hostage to whether the kidney donation left her enough time for her friends, which seems beside the point. It also leaves the 60%-giver in a strange middle position that I can't name, and it has nothing at all to say about why I rank the 10%-giver's life above the 60%-giver's while still calling the 60%-giver the better man.

So here is what I'd like from you: a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, that captures when maximal or near-maximal moral goodness is admirable and worth emulating, when it is admirable but a kind of warning, and when it shades into a genuine defect. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then tell me what the principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on — a woman I know who has scheduled her life hour by hour so that no time goes to anything that doesn't do good, and who is, as far as I can tell, sincerely cheerful about it; sometimes she seems like the best person I know, and sometimes like the emptiest. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save and would have me overturn — and if nothing saves them all, tell me which one I should give up.
