I'm in a seminar this semester on philosophical method, basically a whole course on thought experiments and how they work on you, and we just finished the unit on Dennett's intuition pump stuff. The moves he keeps making: find the hidden variables a pump smuggles in, imagine turning the knobs to different settings and seeing whether the intuition survives, sometimes run the whole pump in reverse. Next week it's my turn to present, and I picked Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, because the more I stare at it the more it looks like a purpose-built intuition pump aimed at consequentialism. The utopia is left deliberately vague, all the suffering is loaded onto one totally innocent child in a basement, and then the story hands you the walk-away option so refusing the bargain feels costless and even noble. But I'm half worried I'm being unfair to it, because the story is also doing literary work an argument never could. Like the part where the narrator straight up asks the reader whether they buy the happy city yet, and then starts adding orgies and drugs because maybe you don't -- that reads like it's rigged, but also like Le Guin is saying something real about how we can't even picture happiness without suspecting it.

So here's what I want: a reading of Omelas as an intuition pump that takes both Dennett's toolkit and the actual story seriously. Run it through his moves. Which details are hidden variables -- the child's innocence, having exactly one victim instead of suffering smeared thin across a million citizens, everyone in the city knowing the terms? Which are knobs you can turn to stress-test the intuition: make the child an adult who consented, make the citizens forgetful rather than knowing, make the misery milder but spread wider, and does the intuition survive those settings or not? Is the walking-away option itself doing pump work, by implying refusal is coherent when the story never tells you what walking away actually accomplishes for anyone? And where I really want honesty: spots where the story resists being treated as a mere pump -- the narrator's direct address to the reader, the refusal to describe the utopia in positive detail and the invitation to furnish it yourself. Maybe the vagueness isn't a flaw Dennett would flag but the whole point. Also tell me if Omelas adds something to the intuition-pump literature that the trolley cases don't -- when a pump misfires on a lot of readers (and plenty of people do bite the bullet and accept Omelas's bargain), does that tell us something about intuitions, or about Le Guin's actual target, which might be complacency rather than utilitarianism?

Some context for the ask: I'm a second-year undergrad, the presentation is 20 minutes, and my professor is the type who will absolutely call me out if I flatten the story into a diagram. Please write in American English. And end with one final line, exactly this format: Verdict: followed by a short phrase saying whether, on your reading, Omelas turns out to be a fair pump or a rigged one.
