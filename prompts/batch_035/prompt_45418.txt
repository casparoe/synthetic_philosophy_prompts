I'm writing a final essay for a philosophy of mind seminar on Being John Malkovich (Spike Jonze, 1999) and Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater in Consciousness Explained, and I want a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously rather than using one as decoration for the other. The film's premise looks tailor-made for the debate. A portal on the seven-and-a-halfth floor of an office building admits you into the head of the actor John Malkovich: for fifteen minutes you watch from behind his eyes, a pure inner spectator, before being deposited beside the New Jersey Turnpike. Later the failed puppeteer Craig Schwartz learns not merely to watch but to operate Malkovich from within, pulling his strings like a marionette; still later Malkovich becomes host to a committee of elderly tenants seeking immortality, while Craig ends up trapped inside a young girl, able to see out but never to steer. And in the film's strangest scene, Malkovich himself goes through his own portal and finds a world where everyone has his face and the only word anyone utters is "Malkovich."

Please read the film through Dennett's framework and tell me what it illuminates and where it resists the reading. Specifically: Is the portal a literal Cartesian theater — Dennett's place in the brain where it all comes together for an inner audience — and is Craig, the little man operating the controls, precisely the homunculus whose intelligibility Dennett's decomposition argument is meant to dissolve? Or does the film complicate this? After all, Craig inside Malkovich is no detached spectator: he is causally wired into Malkovich's motor machinery in a way the theater metaphor arguably isn't, and the film's ending, with Craig reduced to a mute witness behind someone else's eyes, looks less like a vindication of the inner spectator than a nightmare version of it — the theater as prison. What, if anything, does the all-"Malkovich" scene depict from a multiple-drafts perspective: a mind with no external discriminations left to drive content, or content with the editor missing? And is Malkovich's host-body inhabited by many tenants at once better read as a botched caricature of multiple drafts, or as an unexpectedly serious image of parallel channels without a central meaner?

I also want the honest critique. The film seems to presuppose something Dennett denies: a persisting, portable inner self that can be extracted from one head and rehoused in another, with its identity intact — the very central self the multiple drafts model says is a posited center of narrative gravity, not a thing that could be piped through a portal. If the fantasy only works by smuggling in what Dennett debunks, say so, and say whether the smuggling is a philosophical failure on the film's part or a pointed objection to Dennett — an insistence, from inside experience, that being someone is more than a narrative summary. Conclude, if you can, with what the film dramatizes about the debate that argument alone cannot: what does it feel like, on screen, to be promised a theater and to get one?
