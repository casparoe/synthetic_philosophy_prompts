I've spent most of this semester in an anthropology seminar arguing with a friend about whether the agricultural transition was a mistake, and I've come to suspect that my actual judgments don't form any stable pattern, so I want to lay them out and see what they commit me to. The course got me attached to the original affluent society line — foragers working less, eating better, living without entrenched hierarchy, with farming emerging as a trap: more calories per acre but worse lives per person, plus storage, property, and heritable status. But when I try to say what kind of mistake it was, my intuitions start fighting one another, and I'd rather find the principle than keep gesturing at the word "trap." Here are my verdicts, case by case.

One: the first band in the Levant that started sowing stored seed because a bad season had shown them that planted plots were insurance — not a mistake for them; they were responding to what they could see, and their kids were better fed. Two: the whole transition judged from outside, after ten millennia — a mistake, full stop; median welfare fell for a very long time, inequality got locked in, and I don't care that nobody intended it. Three: yet if you count everyone who exists because of it, including billions whose lives are better than any forager's, total welfare is vastly higher than in a counterfactual foraging world — and my verdict of mistake doesn't budge, which makes me suspect I'm not aggregating the way I officially think I should. Four: the ratchet itself, population growing to fill the new carrying capacity so that returning to foraging stopped being possible — this makes the mistake no one's, and yet I still want to say the process went wrong, the way a trap is bad without anyone being at fault. Five: an identical welfare crash produced by a purely climatic shift with no human decision behind it — not a mistake, just bad luck; so choice, or at least the structure of choices, has to appear somewhere in the principle. Six: a foraging group that refuses farming, keeps its way of life, and is eventually conquered and absorbed by farmers — their choice looks correct to me even though their descendants end up worse off than if they'd converted, and this seems to sit badly with two and three. And the case I keep going back and forth on: whether the eventual upshot — civilizations, writing, antibiotics, whatever my own comfortable life rests on — retroactively compensates, such that the verdict flips once the long run is counted; and if it does flip, I can't see why I'm entitled to the short-run verdict I'm so confident about in case two.

What I want is a principle that says what makes a historical process a mistake: for whom, evaluated at what time, against which baseline of alternatives, and under whose information. State it precisely enough to apply, run it against all seven verdicts, and tell me which of them it cannot save — I would rather overturn a verdict of mine than bolt on a clause that exists only to rescue one. Then tell me what the principle says about three cases I haven't decided: a society that adopted farming, suffered the welfare crash, and then voluntarily gave it up for a thousand years before being reabsorbed by farming neighbors (did they undo the mistake?); a sedentary, hierarchical foraging society living off rich aquatic resources, with storage and territoriality but no domestication (is that inside the class of transitions I'm condemning, and if not, why does storage without farming get a pass?); and our own era's most plausible candidate for a Neolithic-style trap, whatever you think it is — I mainly want to see whether the principle is genuinely about the past or is actually a device for judging the present.
