I teach an upper-level course in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I am grading our midterm examination this week. One question on the paper has divided the graders more than I anticipated, so before finalizing marks I would like an independent assessment of one student's response. I am not interested in a general impression; I need a defensible, specific judgment I could defend to a colleague.

The question (worth 20 of the exam's 100 points) read as follows: "Good's theorem is often glossed as the claim that 'information is valuable.' State as precisely as you can what the theorem establishes for a single agent who can acquire costless, decision-relevant information before acting, and then assess how far the slogan 'more evidence is always better, for anyone' can be defended. Make clear where, if anywhere, the theorem's scope ends."

The student's answer, reproduced in full: "Good's theorem applies to a single decision-maker choosing among acts whose payoffs depend on which state of the world obtains. If she must act immediately, her best option is the act with the highest expected utility under her prior; the theorem compares this with the value of first costlessly learning which cell of some partition of the states contains the true one, and then choosing optimally. The proof is essentially an averaging argument: with the information, her expected utility is the prior expectation of the maximum, over acts, of the act's expected utility conditional on the cell; and the expectation of a maximum is at least the maximum over acts of the expectation, which is the best she could do unaided. Hence free, decision-relevant information weakly improves expected utility, and improves it strictly whenever the information would change the optimal act with positive probability. Suppose, for instance, a physician can give treatment A, which cures the patient if and only if she has condition one, or treatment B, which cures her if and only if she has condition two; the prior is 50–50, so without further information either treatment cures only half the time, while a costless test that reveals which condition she has raises the cure rate to certainty. Since the theorem shows that information never lowers expected utility, it follows that being better informed is a weak advantage in any decision situation whatsoever, strategic or otherwise: in a competitive setting, a player with more evidence can never be at a disadvantage relative to the same player with less. And because the expected value is weakly higher, the agent who acts on the evidence cannot in fact be worse off for having gathered it — the theorem guarantees the outcome itself is not worsened, not merely its expectation. The only genuine limit on the slogan is cost: where evidence is not free, the expected gain may fall short of its price, and a rational agent may then decline it."
