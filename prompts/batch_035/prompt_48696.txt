Over the holidays my brother-in-law, who listens to a great many politics podcasts, gave the family his explanation of populism, and I wrote it down afterwards because something about it struck me as too tidy. Here is his summary:

Populism is just what democracy looks like when it's working properly. In every country there is a real thing called the will of the people — one voice that all ordinary citizens share — and democracy means carrying it out. Elites are simply whoever stands in its way: judges, experts, journalists, career politicians. So when a court strikes down a referendum or a legislature waters down what voters clearly chose, that's the establishment overriding democracy itself. Populists are the politicians who take the people's side, which is why they insist the majority must always get its way and treat protections for minorities as devices the elite uses to stay in charge. In the end it comes down to this: if you think the people should rule, you're a populist; if you're suspicious of populism, the honest conclusion is that you don't really trust ordinary people and prefer rule by the few. That's not a criticism of the position — it's just what the words mean.

He is not a foolish man, and none of this sounds obviously wrong, so I don't want a reply that treats him as one. I have been turning the summary over on my own for a few weeks, and my unease centres on the phrase "the will of the people," which seems to do enormous work: I keep wondering whether it is really one thing at all, and whether everyone who uses it means the same thing by it. But I lack the background to say where his account goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong at all.

So here is what I'm asking for. Please take his summary in order, piece by piece, and for each piece tell me whether a political philosopher who studies democracy would accept it as fair, would accept it only as a simplification, or would call it a misrepresentation — and, where it is a misrepresentation, of what exactly. I'm especially interested in the places where he seems to slide from a claim populists themselves would make into something presented as plain fact, and in whether treating "the will of the people" as a single, settled voice is his error or a real feature of the phenomenon. Finally, judge his closing either/or: does the conclusion that suspicion of populism amounts to preferring rule by the few follow from the parts of his summary that are true, or does it only work by assuming the parts that aren't? Lead with your overall verdict in two or three sentences, then the piece-by-piece pass, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
