I lead a small strategic foresight function at a reinsurance company, and one of my recurring jobs is running our quarterly scenario-forecasting exercise. Six analysts independently assign probabilities to a list of questions — whether a specific piece of regulation passes this year, whether a named hurricane season exceeds a loss threshold, whether a major counterparty gets downgraded below a certain rating — and then we meet to produce the single set of numbers that goes into our capital-planning report. The disagreements are persistent: the same two or three people land far apart on the same kinds of questions, quarter after quarter, and the meetings have degraded into a ritual where each side restates its case and we split the difference. Years ago I took a course that touched on Bayesian epistemology, and I've since read just enough about the common prior assumption and Aumann-style agreement results to suspect we're using them badly in both directions: sometimes we treat disagreement as if it must reflect genuinely different information when it's really just different starting beliefs or different gut-level weightings, and sometimes we assume we must share priors when in fact one analyst has a considered structural view the others don't hold.

What I'd like is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a single disagreement, in under an hour, before the number goes into the report. It should walk through, in order: how to establish what evidence each person is actually conditioning on and whether there is real private information in the room; how to test whether the disagreement survives full disclosure of that evidence; what to conclude if it does survive — in particular, when the common prior assumption is a useful idealisation that tells us someone is double-counting or under-weighting shared evidence, and when it is simply false for our team and heterogeneous priors should be admitted as an honest modelling choice; whether and when averaging, mediation, or deferred escalation to an underwriter with authority is the right output; and where the procedure must stop and hand the judgement to a person because the question is one of values or risk appetite rather than probability. Please be explicit about the points where the procedure will misfire — for instance, where 'revealing your evidence' is impossible because it's tacit judgement built from twenty years of pricing, and where pooling opinions can launder correlated errors into a false consensus.

Then run the procedure on three real-shaped disagreements from our last exercise: an analyst who assigns 70 per cent to the regulation passing versus one at 35 per cent, where the optimist has seen early draft language the others haven't; the same spread on a question about an emerging cyber-loss accumulation scenario, where both analysts have read identical material but one lived through a different historical loss event and openly says her 'prior' is different; and a case where four analysts are clustered at 55–65 per cent but the holdout at 15 per cent is the one with the strongest track record. On the third case especially, I expect the procedure may give an answer I won't like, and I'd rather know that than keep chairing these meetings by splitting the difference. Please answer in British English.
