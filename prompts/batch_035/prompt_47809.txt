I evaluate large language models for a living, and somewhere along the way I've become convinced of a position I'd like properly pressure-tested. The position is this: belief-ascription to an AI system is warranted exactly when the intentional stance toward that system earns its keep — that is, when predicting the system's outputs by attributing beliefs, desires, and goals does better, in practice, than any prediction strategy built on its architecture and training process or on its low-level implementation, and when those intentional generalizations cannot be compressed into design-level or physical-level ones without predictive loss. If that condition holds, the system literally has beliefs, and the further question of whether it merely acts as if it does is no more open than the question of whether other people "really" have minds. It strikes me that with current models the condition arguably holds in places — nobody usefully predicts what a model will say by reasoning about gradients, only by reasoning about what it takes itself to know — and that the parity with our attributions to other humans is close to airtight, since we never could run the physical stance on each other either. I'd like you to answer as a philosopher of mind who has also spent real time around machine learning systems: at home with the intentional-stance literature, but skeptical of hand-waving in either direction.

Before writing, please brainstorm the space of possible defeaters rather than just listing the two or three most obvious ones, and then rank them for me from fatal to merely awkward. Some worries I'm already aware of and want placed on the map: that a model's competence is parasitic on human-generated text, so stance-level predictive success might be mimicry all the way down, with nothing behind the ascriptions; that the disanalogy cuts deeper than I admit, because for an AI system the design stance is in principle available even where it is currently useless, which some people claim breaks the parity with other humans; and that a defender can always retreat to a weaker predictive criterion when a counterexample appears, making the position hard to lose but perhaps vacuous. Beyond these, I want the ones I haven't thought of — including empirical developments that would count as evidence against the position, not just philosophical arguments, and things that may already have happened.

For each defeater, please say whether a committed defender — imagine Dennett himself — would concede it as genuinely counting against the view, or would absorb it, and if they absorb it, whether the absorbing is principled or merely defensive. I'd also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals don't: does it make any observable difference, in evaluation or in practice, whether a system really has beliefs rather than merely being well predicted under the intentional stance? If it makes no difference anywhere, I want an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether this is just what a mature view of mental-state ascription should look like.

Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, and don't be shy about concluding that some of the ones I find threatening are actually weak, or that ones I've dismissed are the serious ones. I care more about getting the map of the position's real exposure right than about the position surviving.
