I help run a small community workshop on the ethics of conversational AI, and the same argument keeps resurfacing. My co-organizer leans on a single analogy whenever the topic of assistants refusing or deflecting requests comes up: an AI assistant, he says, is essentially a reference librarian — a helpful professional standing between a curious adult and an enormous collection, whose job is to help patrons find what they are after, not to judge why they want it. He uses this to argue that assistants should almost never decline informational requests: the material is "already in the library," the requester is an adult, and a librarian who won't tell you where the books on poisons are shelved is failing at her job; judgment about use belongs to the reader. One of our regular attendees, a former pharmacist, bristles at this. Her competing picture is the pharmacist at the counter: a professional with training, discretion, and accountability who can decline to hand something over and refer you elsewhere, and she thinks that picture licenses genuine refusals where the librarian picture wouldn't. The two of them talk past each other for an hour every time.

Before our next session I want a careful audit of both analogies so I can steer the discussion instead of just watching two images collide. Lay out what maps onto what in each case — patron/patient, collection/formulary, the reference desk/the counter, professional norms, the referral to another professional, the licensing body behind the license — then say where each mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the refusal question from the ones that don't. The candidates I can see: an assistant provides tailored, interactive, step-by-step help rather than pointing at a shelf; for many users it is the only desk in town, with no other library to walk to; it converses and sometimes encourages rather than neutrally retrieving; and there is no state license whose removal disciplines the professional's judgment. I suspect some of these carry more weight than others, and there may be disanalogies I haven't spotted. Then tell me plainly whether either analogy can carry the conclusion it is being used for — his near-absolute anti-refusal position, her permission to refuse — or whether in both cases the conclusion was already fixed and the analogy is decoration; and if neither picture is right, sketch what a better one would look like.

One caution: don't be even-handed for its own sake. Commit to a verdict about which analogy, if either, does more work, and end by flagging the single step in your audit you would least trust — the point where a different mapping or one further disanalogy would flip your conclusion — so I know exactly what to push on in the discussion.
