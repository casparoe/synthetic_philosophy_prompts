For my graduate seminar on the ethics and safety of machine learning, our term assignment is a position piece on whether Goodhart's law is merely an engineering nuisance or a fundamental obstacle to getting what we want from optimized systems, and we are expected to stake out and defend a view of our own. Before I write mine, I would like my working position audited for internal consistency, since several of my beliefs have been acquired piecemeal and I have begun to suspect they are not all simultaneously tenable. My position currently consists of five commitments.

First, I accept the optimization-pressure form of Goodhart's law as close to universal: whenever a measurable proxy is substituted for a target and then optimized hard, the divergence between proxy and target widens as pressure increases, and in the limit one can achieve maximal proxy value while destroying target value entirely. I take the mechanisms to be several and distinct — regression to the mean when reusing a noisy proxy, breakdown of the proxy relationship in the extreme tail of the distribution, third parties or the optimizer itself intervening in the causal link between proxy and target, and outright adversarial adaptation to the measurement process.

Second, I hold that no explicit, closed-form specification of what we ultimately want from AI systems is achievable: any objective we can write down omits something we care about, and the omission is precisely what the optimizer exploits. So the strategy of simply specifying the true objective correctly is not available to us, and never will be.

Third, I believe human approval is a genuine exception to the second commitment. What we want from these systems is, at bottom, what idealized, well-informed, consistent human raters would endorse; approval ratings are therefore not a proxy standing in for some further target but constitute the target itself. This is why I take training on human feedback to be categorically more secure than training on any hand-designed reward function.

Fourth, I endorse mild optimization as the general remedy whenever the proxy cannot be fully trusted: rather than selecting the single highest-scoring action, one should choose randomly from, say, the top tenth of a percent of actions ranked by proxy score under some trusted base policy, or otherwise satisfice — I have in mind the family of approaches sometimes called quantilization — deliberately trading off some performance for robustness.

Fifth, I hold that a capable model whose outputs have survived a serious human-led red team, meaning a sustained attempt by competent evaluators to elicit harmful behavior, is thereby fit to deploy, since the failure of that scrutiny to find problems is good evidence of their absence.

My question is whether these five commitments hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is, what would have to give, and what that revision costs me; for the merely apparent ones, show me why the appearance dissolves. If a tension can be resolved by saying more rather than by believing less, say so. Finally, if you had to recommend a single revision that would clean up the most, tell me which one and why. I would rather have the tensions ranked by severity than receive an unstructured list, and I would ask that you not simply observe that everything in this area is contested — I want your own audit of my position as stated.
