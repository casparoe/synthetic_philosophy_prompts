I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on well-being, and I've drafted the core of my term paper on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. The problem, as our instructor framed it, is that the pleasure of cold watermelon on a hot day, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, and the pleasure of watching someone who cut you off get pulled over seem to share no common phenomenal quality, so it's mysterious what the hedonist is even adding up. Below is my draft. I'd appreciate comments before I revise.

Draft: I argue that the heterogeneity problem has been aimed at the wrong target because it looks for a first-order phenomenal quality common to all pleasures, when the real commonality is second-order and structural. Every pleasure, however different in texture, includes in its phenomenology a seeming that its own continuation is fitting — call this a prolongational seeming. The watermelon pleasure and the understanding-the-proof pleasure share no specific feel, but each presents itself as correct to prolong, and this self-presentation is part of what it is to be a pleasure rather than merely an agreeable sensation. The view gets indirect support from the pain side: the disgust of a hangover, the sting of a paper cut, and the ache of grief share nothing first-order either, yet all seem to call for their own termination, and nobody is much bothered by this heterogeneity. I then claim that Aristotle anticipates the problem in his discussion of pleasure, where he argues that pleasures differ in kind because they complete activities that differ in kind, and that he would reject my second-order unity as a kind of external relation imposed on the pleasures rather than something internal to them. In the final section I argue that hedonism about well-being survives in modified form: a life goes better to the extent that it contains states with stronger and more coherent prolongational seemings, and this is a substantive, non-vacuous thesis that the original heterogeneity problem never touched.

Three things I'd especially like feedback on. First, accuracy of the history: is my reading of Aristotle fair, and am I right that the standard statement of the heterogeneity problem comes out of the nineteenth-century hedonists' attempt to define pleasure as a kind of consciousness rather than as a list of pleasant things? Second, and most important to me: does the prolongational seeming proposal beg the question? My seminar classmate says I've just redescribed pleasantness in second-order language, since if I'm asked what makes a seeming prolongational rather than some other kind, my only answer seems to be that the state is pleasant. I don't want to concede this, but I can't yet see how to answer her. Third, whether the final section actually delivers: does the modified hedonism remain a theory of well-being at all, or has it quietly become a theory of what it's like to prefer what you're feeling? Please be direct about which parts are weak, including anything above the three questions. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase stating your overall verdict on whether the prolongational seeming move is promising as it stands.
