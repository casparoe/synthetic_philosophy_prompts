I'm an SMT solver engineer -- I spend my days encoding specs as sets of constraints and letting a solver tell me whether they're satisfiable, and when they're not, it hands me an unsat core showing me exactly which subset of the constraints is jointly infeasible. Recently I got into a philosophy podcast episode about whether genuine moral dilemmas are even possible -- situations where you ought to do A and ought to do B but literally cannot do both -- and I keep reaching for my work vocabulary to think about it, but I'm not sure how much of the debate actually survives that move. So here's the job: restate the philosophical debate in the vocabulary I actually use, and then give me an honest residue report.

Some specific mappings I want you to actually work through rather than gesture at: the claim that dilemmas are impossible because "ought implies can" -- does that come out as something like "an infeasible spec was never a spec," or is it more like a meta-level insistence that the 'ought' constraints were really soft constraints with a priority ordering all along? The Ross-style view where duties are prima facie and get outweighed all-things-considered -- that looks to me like lexicographic ordering or weighted MaxSAT, where every conflict gets resolved by the objective function and the solver just outputs the least-bad assignment. And the view where dilemmas are genuine -- is that just declaring the constraint set hard and accepting UNSAT, or is there more to it? Also where do things like slack variables and soft-constraint violation penalties fit, and is "doing the lesser wrong" just the solver's optimal solution, or does the philosophical story treat the violated constraint as somehow still standing afterward in a way a solved optimization problem's aren't?

The residue part is what I really care about. Philosophers talk about there being a moral remainder -- that after you've done the best thing possible, you can still be guilty, or owe apology/compensation, because the duty you failed didn't evaporate when it got outweighed. I cannot see any counterpart of that in my world: once MaxSAT picks an assignment, the violated soft constraints are just recorded as a cost and the solver moves on, nothing "survives" in a normatively loaded sense. So tell me: is that remainder a real feature of the moral domain that the constraint vocabulary is blind to (and if so, is the blindness fixable -- e.g., is there something like an obligation that persists post-solution that I'd recognize as analogous to anything in formal methods, like a proof obligation that stays open), or is the remainder itself the philosophers fooling themselves, an artifact of treating a solved optimization as unsolved? And flip side: is there anything in my vocabulary -- unsat cores, paraconsistency, proof-carrying assignments -- that would let me say something to the philosophers that they can't say in theirs? Don't go easy on either side; if part of their debate just dissolves under translation, say which part and why.
