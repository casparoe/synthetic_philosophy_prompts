I've spent twenty years writing novels whose highest ambition has been craft, and I've lately been goaded into rereading Tolstoy's late treatise on art, in which he argues roughly that art is the activity by which one person, using external signs, deliberately hands on feelings to others so that they are infected by them — and grades art by the sincerity of the feeling, the clarity of its transmission, and the moral and religious quality of what is transmitted. On that standard his own earlier novels, most of the music and literature his contemporaries revered, and my own books come out badly, while peasant songs and simple tales of humble life come out well. Write an essay weighing whether the standard survives once its religious-moral core is removed. In particular, address the sincerity condition, which I have come to distrust in my own case: after decades of practice I can produce prose that reads as deeply sincere whether I am writing from conviction or out of boredom and a deadline, and I cannot see what fact about the finished work could distinguish the two. Take a definite position — either the secularized residue of Tolstoy's theory still yields a workable test for good art, or the whole edifice collapses without the theology that held his three criteria together — and defend it with reasons; do not end by laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
