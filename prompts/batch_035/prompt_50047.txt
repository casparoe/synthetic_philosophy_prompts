My two closest friends have been arguing for months about something I can't referee, and I'd like a careful, point-by-point verdict. Neither of them, nor I, has any academic background — I run a hardware store — but we take ideas seriously, so please treat our vocabulary as an honest attempt, not as claims to expertise.

Some background: Maya was mugged two years ago in the parking structure next to her office. Dev is a photographer, and he thinks a lot about what cameras and eyes actually deliver. The dispute is over what fear has done to Maya's experience of the world.

(1) Maya says that since the mugging she literally *sees* her surroundings differently: the stairwell she uses every day looks darker and narrower than it did before, a man standing at the far end of a parking lot looks bigger to her than he would have looked from the same distance before, and empty doorways seem, as she puts it, to open onto something. She insists this isn't her panicking and then describing the panic — the fear is in the look itself, from the first glance.

(2) Dev answers that this is interpretation wearing perception's clothes. The light reaching her eyes is unchanged, her eyes are undamaged, so the raw visual material is exactly what it always was; what's changed is the meaning her mind attaches to that material the instant it arrives. He compares it to reading a telegram twice — once before and once after bad news — the words are identical, the paper is identical, but "come home at once" lands differently. Nothing in her *seeing* has changed; everything in her *reading of* the seeing has.

(3) Maya replies that she cannot find the neutral telegram. Dev's picture assumes there's a moment where she sees the stairwell plain and unthreatening, and then a second moment where the fear is stamped on. But she has tried to catch that first moment — she'll stand at the stairwell door, breathe, and prepare to see it fresh — and the darkness and narrowness are already there, right at the start. If Dev's story needs a stage she can't find even when she's looking for it, maybe it isn't her story that's wrong.

(4) Dev brings up the line illusion where two equal lines with fins pointing different directions look unequal even after you've measured them and know they're equal. To him this proves the boundary is real and sits where he wants it: what you *know* can't always get into what you *see*, so what you *feel* can't be assumed to get in either. Maya's fear is on the knowledge side of the line, and the stairwell's look is on the seeing side, and her inability to catch the crossing doesn't mean there isn't one — you can't catch the moment you recognize your own mother's face either, but clearly there's a process underneath.

(5) Maya answers in two parts. First, the line illusion only shows that knowledge doesn't *always* reach seeing, not that it never does — one locked door doesn't prove the whole wall has no doors. Second, she offers the everyday case of buying a red car and suddenly seeing red cars everywhere: somehow what's on your mind really does change what shows up in experience, and she doesn't see why fear should be the one thing on earth sealed off from that. Dev says the red car case is attention — a spotlight pointed at the same stage, not a different play — and that Maya is running together noticing more with seeing differently.

Where they've stalled, and where my own confusion sits, is this. I've privately come up with a way of putting it: Dev thinks only the *caption* under the picture has changed, while Maya thinks the *picture itself* is different, and neither of them can say what would count as telling the two apart from the inside. And I've begun to worry it matters for more than words: if Maya's fear is in the seeing itself, then her sense of danger feeds on itself — she feels unsafe because things look unsafe, and things look unsafe partly because she feels unsafe — which seems different, and worse, than if she's just a scared person mislabeling a normal-looking stairwell.

So, three questions. First, who is right at each of the five moves, with your reasons — not just an overall winner. Second, is there any way, even in principle, that someone could tell from the inside whether the picture or only the caption changed, or is that beyond self-observation? And third, does the answer actually make the difference I suspect it makes to whether Maya's fear is well-founded, or have I, like both of them, been led astray by the picture-and-caption way of talking itself?
