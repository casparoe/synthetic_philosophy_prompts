I'm writing up a statement of my epistemological views for a graduate reading group on higher-order evidence, and before I circulate it I'd like an external consistency check, because I can no longer tell whether what I've assembled is a coherent package or an accident of what I happened to find persuasive in different papers on different weeks. Here is the list. (1) Evidentialism: what it is rational for you to believe at a time is fixed entirely by your total evidence at that time; there is no further, history-sensitive component of epistemic rationality. (2) Higher-order evidence is just evidence: learning that a belief of mine was formed by an unreliable process — that I was drunk when I judged the study, that I suffer from a bias I can't correct from the inside — is the same kind of thing as learning that a witness is unreliable; it enters the same evidential pool and gets weighed by the same rules, so there is no separate "meta-level" normativity layered on top of ordinary evidential support. (3) Anti-akrasia: I endorse something like Titelbaum's fixed point thesis — it is never rational to believe p while believing that one's belief that p is not rational; beliefs about what rationality requires are a special fixed point, and the akratic combination is always a mistake. (4) The confidence-drug case: in the standard case where I've learned that a drug I took reliably makes people drastically overconfident in whatever conclusion they happen to be considering, and where from the inside my first-order reasoning still looks impeccable, I hold that the right response is to sharply lower my confidence in my conclusion, even though I can't point to any flaw in the reasoning itself. (5) The a priori version: the same holds when the reasoning in question is a long proof I've checked several times — higher-order evidence of my own unreliability should reduce my confidence even in the face of apparent deductive support.

I keep having the feeling that some pair of these is fighting, but the candidates I identify keep dissolving when I look more closely, which makes me suspect I'm either missing the real conflict or rationalizing it away. My working intuition is that (3) is load-bearing and the rest can be arranged around it, but I don't trust that instinct, since (3) is also just the thesis I've most recently defended in conversation and I may be partial to it. What I want is an audit, not a survey: go through the pairs that pull against each other, rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, tell me which (if any) apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection and why, and then — this is the important part — take a definite position on which commitment has to give, or on what the cheapest revision is, and defend that verdict, rather than laying out the repair options and leaving the choice to me. If you think the set is actually consistent, argue for that conclusion and show me how the strongest objection to your reading fails, rather than merely asserting that it does.
