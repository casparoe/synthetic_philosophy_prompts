I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of science seminar that this term is split between the classical problem of induction and statistical learning theory, and a few weeks ago we had a dust-up I'm still chewing on. The topic was whether machine learning gives induction any kind of vindication — whether the fact that models trained by gradient descent generalize so well should make us more confident that nature is the kind of place where the future resembles the past. A classmate shut the whole discussion down by invoking the no-free-lunch slogan: as he put it, no learner can be justified in expecting to generalize, because for every learning algorithm there are possible worlds where it fails catastrophically, so expecting good performance is getting something for nothing. The instructor let it stand as though it were a theorem rather than a piece of shorthand, and since then the phrase has been circulating around the seminar with less and less content — someone used it against Occam's razor last week, and someone else used it to argue that random search is in principle no worse than any cleverer method. I've realized that nobody, including me, knows exactly what claim is being made when it gets deployed. I gather there is an actual formal result called the no-free-lunch theorem in learning theory (usually credited to Wolpert, I believe), with relatives in search and optimization, and I suspect the proverb has quietly come apart from the theorem in the way people use it.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that the no-free-lunch slogan could be expressing as applied to learning and induction — a menu running from the trivial to the radical? For each reading I want to know: whether it's actually true, whether it's a platitude or a substantive (and contested) claim, and — the part I really need for seminar — whether it would support my classmate's dismissal of induction. Please be careful to keep apart at least these strands: (a) readings that turn on averaging performance uniformly over the space of all possible objective functions or worlds; (b) readings about our priors over the problems we actually face being unknowable or arbitrary; (c) readings about logical underdetermination of the future by any body of past evidence; and (d) any reading under which the slogan is simply false — for instance, because once you fix any non-uniform measure over problems, or restrict the hypothesis class, some learners genuinely dominate others. For each reading, say what extra premise it needs and whether my classmate needs that extra premise to be true for his dismissal to go through.

Present it as a numbered list of readings I can bring back to seminar, each with a short label, a verdict (trivial / substantive-and-defensible / substantive-and-contested / false), and one sentence on whether it gets my classmate his conclusion.
