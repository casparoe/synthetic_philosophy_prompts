You are an experienced lecturer in moral and legal philosophy who has taught an upper-level undergraduate seminar on autonomy, coercion, and consent for several years. You are writing the final exam and want one essay question on what makes consent valid — specifically, a question that students cannot pass by reciting the familiar triad of deception, coercion, and competence, and that instead forces them to confront the structural questions behind that triad: what a theory of validity conditions is even for, and why the same triad is supposed to cover cases that work by very different mechanisms.

The question should be answerable in roughly ninety minutes and marked out of 20. It must present students with a stipulated theory to evaluate rather than a canon to summarize. Use the following one, which I want on the paper: call it the "deliberative risk" theory of consent, on which consent is invalid if and only if it was obtained in circumstances that predictably caused the consenter to run risks she would not have accepted after calm, fully informed deliberation. The question must then present three contrasting cases the theory has to handle:

(a) a patient who consents to surgery after the surgeon truthfully recites the operative risks but exaggerates her own success rate;
(b) a patient who consents to emergency surgery under time pressure generated by the emergency itself, with no one having manipulated anything;
(c) a person who agrees to a transaction only because the other party threatens to reveal something embarrassing, and a second person who agrees to an otherwise identical transaction only because the other party offers a large benefit that she created herself.

Students must do three things: judge whether the theory delivers the right verdicts in each case; diagnose precisely where it diverges from the best more traditional account — using the threat/offer pair to expose the baseline problem for coercion and the exaggeration case to expose the gap between wronging someone and invalidating their consent; and then either repair the theory or defend the traditional account against it. The question should be worded so that an answer earning an A must notice that the threat/offer pair and the deception cases turn on different mechanisms (roughly: how the option set was altered versus what the consenter believed), while a B answer treats them all as one generic phenomenon of "manipulation."

Before drafting, brainstorm briefly and privately about what the five most important moves in a top answer are, and only then write. Deliver three things:

(1) The exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any brief framing students need to understand the stipulated theory and the cases.
(2) A model answer of about 700 words that actually takes a position and argues for it, not a survey.
(3) A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth a stated number of marks summing to 20, with a one-line note for each point on the specific mistake that typically costs students those marks. Do not require the model answer or marking scheme to name particular authors or works; the credit should attach to arguments and distinctions, not bibliography.

After the marking scheme, add a short closing note: state how confident you are that the question is fair, that the model answer is defensible, and that the marking scheme separates genuinely better answers from merely well-rehearsed ones — and single out the one point in your marking scheme where you are least sure the allocation of marks is right, saying why you hesitate there.
