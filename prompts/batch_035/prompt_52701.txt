Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent most of my career as a toxicologist (endocrine disruptors mostly, some work adjacent to the lead exposure stuff), retired last year, and have since fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. Right now I'm on the free will and punishment literature, the Dennett vs. Caruso debate, and one thing that bugs me is that everyone argues for their theory before saying what the theory is even supposed to be for. So before I pick a side, help me write the spec.

What would a theory of criminal desert -- the thing that makes punishment deserved and not just useful -- actually have to get right? Give me the requirements list. I'll start you off with the ones that keep nagging me from my science days, and you add whatever I'm missing:

1. It has to be compatible with what we know about where antisocial behavior comes from: heritability studies, the lead literature (effect sizes there are not subtle), the fact that the brain systems behind impulse control aren't done maturing until the mid-twenties. If your theory says people choose their character from a standing start, it's dead on arrival as far as I'm concerned -- but then it owes me an account of what's left of desert once you concede all that.

2. It has to draw a principled line through the excusing conditions we already use (insanity, duress, juvenile vs adult) rather than just inheriting whatever line the law currently draws and calling it philosophy.

3. It has to deal with the manipulation cases people keep bringing up -- the neuroscientist who rewires your preferences, the cult that does it slower -- and tell me whether ordinary upbringing counts as relevantly different or is just a messier version of the same thing.

4. It has to survive luck. The drunk driver who kills a child and the one who makes it home did the same thing, and we sentence them very differently. Every desert theory I've seen either endorses that asymmetry with a straight face or explains it away badly.

5. It has to say something about the Caruso alternative: if you drop desert and keep incapacitation on the public-health model, why can't you preemptively confine the dangerous, and why do sentences get announced in advance and counted in years while quarantine lasts exactly as long as the disease? Maybe that's a requirement in disguise -- whatever replaces desert has to keep proportionality and a release condition somehow.

6. It should work outside the courtroom. If it can't make sense of me being angry at a colleague for sloppy data, or of a kid earning a gold star, it's a theory of a special case borrowing credibility from the general phenomenon.

Add whatever belongs on the list that I'm not seeing, tell me which items are non-negotiable and which are just the retributivist camp or the utilitarian camp dressing up their preferences as requirements, and point out if some subset can't all be satisfied at once -- my hunch is 1 and 4 together are rough, but it might actually be 4 and 6. Then, briefly, since I want to do that part myself: how do Dennett's compatibilist desert and Caruso's skepticism-plus-quarantine do against your list?

One more thing I keep wondering and never see addressed: does the theory have to be intelligible to the people it gets applied to, or is it fine for the justification of punishment to be a theory that 99% of defendants and jurors could never state? Seems like that's a requirement too, maybe the one everyone skips. Is it a real one or am I being an old scientist about it?
