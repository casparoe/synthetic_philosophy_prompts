I referee popular-science manuscripts part-time for a trade publisher, and the current one — a book pitched as "thermodynamics explains life, and life explains everything" — is built around a single analogy: every living cell is a Maxwell's demon. Ion channels, transporter proteins, and molecular motors are so many trapdoors selectively sorting order out of thermal chaos; evolution is the story of these demons getting better at their sorting over four billion years; and consciousness is the demon perfected. On that basis the author argues that life constitutes a local reversal of the second law, that aging is the demon's memory filling up, and that intelligence is nature's strategy for wriggling out of thermodynamics altogether. My co-referee, a thermodynamicist, thinks the book is sunk at the foundation: the demon was introduced as a challenge to the second law, and the Szilard engine and the Bennett–Landauer analysis of measurement and memory erasure are precisely why the challenge fails, so the cell-as-demon picture, taken seriously, argues against the book's conclusions rather than for them. The commissioning editor wants referee comments in a fixed compressed format rather than an essay. Please produce exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the correspondence map the analogy actually delivers — what plays the role of the partitioned box, the fast and slow molecules, the trapdoor, and, crucially, the demon's memory; (2) the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the book's specific claims, as opposed to (3) the disanalogies that don't — noting, for instance, whether it matters for the author's purposes that the demon sorts a gas in a closed box while a cell is an open system running on a chemical potential gradient it consumes rather than creates; (4) what the analogy smuggles in — whether the cell's molecular selectivity is information-gathering in the sense that forces the demon to pay Landauer's erasure cost at all, or mere passive stereochemistry, and whether the author can even keep the word "sorting" once that is settled; and (5) your verdict on whether any of the book's three conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, plus, if the demon must go, the one analogy you would put in its place that the author could still build a book on.
