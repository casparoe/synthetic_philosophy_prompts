I'm putting together a session on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities for students who already know the original machinery — the neuroscientist, the standby intervener, the poor agent who never suspects he's surrounded. The stock cases have two features I've come to suspect our intuitions are tracking rather than the absence of alternatives: the intervener is hostile, and the mechanism runs on stipulated infallibility. So I want a new case built to the following specifications, and the point of the exercise is to see whether the Frankfurtian verdict survives once those two features are stripped away.

The setting must be ordinary — a workplace, a committee, a hospital shift, nothing science-fictional and no one with superhuman predictive powers. The intervener's standby plan must be something she would genuinely carry out for reasons internal to her role, not because the case stipulates that she is omniscient or vindictive. She should be on the agent's side: her goal should coincide with what the agent, on reflection, already wants for herself, so that her readiness to step in is benevolent rather than adversarial. The trigger must not assume perfect foresight: specify a fallible but reliable early indicator that the agent is wavering, and be deliberate about where you place it, because the case has to face the timing problem head on. If the prior sign deterministically settles what the agent will do, a sharp student will object that the intervener's presence never did any work and the setup, not the case, removes the alternatives; if the prior sign leaves any slack, the leeway theorist will reach for that slack as a flicker of freedom. The case must secure the same act, not merely the same outcome: if the intervener's backup would produce something that differs from the agent's willing act in the way a coerced act differs from a chosen one, say so explicitly and either defend it as close enough for responsibility to transfer or redesign the mechanism. And the fact that the agent could not have done otherwise must be generated by the setup rather than announced — a case that simply stipulates "she had no alternative" is cheating, and part of what I want is to be able to show the students exactly where the generation happens.

Please write the scenario itself as a single paragraph, no longer. Then walk through the case in continuous prose: what the Frankfurtian says about it; the strongest line available to a defender of alternative possibilities, choosing between denying that the setup really removes the alternatives and denying that responsibility is intact, and telling me which you judge more promising here; which feature of the case is doing the work; and what happens to our intuitions if we swap the benevolent motive back for the usual malicious one while holding everything else fixed — if the intuition about responsibility shifts with the motive, tell me what you think it was tracking all along. Write the entire response as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
