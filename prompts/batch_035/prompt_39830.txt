been going down a rabbit hole on the old question of whether someone blind from birth who gets sight back could tell a cube from a sphere by looking alone (molyneux's problem). the thing that really stuck with me is one philosopher's analogy — berkeley i think — where he says the newly sighted person is like a blind man whos navigated his whole life with a cane. the blind man doesn't feel the objects out at the cane's tip, he feels pressure in his hand and has learned to read it as "chair, over there". so the claim is the newly sighted person doesn't see distance either, they just get patterns of light and color they'd have to learn to read, like the blind man learned his cane. and the analogy is supposed to back up the answer that molyneux's man would fail at first, since he'd basically be handed a brand new sense organ with zero training. my question is how far the cane picture actually holds. what's the mapping exactly — what plays the role of the cane, the hand, the learned reading, and the thing at the far end? where does it break down — like, once the blind man is really practiced, don't we want to say he kinda perceives the chair itself through the cane, and if vision is like that, does distance become genuinely visual after learning instead of just read-off? and the honest verdict: does the conclusion (he'd fail at first but could learn) actually follow from the analogy, or is the analogy carrying weight it can't support? a correspondence table would help, and also whether theres a better analogy out there (someone mentioned your two eyes pointing inwards at nearer things — maybe that does the work without any learning story at all)
