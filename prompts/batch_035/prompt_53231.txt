I'm teaching a seminar on metaethics that ends with G. E. Moore, and I want to do one session on the weird back half of Principia Ethica -- the chapter he called The Ideal, where after all the open-question stuff he actually comes out and says what's good in itself, and it turns out the two highest goods are the appreciation of beauty and personal affection. His whole thought experiment there is the method of isolation: imagine a world containing nothing but the thing in question, no observers, no consequences, and see whether it still seems better than a world without it. So he asks you to compare a world with just beautiful scenery in it against an ugly one, and a world where people love each other against one where they don't, and he thinks the answer is obvious enough to build ethics on.

Here's what I want to do with it: read Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through that chapter. The novel is almost a controlled experiment designed by Moore's worst enemy. Stevens is a man who has both of Moore's supreme goods available to him -- he lives in an aesthetically saturated world, he has genuine opinions about the English landscape and what makes it superior to foreign scenery, and there is a real offer of personal affection from Miss Kenton sitting right in front of him for two decades -- and he systematically sacrifices both on the altar of dignity, which turns out to be a conception of duty Moore would have to classify as purely instrumental, a means that Moore's framework can't even register as good in itself. And the novel ends with him crying at a bus stop in the rain while a stranger tells him to stop dwelling on the past.

So the question I want to pose to my students, and to you first: does Stevens's story vindicate Moore or refute him? On the vindication side you could argue the novel is one long intuition pump for chapter VI, that Ishiguro makes you feel in your gut exactly what Moore is claiming you can just see -- that affection is intrinsically valuable, that a life organized entirely around an instrumental good, however nobly conceived, is a kind of catastrophe you register as value-loss even when nothing bad happens to anyone in the hedonic sense. The tragedy works precisely because Moore is right about what matters. On the refutation side there's something to say too, and this is what I actually can't work out myself: Moore's method of isolation asks you to evaluate lives as if they were worlds, but the novel seems to insist that Stevens's affection for the landscape and his flight from Miss Kenton are the same phenomenon -- his aesthetic appreciation is itself a symptom of the repression, the safely non-personal form of the warmth he can't spend on a person. If that's right, then beauty and affection aren't two independent goods you can price separately the way Moore's method requires, and the isolation test smuggles in exactly the atomism about value that the novel undermines.

There's also the Lord Darlington problem, which is really the consequentialism problem: Stevens's defense of his own life is that he served a great man, and the man turned out to be a Nazi sympathizer, so the instrumental good was feeding an intrinsic disaster. Does Moore's distinction between what's good as a means and what's good in itself actually help Stevens at the end, or is the novel showing that a butler, unlike a moral philosopher, cannot know the consequences of anything and so dignity was the only rational bet available to him? I keep going back and forth on whether the book is kinder to Stevens than Moore's framework would be.

So: give me a reading. Where does Moore illuminate the novel, where does the novel resist or embarrass Moore, and what would you say the book adds to the debate that the Principia's arguments alone don't -- some piece of knowledge about value that only narrative delivers? I want to walk into class with a defensible position, not just the observation that the pairing is suggestive.
