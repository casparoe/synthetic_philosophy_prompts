I've been arguing with a colleague about moral testimony. She's a pessimist: she thinks that deferring to another person's word on a serious moral question — how to vote, whether to withdraw a parent's life support, how much to give to charity — is defective even when the source is reliable, because the person who defers ends up with a true belief without the moral understanding that first-person deliberation was supposed to provide. My credence that she's right — that there is a genuine, distinctive norm against moral deference on important matters, not just a sensible admonition to think for yourself — is about 0.4; hers is about 0.8. Give me your own credence, decomposed: the probability that beliefs formed through moral testimony can constitute knowledge in the first place; the probability that acting well on important moral matters requires moral understanding rather than merely true belief; whether moral judgment differs in kind from domains where deference is unproblematic, like mathematics, history, or weather forecasting; and any further terms you think belong, each with a number and one line of justification. Then tell me which term you are least confident in, what argument or case would move it most, and whether my colleague and I most likely disagree about the epistemology of testimony in general or about what acting well requires.
