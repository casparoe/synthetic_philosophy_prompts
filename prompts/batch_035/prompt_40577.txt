At a philosophy of mind reading group, a visiting speaker pressed a version of the zombie argument: we can coherently conceive of a world physically identical to ours but containing no experience, so such a world is possible, so physicalism is false. When someone objected that we can likewise conceive of water not being H2O, even though that's impossible, he waved it off with "conceivability is only defeasible evidence of possibility — that's all my argument needs." The same week, in our logic seminar, a classmate argued that because we can conceive of Goldbach's conjecture turning out false, its negation is possible, and he was laughed out of the room. I want to know what "conceivability is a guide to possibility" actually claims before I decide who deserves the laughter. Lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing — all-out entailment versus defeasible evidential support; prima facie versus idealized, rationally complete conceiving; conceiving that a sentence is true versus conceiving of a scenario that verifies it; positive versus negative conceivability; and, if you find it useful, two-dimensionalist distinctions between kinds of possibility. For each thesis, say whether it's plausible, what the standard counterexamples to it are, and whether it's strong enough to license the speaker's zombie argument, my classmate's Goldbach argument, both, or neither. Finally, tell me whether there is a reading under which the two uses come apart — one that rescues the visitor while sinking my classmate — or whether, once the slogan is precisified enough to help the one, it helps the other too.
