Our university endowment's investment committee, on which I serve, will vote next month on a proposal from our consultant to adopt what he calls an "ambiguity budget": alongside our existing market-risk limits, we would cap exposure to any asset whose return distribution cannot be estimated from a validated model or a long data history. His trigger case is a proposed allocation split between catastrophe bonds and a climate-adaptation infrastructure fund whose two candidate pricing models disagree substantially. Assets carrying well-quantified risk of the same expected return and variance would face no such cap. The policy thus treats unquantifiable uncertainty as worse than quantified risk, in the way subjects in Ellsberg's urn experiments treat an urn of unknown composition as worse than a 50/50 urn with known odds.

The committee is split three ways. One faction considers this prudent stewardship. A second argues it is a cognitive bias enshrined as policy: a perpetual-horizon institution that can learn from repeated decisions should price ambiguous assets by best-estimate probabilities, since ambiguity that would be crippling in a single one-shot choice washes out over many draws. A third faction holds that whatever we privately feel, ambiguity-averse investors as a class leave a premium on the table that we should be harvesting rather than paying.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It must recommend exactly one of: adopt the ambiguity budget as proposed, reject it outright, or adopt a modified version — and if a modification, state precisely what changes and why the change fixes what is wrong with the original. The memo should take a definite position on whether ambiguity aversion is normatively defensible for an institution like ours, not merely map the options. It should also engage the strongest argument on the losing side: if you endorse the cap, explain why paying an ambiguity premium is not simply irrational; if you reject it, explain what the committee should actually do when our best estimate is, by our own admission, somebody's guess.
