Write a dialogue between two mechanistic interpretability researchers, late at night at the office, arguing about whether the stuff they keep finding is actually in the model or just in their heads. One is a realist: the network has its own genuine ontology, superposition is a real fact about how it packs concepts into too few neurons, and interpretability is natural science — slowly discovering the features the model really uses, with sparse autoencoders just being our current, imperfect microscopes. The other is a deflationist: the residual stream is just matrix multiplications, any basis is as good as any other, and calling a direction a "deception feature" is like naming constellations — we're projecting our categories onto geometry because it's useful, not because the sky came pre-carved. Make them argue over concrete cases, not just vibes: an SAE feature that fires on code requests but also, embarrasingly, on sonnets about the sea (is that one messy feature or two that we failed to separate, and is there even a fact of the matter?); whether two models trained on the same data with different seeds count as having the "same" features; whether the fact that different methods — probing, ablation, circuits work — roughly converge is evidence that we're discovering something real, or just evidence that all the researchers share the same training data and the same biases about what counts as a concept. Both should be actually good at this: the deflationist needs a real reply to the convergence point, not a shrug, and the realist needs to concede somewhere that SAE features at least are partly artifacts of the decoder we trained. End it unresolved, but with them having narrowed their whole disagreement down to one precise question they both agree is now the crux — and let the reader tell what that question is without either character announcing it. Keep it maybe 600-800 words.
