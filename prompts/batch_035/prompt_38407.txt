Here's the thing I keep running into in my social philosophy seminar. We do the tragedy-of-the-commons material, and inevitably some student brings up the Scott Alexander-style 'Moloch' framing — the idea that whenever agents compete for selection (whether that's genes, firms, memes, or institutions), any restraint that's costly to the individual competitor but valuable in the aggregate gets eroded, because the agents that keep the restraint lose out to the ones that drop it. Fine as far as it goes, but the strong version of the claim is much bolder: it says this happens even under full information, even when every competitor would prefer the coordinated outcome, and even among agents smart enough to see exactly what's happening to them. My co-author, who I had coffee with after our last seminar, is basically sold on the strong version — he puts something like 0.85 on it. I hover around 0.55, and I'd like a second opinion on where exactly our disagreement lives.

Concretely, here's the claim I want your credence on: in any sufficiently large population of competing agents, where each agent's survival and replication depends on out-competing rivals, and where the competitive behaviors impose costs on third parties or on the agents' own long-term interests, competition will reliably strip away individually-costly, collectively-valuable restraints — even assuming rationality and perfect information throughout. Please decompose this for me rather than just giving a gut number. Some terms I suspect belong in the decomposition, though feel free to add or replace: (a) whether the selection pressure actually operates on the unit doing the restraining — e.g. firms compete, but the people inside firms have their own preferences about how their firm behaves, and it's not obvious they get selected out just for keeping a scruple; (b) whether coordination mechanisms (reputation, regulation, social norms) can durably block the race, or whether they're themselves subject to the same trap — the regulators or norm-enforcers also being competitors who profit from defection; (c) whether the 'bottom' is genuinely a bottom, or whether what looks like a race to the bottom is often just a race to efficiency whose costs we notice more than its gains; (d) whether 'sufficiently large' does the work it's supposed to, since small-group dynamics with repeated interaction and cheap gossip seem to escape the trap a lot.

Give me a number and one line of justification for each term, then show how you combine them into the final credence. Then tell me which term you're least confident in and what kind of evidence or argument would move it the most — I'm especially curious whether you think the real crux is (a), because my co-author thinks competition eventually selects for the kind of person who doesn't keep scruples, which strikes me as doing a lot of unstated work about how fast personnel turnover has to be relative to how fast the scruples matter. If you think my decomposition is just wrong, say so and build a better one — I care more about the structure being right than about any particular number. (Also, if it matters for calibrating your answer: my 0.55 isn't indecision, it's genuine half-credence; I've watched the same argument go both ways in the literature on price wars and on academic publishing, and I can't tell which of those is the more representative case.)
