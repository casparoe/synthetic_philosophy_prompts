My cousin is a hospice nurse, and at a family dinner a couple weeks ago she mentioned something that's been stuck in my head ever since. She said some of her patients on strong opioids will tell you the pain is still there — they can locate it, rate it, describe its quality — but that it just doesn't bother them anymore. Like the pain got demoted from something happening to them to a fact on a chart. She said the care team even splits on what to do: some figures if the patient is comfortable, the sensation itself isn't a problem anymore, while others say pain is pain and you should still be trying to eliminate it.

Two friends who were at the dinner, Marcus and Dee, took that and ran with it, and I lost the thread somewhere in the middel. Can you referee it move by move?

(1) Marcus said the patient cases settle it: if a person can have the sensation and not mind it, then the badness can't be built into the sensation. What's bad about pain is the aversion — the hating it, the wanting it gone. Pain without the aversion is just another bodily feeling, no worse than pressure or warmth, and the point of treatment should be to remove the suffering, not the sensation.

(2) Dee said that only shows a drug can switch off a reaction to pain. You can't find out what a thing is by studying cases where the machinery has been chemically altered — that's like concluding hunger isn't about food because appetite supressants exist. For normal people in normal states, pain and minding it travel together, and that package is the concept we actually care about.

(3) Marcus said it's not just the drugs. He said he'd read about stroke patients with a certain kind of brain damage who report the same thing — they feel pain, rate it, and just don't care, some even laugh when the doctor pinches them. If that's real, then pain-without-badness isn't a contradiction you can define away; it's an actual thing in the world. So calling pain intrinsically bad is just describing the usual bundle, and the badness part lives in the attitude, not the sensation.

(4) Dee granted that the cases exist but said he's proving the wrong thing. Even if the badness is an attitude rather than the sensation, the attitude is a response to the sensation — it doesn't float free. On her picture, the sensation is bad-making the way a threat is fear-making: plenty of people stay calm under threat, and that doesn't make threats not scary. She said defining pain by the drugged and damaged cases is like defining vision by its blind spots — the normal cases are the rule, not the exception that reveals the deep truth.

Neither would budge after that. Please go through the four moves, rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and say who's more right overall. If there's something both of them are missing — including whether my cousin's two treatment camps map onto a real distinction — I'd like to hear that too. Please write your answer in American English.
