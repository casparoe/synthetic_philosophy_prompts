I've been reading the decision-theory corners of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and in all that time I have never once encountered a story about counterfactual mugging that treats it as anything more than a puzzle-box illustration — the characters exist to state positions, the coin exists to be flipped, and the narrative exists to be dispensed with once the intuition pump has fired. I'd like you to write the story I've never been able to find. It should be roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, and it should be recognizable as a story first — with a character whose interior life matters — and only secondarily as a decision-theory parable.

The setup I want: an ordinary person with a broadly rationalist temperament — a mid-career professional, not a philosopher, not a LessWrong character in disguise — is approached by a predictor. The predictor's track record is good but not perfect: publicly verifiable, with something like nineteen unambiguous successes out of twenty public calls, including at least one failure that the predictor itself acknowledges and has never explained away. This matters to me, because the textbook version of the problem lets you dodge the hard part by treating the predictor as infallible, and I want the story to close that escape route. The predictor describes a coin flip that, per its own account, came up tails — the branch in which the character was never asked for anything — and asks for a payment meaningful to the character: enough to hurt, not enough to ruin. The character cannot now verify which branch they are in, and the payment, on the best available evidence, is probably for nothing. That "probably for nothing" has to be felt, not noted.

Two further requirements. First, at some point in the story — before or after the main event, your choice — the same character, or someone close to them, must face a structurally parallel situation that is an XOR blackmail: a letter, a threat to release something costly unless a payment is made, from a party with a comparable reputation record. I want the two situations sitting next to each other in the reader's mind so that the question becomes unavoidable: does whatever reasoning told the character to pay the mugger also tell them to pay the blackmailer, and if not, where exactly does the asymmetry live — in the causal structure of how the two offers were generated, in what each counterfactual version of you would want, or somewhere the standard blog treatments never quite reach? The story should dramatize that question; it should not answer it by authorial fiat.

Second, some constraints on how the philosophy is carried. No character may use decision-theory jargon — no naming Newcomb, Omega, causal decision theory, or any of the acronyms we coined for these positions. If the characters have views, they should have their own homegrown vocabulary for them, the way people actually argue. At least one character should be permitted to articulate the refusal-to-pay position in terms a sympathetic reader could endorse — someone who says, in effect, that you don't pay for counterfactuals you can't see, and who is not written as a fool. The paying position should have a genuine cost: something lost, someone disappointed, a moment where the character's choice looks like it might just be vanity dressed as coherence. And the ending should leave the question open — not a cheap ambiguity where nothing is decided, but a specific, concrete outcome from which a reader could still reconstruct a case for either side.

What I'm asking for, in the end, is the story that the Sequences kept gesturing at and never wrote: fiction in which the choice about an unobservable branch of the world is an event in a life, with stakes that survive the reader's disagreement with the character's decision theory.
