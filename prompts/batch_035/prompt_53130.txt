I'm a PhD student in philosophy and next week I'm presenting to our departmental workshop a paper defending a layered reply to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. Quick sketch: I concede the whole regress — the causa sui horn that swallows every you-can-make-yourself-who-you-are reply — but claim the arguement still fails, because it slides between two readings of how you are: the base-level dispositions you never chose (temperament, intelligence, whatever your childhood did to you) and the practical character you actually act from, which is constantly being maintained or revised by your own reflective activity. Responsibility doesn't need self-creation, it needs ownership at the second layer only, and the regress never touches the second layer because it only ever attacks attempts to get responsibility for origins.

The audience: two people who think the Basic Argument is basically sound (one of them will say my second layer is just more of how you are and the regress restarts one level up), a P.F. Strawson person who'll ask how my ownership condition differs from just being the target of the reactive attitudes, a Frankfurtian who'll claim I've reinvented wholehearted endorsement but weaker, and a Kantian who'll want to know where reasons-responsiveness went. Draft my prep sheet: objections in rough order of arrival, phrased as this crowd would actually phrase them, a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where each reply is soft. End with \answer{...} naming the one objection on the list that could genuinely sink the paper.
