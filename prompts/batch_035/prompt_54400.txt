Please describe the most important implications of the dogmatism paradox for how I should handle disagreements I can see coming. Concretely: in my lab we pre-register predictions, and I have committed myself in writing to a hypothesis about memory consolidation that our just-completed study supports. Next week a colleague who favors a rival theory will present criticisms of our methods at lab meeting. I have read a draft of her criticisms, and I am confident, partly from her reviews of similar designs in the past, that her central objection rests on a mistake — she treats our control condition as lacking a feature it in fact has. This puts me face to face with the paradox: if knowledge that misleading evidence is on the way entitles me to discount it in advance, then my belief seems insulated from an upcoming counterargument I haven't yet heard, which looks exactly like the dogmatism the paradox warns about; if it doesn't entitle me, I seem required to treat as open a question I have already, in my view, correctly settled. Please give the most important implications of the paradox for how I should prepare for and conduct that meeting — including whether having pre-registered my prediction changes the epistemology at all, and how I ought to behave differently when a criticism is anticipated and pre-judged versus merely unanticipated. Take a definite position: say whether rationally discounting anticipated misleading evidence differs in kind from simply ignoring it, say where you would draw the line between justified advance dismissal and dogmatic insulation of my belief, and commit to what I should actually do in the meeting itself rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
