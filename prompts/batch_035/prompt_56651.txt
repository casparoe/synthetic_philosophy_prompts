A chapter in a popular book on reasoning under uncertainty that I've been reading has a section on anthropics, and since I have no background in this area I'd like a careful audit of it before I repeat any of it to others. Here is the gist of what it says, as faithfully as I can reproduce it. It introduces two principles: the self-sampling assumption, which it glosses as the instruction to reason as if you were a random sample drawn from the set of all observers who have ever lived or ever will live, and the self-indication assumption, which it presents as the claim that you should give more credence to hypotheses on which there are more observers, so that between two theories that fit the data equally well, the one with more people in it wins. It then draws two morals. First, it says the self-indication assumption is what generates the Doomsday Argument, since if you think bigger-population hypotheses are more probable, you should expect humanity to be vast and long-lived, and your own early birth rank then comes as a statistical surprise that drags your expectations back down toward imminent doom. Second, it describes a thought experiment it calls the presumptuous philosopher, in which two rival theories of cosmology fit all our evidence equally well, one positing vastly more observers than the other, and a philosopher armed with the self-indication assiduously... rather, confidently declares the bigger theory to be certainly true without doing any experiments. The chapter closes by taking this to show that anthropic reasoning is essentially the gambler's fallacy pointed at oneself: the mistaken feeling that you, a single data point, can shift the probability of world-scale hypotheses.

Something about this summary strikes me as internally inconsistent, but the author is a professional science writer and I can't locate the mistakes myself. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher working on anthropic reasoning would accept as standard, which are subtly misleading (for instance, where a defender of one of these principles would insist the view has been misstated), and which are simply backwards. In particular, I'd like to know whether each of the two morals actually follows from the principles as the author has stated them, and whether his final verdict about anthropic reasoning is a fair assessment of what either principle claims or a rhetorical flourish built on the errors earlier in the section. It would also help if you could flag any point where the author has quietly simplified a real issue that philosophers treat as serious and unresolved, so I know where the summary's confidence is doing more work than its arguments.
