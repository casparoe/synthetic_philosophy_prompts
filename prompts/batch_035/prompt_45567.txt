I am drafting a paper on the afterlife of Kierkegaard's critique of the press, and I need a section that reconstructs, as carefully as the evidence permits, what he would have said about the media environment of roughly the last twenty years. My worry is that this question always gets answered with a one-line verdict — the internet is the public's final victory, Kierkegaard saw it all coming — and I want something far more differentiated than that.

Assume the following background (correct me if anything is off): in Two Ages (1846), written in the aftermath of his deliberate provocation of the Copenhagen satirical paper The Corsair, Kierkegaard presents the press as the executor of a leveling process in which envy, passionless reflection, and phantom collectives — the public, the crowd — dissolve concrete distinctions between individuals. He insisted throughout his authorship that he wrote for the single individual rather than for any collective, while publishing much of that authorship under pseudonyms, whose authorship he also publicly acknowledged in retrospect. Late in life, in the pamphlets of his final year, he turned the same attack on Christendom itself.

Within that framework, reconstruct his probable verdicts on each of the following, keeping the verdicts distinct rather than collapsing them into a single denunciation of modernity:

1. Anonymous commenting. Is the anonymous commenter an instance of the leveling public, or is there a principled difference between the anonymity of the press and his own pseudonymity? If you think there is a difference, state precisely what makes it — accountability, the pseudonym's consistency as a persona over time, the aesthetic control it gave him, or something else? Bear in mind that he published simultaneously under his own name, under elaborate pseudonyms such as Constantin Constantius and Anti-Climacus, and that he picked his fight with The Corsair under yet another, while eventually taking responsibility for the whole authorship.

2. The influencer. A single named person whose entire significance consists in being watched by the public, monetized through attention — an individual only in the mode of being an object of the crowd's gaze. Is this the counterfeit individuality that leveling was supposed to produce, or does the framework lose its grip here, since he never considered that the market might select for celebrity rather than sameness?

3. The shaming pile-on. He courted ridicule from The Corsair in order to expose the dynamics of the crowd. Would he see the modern online pile-on as a pure expression of leveling envy, or might he distinguish some forms of it — coordinated criticism that topples one specific powerful figure — from the diffuse, responsibility-diffused leveling he diagnosed? What in his actual texts supports the line you draw?

4. Algorithmic curation. No human editor, no rival satirical sheet, but a ranking system trained on engagement. Does his concept of the press survive the loss of the human editor? If the leveling is now done by no one in particular and everyone at once, through a metric rather than a journal, is that the phantom public finally completed, or does the absence of any identifiable person who could be envied change the diagnosis?

5. The paid newsletter. A named author with a bounded list of paying subscribers, writing at length for readers she can address directly. Of all modern formats, this looks closest to his own rhetorical situation — and yet it is parasitic on the attention economy. Would he regard it as a defensible refuge for the single individual, or as passionless reflection with a subscription fee?

Constraints: ground each verdict in concepts and arguments he actually had — leveling, envy, the public as phantom, reflection without passion, the single individual — and say which of them does the work in each case. Flag wherever the textual evidence about his views is thin enough that your reconstruction becomes substantially projection. Identify at least one pair of cases where two of his commitments, applied consistently, pull in opposite directions, and argue which commitment he would have sacrificed. Finally, tell me honestly which of the five phenomena his framework simply cannot classify, and what minimal addition — one concept, not a whole new theory — would make it classifiable.
