After a career of some thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, most of it in precision measurement, I have in retirement been reading widely in Indian philosophy, and in my reading group I keep encountering an analogy that I cannot yet evaluate cleanly, though I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. Several members, and a good deal of popular writing on the subject, explain karma as a moral law of nature: the universe keeps a ledger, they say, just as it keeps an account of energy, and every deed of a certain moral character is eventually met with a proportionate experience of the corresponding kind, without anyone administering the balance. One member has put it that karma is to ethics what conservation of energy is to physics — a deep structural feature of how things go, not a rule that anyone enforces, and therefore not in need of an enforcer. The picture is then used to support two conclusions: first, that moral desert is an objective feature of the world rather than a human convention; and second, that since the universe settles its own accounts, institutions of justice are at best a convenience and at worst an interference — much as a physicist would regard someone claiming to top up the energy budget by hand.

My training makes me want to take the analogy literally and see how far it carries. So here is what I would like from you. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the conserved quantity; what the state variables are; what the dynamics are that carry the account forward between deed and consequence; and, if the analogy is to conservation laws proper, what symmetry is supposed to underwrite the conservation — in physics, after all, conservation of energy is not a brute fact but a consequence of time-translation invariance, and I would want to know what the karmic counterpart of that could even be. Then mark where the mapping breaks: I notice, for instance, that conservation laws in physics are exact, measurable, and hold on every timescale, whereas in the karmic picture nothing is measured, the settling time is unspecified, and the proportionality between deed and consequence is asserted rather than read off. But I do not know which of these disanalogies are the ones that matter for the conclusions my reading group is drawing, so please distinguish the fatal ones from those that merely make the analogy loose. Finally, tell me whether either conclusion — that desert is objective, or that human institutions of justice are redundant — survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and if the conservation framing cannot carry that weight, say whether some other physical analogy (a relaxation to equilibrium, perhaps, or a statistical tendency rather than an exact law) would serve the karmic idea better, or whether the idea is doing something that no physical analogy can capture.
