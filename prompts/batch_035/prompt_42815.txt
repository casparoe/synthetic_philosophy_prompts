My study group spent last term on the epistemology of mathematics, and we've rebuilt a Benacerraf-style argument that has now metastasized into something none of us can live with. I'll state it as my friend defends it.

(1) A belief is justified only if we can, at least in principle, explain why our method of forming that belief is truth-conducive rather than its success being a matter of luck. (2) Any such explanation must make the belief's genesis sensitive to the fact believed: had the relevant fact been otherwise, our method would not have delivered the same verdict. (3) Mathematical objects, if Platonism about them is true, are non-spatatiotemporal and causally inert, so they can leave no trace in the causal history of any mathematician's brain. (4) Therefore, had the mathematical facts been different, mathematicians would believe exactly what they in fact believe: the sensitivity condition fails. (5) So if Platonism is true, mathematical beliefs are unjustified. (6) Some mathematical beliefs — that every natural number has a successor, that there are infinitely many primes — are justified. (7) So Platonism is false.

So far, so familiar. But my friend now runs (1)–(2) at everything. Non-natural moral facts: causally inert, so moral knowledge is impossible, so non-naturalism is false. Modal facts: same. Logical truths: he says the argument shows either there are no logical facts or we cannot know any, and he's cheerful about this because he thinks logic is somehow grounded in our inferential practices.

Here is where it starts to prove too much for me. I've noticed that (4) is doing its work entirely through a counterfactual — "had the mathematical facts been different" — and if mathematical truths are necessary, as my friend agrees they are, then there is no possible situation in which the antecedent holds. So either the counterfactual is vacuously true and the sensitivity condition fails trivially for every necessary truth whatsoever, or it fails in a way that can't be an epistemic defect. But it's supposed to be the mechanism of the whole argument. My friend's response is that the vacuity is exactly the point: for empirical facts we can tell the sensitivity story (had the bridge been weak, our inspection would have gone differently), and for anything where we can't tell that story, we don't have knowledge. That seems to make our knowledge of the external world hostage to a difference between kinds of truth that has nothing to do with how we actually come to know them.

Tell me which step to give up. I'd like to keep (6); I have no particular attachment to Platonism; and the thought that we can't know any necessary truth alarms me more than nominalism does. What I most want to understand is whether something equivocates between (2) and (4) — between a causal-explanation requirement and a counterfactual-sensitivity requirement — and if so, which reading (1)–(2) were plausible under, and what the argument left standing costs the Platonist who accepts it in its original, restricted form.
