I serve on the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized public museum, and we keep relitigating, case by case, whether an artist's immorality should bear on what we buy, what we show, and how we frame it. Write a decision procedure the committee can actually run in a single meeting. It must first separate the three ways immorality can matter here: as a flaw in the work itself (its content or perspective endorses, or is structured by, something abhorrent); as a biographical fact causally upstream of the work (the wrongdoing produced or funded it, or the work was made through mistreatment of its subjects); and as a consequence of our institutional choice (whether the artist is alive to be platformed, where revenue and reputation flow, who is harmed by the display). Second, it must treat acquiring, exhibiting, and contextualizing as three independent levers rather than one up-or-down verdict. Third — and this is the part our past debates always fudge — it must not assume that aesthetic merit can be assessed in isolation and then weighed against the moral facts: it has to say what to do when learning of the immorality changes the experience of the work itself, so that the isolation strategy fails on its own terms. Then run the procedure on three cases: a long-dead painter whose cruelty is undisputed but whose canvases are morally inert; a living filmmaker whose crimes are notorious and whose celebrated films never touch them; and a photographic series whose reputation for empathy was built through systematic deception and exploitation of its vulnerable subjects. Mark the points where the procedure must hand the question back to human judgment rather than pretending to settle it, say plainly which theory of the relation between ethical and aesthetic value the procedure is operationalizing, and include at least one case where it yields an answer I will not like.
