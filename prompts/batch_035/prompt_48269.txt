I've been listening to a podcast on Victorian intellectual history, and a recent episode spent a long segment on Henry Sidgwick that left me uneasy. The host's story went roughly like this: Sidgwick lost his Christian faith as a young Cambridge don, and the loss left a theological hole he spent the rest of his life trying to fill. He filled it in two ways. First, he systematized utilitarian ethics in The Methods of Ethics, giving the doctrine the rigorous treatment Bentham and Mill never managed. Second, he threw himself into the Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found in the early 1880s and led for years, holding séances in his own home with his wife Eleanor and pursuing mediums across England. The host claimed these were really a single project: since utilitarianism demands that people sacrifice their own happiness for the general good, Sidgwick supposedly understood that his ethics was only rational if the universe eventually rewarded the virtuous, and so the entire purpose of his psychical research was to assemble scientific proof of an afterlife that would underwrite what the host called the categorical imperative of utilitarianism. Along the way the host explained that Sidgwick's famous dualism of practical reason is the doctrine that mental events and physical events can each cause the other, in both directions. The segment closed by saying that after decades of work with mediums Sidgwick concluded the evidence for survival of death was overwhelming and counted himself vindicated, and that we should therefore read the Methods of Ethics as a work of natural theology in disguise rather than a work of moral philosophy.

Here is my problem. I have actually read the Methods, at least most of it, and I remember it arguing that rational egoism and utilitarianism can each claim the support of reason, with no way to prove one should win -- Sidgwick found this genuinely disturbing and groped toward the idea that the conflict would dissolve only if the universe were morally ordered. So the host's motivational story doesn't seem wholly invented, and some of it matches what I already believe: Sidgwick did abandon his Trinity fellowship in the 1860s because he could no longer affirm the articles of faith, and he really was a central figure in the Society for Psychical Research. But my memory of the book doesn't feel like a work of natural theology at all, and I've also read that Sidgwick was famous for exposing fraudulent mediums rather than crediting them.

So I want an audit, claim by claim. Which parts of this summary would a scholar of Sidgwick accept, which are oversimplifications of things he did hold, and which are straightforwardly wrong? In particular I'd like to know what he actually meant by the dualism of practical reason, what he really hoped psychical research could accomplish, how he and his wife actually conducted their investigations and what he concluded at the end of his life, and whether it's fair to describe either the Methods or the psychical work as filling a religious void. And then the bottom line: does the host's conclusion -- that the Methods should be reclassified as apologetics -- follow even from the parts of the summary that are accurate? Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
