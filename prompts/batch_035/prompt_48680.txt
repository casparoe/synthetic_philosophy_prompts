I coordinate intake at a wildlife rehabilitation center, and I've been quietly freaking out about the decisions we make every single day. A Canada goose comes in with a shattered wing -- do we treat, euthanize, or try to place it as a non-releasable captive? A fawn hit by a mower, a squirrel a cat "played with," a raccoon that might be rabid but might just be concussed. Right now it's mostly vibes plus whatever the most senior person on shift feels like. Last month a new volunteer who studied ethics told me about negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics -- the idea that relieving suffering matters more than creating happiness, maybe even lexically more -- and something about it stuck with me, because honestly our whole job is animals in pain, and almost none of our choices make them happy, they just stop the hurting. But I also don't want to adopt a philosophy that's secretly monstrous (she mentioned something about it implying you'd painlessly destroy the world if you could, which people apparently argue about whether that's even a fair reading), so I'm not looking to convert. I want the usable core.

So here's my ask: turn suffering-focused ethics into a decision procedure I can actually run at intake, in maybe ten minutes per animal, with a vet tech on call but often not physically present. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as enough of an answer to move to the next question, where the procedure terminates in a euthanasia call or a treatment call, and where it has to punt to a human because it's a genuine values question no checklist can settle. It needs to handle the cases that actually break us: the animal whose pain is treatable but whose life afterwards would be permanently compromised in the wild; the healthy animal we legally can't release and someone wants to keep as an education animal; and the grey zone where "it's suffering now but might fully recover" is doing all the work. Run it on two real-ish cases: a red-tailed hawk with a wing fracture that pins it for six weeks of uncertain recovery, and a non-releasable, healthy raccoon that a local zoo has offered to take.

Two things I need you to be honest about. First, run at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- I suspect it exists -- and show me how the procedure defends it or where I'm allowed to override it. Second, and this matters to me: I want the response to clearly separate what's actually settled among people who think about this stuff from what's genuinely contested. Like, is it settled that death doesn't harm the animal itself, or is that a live debate my procedure is silently taking a side on? Does the suffering-focused view really commit you to the world-destroying conclusion, or is that a caricature? Flag every place the procedure smuggles in a contested assumption rather than presenting it as just common sense.

Keep it under 800 words. If the procedure needs more room than that, cut cases, not the honesty about where it breaks -- I'd rather have a procedure I know the failure modes of than a tidy one that lies to me. And feel free to tell me if some part of what I asked for just can't be proceduralized, that's useful too.
