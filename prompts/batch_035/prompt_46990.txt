I got into an argument with my brother-in-law over the holidays about whether law can really be law when it's thoroughly wicked, and now I can't stop chewing on it. I've been reading about the Hart–Fuller debate on my own and I keep flipping sides. So let me just lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and I want someone to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them, and which of my verdicts has to go.

The cases:

1. Apartheid South Africa. Statutes published in advance, clear, enforced by courts, anyone could look up what the rules were. Verdict: definitely law. Wicked, but law.

2. Nazi Germany, the informer scenario. Someone denounces a personal enemy to the authorities for a stray remark, knowing it'll mean death, and there's a Nazi-era statute making the remark a crime. But the whole system was procedurally rotten — decrees were whatever the leadership felt like, retroactive stuff, "law" meaning anything the party wanted that day. My verdict: I want to say it wasn't really law, and I think the postwar German court was right to treat the informer as a murderer rather than shrug and say the statute allowed it.

3. A made-up regime that is substantively decent — no persecution, courts actually fair — but procedurally chaotic: laws constantly renumbered, two statutes on the same subject flatly contradicting each other, no one can tell what's in force. Verdict: law, just bad at it. Like a language people still speak even though the grammar is a mess.

4. A regime that is procedurally immaculate — everything prospective, public, clear, stable — but the content is pure evil: a caste system, some people legally non-persons. Verdict: law. I hate it, but calling it non-law feels like a cop-out that lets the lawyers off the hook.

5. A prison gang that has its own internal court, sanctions, and a recognized procedure for changing its rules. Verdict: not law, obviously. But I can't say why without sounding like I think law = whatever the state does, which my brother-in-law says is circular.

6. International law. Verdict: law, but thin law — I gather Hart says somewhere it resembles primitive law because there's no settled rule of recognition. Fine, I buy that.

And here's the pair I can't reconcile: in (2) I wanted to say not-law, but in (4) I say law even though (4) is at least as evil. The only difference I can point to is procedural rot versus procedural cleanliness, which is basically Fuller's line about the ways a system can fail to make law at all. But then his test says apartheid in (1) is law, my decent-but-chaotic regime in (3) is law, evil-but-clean in (4) is law, and Nazi Germany in (2) is not — and honestly that mapping feels right to me, except it means whether something is law has nothing to do with how evil it is, which is nearly the opposite of where I started and my brother-in-law is going to be insufferable about it.

I also had a tentative principle of my own: a system counts as law iff its rules are identified by pedigree — source, not merit — except with an escape hatch for extreme cases. But I can't state the escape hatch without it looking like "evil enough," and that's exactly the thing case (4) forces me to deny.

So: is there a principle that gets all six verdicts? If not, which verdict do I drop, and would you drop it in Hart's direction or Fuller's? And tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: an occupying military power that issues one-off, clear, public orders — a curfew with penalties, say — with no legislature and no courts of its own, just commands backed by force. Law or not?

Keep it casual, I'm not writing a paper. I just want to know if my intuitions are salvageable.
