At a philosophy of mathematics reading group I attend, two members — a doctoral student and a retired programmer — got into a long argument about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. It went roughly as follows.

1. The programmer argued that the thesis is a well-confirmed empirical hypothesis. Turing machines, lambda calculus, Post systems, register machines, and other independently proposed formal models all turned out to compute exactly the same class of functions, and that convergence, he said, is like several independent measurements of the same quantity agreeing — evidence that we have latched onto a real feature of the world.

2. The student replied that all those equivalences are theorems about formal systems, not observations, so they can't be evidence in any empirical sense. On her view, the thesis functions as a definition — an explication of the pre-formal notion of effective calculability — and treating it as a hypothesis confuses the mathematical fact that the various formal systems are equivalent with the entirely different claim that any of them captures the informal concept.

3. The programmer pushed back: if it were a mere definition, nothing would stop us from defining 'calculable' as, say, computable in polynomial time, or as decidable by some device whose powers outrun any Turing machine, and we plainly resist such redefinitions. Moreover, if someone built a physical machine that correctly settled questions no Turing machine could, we would abandon the thesis, and a claim we would abandon in those circumstances is ipso facto empirical. He added that the thesis might in the end follow from physics, since whether such devices are possible is plausibly a question about what the laws of nature permit.

4. The student answered that what blocks the arbitrary redefinitions is not empirical content but the paradigm cases built into our usage of the word: a calculation proceeds by finite means, mechanically, without insight into infinitely many steps at once. So the thesis is closer to a claim defended by conceptual analysis of an informal notion — one that can be argued for, but neither proved mathematically nor cleanly falsified, since if some apparatus seemed to decide undecidable questions we could always deny that it was calculating rather than merely undergoing a natural process and reporting an outcome.

They eventually gave up on each other and asked me to referee, which I was in no position to do. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each of the four moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then give your own view of the thesis's epistemic status: is it a definition, an empirical hypothesis, something in between, or something none of these categories fits? And if you think both parties share a mistaken assumption that keeps the dispute alive, please identify it.
