Quick context before the ask: I teach the giant intro ethics lecture here — around 300 students, four TAs, and final letter grades are ultimately my signature on the line. Our department policy says course grades come from numerical scores, but it doesn't say how the number turns into a letter, and every semester I get the same fight: a student lands at 89.6, the TA emails me asking whether that's an A- or a B+, and I never have a principled answer, just vibes and guilt. It's obviously a sorites problem wearing a registrar's costume — no single tenth of a point can be what flips the letter, and yet some tenth of a point apparently does — but I'm tired of doing freshman-year philosophy of language on a deadline while an actual freshman waits on the email.

So here's what I want: a decision procedure my TAs can actually run when a score lands in the borderline zone (say, anything within a point of a cutoff), one page they can keep open while grading. It should have questions in an order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and where the procedure terminates with a plain "no bump" versus where it stops and hands the call to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Ground it in what the vagueness literature actually says, but translate honestly. I'm thinking: epistemicism says there IS a fact about where the A-/B+ line is and we just can't know it — what does that counsel, a published sharp cutoff we pretend is the real one? Supervaluationism says the boundary sentences are neither true nor false but the cutoff-in-general structure is fine — what does that counsel, maybe a gap where we resolve cases individually? Degree theories want everything graded rather than cut — but our registrar literally cannot record "0.7 of an A-." Part of what I want the procedure to do is be honest that each theory, operationalized, just relocates the sharp edge somewhere else (if we round, the 89.55 becomes the new battleground), and to say where the TA should notice that and shrug.

Two constraints the procedure has to respect: (1) whatever it tells us to do has to be defensible to the grade-appeals committee, meaning consistent across students and not obviously arbitrary on its face; and (2) it has to run in the five minutes a TA has before moving on to the next stack. If some step can't be done honestly in five minutes, say so and design around it.

Then run it on three cases: (a) the 89.6 regrade request from a student who came to office hours all semester; (b) a regrade appeal where re-scoring three exam answers moves a student from 88.9 to 89.4, still below the line, and now they want the borderline procedure triggered again; (c) the TA who thinks the whole procedure is missing the point and wants to grade holistically with no numbers at all, on the theory that numbers create the false precision. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and flag every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume. And tell me what the procedure cannot do — I'd rather know its failure modes now than discover them in an appeal hearing in May.
