I've spent the last decade in the accommodations office of a large employer — the office that decides which adjustments for disabilities and health conditions the company will actually pay for. Somewhere along the way I noticed that we seem to operate on a rule nobody has ever written down: we fund the removal of obstacles to activities that nearly everyone in the workforce can do, and we decline to fund the pursuit of activities that matter enormously to particular individuals. A deaf employee gets sign-language interpretation for every meeting; an employee who is clinically crushed by the sense that his life has no meaning outside competitive sailing gets nothing, even when the amount that would transform his situation is smaller than what we routinely spend on interpretation for a single employee. The line we draw doesn't track how much suffering is on either side of it, or how much money is involved. It tracks something about the *kind* of disadvantage.

I've been trying to work out what exactly that something is, and my rule keeps misbehaving at the edges. Consider a few cases. First, severe myopia is an impairment of a near-universal capacity if anything is, but because glasses are cheap and the condition is common, nobody calls it an accommodation at all — the employee just buys the glasses. So severity and rarity seem to enter my rule somewhere, not just the type of capacity blocked. Second, there are employees who, after an injury or a long illness, report no needs whatsoever; their wants appear to have contracted to fit their reduced abilities. My rule says they require nothing, which feels wrong in the opposite direction from the sailing case — we somehow sense they are worse off precisely because they've stopped wanting things. A friend told me this is sometimes called adaptive preference, and I can't tell whether that's the same problem as mine or a different one. Third, an employee whose health collapses unless she can live in a particular climate: her condition is simultaneously a barrier to universal activities and a costly personal requirement, and my two categories collapse into each other.

When I describe all this to people with more education than I have, they say something about philosophers and expensive tastes, or about whether fairness is supposed to mean equal money, equal happiness, or some third thing. But the expensive-tastes label doesn't fit my cases, because nothing in my office's practice tracks expense: the sailing man's tastes aren't costly so much as singular, and the interpreter costs far more than we'd ever spend on him. Does the rule I've reverse-engineered have an established name in the literature, and is the kind of equality it delivers — where the benchmark is what nearly everyone can do rather than what a particular person needs to flourish — a recognized position? If it is, I'd like to know how its proponents handle my edge cases, and how the nearest better-known positions would draw each line differently. If it isn't, I'd like you to say so plainly and then coin a term for it, with a definition sharp enough that I could classify new cases by it rather than by gut feeling.
