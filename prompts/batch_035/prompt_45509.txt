I've been reading about how elections actually get run in different countries, and something about Australia's system keeps bugging me. I want to think it through with someone who actually knows social choice theory, so let's say you're a professional philosopher who's taught Arrow's impossibility theorem for years and has strong opinions about how people misuse it. Think carefully before you commit to anything, and please don't rush to a verdict.

Here's the practice, in case you don't know it. For the Australian House of Representatives you rank every candidate on the ballot. If there are eight candidates and you only number seven boxes, your ballot is invalid — it doesn't count at all, not even for your first choice. So even if you know literally nothing about candidate number six, and you'd despair equally at candidates seven and eight, the state still demands you put all of them in some strict order. And that order can decide the election, because preferences "flow" through the count in rounds until someone has a majority. Parties hand out how-to-vote cards instructing supporters exactly how to number every box, and there's a whole documented phenomenon of "donkey votes" where people just number 1, 2, 3 straight down the ballot, which tells you a decent chunk of these lower rankings are basically noise. And yet the whole thing seems wierd to me only sometimes — most of the time it feels completely natural, which is exactly what makes me suspicious.

What I want to know is what this institution presupposes about preferences, both individual and collective. It looks like it presupposes that every voter has a complete preference ordering over all candidates, that this ordering exists before the ballot is issued (so voting is reporting a pre-existing state, not making a decision on the spot), and maybe, deeper down, that there's a real "will of the voters" out there which the count discovers. That last one is what Arrow's theorem is supposed to cause trouble for, right? I don't need a lecture on the theorem itself, but I do want you to use it where it actually bites, and no where it doesn't.
