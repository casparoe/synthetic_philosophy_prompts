I'm a PhD student in behavioral decision research, and for the past year a group of us — a couple of economists, a philosopher, a machine-learning person — have been going in circles on the question of whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. The debate has stalled completely, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another round of the argument itself.

To make the stall concrete: on one side is the orthodox view. Rational choice theory says a rational agent's choices depend only on feasible futures; past expenditure should matter at most as evidence about expected returns, never as a reason in itself. This view gets its bite from money-pump-style arguments and from the observation that the sunk-cost "effect" in the lab is robust, systematic, and expensive. On the other side are the dissenters, who come in at least three flavors and don't always agree with each other: (1) the information-rationalizers, who say much apparent sunk-cost sensitivity is really the agent treating past investment as diagnostic evidence about project value — but note this can't be the whole story, since the effect persists in designs where the subject knows the investment carries no information; (2) the people who appeal to nonseparable preferences — if utility isn't additive over time-slices, then a history in which I abandon a costly project can be genuinely worse than one in which I finish it, without anything irrational being true of me, and some of them argue the money-pump arguments quietly assume separability rather than proving it; (3) the resolute-choice crowd, who follow McClennen in thinking that a plan chosen ex ante can bind a rational agent at a later choice point, so that "honoring" the sunk cost is really honoring the plan.

There's also an irritating comparative wrinkle: Arkes and Ayton famously argued that nonhuman animals don't commit the sunk-cost error, and took this to suggest the effect is a bug of uniquely human, rule-based reasoning rather than a deep rationality failure — though the animal data has since been contested both ways, and my group can't agree on what's actually at stake there either.

Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps aren't disagreeing about one thing. The orthodox are answering a question about idealized, dynamically consistent agents with separable preferences, and the dissenters are answering a question about what could be vindicated in beings like us — bounded, self-trustful, needing policies to survive preference instability — so that each side's "rational" is a different target, and the disagreement is partly verbal, partly about whether idealization is even the right standard for judgment. But I can't tell whether that's a real diagnosis or just a way of saying both sides are right on their own terms, which would make the debate not stalled but finished — and it doesn't feel finished.

So: is this a substantive disagreement with hidden moving parts, a verbal dispute about "rationality," or two different projects (normative idealization vs. practical advice) happening under one heading? Where exactly does the work get done — in the separability assumption, in the treatment of temporal selves as one agent or many, in the evidential versus constitutive role of the past? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a theorem, a better-specified experiment, a conceptual distinction nobody has drawn cleanly yet, or an argument that the question is ill-posed as standardly framed? I'd like an answer that engages what each side actually claims, including the tensions among the three dissenting flavors, rather than just adjudicating the headline question.
