In my ethics elective last week, two classmates got into a prolonged argument about whether it is legitimate to settle a moral question on someone else's say-so, and our teacher refused to referee it, so I am asking you to do it instead.

The occasion: Priya recently became a vegetarian, but only after her older sister, who had spent a semester seriously researching factory farming, told her that eating factory-farmed meat is wrong. Priya never worked through the arguments herself; she trusted her sister's judgment and changed her diet on that basis.

(1) Theo said Priya's position is defective: she is borrowing her sister's conscience rather than forming her own judgment, and a moral belief you cannot defend is not one you really hold.

(2) Priya replied that nearly all her beliefs are secondhand. She believes germs cause disease and that Antarctica is covered in ice purely on the testimony of people who checked, and no one calls those beliefs defective; demanding firsthand verification of everything would be absurd.

(3) Theo drew a line between factual and moral testimony: with germs, the testifier's judgment bottoms out in observations anyone could in principle make, whereas with morality there is nothing behind the judgment except more judgment. Deferring to her sister doesn't answer Priya's question; it just relocates it into her sister's head.

(4) Priya countered with expertise: her sister spent months on the question and is more reflective than she is, and we happily defer to doctors and lawyers, who also exercise judgment rather than mere observation. Refusing to defer to a more careful thinker on a moral question is not conscientiousness but arrogance.

(5) Theo conceded part of this but refined his claim: perhaps testimony can hand you knowledge that something is so while failing to hand you understanding of why — the way a student might know a theorem is true because a trusted teacher certified the proof, yet be unable to reconstruct it. And perhaps in morality, unlike geometry, acting well requires the understanding-why, because the point of moral judgment is to respond directly to the reasons themselves, not merely to register a verdict. Someone who acts on borrowed conclusions is like someone reciting an apology she does not understand.

(6) Priya answered that this relocates the complaint from testimony to understanding, and that if moral testimony transmits knowledge but not understanding, it is in the same boat as almost everything else we believe; moreover, if Theo's demand for first-hand moral reasoning were right, we could hardly hold any moral belief, since parents and teachers shape nearly all of them.

Theo's last word was that parents and teachers transmit reasons and methods, not bare verdicts — moral education is instruction, not testimony — so the cases differ in kind, not degree.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each numbered step, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify each rating. Then say who comes out more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed. I have gone back and forth on this and honestly cannot settle it myself.
