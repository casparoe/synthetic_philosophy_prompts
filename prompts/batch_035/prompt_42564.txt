Every few years my friends and I do a movie rewatch night, and this time it was The Truman Show. Afterwards we got into an argument I haven't been able to settle. One friend said Christof is just a nudger scaled up — the same thing as pension plans that enroll you automatically or cafeterias that put the salad at eye level, only with a bigger budget. I'd been reading some popular articles about that stuff beforehand (the whole "libertarian paternalism" idea, where you arrange the environment so people make better choices while staying technically free to choose otherwise), and it struck me that Christof makes almost exactly the same defense: he insists the door is never locked, that Truman could leave if he truly wanted to, and that the world he built is cleaner and safer than the real one outside. So on paper he looks like the ultimate paternalist with the ultimate choice architecture.

But the more I think about the actual devices in the film, the less they look like nudges. A default you can ignore is one thing; manufacturing a lifelong terror of water by staging your father's drowning in front of you is something else entirely. Same with the schoolteacher shutting down young Truman's dream of being an explorer because supposedly everything's already been discovered, the travel agency poster of a plane being struck by lightning, the traffic jam that melts away the moment it has done its job, and Marlon arriving with beer and a speech precisely when Truman starts asking questions. Some of these shape choices, some seem to remove options, and some (the manufactured fears) look like they work on Truman himself rather than on any menu of options. I never studied philosophy, so I don't know where the accepted lines are — soft versus hard paternalism, influencing versus restricting, the question of whether a paternalist intervention has to be transparent or easy to opt out of to count as benign — but I'd like a reading of the film that uses those distinctions seriously rather than just calling Christof a villain.

Please read The Truman Show through the lens of paternalism and nudging: classify what's actually being done to Truman, say whether any part of Christof's project could survive the standards that defenders of nudging set for themselves, and be honest about where the film resists the reading (after all, Truman's life in Seahaven does look genuinely pleasant — a best friend, a job, no real hardship — so is the deception a failure of paternalism, or a disturbing success, and does the film ever actually show the harm?).
