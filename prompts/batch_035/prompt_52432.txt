I teach a combined seminar in philosophy and economics on rational choice theory — sixteen students, mostly upper-level undergrads, one ninety-minute session — and next week we reach backward induction. I want an in-class activity built around a take-or-pass game in the shape of the centipede: two players alternate, each turn either takes the pot (taking a larger share, e.g. 80/20 in the taker's favor) or passes, which grows the pot, with a fixed and commonly known number of remaining turns. The pedagogical goal is that students run into the tension themselves: the textbook argument proves that a rational pair will never get past the first decision node, and yet no pair in the room will play that way. I've run prisoner's-dilemma exercises before and what worries me is that students will treat this as a warm-and-fuzzy trust game and never notice the logical puzzle, so the design has to make the *argument* visible, not just the cooperation.

Concretely, I'd like two rounds with results recorded so we can compare them in the debrief. Round 1 should be the plain fixed-length game, maybe six decision nodes, payoffs in course tokens or candy — no real money, but the numbers should be chosen so the temptation to take is genuinely felt. Round 2 should change exactly two things: first, before play begins, the whole class reads a short paragraph stating the backward induction argument out loud, so that the reasoning and the assumption of everyone's rationality are out in the open — an informal stab at making them common knowledge; second, the game now includes a die roll at each node that can end the game exogenously (you decide whether the pot is then split, forfeited, or passed to the non-mover, and defend the choice), so there is no certain final node for the induction to anchor to. I'm curious whether the read-aloud shifts play toward earlier takes, and I want the debrief to press on whether what the classroom behavior shows is that the theory is wrong, or that its assumptions fail, or something subtler about reasoning over nodes that the argument itself says will never be reached.
