I am preparing a short position piece on the epistemic case for democracy for a graduate seminar and would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here are my commitments, roughly in order of how confident I am in each:

(1) The strongest epistemic defense of majoritarian democracy is Condorcet-style: if each voter is even slightly better than chance at arriving at the correct answer, and their votes are independent of one another, a sufficiently large electorate will almost surely arrive at the correct answer and can be expected to outperform a small panel of experts.

(2) On most technically complex policy questions, the average individual voter is worse than a coin flip, owing to rational ignorance, pervasive misinformation, and the sheer difficulty of the material. Most voters simply are not in a position to evaluate, say, the likely effects of a given change in tax or monetary policy.

(3) Democratic deliberation — public argument, town halls, structured discussion prior to voting — is not merely fair or respectful but epistemically valuable, and collective decisions should be preceded by it whenever feasible.

(4) Other things being equal, the larger the voting body, the more reliable its collective judgment; hence on any issue where direct democracy is practical, a referendum should be preferred to leaving the decision to legislators.

(5) Compulsory voting would strengthen the epistemic credentials of democratic outcomes, both by enlarging the electorate and by making it more representative of the population as a whole.

(6) The competence premise in the theorem should be read conditionally: voters are competent at judging which candidates and parties will advance their interests and values, even where they cannot evaluate the technical merits of policy instruments; and since the "correct answer" in an election is which option would advance the citizens' interests, this is the competence that actually matters for the theorem.

I honestly do not know where the problems are, and I do not have a clear sense of which commitment I would revise first if forced. Please go through these six commitments, tell me which pairs or subsets are genuinely in tension and which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, explain what would have to give and what that revision would cost me. I want a ranked diagnosis of my own position, not a general survey of the literature. Please answer in American English.
