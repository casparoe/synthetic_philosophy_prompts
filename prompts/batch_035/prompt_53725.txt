In my reading group on machine sentience, two analogies keep doing all the argumentative work, from opposite sides. The skeptic says a large language model is like a weather simulation: however fine-grained the model, a simulated storm leaves no one wet, so a simulated pain hurts no one, and the fact that engineers chose to simulate pain rather than pressure systems is morally irrelevant detail. The enthusiast says that implementing a mind's computation in silicon is like porting a chess program to different hardware: the algorithm doesn't care what runs it, and whatever moral status attaches to the computation should travel with it across substrates just as the chess-playing does. Audit both analogies. Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the storm, the wetness, the simulation, the hardware, the ported program — and identify where each mapping breaks. Pay particular attention to what each analogy quietly imports: the hurricane picture treats pain as an effect a process has on the world rather than something intrinsic to the process, while the chess picture assumes that the morally relevant thing is cleanly individuable as "one computation," which sits awkwardly with the fact that a model's processing is spread across training runs, weights, and transient forward passes. Then say whether either analogy can actually carry the conclusion it was being used to support — that present-day systems do, or do not, warrant moral concern — or whether the disagreement was really a disagreement about which analogy to trust. If neither survives, sketch a better one. End with a final line in the form \verdict{hurricane}, \verdict{hardware}, \verdict{both}, or \verdict{neither}, naming which of the two analogies, if either, survives the audit.
