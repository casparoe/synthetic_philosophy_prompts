I've been going down a rabbit hole on this idea from some contractualism discussions I stumbled into, that moral complaints can't be added up across people — the classic case being one technician who has to spend the night in a transmitter room so millions can keep watching some broadcast, where the claim is you can't justify making him do it by adding up everyone's tiny bit of lost enjoyment, because each person's complaint is just their own and the numbers don't count. I think I've turned that idea into something that proves way to much, and I can't find where it breaks:

1. A rule for how we may act is okay only if nobody has a complaint against it strong enough that they could reasonably demand a different rule.
2. Complaints don't add up. A million people each with a small complaint don't collectively have one big complaint — for any single person, their complaint is just their complaint, whatever anyone else is feeling.
3. When I drive to the store instead of walking, I impose a risk of death on every pedestrian and driver I pass — tiny, say one in a hundred million per trip, but real.
4. From any one pedestrian's point of view, the rule allowing convenience driving says: strangers may gamble with your life to save themselves a twenty minute walk. That's a complaint about the most serious thing there is — your life — not about a minor inconvenience.
5. That complaint's strength can't depend on how many other pedestrians also have it (that's the forbidden adding-up), can't depend on how many drivers benefit (same reason), and can't depend on the size of the risk either — a small chance of my death is a harm of the same kind as a big chance, just scaled down, and if a big burden on one person can't be outweighed by benefits to millions, a small risk on each of them shouldn't be outweighed by my convenience.
6. So every pedestrian can reasonably reject the rule permitting convenience driving, and the driver's convenience is just one more complaint that isn't allowed to be outweighed by anything.
7. So driving to the store when I could walk is wrong — and the same goes for anything that imposes any risk on anyone, which is basically everything.

My gut says the trick is somewhere in (5), but I can't name it, and every fix I try seems to cost me the transmitter room conclusion I started with. If I say the pedestrian's complaint is weaker because the risk is smaller, fine — but doesn't that mean everyone's individual complaint gets scaled by probability, and then a million small complaints really do feel like a lot of expected harm, back to adding things up again? Where exactly does this break, and what do I have to give up to say so?
