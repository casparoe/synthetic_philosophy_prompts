I spent thirty-eight years as a natural scientist — the last twenty-five modelling nonlinear dynamical systems, first in plasma physics and later in climate — and since retiring I have been reading history seriously for the first time, mostly with a group of former colleagues who, like me, have no professional stake in the field. One debate we keep circling has a strangely stalled quality to it: whether the course of history is fundamentally contingent, the kind of process in which a small perturbation — the proverbial lost horseshoe nail, a delayed messenger, a somewhat shorter nose — can cascade into wholesale change; or whether contingency is a surface phenomenon, with the slow, deep variables of geography, demography, and economics doing the real explanatory work. Historians in the lineage of E. H. Carr's What Is History?, and the Annales school with its emphasis on the very long term, have tended to treat the counterfactualist's 'what if' as a parlour game at best, something that smuggles the historian's wishes into the past; the other side, of which Niall Ferguson's Virtual History is the loudest recent example, answers that unless one can say how things could have gone otherwise, one cannot say why they went as they did. The two camps have been at this for decades, publish in the same journals, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: in my own field we would frame this as an unresolved empirical question about which dynamical regime a system is in — whether small perturbations to the initial conditions damp out over the relevant timescales, or amplify without bound. The structuralists implicitly claim the first, the contingency partisans the second, and neither side can produce the data that would settle the matter, because history, unlike a tank of turbulent fluid, runs only once. But I can see an alternative diagnosis: that the two sides are not disagreeing about the facts at all, but about what an explanation of a historical outcome must look like — something close to a covering law over slow variables, versus a narrative that traces the actual causal path from the actual initial conditions. If that is right, the dispute may be largely verbal, with both parties giving correct answers to different questions under a single heading.

I would like a diagnosis, argued from what each side actually claims rather than a survey of the literature, together with an honest assessment of whether anything could count as progress in this debate — a conceptual distinction, a methodological reform, some class of cases where the two frameworks yield different and testable predictions about, say, the persistence of an institutional arrangement after its originating accident. And if your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected here at all, I want to be told that plainly, with the reasons stated, rather than handed a diplomatic both-sides summary. My colleagues and I have no reputations at stake; what we want is to understand what kind of disagreement we have been listening to.
