Next month I defend my dissertation chapter before the full faculty. The chapter argues that Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility is sound and, more importantly, that it survives the standard compatibilist replies. The core argument I develop is roughly this: an agent acts as she does only because of how she is — her character, will, values — at the moment of action; so to be morally responsible in the basic-desert sense (deserving blame or punishment in a way that would make it deserved even if there were no further good consequences of blaming), she would need to be responsible for how she is; but being responsible for how she is would require having made herself that way through some earlier act, which in turn would require responsibility for an even earlier self, launching a regress that can only terminate in a state of affairs for which no one is responsible; since nothing can be causa sui, no one is ever responsible in the basic-desert sense. My second chapter claims the argument is independent of the free-will debate: it applies with equal force under determinism and under libertarianism, since any indeterministic contribution to action would, from the agent's standpoint, be a matter of luck, and luck grounds responsibility no better than determination does.

The audience: a mid-sized analytic department whose senior members are nearly all compatibilists in the reactive-attitudes tradition. They take P. F. Strawson as their touchstone, frame responsibility through the interpersonal stances of resentment, gratitude, and indignation, and speak of holding responsible as an exercise of our moral sentiments rather than a theoretical judgment about desert. Among the juniors, one works on reasons-responsiveness and guidance control, one on ownership conditions for agency, and there is a visitor who already denies basic desert and so will push me to draw conclusions more radical than I am prepared to defend. I expect the supervisors' questions to come at me in their vocabulary — talk of the quality of our wills, of what resentment is a response to, of demanding an impossible revision of human life — rather than in the terms Strawson's critics usually use.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this audience is actually likely to raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my defense, each phrased as one of them would phrase it in a question period. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where the reply is soft, and whether I should concede anything up front to buy goodwill. Make sure to include the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if asked and fumbled, is most likely to turn the room — and say what makes it so dangerous and how I should handle it if it comes. Bear in mind the visitor who agrees with me: flag where he will press me to go further than my argument licenses, and what I should say to keep my conclusion narrower than he wants it.
