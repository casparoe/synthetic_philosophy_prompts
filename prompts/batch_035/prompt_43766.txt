I spent about fifteen years as an odds compiler and then an in-play trader at a sportsbook, and these days I do consulting for a couple of smaller books plus some work on the exchanges. Over the holidays my nephew, who's doing a masters in philosophy of science, was telling me about this apparently eternal argument his professors have about what probability *is* — whether it's long-run frequencies, degrees of belief, or some propensity thing. He kept using examples with coins and urns and I kept thinking, I already know all this, we just never call it that. So: restate the three-way debate between frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity theories for me in the vocabulary I actually think in — lines and prices, the overround, sharp money vs square money, steam moves, closing line value, calibration of tipsters, liquidity on an exchange, that kind of thing. Be as faithful as you can, don't dumb it down, and don't smuggle in some assumption from my world and pretend it's part of their debate.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophers' disagreement map cleanly onto stuff every trader deals with, which parts only fit if you bend them, and is there anything in their debate that has literally no counterpart in my world? My working guess is that we settled this implicitly decades ago: the market price is the subjectivist's degree of belief made public, my calibration spreadsheet over thousands of markets is the frequentist's long-run frequency, and the propensity people are basically the guys who insist there's a true price for a single match that the market is trying to discover — the ones who'll tell you a steam move is the market converging on reality rather than just a whale with a model. But tell me honestly if that's too glib, and whether whatever's left over points at something bookmaking can't say or at something the philosophers are confused about.

Keep it under 750 words — dense is fine, padding isn't.
