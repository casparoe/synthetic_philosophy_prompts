The phrase "the banality of evil" has started showing up everywhere in my life and I've realized I don't actually know what it claims. Last week a guy in my book club used it to describe the middle managers at a social media company who tune the engagement algorithm — not monsters, he said, just people doing a job. Then a podcast I listen to used it about clerks who processed pollution permits, in almost the opposite direction: for him it seemed to mean something like "evil doesn't need evil people, just paperwork." And my aunt used it to mean the perpetrators were basically dim, small-minded men, which felt like it was doing almost no work at all as a claim.

I know the phrase comes out of Hannah Arendt reporting on the Eichmann trial, and I've heard she later pushed back on readings of it, something about Eichmann being thoughtless rather than stupid or demonic — but I don't want a history lesson. What I want is a menu. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses this one phrase could be asserting? For each one: is it a near-tautology, a substantive empirical claim, or false? And crucially, say which reading would actually support the uses I described — like, which version gets the book club guy his conclusion (that the algorithm-tuning managers are implicated even though they're ordinary), and does any version support my aunt's use (which seemed to slide from "ordinary" to "not really culpable")?

The thing I keep snagging on is whether the phrase is a claim about the perpetrators' inner lives, or about the structure of the situations they're in, or just about how evil looks from the outside. Those feel like three very different claims that all fit inside the same five words. Sort it out for me in a list I can actually use, please — and flag which readings are doing real argumentative work when someone deploys the phrase to end a discussion, versus which are just mood lighting.
