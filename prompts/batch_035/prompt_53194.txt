My flatmate reckons he's found a proof that the doctrine of double effect — the thing Philippa Foot got the whole trolley business going for back in 1967, before Judith Jarvis Thomson dubbed it the trolley problem — actually forbids the bystander at the switch from ever turning the trolley, and I honestly can't find the step I'm suppossed to reject. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) It can be permissible to bring about a harm you merely foresee, as a side effect of achieving some good, but it is never permissible to bring a harm about as a means.
(2) A harm is brought about as a means when you are counting on it: had the person not been there to be harmed, your plan would collapse and you'd have to do something else.
(3) Bystander at the switch: turning is permitted, since the bystander isn't counting on the one's death — had that one wandered off the side track, the five would be saved just the same, and he'd still flip the switch.
(4) Footbridge: pushing is forbidden, since the whole plan is that the large man's body stops the trolley — had he wandered off, nothing stops it and the plan collapses.
(5) Now, and this bit is his own twist, suppose the side track loops back round and rejoins the main track just short of the five, so that if the trolley isn't stopped by hitting the one, it comes round and kills the five anyway. Had that one wandered off here, the plan collapses (the trolley isn't stopped, it loops back, the five die), so by (2) the bystander is counting on his being hit, and by (1) turning in the loop version is forbidden, exactly like pushing.
(6) But whether the side track loops back round half a mile away, behind a hill the bystander can't see past, cannot change the moral status of the movement of his hand. From where he stands the two cases look identical, he'd give the same one-line explanation of what he's doing and why, and the shape of the track beyond his line of sight is just geography.
(7) So turning is forbidden in the loop version, and since the plain version differs from it only by geography, turning is forbidden there as well. The bystander may never turn — whenever flipping the switch would kill one, he has to stand there and let the five die.

He is definately delighted with all this, which is the part that worries me, since he thinks it's fine to conclude the bystander must always just stand there. My instinct says (6) is where it smells, but whenever I try to say what the difference is, it comes out as "he secretly intends the one's death whenever the track might loop", which seems worse than conceding the whole thing. So: which step do I reject, why did it look so reasonable, and what does rejecting it cost me?
