Retired physical chemist here — 35 years of staring at spectra, now reading philosophy for fun. There's a pattern I keep bumping into and I can't find the right handle for it.

Whenever I look at any message — a page of text, a signal we might beam at Alpha Centauri, a stretch of DNA — it looks to me like three different jobs are getting done at once. First, something announces "I'm a message, I'm not noise" — like how an artificial signal would presumably be pulses at prime-number intervals, or how a stretch of DNA has machinery that says "start reading here." Second, something tells you *how* to read it — which alphabet, which code, which conventions. Third, there's the actual content, the payload. And I noticed the same thing in my spectroscopy days from the other side: an emission line in a stellar spectrum is also a message of sorts, and it does the announcing and the decoding-instructions and the content all in one go, because the wavelength maps onto an element via physics you already know.

Two things bug me about the three-way picture. One: the middle job seems to be in a bind. Whatever carries the "how to decode me" part has to be readable *without* already knowing the code, or you get a regress — instructions for reading the instructions, forever. So maybe the decoding key is never actually in the message at all; maybe it lives in what the receiver already knows or does automatically, and the "instructions" are just triggers for pre-existing machinery. Two: in real messages the three jobs blur together hopelessly. Repetition tells you it's a message AND helps you crack the code. A start codon flags and decodes at the same time. The tidy three-part split keeps leaking, and I wonder whether anyone has studied the leakage rather than just the split.

So, questions. Is there an established framework for carving messages up this way, and for asking where the meaning actually sits — in the message, in the receiver, somewhere in between? Is the regress problem (the key can't be in the same code it unlocks) a recognized thing with a name? And is there a term for messages deliberately built to be decodable by a stranger with no prior agreement, like the ones people designed for extraterrestrials? I have a vague memory of a book from the late 70s, full of wordplay and Escher prints, that had a chapter about where meaning lives — but I lost my copy decades ago and don't trust my memory of it, so tell me if that's a real thread to pull or a false alarm. And don't just give me a book title: I want to know what the framework says about where the edges are, which cases break the carving, and what to call the blurry double-duty parts if nothing established covers them.
