I am preparing a seminar in philosophy of science on the concept of genetic explanation, and a popular science book I have been reading closes its chapter on behavioral genetics with a summary of heritability that I want checked before I recommend it to students. The author writes: "Twin and adoption studies compare the resemblance of identical twins with that of fraternal twins, some raised together and some apart, and from the difference they estimate heritability — the proportion of variation in a trait, within the population studied, that tracks genetic variation. For IQ these studies converge on roughly fifty percent, which means that for any given child, about half of their intelligence comes from their genes and about half from their environment. And because heritability measures the relative contributions of nature and nurture, it is a stable property of the trait itself — which is why the estimate has held up across decades even as schooling has improved." My training is in philosophy, not genetics, and while something here strikes me as a conflation I have seen flagged in the literature — a slide from a population-level statistic to a claim about individuals — I do not trust my own ear for where this author goes wrong. Please audit the passage claim by claim: identify what a behavioral geneticist would accept as stated, what is loose but ordinary shorthand, and what is a substantive error that would mislead a reader. Specifically, I want your verdict on three things: whether the individual-level reading ("half of any given child's intelligence") follows from anything true in the passage; whether the claim that heritability is a stable property of the trait, rather than of a population in a particular range of environments, is defensible; and whether the closing remark about the estimate holding up as schooling improved supports the author's larger point or actually undermines it. Finally, tell me which of the errors, if any, matter for the philosophical issues my seminar addresses — namely, whether heritability figures in genetic explanation at all, and whether a high heritability estimate in one population licenses any conclusion about the malleability of the trait in another.
