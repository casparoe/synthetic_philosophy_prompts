Okay so I work at a policy institute and a big chunk of my job is writing short briefing memos about AI governance, maybe 1500 words, due fast, for readers who are smart but have zero history background and zero patience. And the single most annoying pattern in our drafts is the historical analogy. Every time something happens with AI, somebody reaches for one: it's like the printing press, it's like nuclear weapons, it's like electricity, it's like the Industrial Revolution, it's like the internet, it's like railways. Sometimes the analogy is carrying the whole argument — like the actual claim is "therefore we should/shouldn't regulate now" and the analogy is the only support given. My senior editor is basically allergic to this and keeps writing "so what?" in the margins, but when I push back on analogies I honestly don't have a principled reason, I just have vibes. Sometimes an analogy seems fine to me and she hates it and neither of us can say why.

So what I want from you: a decision procedure I can actually run on an analogy in about ten minutes while drafting, not a treatise on analogical reasoning. Concretely I'm imagining something like: the questions to ask in order about any proposed analogy, where each question has a way of telling "this check passes" from "this check fails" from "can't tell without real research", a rule for when the analogy is allowed to do argumentative work in the memo vs. when it's demoted to flavor, and a point where the procedure stops and says this one is a values call about how much weight to give the past at all, which is above my pay grade.

The hard part I want you to actually grapple with: the thing that makes an analogy good or bad here isn't surface similarity, it's whether the causal mechanism transfers. Like, "nuclear weapons" and "the printing press" are both "a transformative technology" but the mechanism by which one concentrated power in a handful of states and the mechanism by which the other diffused power to everyone who could buy a press are basically opposite, and that difference should decide which one you cite when arguing about whether AI capability will concentrate or spread. I want the procedure to force me to dig out the mechanism instead of pattern-matching on vibes. But I also don't want it so demanding that no analogy ever survives, because honestly sometimes a well-chosen analogy is the only way to make a reader feel the stakes in 1500 words, and a memo that's pure abstractions doesn't get read.

Then run the procedure on three cases from stuff we've actually had in drafts: (1) "large AI models are like nuclear weapons, so we need something like non-proliferation treaties and export controls on chips"; (2) "AI is like the printing press, so attempts to control it from the top will fail and we should plan for diffusion instead of containment"; and (3) "AI is like the Industrial Revolution, so the big effects will be slow and uneven and mostly good on net if we just don't panic". Include at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'm personally very attached to the printing press one, so if the procedure kills it, kill it anyway and tell me why. And be honest about which steps of the procedure are just my judgment wearing a lab coat, since I'd rather know that going in than pretend I've got an algorithm when what I've got is a slightly disciplined gut.

One more thing: I can't ask you for sources and my fact-checker only has so much time, so build the procedure around structural features of the cases (who could access the technology, how fast it spread, how visible it was to governments, whether the costs fell on the people who benefits) rather than around specific dates or numbers, and flag for me the places where I really do need to verify a historical detail before the memo goes out. Thanks!
