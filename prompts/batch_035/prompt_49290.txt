For the migration unit of my political philosophy seminar I want to replace one of the assigned articles with a film, and I have settled on Casablanca. The unit covers the standard open borders debate: the argument that freedom of movement across borders should rank among the basic liberties, alongside freedom of occupation and residence, and that birthplace is an accident of birth no more defensible as a basis for life chances than feudal station; the reply that political communities, like individuals, may have a legitimate interest in self-determination that includes some discretion over membership; and the familiar compromise view that even a state with a general right to exclude acquires special duties to admit the persecuted. Casablanca seems like an unusually rich pairing, and I would like a reading of the film through these debates that I can trust in front of a class.

The obvious starting point, and I think the deepest one, is that the film is about exit rather than entry. Everyone in Casablanca in December 1941 is stuck trying to get out, and the instruments of their entrapment are pieces of paper: the letters of transit that cannot be questioned or rescinded, the exit visas Captain Renault sells or withholds. The human right to leave any country, including one's own, is recognized in the Universal Declaration (Article 13) alongside the right to return, but there is no corresponding right to enter anywhere else — and the film seems to dramatize precisely this asymmetry, though I am not sure it does so deliberately. So a first question: what exactly is the injustice the film depicts? Is it that movement is rationed at all, or that the rationing is corrupt — that Renault sells what the state should allocate on some other basis? The Bulgarian couple at the roulette table, with the wife preparing to trade herself to Renault for the visas, suggests that when the right to move is throttled, the regime's chokehold reaches all the way into a marriage. If that is right, the film is making a point about the priority of movement rights that the philosophical literature usually leaves implicit.

But here is where I worry the pairing misleads, and I want the reading to face it. The villains of Casablanca impede exit, not entry: the obstacles are the collaborationist prefect and the Nazi major, while America exists only as an absent ideal at the end of a flight to Lisbon. The receiving state never appears and never refuses anyone. So the film cannot dramatize what is arguably the hard question of the open borders debate — whether a destination state may turn you away, and on what terms — and an unsympathetic viewer could say the film smuggles in the assumption that admission is guaranteed once exit is achieved. A second, related worry: the film's sympathies are not distributed evenly. Laszlo is the paradigm of the admissible refugee — the famous, morally serious, politically persecuted hero whose admission any state should be proud of — while the general mass of the desperate is filmed with a cynicism that shades toward contempt. Do the film's affective verdicts track the distinction between political refugees, who have a strong claim to admission even under a right-to-exclude view, and mere economic migrants, who on most accounts do not? If so, the film may be a better illustration of the compromise position than of open borders proper — and that should be said plainly.

Please give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that uses the open borders debate to illuminate particular scenes — the visa economy at Rick's café, Ugarte and the letters, the roulette scene, the airport ending, where Rick's private sacrifice is made to serve Laszlo's public work — but that also says clearly where the film resists the philosophical frame rather than confirming it. I would like to know which scenes you would actually show in class and what you would say about each, and I would like the reading to end by stating what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not — whether, for instance, it shows something about what it is like to live in a world where one's most basic entitlement has been converted into a scarce commodity and a lucky draw.
