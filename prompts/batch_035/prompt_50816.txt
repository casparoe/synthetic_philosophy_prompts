I'm a second year history PhD student, and twice this week the 'for want of a nail' rhyme came up in my seminar, used in opposite directions, and I realized I can't say what the thing actually claims. First my advisor — total structures-over-agents person, long-run forces, sneers at Great Man narratives — used it as her stock example of a FALSE folk theory of history: battles and revolutions are massively overdetermined, she says, so no individual nail ever matters, and the rhyme is basically a fairy tale about how causation works. Then two days later a guy in my cohort used the exact same rhyme to argue the opposite: that history is full of unstable situations sitting near thresholds, where a genuinely tiny perturbation flips the outcome, and there's nothing overdetermined about it. Nobody flagged that the two uses can't both be right, or they noticed and enjoyed it.

What I want is the menu: what distinct precise claims can 'for want of a nail' be making? Like — is it a claim of counterfactual dependence (the battle is lost if and only if that nail is missing)? A claim about thresholds and criticality, that some states of the world are knife-edge sensitive to small inputs? A claim about where you draw the explanatory cut-off in a causal chain that regresses backwards forever, so the rhyme is really about the arbitrariness of stopping anywhere? Or something merely rhetorical about narrative attention — that we enjoy pointing at small proximate causes, which wouldn't be a claim about the world at all? For each reading tell me whether it's trivial, substantive, defensible, or just false, and crucially which reading makes the rhyme support my advisor's debunking use vs the cohort guy's cascade use — or is one of them straight up misusing the slogan? Related thing I get tangled in: overdetermination proper (multiple sufficient conditions, each independently enough) vs a threshold system where nothing is sufficient and everything is jointly fragile — if you could spell out that distinction and how each reading of the nail maps onto it, that'd help alot, since I suspect the whole fight is that she's reading the rhyme as claiming non-redundant necessity for the tiny cause and he's reading it as claiming low redundancy in general, but I lose the thread once we're past necessary-vs-sufficient. Lay it out as a list I can bring to seminar — casual is fine, but keep the logic tight enough that neither of them can wriggle out.
