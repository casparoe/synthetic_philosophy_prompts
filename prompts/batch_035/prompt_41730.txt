My friend Dara and I got into a two-hour argument last night about her job offer and I still can't let it go. She's choosing between a research job at a big lab that pays a ton but in a city where she knows nobody and the work is basically corporate, versus a small nonprofit gig near her family that pays half as much but is the actual science she cares about. We ended up arguing about whether a choice like that can even be made rationally, and it went something like this:

(1) Dara said it's just decision analysis: list the factors, weight them, score the options, multiply and add. The math doesn't care that the factors are different in kind -- money, meaning, proximity to her mom -- that's the whole point of a utility function. If you refuse to do it, that's not philosophy, that's just being squeamish about your own priorities.

(2) I said some comparisons aren't hard, they're incoherent. How many weekends with your mother is a 40k raise worth? There's no exchange rate because the question misconstrues the goods -- some values are partly constituted by us not pricing them, like how offering to pay your friend for helping you move changes what the help is. Making the spreadsheet doesn't clarify the choice, it quietly replaces the values with proxies.

(3) Dara said that's just an epistemic limit -- the number exists, she just can't introspect it precisely -- and anyway revealed preference settles it: everyone accepts extra risk for extra pay at some point, so even "priceless" stuff like safety has a market price. Nobody's values are actually off the scale; people just don't like looking at the receipts.

(4) I brought up the idea that two options can be "on a par" -- neither better than the other, but not equally good either. The tell is that a small improvement on one side doesn't tip the choice, the way it would if they were genuinely tied. Dara said that's just imprecision in the numbers, a fuzzy utility range, nothing deep.

(5) I also tried the lexical ordering thing -- that no amount of money buys certain things, so no trade-off is even possible. She shot back that safety regulations put a price on statistical lives every single year, so every supposedly lexical value gets traded in practice, and my "no amount of money" claim is just a refusal to look.

(6) Finally I fell back on: even if there's no fact of the matter about which is better, the choice isn't arbitrary or a coin flip -- she's deciding who to be, and the choice makes the reasons rather than finds them. Dara said that was romantic mush and that "creating a reason" is just a fancy word for deciding without one.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right about which specific point? Like, actually rate each move, not just declare a winner. And tell us what, if anything, we're both missing -- I have a feeling the whole thing turns on something neither of us said out loud. Also honestly tell me if one of us was just talking nonsense at some point, I can take it.
