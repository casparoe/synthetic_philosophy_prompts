I teach an introductory course in the philosophy of language and I'm building a unit on semantic externalism. Rather than assigning yet another round of thought experiments, I want to pair Putnam's Twin Earth case with Ridley Scott's Blade Runner, and I'd like a reading of the film that takes the philosophy and the film equally seriously.

Here is the angle I want pursued. Rachel, we learn, is a replicant whose memories are implants — copied, the film has it, from Tyrell's niece. Set aside for now the film's more familiar questions about personhood, empathy, and what it is to be human. Consider her instead as a case in the philosophy of language and mind. Twin Earth is supposed to show that two beings who are internally indistinguishable can nonetheless mean different things by their words, because their terms are keyed to different histories and different environments — the moral being that meanings are not determined by what is in the head. Rachel appears to dramatize the same lesson about the past: internally she has everything a person with a childhood has — vivid episodic memories, sincerely and movingly reported — yet the events she recalls never happened to her. On an externalist account, what do her memory reports mean? When she speaks of her childhood — the piano, the spiders in the scene the film uses to undo her — does that phrase refer to nothing? To someone else's childhood? Is she a walking Twin Earth case, or is she closer to Davidson's swampman, a being lacking the right causal history, and does that difference matter for what her words mean?

Second, the Voight-Kampff test. It detects replicants by measuring physiological and emotional responses, working entirely from the inside; it presupposes that whatever distinguishes a replicant from a human is available to internal inspection. But if externalism is right, mental content depends partly on history and environment, which no internal scan can read. Does the film's central detection device thus presuppose exactly the internalism that the Twin Earth argument is meant to undermine? And is there a deliberate irony in the fact that the test's failures — the moments when it cannot do its work, or when Deckard stops relying on it — are what carry the film?

Third, I want the causal theory of memory, in the tradition of Martin and Deutscher, worked in. On that view, remembering requires an appropriate causal connection to the remembered event, so Rachel's implants can give her at most quasi-memories. That would secure an externalist verdict about her past without settling whether she is someone who has had a past. Can the reading use this to show something the thought experiments don't — namely, that Rachel's case splits the question of what her memories mean from the question of whether they are memories at all, and that the film is far more interested in the second?

Finally, I want honesty about where the reading strains. Rachel is not internally identical to the niece whose memories she carries — different body, different brain — so she is not, strictly, anyone's doppelgänger, and perhaps the Twin Earth analogy limps from the start. The film's own interests may lie more with personal identity and with empathy than with reference, and a reading that insists on the philosophy-of-language frame may distort it into an illustration. Say where the film resists the reading rather than forcing it, and close with a judgment: does Blade Runner dramatize something the externalism debate needs but the papers by themselves do not supply, and if so, what?
