So I've been going back and forth for weeks on two job offers, and my friends Dana and Marcus got into it at dinner about whether there even IS a right answer for me here, and honestly I got lost. Quick setup: offer one is a research-adjacent job that pays alot more and is objectively prestigious, but it's in a city where I'd know nobody and the work is kind of soulless. Offer two pays enough, the work actually matters to me, and it's in the town where my parents live (they're getting older). Neither is a slam dunk, that's the whole problem.

Dana said look, this is just multi-dimensional decision making, and you do it all the time. Every choice trades money off against other stuff, and the fact that you can't name a dollar figure for "living near mom" doesn't mean there isn't one — it just means you haven't thought hard enough. Somewhere in there is an exchange rate, maybe a fuzzy one, but real. Otherwise every hard choice would be arbitrary, which she said is obviously false.

Marcus said that's exactly the confusion he wanted to flag: comparing two things doesn't require putting them on a common scale. He said I can obviously rank these two offers against each other without there being any single currency both are denominated in — like how you can just hear that one piece of music is better than another without there being "music units" you're summing up.

Dana asked him point blank: so is offer one better, is offer two better, or are they exactly equal? Those are the options. Marcus said none of the three. She said that's definately incoherent. And this is where it got good. Marcus said think about what "exactly equal" commits you to: if the offers were precisely tied, then any tiny bump to one of them — five grand more salary, one extra visit with my parents a year — would break the tie and make that one strictly better. But it obviously wouldn't. A small improvement wouldn't make either one the winner, they'd still be neck and neck. So "equal" is the wrong relation too. The two offers are just different in kind, and "better than" runs out.

Dana wasn't done though. She said fine, suppose "better than" runs out — then the choice between them is unguided, a coin flip with extra steps, and if I go around saying my eventual choice reflects something about which life is better for me, I'm just rationalizing after the fact. Marcus said no — and this is the part where I really lost the thread. He said that for choices like this, where reasons run out, the choice isn't a report of pre-existing value. It's more like a commitment that itself creates a reason going forward, and there's a real difference between choosing something and merely picking it, even if that difference sounds like wordplay. Dana said that seemed to her like a fancy description of what brains do after the fact to feel okay about arbitrary decisions.

They both turned to me and asked what I thought, and I had nothing. Can you adjudicate? Specifically: is Dana right that better / worse / exactly equal exhaust the possibilities, or does Marcus's tie-breaking point open a genuine hole in that trichotomy? If he's right that the offers are neither better nor worse nor equal, does her coin-flip conclusion actually follow, or is there something real in his choosing-vs-picking idea? Please go through their moves in order and say who's right about what, since they were each making points I couldn't answer on the spot — and tell me who comes out more right overall, if that question even makes sense here.
