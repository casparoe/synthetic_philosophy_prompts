Stats PhD student here, TAing intro probability for the second year running, and something that's been bugging me since undergrad finally boiled over while I was prepping a recitation. I keep having strong gut verdicts about which "random choice" procedures are right and which are subtly wrong, but I can't articulate what the rule is. So: verdicts first, then what I want.

1. Random point in a disk. Picking the angle uniform and then the distance-from-center uniform is WRONG — it piles points near the center. Uniform over area is right. I'm not even slightly tempted the other way on this one.

2. Random chord of a circle. I genuinely can't settle on a verdict. Two uniform points on the circumference: probability 1/3 the chord beats the inscribed equilateral triangle's side. Uniform midpoint over the disk's interior: 1/4. Uniform point along a random radius: 1/2. All three feel like "picking a chord at random," and my gut flips between them depending on which one I did last. (Yes I know this is the old Bertrand chord thing — that's part of the story but not what I'm asking.)

3. Unknown coin. Hand me a coin of unknown bias and ask my probability the first flip is heads: 1/2, easy. First two flips both heads: 1/4 feels right (treat flips as iid given the bias, integrate it out). Ask me my probability the bias is above 1/2 at all and 1/2 also feels right. So I'm fine assigning a flat prior over the bias, even though I know there's no fact of the matter about "uniformly random coin from nowhere."

4. "A uniformly random positive integer" is not a thing, and no clever workaround makes it one. But "uniform integer in 1..N, then mod 2" with N even is a thing and 1/2 is just correct. This feels like a different kind of correctness than in case 3, though I can't say why.

5. A friend says "the trillionth digit of pi is 7 with probability 1/10 because I'm indifferent between the digits." As a statement of her credence: fine. As a statement about pi: somehow WRONG to me, like indifference is being dressed up as a discovery about the world. Again I can't defend the distinction, I just have it.

6. Random inscribed triangle. Three uniform points on the circumference feels like clearly the right procedure, and procedures where you pick the sides or angles directly feel fishy. Which is weird, because it's the chord problem wearing a hat, and there I had no verdict at all (case 2) while here I'm firm.

So what I want: is there one principle that gets all of these — some invariance-under-the-problem's-symmetries idea, or max entropy, or something about fixing what counts as an "outcome" before you're allowed to be indifferent — and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? I suspect case 2 is the sore thumb: three genuine symmetry groups, no fourth thing that adjudicates. If the principle can save my firmness in case 6 while excusing the wobbliness in case 2, great, but I'd like that argument made explicitly rather than waved at. A few specific tests I'd want the answer to pass:

- What does the principle say about the old trick where you're indifferent between the values of some quantity x and also indifferent between the values of 1/x, and the two assignments contradict each other?
- Does it reproduce my case 1 verdict, or does it shrug at that one too?
- If it can't accommodate everything, tell me straight which verdict of mine is the sentimental one that has to go, and what the principle predicts on a case I haven't listed (say, a random line intersecting a sphere).

Also, half-remembered: someone early in the 20th century had a go at restricting the indifference principle so it only fires under conditions like this — I don't trust my memory of who or what the restrictions were. If you know that literature, tell me whether their fix actually handles the chord case, since that seems like the acid test. Happy to be told my verdicts are just vibes with a domain wall between the comby ones and the geometric ones, but make the case rather than asserting it.
