Nuclear regulators in several countries are legally required to design warning systems for deep geological repositories that will remain hazardous for on the order of a hundred thousand years — a design problem that led to multidisciplinary expert panels on how to communicate danger to people in the far future, deliberating over hostile architecture, forbidding landscapes, and messages couched to survive the loss of every current language, and settling on the famous inscription idea of a place that is not a place of honor, telling future intruders nothing valuable is buried there. Enormous care and money go into protecting these particular far-future people from this particular harm. Yet the same governments, in ordinary cost-benefit analysis, apply discount rates that make harms and benefits a few centuries out nearly vanish from the calculation, and they decline, for instance, to spend comparable sums on reducing fairly certain risks of catastrophe to people in the same remote future. What commitments about future people does the marker-and-repository regime actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that the interests of people ten millennia hence count equally with ours, or only something much weaker — a duty not to impose our residual hazards on them, or a duty to warn — which even a thoroughgoing discounter of future welfare could accept? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, say whether the apparent inconsistency with discount-rate practice is real or whether the two can be reconciled under a single coherent theory of our obligations to the unborn, and tell me what the repository regime would have to look like if it were redesigned on a strong longtermist view: would it license more than markers and passive barriers, and what would it conspicuously refuse to do?
