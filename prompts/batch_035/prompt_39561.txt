Suppose H. P. Grice, having died before large language models existed, could be shown one in action: a system producing fluent assertions, instructions, even jokes, without any evident intentions behind them. Judging from his distinction between natural and non-natural meaning and his intention-based analysis of what it is for a speaker to mean something, how do you think Grice would classify the meaning of an LLM's outputs? Consider three possibilities he might weigh: that such outputs have at most natural meaning, as a fever means infection — reliable signs of patterns in human text without anyone meaning anything; that they have no meaning in his sense at all, a distinction his framework never anticipated; or that the case would push him to refine his analysis so that some machine utterances could count as genuine non-natural meaning, perhaps by relocating the relevant intentions in the system's designers or users rather than the system itself. Then address the pragmatics: can an entity without intentions flout a maxim of the cooperative principle, or violate one while going undetected? Would Grice treat the apparent conversational implicatures a user recovers from an LLM as genuine implicatures, or as counterfeits produced by training on human dialogue — and does it matter that the recovery happens on the audience's side even if nothing was intended on the speaker's? Develop what you take to be his most likely settled position, and say which of his own commitments the case would strain the hardest.
