I have no academic background, and I want to be upfront about that, because it shapes how I've come to the topic. My whole working life has been in call centers and their back offices, so my thinking about measurement comes from that, not from books.

For several years I've managed a team whose performance is judged mainly by a single number: the share of customer issues resolved on the first call. It isn't a stupid number. Customers who get things fixed in one call really are happier, and agents who can do that really are better at the job. But once the number became a target — the difference for my people between a bonus and a write-up — it started to come apart from the thing it measured. Some agents began rushing callers off the phone so an issue would count as resolved and reappear as a fresh call on someone else's log. Others quietly discouraged customers with genuinely complicated problems from pursuing them, since those cases could never be closed quickly and would only drag the number down. Nothing in our reports was ever false; the paperwork was all accurate. And yet the better the number looked, the less it meant. I've since learned this has a name — Goodhart's law, the point that when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure — along with the related idea of specification gaming, where you hand an optimizer a proxy and it satisfies the letter of the proxy while defeating its spirit.

Here is what I want: an analysis of the Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal through this lens, done carefully rather than as a slogan. The facts as I understand them, so we're working from the same story: from the late 1990s onward, Wells Fargo pushed employees hard to sell existing customers more products, with daily quotas for opening new accounts and a stated ambition of eight financial products per household — management even put a punning slogan on the number in its annual reports. Under that pressure, employees opened something on the order of three million accounts customers had never asked for, with fees attached to many of them. The scandal broke publicly in 2016; the bank paid fines and settlements running well into the billions, the CEO resigned, and thousands of employees were fired.

Start with the mapping, and be disciplined about it. Who or what was the "optimizer" here — the branch employee chasing a quota, the regional manager aggregating those quotas upward, or something at the level of the shareholder valuation that rewarded cross-selling numbers? What exactly was "products per customer" a proxy for, and why did anyone believe more of it meant a healthier bank? Then tell me where the fit is too neat — where the theory, applied naively, misdescribes what actually happened.

Then there is the part I especially want help with, because it's the thing my own experience keeps pushing me toward and I can't resolve it alone. It seems to me there are at least three different things lumped together under this law, and the Wells Fargo case involves the third and strangest of them. First, outright faking of the number — cooked books, invented statistics. That doesn't seem to have been Wells Fargo's main problem. Second, hollowing: you do the measured activity, but only in the minimal sense that makes the counter tick, like the rushed "resolution" on my call floor. Third — and here I'm coining a term because I don't know if there's a real one — what I'd call world-warping: you really and fully do the thing the measure counts. The accounts really existed, with real customers and real paperwork, so in an important sense the number was true. But you bring the truth about by means that destroy what the measure was a proxy for in the first place. Cross-selling volume was presumably meant to track deep customer relationships; the campaign produced the volume by violating the customers, and so destroyed the very relationships it was counting. Is that third thing just the second thing in disguise, or genuinely different? Does the standard formulation of the law distinguish them, or is it silent there? And does the difference matter for what the law predicts — for instance, about why hollowing can stay invisible for years while world-warping eventually blows up?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. Does the episode confirm the theory, or expose something it never anticipated? Two things in particular. The standard remedy people draw from my call-floor version of the law is "don't put so much weight on one number" — diversify the metrics, add checks. But at Wells Fargo, the customer-satisfaction surveys and compliance audits didn't stop it; employees gamed or evaded those too, and the pressure to game them came from the same place. Is the theory silent on what to do when every added safeguard is itself something that can be gamed? And the thing I can't stop chewing on: after everything came out, the bank fired the employees, but for a long while kept the structure of targets that had produced the behavior. If the law says the target is the problem, why is the standard institutional response always to blame the people standing between the target and the number? I'd rather have your honest account of where the theory runs out than a tidy story that forces the episode to fit.
