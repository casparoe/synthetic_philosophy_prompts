For a philosophy society evening on the Epicurean argument I have been asked to state my considered position on whether death is bad for the one who dies, and I would like a consistency audit before I sign my name to it. My commitments as they currently stand: (1) the Epicurean conclusion is correct — death is not bad for the person who dies, since nothing that obtains once one no longer exists happens to anyone; (2) the Lucretian symmetry argument is sound: our prenatal non-existence was no misfortune for us, and the days after our deaths are relevantly the same; (3) it is nonetheless prudentially rational for a typical sixty-year-old to endure a gruelling course of chemotherapy for a good chance at a few extra years; (4) some fates are worse than death, such that a person in unrelievable agony is better off dead; (5) a promise made to a dying woman can be broken after her death in a way that wrongs her — posthumous harm is genuinely possible; and (6) killing someone painlessly and without warning wrongs the victim themselves, not merely their family or society at large. I genuinely do not know where the trouble is, if there is any, so do not assume I have flagged anything by my ordering or phrasing.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are in real tension and how badly, which pairs only look to be in tension until one thinks harder, and for each genuine tension what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally grave, and finish by telling me which single revision would restore the most coherence to the whole package. I am not after a history of the debate from Epicurus to the present; I want the audit, addressed to my six commitments specifically. Hard limit: 700 words.
