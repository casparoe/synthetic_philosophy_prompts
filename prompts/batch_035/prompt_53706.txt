I'm writing a piece for a criminal-justice policy blog where I imagine Jeremy Bentham getting a full guided tour of Halden prison in Norway in 2025 and then being asked for his verdict. I keep going back and forth on what he'd actually conclude and I need someone to think it through with me. Help me figure out his likely position.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: Bentham designed the Panopticon in the late 1780s, and its whole engine was the inspection principle -- prisoners never know when they're being watched, so they police themselves, which makes them cheaper to manage and (he claimed) reforms their habits. Halden almost inverts this. No bars, guards are trained in a flat hierarchy not to assert authority, inmates cook meals with real kitchen knives, and the landscaping is deliberately arranged so prisoners mostly can't see the wall -- the architecture hides confinement rather than displaying it. Guards spend their time playing chess and volleyball with inmates (this is called "dynamic security" now) rather than watching from a hidden central tower.

Here's where I see the tensions. Bentham was a utilitarian who held that punishment is in itself an evil, justified only if it prevents greater harm, and he genuinely listed moral reform of the prisoner as a central aim of his prison. So would he say Halden achieves the Panopticon's actual goals by opposite means -- a nice irony -- or would he insist that without continuous visibility and deterrence the reform won't stick? And there's the money angle: he was obsessed with cheapness, pitched the Panopticon as more economical than any existing prison, and even had a profit-sharing scheme for the prison manager. Halden costs a lot per prisoner, and Norway's per-capita incarceration rate is tiny compared to America's. Would he treat the cost as a dealbreaker or say that if recidivism stays low it pays for itself in his own felicific arithmetic?

Please give me: (1) what his considered position would probably be, (2) the two or three points where his own commitments genuinely pull in opposite directions, (3) what he'd predict would go wrong at Halden if he had to name something, and (4) one line of his attack on the American mass-incarceration model, framed in his own terms (unproductive pain, expense, the state manufacturing crime rather than preventing it -- whatever follows from his framework, not just vibes). I'd rather it read like reasoned extrapolation from what he actually argued than like fanfic, though a little personality is welcome -- he was famously combative in print.
