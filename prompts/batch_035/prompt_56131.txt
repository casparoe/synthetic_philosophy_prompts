Historical-Jesus scholarship works by sifting the gospels through "criteria of authenticity": a saying or deed is credited to Jesus when it appears in multiple independent sources, when it would have embarrassed the early church, or when it is dissimilar to both early Judaism and the church's own theology; material is denied to Jesus when it looks like something the church could have invented. The surviving residue is then assembled into a coherent figure — typically an eschatological prophet — and fitting that reconstructed figure is itself used as a mark of authenticity for further material. What theory of evidence does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that the tradition was inventive rather than conservative — that the church produced sayings freely, so that only what it had no motive or capacity to create can be Jesus' — and is that a substantive historical assumption presented as a neutral method? Is the combination of criteria even coherent, given that double dissimilarity credits an item only by cutting Jesus off from his context, while coherence credits an item by tying him to a portrait built out of whatever survived dissimilarity? Compare the practice with how historians treat comparable figures such as Socrates, whose words are accepted on multiple attestation and plausibility without any demand that they be dissimilar from Plato, the very source preserving them. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, say where it is underdetermined — several different views of the tradition's reliability might license the same criteria — and tell me what would have to change if the gospels were treated with the ordinary canons applied to other ancient figures.
