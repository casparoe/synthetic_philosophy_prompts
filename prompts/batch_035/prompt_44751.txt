I should say at the outset that I would like your response written in British English.

I am an educational psychologist by training, and this past year I have been attending a community reading group that has worked through Mencius and Xunzi in translation. A recent discussion there has stayed with me. Another member dismissed the entire Mencius–Xunzi dispute as a simple contest of optimism against pessimism: on his telling, Mencius holds that people are born good, Xunzi holds that they are born bad, and that is the whole of it. The framing struck me as far too flat. It makes the two thinkers sound as though they are issuing rival forecasts about human beings, when what I seem to be reading in the texts is a more structural disagreement. Xunzi appears to define xing — human nature — as whatever is present in us spontaneously, prior to any deliberate effort or learning, which means his claim that our xing is bad is partly a ruling about what counts as "nature" at all; and Mencius's famous sprouts are, on his own showing, incipient tendencies rather than finished virtues — a bystander's spontaneous alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well is not yet benevolence, but the germ from which benevolence must still be extended and cultivated. (I am paraphrasing the canonical examples and metaphors — the well, the sprouts, Xunzi's crooked wood that must await the pressing frame — not quoting any translation.)

Please compare the two thinkers with that framing in view rather than the optimism/pessimism gloss, and along three axes in particular. First: to what extent is their disagreement a disagreement about the boundary between the inborn and the acquired — about what xing denotes — rather than about what human beings are factually like? Second: how does each of them explain the very possibility of moral education, given his starting point — Mencius needing to extend what is already there, Xunzi needing to impose shape on what is not (even while insisting, if I remember the texts correctly, that anyone on the street can become a Yu, which hardly sounds like despair about human capacity)? Third, and this is the question I genuinely cannot settle for myself: does Xunzi's account of how ritual, teachers and accumulated practice transform us quietly depend on some Mencian raw material — that is, can anything work on us if there is nothing in us that responds to it — or does he have an independent story about why the medicine takes? If, having worked through all this, you conclude that the optimism/pessimism label is defensible after all, say so and defend it; but do not simply assume it at the outset.
