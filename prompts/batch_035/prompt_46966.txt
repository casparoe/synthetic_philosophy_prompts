For a graduate seminar on the epistemology of mathematics, each participant has been asked to circulate a short statement of their own position so the others can probe it. Mine is below, in six theses, and I would like a rigorous consistency check before I send it around.

(1) Semantic uniformity. Mathematical discourse should receive the same kind of truth-conditional, compositional semantics as the rest of our language. 'There are infinitely many primes' does not differ from 'there are infinitely many galaxies' in logical form or in the workings of its quantifiers; it differs in subject matter, not in how meaning and truth are assigned to it.

(2) Platonism. Arithmetic is a body of truths about abstract objects — mind-independent, non-spatiotemporal, causally inert — and those truths are literally true, in the same sense in which the claims of the natural sciences are true.

(3) Epistemological naturalism. Whatever knowing turns out to be, it must be explicable without remainder in natural terms; at minimum, knowing that p requires some appropriate connection between the believer and the subject matter of the belief, paradigmatically a causal one. I know of no exceptions to this.

(4) The certainty asymmetry. Our warrant for elementary arithmetic is stronger, not weaker, than our warrant for claims about the remote physical world. If a well-confirmed physical theory entailed the falsity of some theorem of arithmetic, I would revise the physics and keep the mathematics.

(5) The identification problem. Benacerraf's classic argument that the natural numbers cannot simply be sets: both the Zermelo and the von Neumann constructions satisfy the Peano axioms, yet on the von Neumann reading 3 is a member of 17 and on the Zermelo reading it is not, and nothing in arithmetic or in set theory settles which reading is correct. I take this to show that there is no fact of the matter about the set-theoretic identity of the numbers.

(6) Singular reference. I hold that the numeral '3' functions as a genuine singular term standing for one particular object, and I accept the broadly causal-historical picture of reference on which a term refers to whatever the historical chain of its use is appropriately connected to. I see no principled reason to treat numerals and other mathematical expressions as a special case.

My question: is this package jointly tenable? Which pairs of theses pull against each other, and how badly? For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give, what the least costly revision is, and what that revision would cost elsewhere in the package — I want to know which commitments are load-bearing for others. I would also like you to flag any tensions I might be tempted to see that are merely apparent. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally grave, and be specific about which thesis does the damage in each case.
