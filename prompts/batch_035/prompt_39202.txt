I'm an undergraduate taking a course on philosophical logic and epistemology, and for my term paper I've chosen the surprise exam paradox: the teacher announces that there will be an exam next week, that it will be on exactly one of the five days, and that on the morning of the exam the students will not know that it is that day; the students then reason by backward induction that the exam cannot be held at all; and yet it is held on, say, Wednesday, and they are in fact surprised. My instructor keeps telling us that the literature is a swamp of incompatible "solutions" that aren't even answering the same question, so before I read any of them I want the specification first.

Please write the desiderata: what an adequate solution to the paradox has to deliver. To my mind the list should at least cover the following, though tell me if I've got the shape of the problem wrong:

- It must handle the one-day case, where the announcement reduces to "there will be an exam tomorrow and you will not know it," and say whether that case is the real crux or a degenerate special case — and it must handle it in a way that doesn't sabotage the five-day case.
- It must not block the backward induction merely by declaring one step invalid; it has to say which step fails and why that step looked valid, since each step individually seems exactly as good as the one before it.
- It has to take a stand on what kind of item the announcement is: a proposition about the students' future epistemic states, a self-referential one, or something that can fail to be common knowledge even when it is publicly stated — and it must say whether such an announcement can be true, false, or lack a truth value altogether, including whether its truth value can change over the week.
- It must respect the distinction between the students lacking knowledge, lacking justified belief, and being unable to deduce the day from the announcement plus what is common knowledge among them; a solution that equivocates on this has, I suspect, not yet started.
- It must explain the observed asymmetry: the students' a priori argument that the exam cannot occur is somehow worthless in practice, and the solution should account for why, rather than just noting that they turn out wrong on Wednesday.
- It should generalize to the kin cases — the unexpected hanging, and announcements of the form "you will not believe this very announcement" — without collapsing all of them into one phenomenon if they genuinely differ.
- It should say whether it needs assumptions about the logic of knowledge, e.g. whether knowledge iterates (the KK principle), or whether it can remain neutral; a solution that quietly buys its result from an epistemic logic side should own that.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, mark which are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particular camps, and flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see, since if it exists then the paradox is in some sense a theorem rather than a confusion. Then, briefly and without dwelling on it, sketch how the main solution families fare against the list: the Quine-style response that the students cannot be sure the announcement will be carried out; the epistemic-blind-spot treatments; the step-by-step indictments of the induction; and the self-reference treatments on which the announcement cannot be common knowledge. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard — I will do the scoring myself in the paper.
