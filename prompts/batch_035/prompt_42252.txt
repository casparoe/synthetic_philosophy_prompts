I've been asked to contribute a chapter laying out my modal profile for a volume on conceivability and possibility, and before I commit anything to print I want an audit of whether the views I've drifted into over twenty years of teaching this material actually hang together. Here is the set. (1) I'm a Millian about proper names: a name contributes its referent, and nothing more, to the propositions expressed by sentences containing it. (2) I hold that ideal positive conceivability — conceivability that survives unlimited rational reflection, with all a priori consequences of one's concepts in view — is an infallible guide to metaphysical possibility, and I take this to be the core of modal epistemology: all substantive modal knowledge is grounded in it one way or another. (3) I think the zombie world — a complete physical duplicate of our world lacking phenomenal consciousness — is ideally conceivable, not merely prima facie conceivable: no amount of reflection or physical knowledge would render that description incoherent. (4) I am a physicalist of the type-B stripe: phenomenal states are identical to physical states, and if this is so it is so as a necessary a posteriori truth, on exactly the model of water being H2O. (5) I hold that the ancients' inclination to suppose Hesperus might have failed to be Phosphorus is defused in the ordinary Kripkean way — what was genuinely conceivable was only that the evening-star description and the morning-star description fail to co-refer — and I regard that case as showing that apparent conceivability claims yield to reflection, which is part of why I'm comfortable insisting that ideal conceivability never misleads. (6) I also hold that belief and rational agency require phenomenal consciousness: a system with no phenomenal life has no beliefs, however sophisticated its information processing. That last one is not idle, because I want to say that the inhabitants of the zombie world, being our exact physical duplicates, would sincerely report pains and reasons and would deliberate — yet I can't see how they'd count as believing anything if (6) is right.

Go through the six commitments and tell me which pairs or trios are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what would have to give in each case, along with what the revision would cost me elsewhere. I have my suspicions about where the trouble lies, but I'd rather not bias you: please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and tell me which single change would clean up the most. If some tension turns on a distinction I've misdescribed rather than a view I can't hold, say so — I'd rather learn that my formulation is sloppy than that my views are inconsistent, though I'd rather learn either than publish the chapter as it stands.
