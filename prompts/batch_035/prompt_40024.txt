My two friends had a blowup at dinner about something my sister did and I still can't decide who was right, so I'm outsourcing it to you, sorry.

Background: my sister spent two years going back and forth about whether to go no-contact with our brother. He's not dangerous or anything, just a long slow accumulation of borrowing money and lying about it, showing up sweet at exactly the right moments, that kind of thing. She was going in circles. So she finally asked our aunt Ruth, who has known all of us our whole lives and is honestly the most morally level-headed person in the family, and Ruth told her plainly: yes, cut him off, you've done enough. She did, and it's been mostly good for her, though it's obviously painful.

At dinner, my friend Talia said taking Ruth's word on that was a mistake in principle -- not because the advice was bad, but because of what kind of thing it was. Here's roughly how the argument went:

(1) Talia: some judgments you have to make yourself, and this was one of them. If you decide to cut off your own brother because someone else told you to, the decision is never really yours -- you won't be able to explain it at Thanksgiving, you won't know how to revise it as things change, and when the guilt hits at 2am you'll have nothing to stand on but "Ruth said."

(2) Marcus (my other friend): that proves too much. We take people's word on everything important -- Ruth also told her which lawyer to use and nobody thinks that undermined her ownership of the lawsuit. If Ruth is genuinely better placed to see this situation clearly, why is her moral read different in kind from her read on the lawyer? Because it's about *values*? Ruth wasn't telling her what to value, she was telling her what was going on, which my sister was frankly too exhausted to see.

(3) Talia: it's more like asking a film critic whether a movie was moving. Even if the critic is right, and even if you go and cry at the right scenes, there's something off about it -- the judgment was never really had by you. And with family it's worse, because the whole point of the deliberation wasn't just to get the right answer, it was to become the kind of person whose answer it is.

(4) Marcus: two things. First, my sister didn't just take the verdict -- she made Ruth walk her through why, and she's since articulated it to me better than Ruth did. Second, and this is the part Talia keeps skipping: my sister was the *worst* positioned judge in the room. Twenty years of being charmed by him and then furious at herself for being charmed -- that's not a pristine moral faculty being polluted by outside input, that's a compromised instrument. Telling her she owed it to herself to reason it out alone is like telling someone with heatstroke that they should navigate by their own sense of thirst.

(5) Talia: fine, but then say that, don't say she should defer to moral expertise, because those are different defenses. If the issue is that her judgment was distorted, the cure is time and distance and talking to more people, not installing Ruth's conclusion in the slot. And notice she still can't answer the 2am guilt thing -- borrowed conclusions don't generate their own reassurance, you have to go back to the lender every time.

They both looked at me to referee and I punted. Can you go through the five moves and rate each one -- who's right about what, whether Marcus's two defenses in (4) really are different like Talia claims, and where the truth about "moral testimony" (I gather philosophers have a name for this, feel free to use it) actually lands. I especially want to know if Talia's critic analogy is a good argument or just a nice picture.
