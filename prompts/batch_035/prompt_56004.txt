I'm on the hiring committee in my department, and our latest candidate's job talk has left me confused in a way I can't quite name. My own notes from the talk itself were quite positive — the project was interesting, the work solid — though there were genuine weak spots. At the Q&A, our most senior member, who is famous for being harsh with everyone, opened with an aggressive question about a methodological choice, and the candidate handled it badly: flustered, defensive, visibly rattled. From that point the room's temperature changed. Every subsequent question was sharper, and by the debrief even the two colleagues who had championed this candidate's written work had cooled on them noticeably. What unsettles me most is my own trajectory: what I said in the room was more negative than the notes I'd written during the talk, and I can't fully reconstruct which judgment was mine.

We still have two more candidates to see before we rank anyone, but the chair has floated doing a straw poll at the next meeting in the traditional way — by seniority, out loud, one at a time. A newer colleague pushed back and suggested we each submit sealed ratings before any discussion begins, and only then talk through our differences. Another colleague objected that this throws away exactly what we want: the point of discussing is that people share reasons, not just numbers, and sealed ballots treat the committee as a pile of isolated judges when it should function as one pooled brain. Meanwhile I'm no longer sure my own view is trustworthy: I know the room reacted badly, I know two champions defected, and I genuinely can't tell whether the candidate was that weak or whether one rocky answer early on set a tone that everyone — me included — has been reading off each other's faces rather than off the work. When I described all this to a friend outside the department, he said there's nothing wrong with any of it: if ten smart people who all saw the same talk ended up lukewarm, that convergence is real evidence I should be updating on, not a process failure, and sealing our ballots would just discard free information. My partner, hearing the same story, said the opposite: the first negative signal came from the one person who is negative about everyone, and once it landed, the later reactions were partly performances of seriousness for the room — so the convergence was manufactured, and "everyone converged" is exactly what you'd observe whether or not the candidate was any good.

I don't want a menu of procedural options with pros and cons attached — I want your actual recommendation, with reasons. Should I voice my original positive assessment now, before any straw poll, or does speaking up early just make me the new first signal that everyone else recalibrates around? Between sealed ratings and seniority-ordered oral polling, which should I push for, or is there a third mechanism that answers both the "pooled brain" worry and my worry that we've been reading each other instead of the candidate? And does the fact that I can tell you which colleagues have a long track record of harshness and which of enthusiasm — and that everyone on the committee knows these reputations too — change your answer about how our votes should be sequenced, weighted, or disclosed? Please take a definite position: if you think one of my two friends is basically right about what happened in that room, say so and tell me what follows from it.
