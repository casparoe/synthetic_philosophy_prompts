You're a philosopher of history who moonlights thinking about how science actually evolves, and you've read enough economic history to be comfortable with path dependence talk (Paul David's QWERTY story, Brian Arthur on increasing returns, that literature). I want you to make an actual argument for a specific position, not just survey views. Here's the position:

Once you separate science from technology and institutions, path dependence in science is much weaker than people assume. In tech and institutional life, an early contingent choice (QWERTY layouts, different railway gauges, internal combustion beating steam cars, a country picking a legal system) can lock in permanently because nothing outside the system forces a correction — users adapt to whatever won, network effects compound, and the "better" alternative just never gets its shot. But science, on this view, faces an external referee: the world itself. Notations, paradigm choices, and research programs can drift on for a while, but eventually empirical failure accumulates and somebody gets pulled back toward whatever actually fits the phenomena, roughly regardless of where the contingent starting point was. So if you replayed history with different early scientific choices — a different notation for calculus, a Chinese or Islamic scientific tradition becoming dominant, phlogiston surviving another century — you'd converge on roughly our current physics anyway, maybe with different names and a different order of discovery. History is "locked in" for keyboards and much less locked in for, say, the periodic table.
