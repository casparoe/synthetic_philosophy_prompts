I help allocate a small grantmaking budget, and in the cause-prioritization discussions I sit in on, a recurring move is to treat philanthropy as an inefficient market and the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework as value investing transplanted. The mapping runs roughly: a cause's importance is the size of the prize, like a company's total addressable market; its tractability is the share of that prize a competent funder could actually capture, the margin of safety; and its neglectedness is the stock being cheap, because other investors are irrationally crowded into glamour issues while the boring ones trade below intrinsic value. The conclusion this is used to license is that donors should systematically favor neglected causes, and that a cause's priority can be scored by multiplying the three factors together, the way a valuation multiplies independent inputs.

I'd like a serious audit of this analogy. Please lay out the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the price, the investors, the crowd, the arbitrageur, the short-seller — and then work through where the mapping breaks. I can see at least four pressure points worth your attention, though I'd welcome others. First, markets have a price that aggregates dispersed information, and mispricing attracts capital until it corrects; philanthropy has no price, no shorting, and no mechanism that bids up a neglected cause — but it's unclear whether that strengthens the opportunity (the neglect persists, so you keep getting the bargain) or wrecks the investment logic (in a market, being early is rewarded because others will pile in behind you; in philanthropy, they won't). Second, value investors worry about value traps: a stock that looks cheap may be cheap because the market knows something you don't, so the cheapness is evidence against your own judgment. If a cause is neglected because earlier funders rationally discovered it was intractable, then neglectedness isn't an independent input but endogenous evidence about tractability — and multiplying the factors double-counts. Third, whose participation counts as "the market"? Climate change absorbs enormous government spending, yet from the standpoint of what a private donor can move it may still be relatively neglected — does the benchmark shift with the size of the actor, and can the analogy survive a benchmark that changes with the investor? Fourth, buying a large stake doesn't shrink a stock's value, but pouring money into a cause plausibly runs into diminishing returns, so position sizing feeds back into the price in a way equity investing doesn't.

Close by telling me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: can the case for prioritizing neglected causes be rebuilt on grounds the value-investing picture can't carry, and if the multiplicative scoring is illegitimate, what — if anything — should replace it as a way of ranking causes? If you think a different financial analogy fits better, say which and why; and if you think the whole market framing should be abandoned, say that too, and what we'd be left with.
