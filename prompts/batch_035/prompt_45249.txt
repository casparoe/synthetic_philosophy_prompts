I am a novelist, and I have argued myself into a corner with a piece of philosophy. My current book is set in a village; one of its characters is an old woman whom the community has cast out — no one greets her, no one uses her name, and it is understood that when she dies no one will bury her as one of their own. I had meant to give her interiority through free indirect discourse, the narrator's voice settling into her own, so that the reader feels what the village refuses to see. Then, at a residency, I fell into the literature on ubuntu and on relational conceptions of personhood — the thought, common to several African philosophers, that a person is not a self-contained thing that then enters into relations, but something constituted by relations: one becomes a person through other persons. I constructed an argument to secure what my novel needed, and it now seems to prove too much. Here it is.

(1) On the relational conception, personhood is constituted by relations of recognition with others: it is through being greeted, named, answered and mourned that one is a person.
(2) If relations constitute a thing, they are not merely evidence of it; so where the constituting relations are absent, what they constitute is absent. A community that withholds recognition does not merely misjudge a person; it withholds the conditions of personhood.
(3) My character's community withholds recognition completely — she is not greeted, not named, and will not be buried among them.
(4) So on the relational conception she is not, or is no longer, or is not fully, a person.
(5) Yet every proponent of the view I have read would say the village is wrong about her: that she remains a person, and that the refusal is a failure to see what is there — which is what makes the refusal an outrage rather than a mere report.
(6) But if she remains a person independently of their recognition, then personhood was never constituted by recognition in the first place, and the relational conception collapses into the very individualism it was meant to displace — a person complete in herself after all, whom society merely decorates or abuses.

The upshot is unbearable for my book: either my character is a person and the relational view says nothing constitutive about it, or the view is right and the village has, in the deepest sense, succeeded. Of all the steps, I would most like to keep (5), since the novel depends on her remaining a person; but I do not want to be told simply that (2) is harsh and (5) is kind. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent when I first wrote it down, and what each available rejection costs — including what it costs the view's own ambitions, not only my story. And if it helps, say what this means for the narration: whether, on the version of the view you would let me keep, there is a her for the narrator's voice to settle into at all. Please answer in British English.
