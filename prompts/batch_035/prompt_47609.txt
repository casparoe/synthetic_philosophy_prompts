I chair a monthly review commitee at a mid-sized logistics company where we decide which internal projects keep getting funded and which get killed. Think software migrations that are two years late, a rebrand that's half rolled out, a warehouse automation pilot eating money — you know the type. The problem is that every month, the person running each project shows up and argues that they're 70% done and it'd be a waste to stop now, and every month we nod along and approve another tranche. I've read enough pop-management stuff to know "sunk cost fallacy, just ignore sunk costs, next question" but I've also started to suspect that advice is too glib, and here's why: I once read about the dollar auction, where the top bidder wins the prize but the *second* bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing. The nasty part is that at every point after you're in second place, bidding a bit more is actually the cheaper move compared to eating your loss and walking away — it's locally rational to keep going even though the whole sequence is ruinous. That maps onto our projects uncomfortably well: when we've sunk 2 million into a migration, "one more quarter" often genuinely looks better than abandoning it, and I don't think the standard sunk-cost lecture captures that. The trap isn't just bias, it's structure.

So what I want is a real procedure my committee can actually run in a 20-minute slot per project, written so the least philosophical person in the room can follow it. Please base it on whatever escalation-of-commitment research and the dollar-auction logic actually support, not just vibes. Specifically I'd want it to cover: (a) questions that separate "this is genuinely 70% done and the last 30% is cheap" from "this is the auction talking"; (b) some way to handle the fact that the local-rationality trap means we can't trust our own month-to-month judgements — maybe pre-commitment devices or reframing what's on the table at each decision point, you decide what's actually workable; (c) where the procedure has to stop and hand it to human judgement because it's a values question, not a facts question; and (d) it should be honest about where it will misfire, e.g. projects that get killed and restarted later at higher cost.

Then run it on three cases: the warehouse software migration that's 70% "done" (the vendor says the last 30% is always the hard 30%), a modest rebrand that is on budget and on time but where nobody can articulate what the rebrand is *for*, and a case where the procedure should say keep going even though the gut says kill it — I want at least one of the three to come out as an answer I won't like, because otherwise I won't trust the thing.

Please write the whole answer in British English, and keep it usable — I'm going to print it and put it in front of five tired people with a spreadsheet.
