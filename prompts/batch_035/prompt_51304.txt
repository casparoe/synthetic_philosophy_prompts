A friend of mine who's really into ethics podcasts tried to get me up to speed on negative utilitarianism the other day, and I want to know how much of what he said to actually trust. Here's roughly his rundown:

"So classical utilitarians put pleasure and pain on a single scale -- suffering is just negative pleasure, and the two trade off at some exchange rate. That means there's some huge number of mildly pleasant experiences that outweighs a torture session, which already sounds off. Negative utilitarianism is the fix: only suffering counts morally. The version worth taking seriously isn't the crude one, though, it's the lexical threshold version, which says there's a threshold of suffering above which no amount of happiness can compensate -- intense agony is lexically worse than everything below the line, so pleasure and pain aren't commensurable in that range. Below the threshold, happiness can still outweigh minor discomfort. But here's the thing: on this view creating a new happy person adds nothing, it's morally neutral, and creating a person whose life contains any suffering whatsoever is wrong. And the view has a brutal implication that basically everyone accepts as a refutation: if you could painlessly end all life instantly, you'd be obligated to, since a world with no people in it has no suffering and you can't do better than zero suffering. So in the end the two views are mirror images -- classical utilitarianism maximizes happiness, negative utilitarianism minimizes suffering, and the whole debate is just which direction you point the arrow."

I have a feeling some of this is right and some of it is sleight of hand, but I don't know the literature well enough to locate the seams. Can you go through it piece by piece? Specifically: what a careful negative utilitarian would actually sign off on, where he's conflating different versions of the view (I suspect the threshold thing is doing more work than he lets on -- like whether "any suffering whatsoever" can even be right given how he described the threshold himself), whether the painless-wipe-out-everything point really follows from the lexical threshold version or is the objection that version was invented to dodge, and whether the "mirror image, just flip the arrow" framing at the end holds up or paper over real asymmetries between the views. Also tell me if the bit about suffering being "just negative pleasure" is something utilitarians assume or something he's smuggling in as a definition.
