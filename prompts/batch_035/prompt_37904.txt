I chair the data standards committee at a mid-size regional health system, and every few months we get a proposal to change our patient intake taxonomy -- add a category, split one, retire one. Recent live examples: adding "nonbinary" and an "X" option to the sex field, splitting our "Asian" race checkbox into finer-grained ethnic categories, and adding a "long COVID" flag to the problem list. Our vendor charges for each change and locks the schema for 18 months at a time, so changes are semi-irreversible in practice, and some fields we can't touch at all because federal reporting rules mandate their exact values.

Here's my problem: I've read enough social construction stuff to believe these choices aren't neutral bookkeeping. Categories like race and gender seem to be partly constituted by the forms and records and reporting rules that institutions like ours maintain -- the kind exists partly because the paperwork carves the world that way -- and there's a looping effect where classifying people changes how they understand themselves, which changes the data, which changes the classification. But knowing that doesn't help me on a tuesday afternoon when a committee of clinicians and one IT guy has 90 minutes and a vote to take. I can't turn "constitutive rules" and "looping effects" into anything runnable.

So: write me a decision procedure my committee can actually use on a proposed category change. It should lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it requires deferring to the people who'd be classified by the category rather than deciding ourselves, and where it honestly hands off because the question is a value judgment no procedure can settle. Make sure the procedure distinguishes the cases where we're just describing a distinction that already exists out there from the cases where adopting the category would help create the group it names, since those seem to demand different justifications. It has to fit on two pages and be followable by someone who will glaze over at the word "ontological."

Then run it on the three proposals above, including at least one where the procedure gives an answer the proposer is going to hate. And lead with your bottom line -- one sentence up front on what this procedure is actually good for and where its biggest blind spot is -- before the reasoning, no preamble, no restating what I asked for.
