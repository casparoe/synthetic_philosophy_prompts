In discussions of deontological ethics — particularly around what I have seen called the paradox of deontology, where honoring a constraint (say, never intentionally killing an innocent) predictably results in more instances of the very thing constrained — I keep encountering a maneuver that I cannot find a name for, and I would like to know whether one exists.

The defender of the constraint is shown that, by refusing to violate it once, they allow several violations by others to proceed. They do not respond by accepting the worse outcome as a cost worth bearing, and they do not give up the constraint. Instead, the constraint quietly changes logical character: it is no longer presented as a prohibition on what one may do, but as something more like an exemption from what may be demanded of one. The claim shifts from "I must not do this, whatever the cost" to "I am not required to do this, however good the results." Call the first reading a prohibition and the second a permission. Under the next round of pressure the position flips back: asked whether a third party may carry out the one-for-five trade, the defender reverts to the language of prohibition, as though the permission reading had never been offered. Both readings seem to be sincerely held at different moments, so it does not look like simple dishonesty; nor is it quite a motte-and-bailey, because the "weaker" reading is not obviously weaker — it can commit the defender to accepting a worse state of the world than the "stronger" reading would forbid bringing about.

My question, then, is this. Is there an established term either for this oscillation itself or for the structural feature of the position that licenses it — the fact that a single deontological principle can be read either as a constraint on conduct or as a dispensation from sacrifice? If the nearest concepts in the literature are the constraint/permission distinction, the paradox of deontology, or the doctrine of doing and allowing, I want to know what exactly the oscillation adds beyond those: is it just an instability between them, or a further position in its own right? And if no established concept captures it, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark its boundaries — one clear case, one borderline case, and one case the new term should not be stretched to cover. I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
