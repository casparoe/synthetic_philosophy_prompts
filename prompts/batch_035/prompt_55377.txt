I'm a PhD student in behavioural public policy, and for the past year I've spent two days a week seconded to a mid-sized city's behavioural insights unit. The unit no longer runs its own trials; it reviews everyone else's. Any department that wants to alter choice architecture — a new default, added or removed friction, a reordering of options, a reworded letter, a reminder scheme — has to clear us before the proposal reaches the council. We see roughly eight proposals a month. The weekly review meeting allots about forty-five minutes per proposal, nobody has read the documents in advance, and the attendees are economists and designers, not philosophers. The stakes are real but not catastrophic: what we approve quietly shapes what a million residents confront when they enrol a child in daycare, register as an organ donor, pay a fine, or consent to the city's use of their data.

Our problem is that we have no principled review procedure, only instinct and a shared folder of Sunstein PDFs. Half the team believes that any intervention passing an asymmetric-paternalism test — one that helps the inattentive or boundedly rational without materially harming the attentive — should be approved; the other half objects that this test says nothing about autonomy, manipulation, or the awkward fact that the same toolkit serves sludge as readily as it serves welfare. Our director has asked me to draft a one-page procedure the whole team can run in the meeting.

Please write that procedure for me, and then stress-test it. In constructing it, I'd like you to preserve the distinctions that do real work in the literature — means versus ends paternalism, error correction versus preference override, the difference between a nudge and a shove, and between a nudge and sludge — while dropping whatever cannot survive a forty-five-minute meeting attended by non-philosophers. The procedure should specify, in order: the questions to ask about a proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a no, where it terminates with an automatic yes without further debate, and where it explicitly hands the question upward to the council as a value judgment the unit is not empowered to make. Be honest about the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the situations in which the procedure can be expected to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three actual proposals on our current docket, chosen because they break the easy cases:

1. Switching organ-donor registration from opt-in to opt-out, with no individual notification that one has been enrolled by default.
2. Making the default daycare enrolment the "standard" package — meals, extended hours, a surcharge — with parents free to opt down to the bare package.
3. Imposing a mandatory twenty-four-hour cooling-off period before a payday-loan contract becomes binding — deliberately added friction, aimed against the borrower's own expressly stated immediate preference.

I want the third case to be the one where the procedure returns an answer I won't like: I am broadly sympathetic to the cooling-off intervention, and if the procedure clears it too easily, tell me why that ease should make me suspicious of the procedure rather than comfortable with the verdict.

Please structure your response under these headings, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

- **The procedure** (500–700 words): the numbered questions in order, each with its sufficient-answer standard and its terminal outcomes (no / automatic yes / hand up to the council).
- **Worked application: the organ-donor default** (about 200 words).
- **Worked application: the daycare default package** (about 200 words).
- **Worked application: the payday-loan cooling-off period** (about 250 words, including the uncomfortable verdict).
- **Where the procedure misfires** (about 150 words): the predictable failure modes, not the exotic ones.
- **What the procedure cannot register** (about 200 words): the live objections to nudging — for instance, that the enterprise habituates citizens to expect being steered, or the critic's worry that "bias" sometimes just means "disagreement with the policymaker's model of the good life" — that no well-run application of this procedure could ever catch, and why.
