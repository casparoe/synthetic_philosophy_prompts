I teach an intro ethics course, and for the unit on the ethics of procreation I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe by listing cases and writing down my gut verdicts, like a good reflective-equilibrium person. Here's what I've got so far. A couple who could easily have a child who'd have a wonderful life but just don't want kids: nothing wrong there, not even a little. A couple who conceive knowing (say from genetic testing) that the child will have a life of unremitting misery: clearly wrong, and wrong *to the child* somehow, even though the alternative was no child at all, and you can't wrong someone by not creating them. A couple who conceive now rather than waiting two years until they'd be more financially stable, so the child (a numerically different child, since the timing matters) has a merely fine life instead of a great one: I say not wrong, and nobody was wronged. A couple who conceive when there's a 30% chance the child will have a condition that makes life barely worth living, and 70% chance of a good life: this feels wrong to me, like gambling with someone else's welfare, though if the gamble comes out bad and the grown child says their life is worth living, I can't say who was wronged, which bugs me. A couple who conceive in poverty knowing the child's life will be good but much worse than if they'd waited: not wrong. A donor who declines to donate eggs that would produce a happy child: obviously not wrong, there's no obligation to create happy people.

So there's this asymmetry I keep feeling — that it's bad to create miserable people but not bad to fail to create happy ones — and I want to know if there's a principle that captures all of it. My utilitarian friend says the whole thing collapses if I just count up total wellbeing, but I think that view implies we're obligated to crank out as many kids as resources allow, which I absolutely reject, so total utilitarianism is out as an explanation of my own verdicts. And I also have a pair of verdicts that look hard to square: I'm fine with a couple having a child whose life will predictably be just barely above neutral (chronic low-grade unhappiness, but the person ends up genuinely glad to be alive), yet I judge the 30%-chance-of-bad-life couple harshly. A certain mediocre life is okay but a gamble with a good expected value... wait, actually I'm not even sure the gamble has a good expected value, anyway, the certain mediocre one is fine and the risky one isn't, and I can't currently say why without being embarrassed. Also, somewhat at the margin: I feel a real difference between the misery case (clearly wrong) and a case where the child's life is predictably a bit *below* neutral — mild chronic suffering, never quite glad to be alive — where I waffle between "wrong" and "shabby but not wrong."

You're a professional ethicist who works on population ethics, so please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a few candidate principles before settling. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the single principle (or small set of principles, if that's really what it takes) that best captures my verdicts — and then walk through the reasoning with no preamble and no restating of my question. I want you to state each clause precisely, tie it to the case that motivates it, tell me its verdict on a couple of cases I haven't listed (say, a case where delaying conception would prevent a definite short-but-decent life in favor of a longer better one, and a case involving a "right to be born once conception has occurred"), and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate — especially whether I have to give up either the barely-above-neutral verdict or the 30%-gamble verdict, because I suspect I do.
