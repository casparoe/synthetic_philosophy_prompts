I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and a recent column of mine on the rationality of voting in large elections provoked a disagreement between two readers whose exchange I'd like to see adjudicated. The column made the familiar point that a single vote is almost never causally decisive, so the direct instrumental case for voting is weak. One reader, a mathematician, wrote in defending the practice on what he called superrational grounds: in an electorate of millions, there are bound to be many voters who deliberate as he does, weigh the considerations as he does, and reach their decision through much the same process — so his choice is not an isolated event but one node in a pattern, and the right way to reason is as if deciding for the whole class of similar deliberators, which multiplies the impact of his ballot enormously. A second reader, a philosophy PhD student, replied that this is an elementary confusion of correlation with causation: the similarity of other voters' deliberations explains why their choices tend to co-vary with his, but their ballots are already sealed or will be marked independently, and no amount of resemblance makes his pencil cause theirs to move. The mathematician answered that this objection proves too much, since it would also condemn one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, where the payoff correlations plainly seem worth acting on even without causal influence; the student countered that Newcomb involves a predictor whose behaviour depends on your earlier type, whereas the electorate contains no such mechanism, only a statistical resemblance — and that the superrationalist quietly helps himself to a symmetry condition ("people who reason like me") that is never actually satisfied, since even two people who reach the same conclusion typically do so from different private information and for different reasons, and in any case a resemblance in reasoning is not a channel of influence. The mathematician's last word was that decision theory which cannot distinguish choosing in a crowd of your own kind from choosing alone has missed something fundamental about what agents are. They have each written to me asking for a verdict, and I am out of my depth beyond a certain point.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say who is right at each step and to what degree, identify what (if anything) each is missing in the other's argument, and give an overall verdict on whether superrationality rescues the rationality of voting in large elections — or whether it rests on a genuine but different insight that the argument misuses. Where the disagreement turns on the contrast between evidential and causal ways of valuing outcomes, please make that contrast explicit rather than gesturing at it, and address the one-boxing analogy directly rather than treating it as obviously decisive for either side.
