I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this semester on intentionality, and our professor has been running a recurring segment he calls verdict day: he brings in a system, we argue, and he commits to a ruling on whether it genuinely has beliefs or understanding, as opposed to merely being something we can usefully talk about as if it did. We're compiling the rulings to prepare for the final session, and I'd like help predicting the verdict on the new case before we discuss it.

The track record so far. Ordinary adults and dogs: genuine beliefs, full stop. A thermostat: nothing remotely worth calling a belief, though he granted that even there, intentional-stance talk can be convenient shorthand. Searle's Chinese Room considered as a whole system, not just the man inside: no understanding — the symbols mean nothing to the room, and whatever meaning there is lives in the rulebook's authors and the users outside. Dennett's wandering coke machine, the one moved to a country where a different coin of the same size and weight starts operating the mechanism: when we pressed him on Dennett's claim that the machine, after the currency change, really does believe the coin is a quarter, in an attenuated but genuine sense, he flatly rejected it — the machine believes nothing, and saying it does is a manner of speaking about a mechanism whose designers had the beliefs; notably, though, he conceded the intentional-stance description is predictively handy right up to the point where the coin changes, and then a human has to physically recalibrate the machine. Finally, a strong chess engine: no beliefs, not even the modest belief that its queen is in danger, though he again allowed that treating it as if it wants to protect its queen is a useful heuristic for describing its search behavior.

The new case, which he has announced for the final session: our campus has replaced the old vending machines with smart ones. These recognize coins and banknotes through a camera running a model trained on millions of images; when a country redesigns a coin, the machine eventually recalibrates itself after mis-classifying a few samples, and it flags suspected counterfeits in ways that vary with lighting, wear, and the machine's own recent error history. Nobody on the engineering team wrote the coin-recognition dispositions into the code; they emerged from training, and the team members apparently cannot fully articulate why it draws the boundaries it draws. The question is whether this machine believes, even minimally or derivatively, that the thing in its slot is a quarter.

It seems to me at least two rules fit his verdicts equally well. On one reading, the operative variable all along has been origin: genuine intentionality requires the right kind of source — biological, or at least not merely derived from designers' intentions — so the smart machine is just a fancier two-bitser, and the answer is no. On another reading, what did the work in his earlier verdicts was calibration: the two-bitser and the chess engine fail not because they are artifacts but because their dispositions are frozen — only an engineer's physical intervention re-tunes the two-bitser, and the chess engine never learns from a loss — whereas a system whose classifications are reshaped by its own errors is at least a candidate for minimal belief. Those rules agree on everything so far and disagree on the new case. And I can imagine a third rule, something about whether intentional talk is strictly more predictive than the mechanistic alternative rather than merely convenient, since that seems to be the distinction he was drawing when he granted the heuristic usefulness of as-if talk about the chess engine while denying it beliefs.

What I'd like from you: lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record (including any I've missed, and telling me honestly if one of my two is a bad fit to any verdict), say what each predicts for the smart vending machine, and give me your best guess at his verdict with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals. Then tell me what further case would most cleanly separate the rules — I'm half thinking of a recognition system built from biological neurons or a controller produced by selection with no designer at all, but you may see better ones, and I'd like the machine you'd put in front of him if you wanted to force his hand. Finally, since this is a prediction about a person and not a theorem: tell me how confident you actually are, what consideration could most plausibly flip his answer one way or the other, and where your reading of his track record is most likely to have gone wrong.
