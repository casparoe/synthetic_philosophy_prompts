Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about Parfit's hitchhiker, and we finally had to call it a draw over a question neither of us could resolve cleanly. I'd like your help thinking it through.

To set the stage: the hitchhiker is stranded in the desert and will die unless a passing driver takes him to town. The driver will stop only if she believes the hitchhiker will pay her once they arrive, and — crucially — she is an excellent judge of character and can predict his behavior reliably. Once in town, with no contract and no enforcement, paying appears to serve no further interest of his; refusing would seem to be the individually rational move. Because the driver knows this, she drives on, and the perfectly rational hitchhiker dies in the desert while a somewhat more sentimental one rides home and pays.

In the course of our argument, two different diagnoses of the case emerged, and I want them compared properly.

The first, which my friend defended, is the older idea I'd call "rational irrationality," in the spirit of Schelling's work on commitment. On this view, the hitchhiker's problem is genuinely one of momentary irrationality: at the moment of payment, paying really is against his interests, all things considered. What he needs is not cleverness but a disposition — stubbornness, pride, a sense of honor — that operates against his own interests in the moment. Possessing such a disposition is rational, even though exercising it is not. Rationality, on this picture, attaches to what you are rather than to what you do, and the case shows that being the kind of agent who sometimes acts irrationally can be a rational investment.

The second diagnosis, which I found myself defending, comes from decision theory: the updateless or policy-based approach. Here one argues that the hitchhiker never really faces a choice between paying and not paying in the ordinary sense. In the desert, before knowing how the driver's prediction turned out, he chooses (or should reason as if he chose) a policy, and the policy of paying maximizes expected value — after all, it's the policy that gets him out of the desert at all. On this view, when he later hands over the money, he isn't being irrational and isn't being rescued by a convenient character flaw; he is carrying out the only decision he ever rationally faced. The "irrationality" was an illusion created by evaluating the moment of payment in isolation.

So here is what I want from you: a careful comparison of these two positions. Specifically — do they amount to the same recommendation dressed up differently, or do they genuinely disagree about the structure of the case? Does the rational-irrationality view concede something the updateless view avoids, for instance about whether the hitchhiker's payment is really against his interests once we've fixed the context of choice? Are there cases where the two views come apart in what they would recommend — perhaps situations where an agent can't choose or commit to a policy in advance, or where the "disposition" at stake is repugnant rather than admirable? And finally, does either view better explain why cases like this make us suspect that something is wrong with the standard picture of instrumental rationality in the first place, or is that suspicion itself confused?

Please reason through this rather than appealing to authorities by name; I'm interested in the structure of the disagreement, not in who holds which view.
