I'm a PhD student in epistemology and our department has a little reading group that this term is doing the classics on the ethics of belief. Last week was Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," and our discussion leader kept reaching for the famous dictum -- the one about how it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence -- to swat down basically every attempt to defend the shipowner. Thing is, I went back to the actual text afterwards and noticed the argument doesn't quite run on the version of the line everyone repeats. What Clifford keeps insisting on is stuff like the claim that the shipowner had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him, and that the question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it -- not whether it turned out true, but how he got it. That's noticeably different from a threshold reading where you just check current evidence levels, since the whole guilt story on Clifford's telling is about the guy having knowingly worked himself into that frame of mind. The repeated dictum has come loose from that context and I honestly don't know anymore what the one-liner is claiming.

So here's what I want: treat the dictum itself as the object, not Clifford's essay, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I have a rough sense there are at least these candidates and probably more you can think of: (a) a bare evidentialist claim about epistemic justification, which most epistemologists already accept and which would make the dictum close to a platitude; (b) a moral claim, that the belief itself, not just actions taken on it, is morally wrong; (c) a presupposition about doxastic voluntarism -- that we can choose what to believe, since otherwise the "wrong" doesn't get a grip; (d) a process claim, roughly Clifford's own, that the wrongness lives in how the belief was formed and maintained, so that two people holding the same belief on the same evidence could differ in culpability; (e) a strict threshold claim, that there is some amount of evidence short of which believing is always impermissible, with no room for the pragmatically urgent cases James wanted to carve out. For each reading tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or just confused, and -- this is the part I actually need for seminar -- which reading is required for the uses the dictum gets put to in practice. Like, when my discussion leader used it against the shipowner, did that need the process reading, or would the threshold reading have done the same work? And when people deploy it against, say, religious believers or people holding optimistic beliefs about their own projects, which reading is doing the labor there, and does it hold up?

Give it to me as a numbered menu I can literally bring to reading group, with a verdict per item, and then a short section at the end on which precisifications would actually support the anti-shipowner verdict and which would leave him off the hook. I'd rather be told the dictum on some readings collapses into something everyone already believed than be handed a heroic unified interpretation, so if you think that's the honest upshot, say so.
