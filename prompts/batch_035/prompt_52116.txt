you've probably seen this one play out a hundred times on lesswrong and in the surrounding blogosphere, but I want your read on why it never goes anywhere. The debate is epistemic akrasia: believing p while also believing that your evidence doesn't support p, or doesn't support it as strongly as you believe it. The mainstream philosophy position, going back to Feldman and Christensen, is that this is somehow uniquely bad, the epistemic equivalent of knowingly contradicting yourself -- not just having a false belief but having a fractured one. Then a few years ago Sophie Horowitz came along and argued that akrasia can be perfectly reasonable: someone who is, as a matter of fact, excellent at getting things right but terrible at tracking how good her evidence is should trust her conclusions over her self-assessment. And the rationalist-adjacent version of the same fight shows up constantly: "should I trust my inside view over the outside view," "should I trust my math over my intuition when they conflict," "should I discount my own judgment because I know I'm biased," and the perennial "if you know you're not calibrated, shouldn't you adjust for that, and doesn't adjusting-for-that also use the same uncalibrated brain." I've watched this specific exchange -- one side says akrasia is incoherent, the other says no, it's just using your best info about your own reliability -- go in circles for what feels like a decade, with everyone from graduate students to very smart hobbyists, and nobody ever moves.

My working theory, and I want you to actually evaluate this rather than just nod along, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one label. One side is talking about what a perfectly rational agent does at an instant: at the moment of belief, if your evidence says .4, believing at .4 is just what it is to believe on your evidence, and believing at .7 is by definition a malfunction. The other side is talking about agents with a history -- agents whose faculties have a track record, whose self-models can be more or less accurate, and for whom "overriding the meta-level verdict" can be the policy that has always worked, like the person who knows she underestimates herself. If that's right, the disagreement is partly verbal, but not entirely verbal, because lurking underneath there seems to be a real question about whether epistemic rationality is even the kind of thing that can be assessed at an instant, or whether it's necessarily a property of procedures and dispositions. That part doesn't feel verbal to me, but I can't tell whether it's substantive in the way that could in principle be settled, or in the way where it's just everyone's bedrock intuition about what "reasons for belief" are made of.
