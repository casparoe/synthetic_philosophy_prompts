My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I'd like someone to adjudicate the centerpiece of the dispute rather than the whole war.

She takes the Epicurean line: death is nothing to us, since when it arrives we no longer exist to be harmed, and nothing can be bad for someone who is not there to suffer it. Her favorite support is the symmetry argument from Lucretius: the time after your death is a mirror image of the time before your birth. We regard the eons of nonexistence before we were born with perfect equanimity — no one lies awake grieving that they didn't exist in the Jurassic — so it is irrational to dread the nonexistence after death. My reply was the deprivationist one: death is bad not because of anything that happens while we're dead, but because it cuts off goods we would otherwise have had. She says that just begs the question — it assumes the future is ours by right, and the prenatal past refutes that assumption: those future goods were no more ours than the goods of the Jurassic were.

What I'd like is a proper audit of the mirror analogy rather than another round of slogans. Please lay out what maps onto what (prenatal past to posthumous future, birth to death, the nonexistence on each side), and then work through where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies to weigh: our practical and emotional lives are oriented forward in a way they are not oriented backward (we have projects and expectations about a future we might have, but no analogous expectations about a past we never had); we can act now on the posthumous future but not on the prenatal past; death removes goods that are specifically desired and anticipated, while prenatal nonexistence removes nothing that was ever ours in prospect. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the conclusion at issue or is mere detail. Then give your verdict: does the Epicurean conclusion — that death is not bad for the one who dies — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And if the mirror is a bad picture, sketch what a better analogy for death's badness would be, if there is one.

Please answer in American English.
