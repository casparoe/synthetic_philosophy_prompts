So I'm a novelist, about halfway thru a book set in a small Virginia town, told through one family whose men have been Confederate reenactors for three generations. Before I keep going I want to check whether the things I actually believe hang together, because I keep saying versions of them in interviews and I'm no longer sure they do. Here's the set: (1) the Lost Cause is a deliberately constructed myth and I want zero part in rehabilitating it; (2) the novelist's job is to render every character from the inside, with real sympathy, and never step outside the scene to judge; (3) the novel is itself an instrument of public memory, and romantic fiction about the Old South is one of the main ways the myth actually got built and passed down; (4) the readers I most want this book to reach are precisely the ones for whom this is family history and who half-believe the gentler version of the story; (5) I have no patience for didactic fiction and would be embarrased to have the book's verdict delivered through some mouthpiece character. It seems to me that (2) plus (4) commits me to a book that humanizes the believers, while (1) plus (3) tells me humanizing the believers is exactly the mechanism that kept the myth alive, and then (5) maybe makes the whole problem worse by taking away the easy way out. Is that right, or am I creating a dilemma where there isn't one? Go through the set, tell me which of these genuinely fight, which only look like they fight, rank the tensions by severity, and if there's a cheapest fix say what it costs the book.
