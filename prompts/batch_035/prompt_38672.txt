I've been testing my intuitions on moral responsibility and alternative possibilities, and I've ended up with a set of verdicts I can't reconcile. I'd like to know whether some single principle captures them, or which ones I should give up.

Take Frankfurt's classic setup: Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain and will force Jones to vote for the Democrat if Jones shows any sign of wavering; as it happens, Jones decides entirely on his own. Jones had no alternative, and I think he's fully responsible. Relatedly, suppose the only thing Smith could have done otherwise is trivial — she was free to wiggle her toe while casting her corrupt vote, but every live alternative to the vote itself was blocked. I still hold her responsible, so worthless alternatives don't seem to help. Yet when someone reasonably believed the door was locked when it wasn't, and so never considered escaping the room to help a person in distress, I don't hold them responsible even though a real, robust alternative existed — it was just epistemically inaccessible to them.

History seems to matter to me too, but inconsistently. A woman who knowingly took a drug that destroyed her self-control, and who then wrongs someone while under it, is responsible — the impossibility is her own doing. But a man covertly conditioned since childhood to want exactly what he wants, who wholeheartedly endorses his (awful) values and wrongs someone, is not responsible, even though he's structurally in the same position as Frankfurt's Jones: no alternatives ever existed, and he acts from desires he owns. And in a genuinely indeterministic case — where there's no intervener at all, but the agent's decision could have gone either way by chance — I find myself holding the agent responsible, yet without confidence, and I can't say what the chance bought him.

So: is there one principle here? It looks like I sometimes care about the quality and accessibility of alternatives, sometimes about where an inability came from, and sometimes about neither. Please either give me a principle (or a principled division of labor between several) that fits these verdicts, or tell me which verdict to overturn and why that one rather than another. Then tell me what the resulting account says about a case I haven't decided: the intervener has already partially acted — she has quietly dampened the agent's capacity for resistance without removing it, so the agent could still have done otherwise, but only with much greater effort than a normal person would have needed. Responsible or not, and for what reason?
