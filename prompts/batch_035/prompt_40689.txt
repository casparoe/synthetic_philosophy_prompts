I've been going around in circles on Fitch's paradox — the proof that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is already known. I'm basically sympathetic to the verificationist / anti-realist idea behind the knowability principle, but I don't want to just grab whatever patch is closest. So before I commit, I want the spec: what does a restriction of the knowability principle have to deliver to count as a real solution?

My tentative list, which you should fix and extend: it has to keep the principle substantive (not quietly restrict it to truths we already know); the restriction has to be independently motivated, not reverse-engineered to block Fitch's derivation specifically; it has to survive the strengthened versions of the paradox (the ones showing that even restricting to "not currently known" truths, or iterating the knowability operator, re-derives the bad conclusion); and it has to preserve whatever motivation the anti-realist had for the principle in the first place, so the cure isn't worse than the disease. For each requirement say what it's for and whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want to see, since I have a feeling the "independently motivated" requirement is doing most of the work and might be unsatisfiable in practice.

Then, briefly, score Tennant's restriction to cartesian propositions and Dummett's restriction to basic decidable statements against the list. Be honest if one of them just fails outright.
