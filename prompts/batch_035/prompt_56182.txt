I am a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years in thermodynamics and statistical mechanics — who has lately and somewhat belatedly turned to philosophy of science. I have drafted the short essay below for a local reading group, and before I embarrass myself before an audience containing actual philosophers, I would be grateful for comments. The essay concerns Dutch book arguments, which struck me immediately as a species of impossibility proof, and I have run with that observation, possibly further than it deserves to go.

Here is the draft:

For a chemist trained on the second law, the Dutch book argument has a familiar shape. Thermodynamics is, at bottom, a catalogue of things one cannot do: no process whose sole result is the complete conversion of heat into work, no machine that pumps heat from a cold reservoir to a hot one for free. The Dutch book theorem says something structurally similar about an agent whose betting quotients violate the probability axioms: anyone who can trade with such an agent at her own quoted odds can extract money from her with certainty. Both are no-go theorems about a system coupled to an environment. In both cases, the theorem is a statement about an idealized interface, and this, I want to suggest, is both the strength of the argument and the source of its limitations.

Three idealizations do the work, and each has a thermodynamic analogue. First, frictionless trading: the bookie faces no transaction costs, has unlimited liquidity, and can buy as well as sell at the agent's quoted quotients. This is the analogue of the frictionless piston and the quasistatic process. Carnot's theorem bounds the efficiency of an idealized engine; no real engine attains it, and we do not conclude that real engines are irrational. Second, the synchronic argument targets an agent's announced odds, not her beliefs as such. An agent who simply declines bets that displease her cannot be booked, just as an isolated system that is never coupled to a reservoir cannot be drained, whatever its internal state. The argument constrains the agent-market interface, not the agent. Third, and I believe least appreciated, the diachronic argument for conditionalization smuggles in what I can only call a quasistatic assumption: the construction requires that between the time the conditional bets are sold and the time they are settled, the agent's probabilities change exactly and only as a function of the evidence received. If her quotients drift for any other reason — fatigue, mood, a cleverly arranged drink — the bookmaker's arbitrage evaporates, and no violation has been demonstrated. In thermodynamics we know perfectly well which processes are reversible and which are not; in the philosophy of probability, the analogous assumption is built into the construction without acknowledgment.

My tentative conclusion is that Dutch book arguments should be read as engineering feasibility constraints on the design of agents that must operate in markets, rather than as constitutive norms of rationality. That reading preserves the theorem, discards the pretension, and has the pleasing feature that it explains why the arguments feel so much more compelling to decision theorists than to the rest of us.

Now, my requests. First, and most importantly, accuracy: have I characterized the synchronic and diachronic arguments fairly, or set up a strawman? In particular, is the point about the diachronic construction depending on the agent's probabilities changing only via the evidence received something already recognized in the literature, or have I misdescribed the construction itself? Second, I would like to know whether you think the thermodynamic analogy is earning its keep or is mere decoration by a man with a hammer. If the analogy breaks down somewhere, tell me where, concretely. Third, tell me whether the abstention point — that refusing bets is a trivial escape from the theorem — is a familiar objection with standard replies, described in general terms; I am not asking for reading lists or references. Fourth, an honest assessment of the essay's weakest section as a piece of philosophy, and what a stronger version of my conclusion would look like. Throughout your reply, please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point about which you are least sure. I would rather be corrected now than in front of the reading group.
