My father was diagnosed with early-stage dementia last spring, and at a family meeting the memory-care counselor reassured us by saying his situation was like a computer whose hardware was failing while the software stayed intact: the man we knew was all still there, and only the machinery running him was degrading. Since then this picture has been doing a lot of work in our household — it underwrites my mother's insistence on talking to him exactly as before (the program is still running, it's just slow), my brother's hope that some future intervention will "restore access" to him as if he were data on a damaged drive, and my own worry that we're using it to avoid facing what's actually being lost.

I know the analogy descends from functionalism in philosophy of mind — mind as software, brain as hardware, the same mental states realizable in silicon or neural tissue. But I'd like a careful audit of it in our context. What is the correspondence supposed to be — what plays the role of the program, the processor, the storage medium, the input devices — and is any of it faithful to how dementia actually works? Where does the mapping break down: does the brain store anything like a program separable from its implementation, and is the pattern of degradation in dementia anything like the way failing hardware affects a computer? Which features of the computer side does the analogy quietly import that the brain lacks — and which of those imports actually matter for the conclusions we've been drawing, as opposed to being harmless idealizations? Finally, does the reassurance the counselor offered survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it rest on the very features of computers that brains don't share? If the software/hardware picture fails here, is there a better analogy for what's happening to him — and would it be more or less comforting?
