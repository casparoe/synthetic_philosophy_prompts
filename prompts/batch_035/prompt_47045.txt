I've been reading a pop economics book (won't name it, it's not famous anyway) and there's a chapter on David Hume's economics that left me feeling like I can't tell the wheat from the chaff. I know Hume wrote a bunch of essays about money and trade way back in the 1750s — I think one of them is literally called Of Money, but I could be misremembering — and the book presents him as having basically destroyed mercantilism before anyone else even took the problem seriously. Here is roughly what the chapter says, condensed:

Hume saw that money is not wealth but only the counter we use to measure and exchange real things. Since money is just a counter, the quantity of it makes no difference whatsoever to a nation's prosperity — if you magically multiplied the money in everyone's pockets overnight, the only effect would be that prices would rise in proportion, and the country would be exactly as well off as before, just with bigger numbers on everything. He also worked out that trade balances regulate themselves: when a nation runs an export surplus, gold and silver flow in, prices rise there relative to its neighbors, its goods get expensive, and the surplus evaporates on its own — so trying to get rich by hoarding treasure and holding down imports is self-defeating. Because trade is self-regulating in this way, Hume concluded that tariffs should be abolished entirely and commerce should be as free as possible between all nations. And since money is a mere counter, he also regarded banks and paper credit as adding nothing real to an economy.

Then the chapter wraps up by saying Hume essentially invented monetarism two centuries early and would have been against any central bank creating money, so all the attempts to stimulate a sluggish economy with new money are refuted in advance.
