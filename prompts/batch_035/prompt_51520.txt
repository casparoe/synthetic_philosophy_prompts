For a workshop on democratic theory, each participant has been asked to circulate a short position statement in advance so the others can interrogate it. Here is mine, on the ethics of voting, in five claims:

(1) I accept the standard empirical picture: in any large election, the probability that my single ballot is decisive is so small that the expected causal effect of my voting — on any remotely plausible valuation of the outcome — is negligible.

(2) I nonetheless believe there is a genuine, though pro tanto, duty to vote in most elections, grounded in something like fairness or equal participation in a collective scheme rather than in outcomes; and I suspect this duty is stronger in low-turnout local elections than in national ones.

(3) When one does vote, and two candidates are viable while one's sincerely preferred candidate is not, one is often obligated to vote for the lesser evil, because of what the vote does with respect to the outcome.

(4) Where a voter cannot in conscience support either viable candidate, she should abstain rather than cast a ballot for a minor candidate in a close, high-stakes election: abstaining is permissible, voting her conscience is not.

(5) I find the expressive account of voting most convincing — the primary thing a ballot does is communicate what the voter endorses, and its causal role is largely vestigial.

Before I send this around, I want an audit. Go through the claims and tell me which pairs stand in genuine tension, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, and, for each genuine one, how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be — what that revision costs, not just what it says. Then tell me which single change, if any, would clean up the most. I honestly do not know where the problems are, so do not soften anything: I would rather repair the position now than defend an incoherent one in the room.
