i'm TA-ing a personal identity course and next week we close out the Parfit unit. the students did fine with the standard teletransporter cases but what actually got them arguing was the variant where the machine on Mars breaks after the scan: repairs take a few days, so the original gets to walk around on Earth knowing he'll die before the replica gets built. Parfit reports that when he imagined this he felt relief rather than terror, and that the relief is the interesting part: we dread death because of what it cuts off, and most of that (uninterrupted projects, someone continuing your life) is still there, even though identity itself — which is all-or-nothing by definition — is gone. what the seminar couldnt agree on was whether the relief is insight or self-deception.

Write a sonnet in the voice of that person, say on day three of six. the octave should be the fear arguing the way the identity theorist does — that won't be me, so i'm about to be annihilated, no different than ordinary death — but keep getting undercut by the concrete facts the fear bumps into: nobody is widowed mid-sentence, the book still gets written, the chapter isnt ended, only one body stops. the volta at line 9 should be the move from the question is it me? to the question what was I actually dreading about dying anyway. and the closing couplet must not claim the replica is him, and must not fall back on any living-on-in-metaphor line either — let it land on the thought that the identity question hasn't been answered but has stopped mattering, which is roughly were Parfit himself ended up.

one more constraint: somewhere in the poem put a line that would have to change if the machine on Mars never got fixed and there was no replica at all — so the poem registers the difference between dying and merely not being me. it should be accurate enough that someone who knows Reasons and Persons wouldn't wince at the psychology, but it also has to work as a poem for a reader who just picks it up cold, maybe 30 seconds before they decide whether to step in the cubicle themselves.
