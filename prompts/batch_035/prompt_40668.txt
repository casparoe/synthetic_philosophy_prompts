I am to give the principal toast at my sister's wedding, and I would like your help writing it.

Some necessary background. My sister is completing a doctorate in social theory; her dissertation concerns Marx's account of alienation. She has a well-known party trick in which she explains, after a second glass of wine, the four ways labor can be alienated — from its product, from the activity itself, from what Marx called our species-being, and from other people — followed immediately by her own cheerful thesis that a life is well spent to the degree that it reverses all four. The wedding guests will include roughly a dozen of her colleagues from the sociology department, who will catch every term and every misused term, and who will judge the toast accordingly. The remainder will be family, among them the groom's parents, who own a small manufacturing company and grow visibly uneasy at the mention of Marx by name. The groom himself runs a start-up, and my sister's long-standing joke about the engagement is that she has at last found a form of labor he cannot exploit.

What I want is a toast of no more than five minutes that takes her fourfold scheme seriously enough to build on it: a marriage as the place where what you make belongs to you, where what you do is chosen rather than compelled, where your capacities are developed rather than stunted, and where the person across from you is an end rather than an instrument. But it must never become a lecture; it must contain nothing a sociologist would wince at; it must require no one in the room to have read anything; and it must not say Marx's name aloud where the groom's parents can hear it. It should close with the exploitation joke, arranged so that it lands as affection rather than as an insult to the groom or a capitulation to his investors.

Please write the toast, and append a short note on what you had to leave out in order to keep it a toast rather than a seminar — including anything in her scheme that resisted translation into the language of a wedding.
