I edit a small blog that sits between philosophy of law and criminal-defense practice, and I want to run an interactive feature called "Which position on moral luck do you actually hold?" The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so the questions must be answerable entirely on intuition, with no philosophical jargon and no reference to any author, position, or paper. A reader who has never heard of the luck debate should be able to complete it, and a reader who has should not be able to tell which answer is supposed to be the sophisticated one.

Please design the quiz as follows:

1. Ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases must include at least the following, though you should add others as needed: (a) two equally drunk drivers, one of whom kills a child who runs into the road while the other arrives home uneventfully; (b) a contrast between a sniper whose bullet is deflected by a bird flying past and one whose rifle jams because of a one-in-a-million mechanical fault, so that the reader is forced to say whether it matters how close the nearest world is in which the intended harm occurs, and not merely whether it occurred; (c) the praise parallel, which most discussions ignore: a rescuer who saves a life partly through luck, testing whether readers treat resultant luck symmetrically between credit and blame or asymmet-rically; (d) constitutive luck: someone recruited into a brutal movement at age eight and indoctrinated, contrasted with someone who despises that movement but, had they been born forty miles away, would have been recruited themselves; (e) discovery luck: two identical acts of embezzlement, one of which comes to light through an unrelated audit and one of which never does, forcing readers to say whether being caught changes anything beyond the practical consequences.

2. At least two of the ten questions must present a shared scenario whose options split two different axes: what the agent is guilty of (the content of the offense, its severity) versus what it is now fair for others to do in response (punishment, blame, exclusion, demands for apology). These axes are routinely run together in conversation, and my readers argue about them constantly, so the quiz should be able to catch someone whose view is that outcome luck affects the second but not the first.

3. One further case should involve aftermath rather than act: two people cause the same harm, but for one of them the harm leaves ongoing traces — an injured party they must face, memories, a debt to repair — while for the other it does not (the victim dies instantly with no family, say, versus surviving). This is meant to catch readers whose real view is that outcome luck matters only through the traces it leaves in the agent's subsequent life, not through the bare fact of the outcome.

4. Build the scoring key from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first set out the combinations of answers the ten questions permit, then locate positions in that space. The key should at minimum be able to identify: the strict view that luck in outcomes never affects blameworthiness; views that accept such luck for how serious the offense is but not for how bad the person is; views that accept luck about circumstances and constitution while denying it for outcomes; the wholesale revisionist conclusion that if luck penetrates this far, blame itself should be abandoned or radically restricted; and views that relocate the whole difference to forward-looking matters — what the agent now owes, or what it is fitting to feel toward them, rather than how much they are to blame. Where a standard label exists for a leaf, use it; where one does not, say so.

5. Flag explicitly any pair of positions your questions cannot separate, rather than pretending the scoring is finer than it is. And note any answer combinations that are coherent but that no one, to your knowledge, defends — with a sentence each on why: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?

Do not include a reading list, citations, or references to particular books or articles; the quiz and key should be usable by someone with no way to look anything up.
