I'm in the upper sixth writing my Extended Project Qualification on whether morally objectionable art can still be good art. My supervisor, who runs the philosophy society, is a committed moralist about this: she holds that a work's moral flaws are genuine artistic flaws, so a morally corrupt vision necessarily detricts from a work's achievement. My EPQ co-examiner, the head of drama, is an equally committed autonomist: he thinks aesthetic and moral evaluation run on separate tracks, so a film can be morally repellent and aesthetically magnificent at once, with neither verdict touching the other. They have been arguing past each other all year — usually over the same examples (a technically brilliant piece of Nazi propaganda, a beautifully written novel narrated by a repugnant character, a comedy whose jokes trade on cruelty) — and I've come to realise that at least some of their shouting matches are fuelled by them using words like 'good' and 'flaw' differently rather than by genuine disagreement, though I can't always tell which parts.

For my project I want to do what they should have done long ago: draft the joint statement that would come out of a proper adversarial collaboration between the two camps. Please write the document both of them could put their names to. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims they can both sign — I'd expect some of these to be about cases where their verdicts coincide, about the fact that moral content can be part of what a work expresses, and about the difference between a work depicting something and endorsing it; (2) a table stating their exact residual disagreements, with each side's position phrased in wording that side itself would accept (my supervisor would not sign 'moralism just means being squeamish', and my examiner would not sign 'autonomism means anything goes'); (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the experience of being morally disturbed by a scene is part of the aesthetic experience of the work or merely a separate reaction to it, and another is whether a work can invite its audience to enjoy what it depicts without endorsing it; but please work out the cruxes properly rather than taking my word for it.

For each crux, if you can, give a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict about that case — for instance, if we discovered that a morally transformative reinterpretation of a novel changed how beautiful readers found its prose, or the reverse, what would each camp expect and why? If you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal (say, over whether 'good art' means 'art good at being art' or 'art good all things considered), please say exactly which part and why. Please write the whole answer in British English, and keep it at a level I could actually present to both of them without either feeling strawmanned.
