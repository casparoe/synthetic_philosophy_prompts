Just got back from a 10-day silent retreat, and the anatta teaching has me arguing with myself. A friend from the retreat and I worked out this argument on the drive home and neither of us can find the hole, but the conclusion can't be right.

(1) The anatta teaching says that when you examine experience carefully, you never find a self: each of the five aggregates — body, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness — is impermament and not under your control, and a self is supposed to be permanent and in control, so none of the aggregates can be the self.

(2) But the examination itself is an activity. Noticing, attending, comparing a candidate to the concept "self" and rejecting it — those are things that get done.

(3) Whatever gets done is done by someone. An act of searching presupposes a subject doing the searching.

(4) The subject doing the searching can never turn up in the results, because it's the one doing the looking — the eye can't see itself. So the failure to find a self among the contents of experience was guaranteed in advance.

(5) So all the retreat really showed is that the self isn't among the objects of experience. Going from "no self observed" to "no self at all" is a scope mistake.

(6) Worse: the meditator who reaches the no-self conclusion is trusting a process that presupposes the very thing it concludes doesn't exist. So the conclusion is either self-refuting or it came out of a method that couldn't have come out any other way.

I'd rather keep the anatta teaching than give it up, but I honestly can't see which premise to drop, and my friend is now convinced some Advaita-style witness talk is the way out. Give me your diagnosis in this shape please:

- "Where it breaks" — name the specific premise or inference you reject and why, in 3-4 sentences
- "Why it looked fine" — a paragraph on what makes that step so easy to accept
- "The price" — a paragraph on what the Buddhist has to pay once that step is rejected (be concrete: if a premise gets dropped, what claim about actions and agents goes with it?)
- "Does it generalize?" — a short section on whether the argument, if it worked, would break other first-person investigations too

400-500 words total is fine. And no citations or reading lists please, I want the reasoning itself, not a bibliography.
