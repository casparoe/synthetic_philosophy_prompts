I've been convinced for a while that the self-sampling assumption is the right way to do anthropic reasoning: reason as if you're a random sample from the observers in your reference class, but give no extra probability boost to theories just because they predict more observers exist. The big payoff, for me, is that it dodges the presumptuous philosopher problem -- if two theories make identical physical predictions but one has trillions of trillions more observers in it, the self-indication assumption forces you to be almost certain of the big-world theory, purely because your existing is more probable if observers are everywhere. That seems insane, and SSA avoids it. So what would actually count against my view? Give me the defeaters: cases where SSA-driven reasoning lands somewhere I should find unacceptable (does it really saddle me with some version of the doomsday argument?), places where the answer swings wildly on how I draw the reference class, anything from cosmology or decision theory that puts pressure on it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed SSA-er would concede is a genuine cost versus wave away, and be honest about whether the freedom to redraw the reference class has insulated SSA from any possible counterexample -- and if it has, whether that's a fatal flaw or just par for the course in this corner of philosophy.
