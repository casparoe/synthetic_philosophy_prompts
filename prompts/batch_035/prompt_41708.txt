I'm a PhD student TA-ing a grad seminar on rational choice / decision theory (about 12 students, we have a 90-minute block), and next week's topic is imperfect recall. I want to open with the absent-minded driver rather than end with it, so they hit the weirdness before any formalism. Design me the in-class activity.

Quick setup of the problem in case it helps you design around it: a driver is on a highway at night, has to get off at the second of two identical exits. Payoffs: get off at the first exit and you're in a bad area (0), get off at the second and you're home (4), miss both and you end up in a motel past the second exit (1). The catch is the driver is absent-minded in the specific sense that they cannot remember whether they've passed an intersection already, and they know this about themselves. The ex ante best plan turns out to be a mixed one (roughly: go straight with probability 2/3 at each intersection), which beats both pure plans. But there's a standing fight about whether, standing at an intersection and knowing you're equally likely at either one, you're rationally required to stick to the mixture — the "action" perspective and the "planning" perspective seem to give different advice, and people have argued about whether the problem is even well-posed (whether an agent with that kind of memory loss can really be modeled as making decisions at all).

The activity should make them feel the problem from the inside, not hear about it. My vague idea is something where students take turns being the driver, with the facilitator tracking the true state privately and only ever revealing final payoffs, plus some kind of distractor task between intersections so they can't quietly keep count — but you should feel free to redesign or replace that. What I want is: (1) a runnable structure with rules, materials, and a point/token payoff scheme (no real money), that fits 90 minutes with 12 people including the debrief; (2) the debrief mapping what happened onto the planning optimum, why the mixed strategy beats the pure ones (have them compute it themselves from the class's accumulated results if possible), and where exactly the action-vs-planning dispute bites; (3) an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I especially worry about students smuggling memory (counting, checking the clock, positional cues in the room) and about someone in the cohort refusing to randomize on principle and derailing things mid-game; and (4) a frank note on which features of the problem the activity can't reproduce no matter what I do — e.g., simulated absent-mindedness is voluntary, and there's a difference between forgetting you decided and being unable to retain anything at all, and I suspect the availability of a physical randomization device during the game changes what the paradox is supposed to show. If you can also suggest a short follow-up homework that exploits the token data from the session, that'd be a bonus, but the in-class design is the priority.
