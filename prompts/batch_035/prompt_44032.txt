I compete in Lincoln–Douglas debate at my high school, and over the winter break my cousin, who used to coach our team, walked me into an argument that I cannot now get out of. Every step still looks right to me when I check it alone, and yet the conclusion is one I refuse to believe. I would like to know which step to give up, and what giving it up costs.

Two definitions first, so that we mean the same things. Prioritarianism, as we use it in rounds: the moral value of a state of affairs is a weighted sum of everyone's welfare, where the weight attached to a person is strictly greater the lower that person's welfare level, so that benefits to the badly off count for more. The leveling-down objection: it cannot be better in any respect that some people are worse off and no one is better off; a theory that implies a pure leveling-down is an improvement in even one respect is thereby false. My cousin handed me this objection in an article, I have since seen it deployed as the standard weapon against egalitarians, and I believe it.

His argument:

(1) A change that harms some and benefits no one cannot make the world better in any respect; so any theory implying that it can is false.
(2) Egalitarianism of the relevant kind, on which inequality is bad in itself, implies exactly that: moving from (8,10) to (8,8) makes no one better off, makes one person worse off, and is an improvement in respect of equality. So that kind of egalitarianism is false. I accept this.
(3) Prioritarianism appears to escape: since each person's weighted contribution rises with her welfare, a pure leveling-down always lowers the weighted sum, so prioritarianism never ranks the leveled-down state as better. I accept this too.
(4) But the escape works only if the weights are a function of how a person's life goes taken just by itself. If the weights instead depend on how she stands relative to others, then prioritarianism values states of affairs partly in virtue of people's relative standing, which is what the egalitarian does; and relative standing is improved by pure leveling-down, so by (1) the view would be false for the same reason.
(5) The weights cannot be a function of how a person's life goes taken just by itself. "Worse off" is a comparative: worse off than whom? Remove every other person from the universe and leave one person's life exactly as it is; the claim that benefits to her deserve extra weight now has nothing to rest on. Priority is a ranking, and a ranking of one person is no ranking at all. So the extra weight must track standing relative to others.
(6) The weights therefore depend on relative standing, and by (4) and (1) prioritarianism is false.
(7) What goes for prioritarianism goes for every special concern for the badly off. A hospital that gives its last dose to the sickest patient rather than the one it would help more is moved, at bottom, by a concern for relative standing, and by (1) that concern is condemned whenever it does less total good; the only defensible ground for directing a benefit anywhere is its effect on the total. And if the grammar point in (5) is right, the same holds within a single life: nothing makes a person's worst hour matter more than her best hour except comparisons with her own other hours, which would make the relief of suffering mere egalitarianism about one person's timeline.

I can accept that the egalitarianism in (2) is false. What I cannot accept is the conclusion of (7): that there is never any reason, over and above the total, to direct help at the person whose life is going worst, and that the pull I feel toward the patient at the bottom, when I could as easily help someone at the top, is nothing but disguised egalitarianism, condemned in every respect. Yet (5) is the step that sounds most obviously right to me: "worse off" does look comparative, and "priority" does look like ranking-talk. (1) and (2) I would very much like to keep. Which step should I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost?
