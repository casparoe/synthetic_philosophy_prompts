I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and this term I'm leading a discussion section of fifteen second-year undergraduates for a course on infinity and the limits of formal reasoning; the section meets for fifty minutes in a flat-floored seminar room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and a stopwatch on my phone. Next week we reach infinite processes, and I want the students to arrive at Thomson's-lamp-style puzzles from the inside rather than from the whiteboard — in particular, I want them to feel the contrast between an infinite process whose end state is fixed by the setup (Zeno-style convergent cases, where the total is forced) and one whose end state is underdetermined, so that when the debrief finally names the puzzle, the "there's no fact of the matter" reaction is something they've already earned rather than something they're told.

Design the session for me. I want a runnable game or pair of games with explicit rules, a timeline minute by minute, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money — my current thought is a prediction market with worthless tokens that students wager on the final state of each staged process, but make your own call about whether that's the right mechanism and, if you keep it, how the payouts are set. The idea should show up in what the students do, not in anything I say: no lecture before the games, and the terms "supertask" and "Thomson's lamp" must not appear until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the games onto the formal distinction (a convergent geometric series versus an alternating sequence with no limit), and it should close by asking them what a completing stipulation for the indeterminate case could even look like. Also be honest about which feature of the puzzle the activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I already suspect the actual completion of infinitely many acts is one of them, since we'll manage perhaps a dozen toggles before human hands give out. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect someone to protest that the lamp burns out, and someone to try to win the betting market by meta-reasoning about my buzzer finger) and how to handle each.

A final request about the form of your answer: please be decisive. Make definite choices — exact timings, one payoff scheme, one debrief structure — and give your reasons for them, including where you override my suggestions above; don't lay out two or three options and leave me to pick, and don't hedge with "it depends on the group." If you think a third staged process is needed for the contrast to land, commit to including it; if you think the prediction market should be scrapped for something simpler, scrap it and say why.
