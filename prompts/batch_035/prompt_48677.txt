I have what seems to me a serious worry about the idea that the brain is a computer, but I have never found a standard name for it, and I would like to know whether one exists, and how philosophers who take the brain-is-a-computer idea seriously have responded to it.

The worry is about what makes a physical system count as running a particular computation in the first place. Suppose my friend and I each claim to have found a running instance of some program, say one that computes the decimal digits of pi. When I look at his system, it appears to be a rock with water running over it; when he looks at mine, it appears to be the traffic pattern on a particular stretch of highway. Each of us insists that if you group the physical states in the right way, define the right counterfactual dependencies, and label some states as inputs and outputs, the computation is there. The physical laws guarantee that the states succeed one another in a regular way, and once the grouping and labeling are fixed, the mapping onto the program's steps is exact. It seems that with enough patience I can find my pi-computation in almost any chunk of matter, and likewise a different, equally good slicing of the very same chunk will yield a different program entirely.

Two things strike me as bad about this. First, if every suitably complex physical system computes every program, then the claim that the brain computes, say, a predictive model of its environment is true but vacuous, since the rock does too. Second, and worse, the facts about which program is running seem to be fixed not by the physical system but by our choices about how to describe it, which makes computation sound like a projection of ours rather than a discovery about the world.

So my questions: Is there an established name for this triviality or over-permissiveness problem, and who, if anyone, has pressed it? What are the main strategies for answering it — for instance, demanding that the mapping between physical states and computational states be principled, or natural, or counterfactual-supporting, or that the computation be causally and mechanistically embedded in the system rather than merely read off it — and do any of them actually succeed in making it false that the rock computes pi? In particular, I would like to understand what separates a successful answer from one that merely relocates the problem: if we require some special relation between the physical dynamics and the abstract computation, what stops that relation from being just one more labeling choice? And how, if at all, does this interact with the familiar point that mental states are supposed to be individuated by their roles rather than their material makeup? If the problem has no settled name, please say so plainly, propose one, give a precise definition that distinguishes it from nearby worries such as the one about whether things like screwdrivers have their functions only relative to our interests, and explain where the edge cases fall — for instance, analog computers, ordinary laptops with degraded components, or brains under anesthesia. A bare label will not satisfy me; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and which of the standard replies draws the line where I would want it drawn.
