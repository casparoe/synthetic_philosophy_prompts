I've drafted a short essay (about 700 words) that I'm thinking of submitting to a general philosophy journal. It concerns the debate over whether qualia, as standardly conceived, are a coherent posit. Before I revise it, I'd like comments on several specific points, listed after the draft.

---

**Drift (draft)**

Dennett's "quining" strategy is often summarized as the claim that qualia don't exist, but the argument actually runs through a characterization: as I read him, qualia are conceived as having four marks — ineffability, intrinsicality, privacy, and immediate apprehensibility. A state's qualitative character, so conceived, is something that can be known from the inside without the possibility of error, cannot be shared or compared across subjects, and does not depend on its relations to anything else. The quining cases are then supposed to show that nothing could satisfy all four marks at once: any candidate for a quale either turns out to be describable, or relationally constituted, or corrigible, and so falls short.

Chase and Sanborn sharpen this with three scenarios, all involving coffee. In the first, Chase used to like coffee and no longer does; he cannot tell whether his tastes have changed or his standards have. In the second, Sanborn's pleasure in coffee has gradually faded over the years, and since his memories faded along with it, he cannot detect the change from the inside. In the third, the change happens all at once, but simultaneously with a shift in the comparative judgments by which he would have noticed it. Their conclusion is that our introspective judgments about experience are constructions: what seems like a direct reading off of the qualitative character is really an inference that can go wrong in ways the subject can never catch.

I want to put this in terms of an analogy, which I think is more than a restatement. Treat introspective report as a measuring instrument, and the qualitative character of an experience as the quantity it measures. Every real instrument can drift, and the standard remedy is calibration against an external standard that is known to be stable. For subjective qualitative character, no such standard is available: the only candidates are memory of past experiences and current comparative judgments, and both of these are parts of the instrument itself. The Chase–Sanborn cases are exactly the cases in which the instrument recalibrates itself, so that the drift is invisible from the inside. The moral seems to be that immediate apprehensibility — the mark on which the whole conception rests — could only be satisfied by an instrument that cannot drift, and nothing can satisfy that if it changes over time at all.

But notice that this moral, by itself, is epistemic. It shows that certain facts about qualitative character, if there are such facts, are unknowable from the first-person point of view. It does not show that there are no such facts. We are in the position of someone trying to determine whether a particular grain of sand on a beach occupied a given spot a century ago: there was presumably a fact of the matter, and we will never know it, and nothing about the case tempts us to deny that there are grains of sand. To get from the calibration problem to the eliminative conclusion, Dennett needs an extra premise.

The reply I expect is that the analogy fails precisely here: grains of sand are not conceived as fixed points of judgment, whereas qualia, on the ordinary conception, are — they are supposed to be the terminus of comparison, the thing that cannot be mismeasured by the subject whose experiences they are. Something that can drift undetectably cannot play that role, so the posit is incoherent rather than merely unverifiable. I suspect this reply is right, but if so, it makes the argument hostage to a substantive claim about how ordinary people conceive of their experiences, and the conclusion ought to be reformulated: not that qualia don't exist, but that the ordinary conception of experience is internally conflicted — a conclusion many philosophers would accept without giving up on colors, aches, and itches. Whether that reformulated conclusion still deserves the name of elimination is where I want the essay to end up.
