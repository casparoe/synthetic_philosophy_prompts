I host a small-run philosophy podcast that mostly covers metaphysics, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending perdurantism — the view that persisting objects are extended in time as well as space, so that you and she are four-dimensional wholes made up of momentary temporal parts. In recent work she has been pushing a version closer to stage theory than worm theory: she claims that ordinary names like "you" typically refer not to the whole spacetime worm but to a single, extended-in-time temporal stage, with persistence statements made true by relations among resembling stages rather than by a single thing being wholly present at different times. She is also fond of two less standard claims: that perdurance handles fission cases in personal identity more gracefully than any endurantist story, because there is no pressure to say one thing became two; and that the endurantists' adverbialist reply to the problem of temporary intrinsics — the move of saying objects bear properties in time-relative ways rather than tenselessly — secretly reintroduces temporal parts at the level of facts about having, even if not at the level of things.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. I want at least one question each on the following, with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: the temporary intrinsics problem and whether the worm theorist and the endurantist are really offering rival explanations or just rival bookkeeping; the counting objection — the complaint that stage theory tells us that the person sitting in the chair is a numerically different thing from the one who entered the room an hour ago, which many listeners will find incredible; the ethical or practical worry people raise about stage theory, namely that if strictly speaking you will soon cease to exist and be replaced by a successor stage, prudence and anticipation of the future seem unmotivated; and her claim that endurantist adverbialism smuggles in temporal parts, on which I would like her to say precisely what the smuggled entities are supposed to be, since opponents will say a way of having a property is not a part of anything. If you can work in a question about how her stage-theoretic treatment of fission interacts with the common judgment that the pre-fission person survives as both post-fission people, please do, since she tends to deflect that one by saying the question presupposes a one-one notion of survival, and I would like a formulation that makes that deflection harder.
