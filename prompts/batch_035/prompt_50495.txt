I'm a grad student TAing an intro philosophy of science course, and next week I get one fifty-minute section on the substantivalism vs relationism fight over spacetime, built around Newton's bucket. The audience is first-year physics majors — they know they're mechanics cold (rotation, fictitious forces, all of it) but have read zero philosophy. Don't explain substantivalism to me; plan how I should run the section.

Specifically: what has to be in place before the bucket even gets wheeled out — I suspect they need the distinction between "there's a fact about whether the bucket is really rotating" and "rotation is just motion relative to other stuff" made vivid before the example does any work — and then the order after that. And here's my actual dilemma: which example should carry the whole thing. Newton gives two. There's the bucket, and there's the two-globes case, where the globes are connected by a cord and you're supposed to read the true rotation off the tension in the cord even with nothing else visible at all. The globes are cleaner in a way (no water surface doing double duty) but the bucket is the icon everyone's already half-heard of. Tell me which one to build the section around and why, and what the carrying example has to have vs lack. One thing I've noticed about the bucket and want your read on: the surface is flattest when relative motion between water and bucket is at its maximum, and most curved when that relative motion has gone to basically zero, and I can't tell whether that inversion is the load-bearing part of the argument or just a trap for students who'll confuse themselves tracking two rotations at once.

Misconceptions — I expect (a) they'll think the concavity just shows rotation relative to the lab, so Newton wins on the spot, debate over; (b) someone will have half-heard that Einstein settled all this and spacetime being "real" means substantivalism vindicated; (c) they'll slide between absolute space as an invisible container you can in principle detect motion against, and absolute space as just the bare claim that rotation facts aren't relational facts. Where exactly in the sequence do I head each one off? And should the relationist countermove — that the water is really rotating relative to the distant stars, or to the bulk of the matter in the universe — get a fair hearing inside the fifty minutes, or do I flag it, name it, and postpone it?

Also tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (the hole argument? whatever general relativity did to this debate?) and how to say I'm skipping it so it doesn't sound like a cop-out. And one question I can ask at the very end that would show me whether they got the actual point rather than just learned the vocabulary.

Last thing, and it's a real constraint on how you answer: wherever you make a claim about what Newton did or didn't argue, or about how the debate actually runs, say how confident you are in it, and single out the part of the plan you're least sure of — I'd rather know what I need to go double-check in the Scholium myself than find out from a student.
