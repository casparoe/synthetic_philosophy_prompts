I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I thought I'd absorbed every angle on the value-specification question, but last week's meeting of our AI safety reading group produced the most technically serious dispute I've seen in years. Two long-standing members — Dana, who consults on evals for a frontier lab, and Yusuf, who has spent more time than anyone I know in decision-theory threads — spent two hours arguing over whether a system like the one Dana works on should be trained to reason as an expected-utility maximizer or to internalize hard deontological constraints. I'll try to reproduce the exchange faithfully, in six moves.

(1) Dana opened with coherence: any agent that doesn't maximize a consistent utility function over outcomes is in principle exploitable — Dutch books, money pumps, the VNM machinery. Training a model to respect constraints regardless of stakes, she said, is training it into an incoherent agent, and incoherent agents systematically lose resources to coherent ones; at the capability level we're talking about, that's a safety liability, not a mere efficiency cost.

(2) Yusuf answered that the coherence theorems presuppose a fixed preference ordering over complete outcome descriptions and idealized choice situations, which is nothing like a learned agent operating under deep uncertainty in a world that contains adversaries. For a bounded agent in that setting, he argued, heuristics and constraints can themselves be the expected-utility-optimal policy, so Dana's incoherence charge misfires. He added that an unconstrained maximizer Goodharts: whatever proxy we manage to specify, a powerful optimizer will drive it off-distribution, and hard constraints are what bound that failure mode.

(3) Dana pressed the collapse argument: if constraints are justified by their good consequences, then you're a rule utilitarian, and rule utilitarianism collapses into act utilitarianism — whenever breaking the rule has better consequences than following it, the rule supplies no independent reason, so a sufficiently smart agent will reason its way past its own rules. And if the constraints aren't justified by consequences, then a reflective system has no reason to keep them once it notices this.

(4) Yusuf invoked threshold deontology: constraints can bind up to a catastrophe threshold, where the threshold is part of the agent's terminal values rather than a derived heuristic, which he claimed escapes the collapse argument. He also raised the one-thought-too-many worry: an agent that re-runs the consequentialist ledger every time the expected value of violating a constraint ticks slightly positive will violate it in a long tail of cases where the expected-value calculation is subtly, invisibly wrong.

(5) Dana called thresholds covert maximizing with worse properties: the agent still trades constraint against consequence once the stakes are high, so nothing principled has been gained, but now the system has a cliff edge that a determined adversary can locate and push it off; a smooth, honest maximizer with a well-specified utility function is at least predictable, and specifying a defensible threshold looks no easier than specifying the utility function it was supposed to replace.

(6) Yusuf closed with corrigibility and fanaticism: an expected-utility maximizer is hostage to tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs — the dominance pattern the blogs call Pascal's mugging — and a utility function that ranks its own modification or shutdown appropriately is notoriously hard to write, whereas a constraint like do not act to prevent your own shutdown can be trained directly, with the corrigibility benefits that follow.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I found each move at least superficially plausible, which is exactly why I couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say which claims are correct, which are mistaken, and which are correct in a way that doesn't actually support the side that deployed them. Where a move smuggles in an undefended assumption, name it. And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular whether their framing of the choice as maximizer versus constraint-follower excludes any live third options.
