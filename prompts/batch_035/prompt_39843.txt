I've been going down a rabbit hole on procrastination and now I've argued myself into a corner and can't get out. Here's the background I'm working from: I read some pop-science stuff about how we discount the future in a weird non-linear way. The idea, as I got it, is that we don't just care less about future rewards by a fixed rate per unit of time — instead we care a LOT about the difference between now and tomorrow, but barely at all about the difference between year 5 and year 6. One consequence is preference reversal: if you ask me today, I'd rather get $120 in ten weeks than $100 in nine weeks. But ask me again when the $100 is one day away, and suddenly I want the $100. The rewards didn't change, only where I'm standing in time when I choose.

Okay so now my argument:

(1) Weakness of will is when you act against your own better judgment — you judge that one thing is best to do, and then you do something else anyway.

(2) With this kind of discounting, my preferences flip depending only on how close the reward is. Two weeks out I prefer studying over the party, the night of the party I prefer the party.

(3) Both of those preferences are equally mine. The far-off me who plans and the present me who bails are both just me at different times — there's no privileged vantage point from which one of them is the "real" me whose preferences count.

(4) So when the near-term preference wins, that isn't a self being defeated by something alien to it. It's just me doing what I most wanted at the moment I acted.

(5) Doing what you most want at the moment you act can't be acting against your better judgment, because your judgment at that moment just is whatever you want most then.

(6) Therefore there's no such thing as weakness of will. Nobody has ever procrastinated against their own will — including me, every single night this month.

And (6) can't be right. There's clearly a thing where you watch your own hand hit the snooze button while something in you is yelling to stop. The argument seems to cover the yelling part too, and it also seems to prove that the person who takes one bite of cake, immediately regrets it, and keeps eating never acted against their will, which feels like proving way too much.

Some step has to go, but I honestly can't tell which one. For what it's worth I'd most like to keep (3) — the "no real me" part — because I actually believe that bit. Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me, since they all feel load bearing right now.
