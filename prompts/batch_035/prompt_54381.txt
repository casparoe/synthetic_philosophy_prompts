I keep going back and forth on when deals between people with different moral views are actually okay, so let me just lay out my gut verdicts and see if they add up to anything. The general idea, in case it matters, is moral trade: people who disagree morally can sometimes each give up something they care less about to get something they care more about, so both end up better off by their own lights — the classic example is a vegan paying a meat-eater to eat less meat.

My verdicts on cases:

1. I pay my roommate $25 a month to not cook meat on days we eat together: fine, easy, we both win.
2. A pro-life group and a pro-choice group each cut their advocacy spending by the same amount at the same time, so their attack ads cancel out and both redirect the savings to direct services (adoption support vs. contraception access, say): fine, honestly this seems great.
3. Vote swapping, like in 2000 when Nader supporters in swing states traded votes with Gore supporters in safe states: fine, clever coordination.
4. Paying my neighbor $50 to vote for my candidate in the local council election: not fine. Feels corrupt, even though structurally it's alot like the roommate deal — money changing someone's behavior in a direction I prefer.
5. An animal welfare charity pays a poor guy $10 to sign a month-long no-meat pledge, knowing he eats meat maybe once a month because that's all he can afford: not fine, it's theater — they're buying nothing, and it smells like using him for a stat in the annual report.
6. Same pledge program, but targeted at people who eat meat daily, with the payment roughly covering their extra grocery costs: fine.
7. A tobacco company offers a health charity a large donation on the condition that the charity stops running anti-smoking ads, and the charity takes the money: not fine, feels like a sellout — even though if you squint it's just trade between parties who value different things, and both parties prefer the deal to no deal.

So my tentative principle is: a deal is fine iff it actually changes the counterfactual behavior (rules out the no. 5 kind of theater) and both sides genuinely prefer it to no deal. That handles 1-6 but says no. 7 is fine, and I'm pretty sure no. 7 is not fine. The obvious repair is to add a clause about money entering democratic or political processes — that splits 3 and 4 nicely — but 7 doesn't involve any election, and 2 and 7 look identical to me (mutual stand-down, both sides save resources for what they actually care about), yet one feels great and the other feels rotten. Maybe the missing ingredient is something about dealing with an adversary whose values you despise? But that seems to smuggle alot back in, and it's vague.

So: is there one principle that gets all seven verdicts, or close? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? And tell me what the resulting principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on — I buy certified carbon offsets for my flights from a reforestation project that I suspect would have been planted anyway once the next round of goverment subsidies came through. Does that fall on the theater side or the real-trade side, and does your answer change if the subsidy program gets cancelled later, so the planting only happens because people like me paid?
