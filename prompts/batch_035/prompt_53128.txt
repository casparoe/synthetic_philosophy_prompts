I spent the better part of four decades as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and for most of that time my real daily craft was induction, even though nobody in the lab called it that: fitting spectra, deciding between models of comparable goodness, judging which anomalies deserved a week of beam time and which were noise. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest, and I have been struck by how differently Hume's problem looks once one has properly absorbed the no-free-lunch results from the learning-theory literature — Wolpert's observation that, averaged over all possible problems, no learning algorithm outperforms any other, random guessing included. If there truly is no free lunch anywhere in the space of possible worlds, then every classical response to Hume seems to carry a hidden cost: some substantive, contingent claim about which sort of world we happen to inhabit, smuggled in under the respectable labels of logic, method, or rationality. In my notes I have taken to calling this the free-lunch debt of a position: how much factual structure it must quietly assume about the universe in order to purchase its inductive license.

Here is my request. Consider six familiar responses to the problem of induction: Hume's own skeptical dissolution, in which custom and habit rather than reason carry the inferential load; Kant's move of locating the uniformity of nature in the structure we impose rather than the structure we read off; Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication of the straight rule; Popper's contention that falsificationism dispenses with induction altogether; Bayesianism, with its priors and convergence theorems; and the Occam-flavored family of minimum-description-length and cross-validation methods — the ones my own field actually used, usually without philosophical ceremony. Assign each a free-lunch debt score from 0 to 10, where 0 denotes a position assuming nothing contingent about the world and 10 one that quietly assumes nearly everything, and then sort the six into tiers. For each, indicate in a few sentences where exactly the debt is hidden: which factual assumption is doing the work, and whether it is the same assumption Hume himself located or a genuinely different one.

Finally, I would like two judgments beyond the scores themselves. First, which position achieves the best ratio of free-lunch debt to inductive coverage — the most inductive license per unit of assumed structure — and why? Second, and this is the question I argue about with my old colleagues over coffee: do the no-free-lunch theorems genuinely deepen Hume's problem, or do they merely restate it in modern dress, so that Wolpert is Hume with linear algebra? My amateur suspicion is the latter, and I would welcome a careful correction if it is wrong — but I want the answer argued, not asserted.
