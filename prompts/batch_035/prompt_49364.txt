I have been trying to work out my actual position on consequentialist cluelessness — the thought that even a full and correct description of a choice's proximate effects leaves us ignorant of its long-run value, because every act ramifies through the world in ways we cannot trace — by recording my verdicts on cases and seeing whether anything unifies them. Here they are. Pulling a drowning child from a pond: the ripple effects of the rescue are exactly as unknowable as anyone's, and I do not care; the first-order effect settles it. Taking a different route to work, thereby changing the timing of a conception and so which particular people exist a generation hence: total cluelessness about the value of the downstream difference, and again I do not care, and I do not think I am making a mistake. A choice between two jobs I want equally, one with a modest but contained benefit to others, the other riskier with a larger potential benefit: here I feel the cluelessness, and it genuinely weakens my confidence, even though the ripple effects are no more unknowable than in the route-to-work case. Five thousand dollars to malaria nets versus five thousand dollars to work on reducing existential risk: my verdict is nets, on the ground that the far-future case depends on multiplying a probability I cannot estimate by a benefit I cannot size, and I do not trust myself to do that. Yet in an election I vote, and I would defend it by multiplying a vanishing probability of decisiveness by a very large benefit. And when a canvasser with a clipboard tells me my signature will avert a catastrophe with probability one in ten to the fortieth, I decline, and do not feel inconsistent — though on reflection I cannot see what distinguishes that case from the voting one except the size of the numbers. Is there a single principle here — perhaps about which orders of effects are legitimate inputs to deliberation, or about when second-order unknowing defeats first-order reasons and when it does not — that captures all of these verdicts? I suspect not. Tell me which verdicts a coherent position would force me to give up, state the principle you would put in their place precisely enough that it decides new cases, and tell me what it says about a case I have not made up my mind on: whether to keep eating meat, given that the effect of one person's diet on animal suffering is real only statistically, while the ripple effects of changing it are as opaque as any other.
