In a metaphysics course I am auditing, the professor distributed a handout summarizing the debate between counterpart theory and transworld identity. It went roughly like this: on Lewis's counterpart theory, nothing exists in more than one possible world. Instead, when we say that someone could have done otherwise, what makes this true is that in some other world there is a person who closely resembles him in the relevant respects, his counterpart, and who does the other thing. Each person has exactly one counterpart in each world, namely the person there most similar to him overall. Strictly speaking, then, statements about what an individual might have done are about someone else rather than about him; when we say the counterpart wins the election, we are speaking loosely, and what we really mean is that he himself does not win. Counterpart theory also carries a heavy metaphysical price tag: since worlds must contain literal people standing in resemblance relations, possible worlds have to be concrete entities, so anyone who prefers to think of worlds as abstract objects like stories or propositions cannot accept it. The rival view, transworld identity, holds that the very same individual exists in many worlds, but it faces the problem of transworld identification: we would need a criterion for picking out, among all the people in another world, which one is ours, and no non-circular criterion can be given. Kripke's famous remark about Humphrey, who would deplore a theory on which he himself never wins the election, only someone else resembling him, seems to settle the matter, and Lewis is usually read as conceding this in his later work.

Something about the handout bothers me: it makes Lewis sound as though he thought ordinary modal talk about people was disguised talk about other people, whereas I had understood him to claim he was giving truth conditions for claims about us. But the professor is a professional and I may be wrong. I am especially unsure about the claims that the counterpart relation is unique, that modal statements about individuals are strictly speaking about someone else, and that counterpart theory requires concrete possible worlds. And does the conclusion the professor drew in lecture, that counterpart theory is really a theory about qualitative resemblance between distinct people and so cannot capture what is distinctive about de re modality, follow from whichever parts of the handout survive your audit?
