I've been following the responsibility-gap debate since it was mostly about lethal autonomous weapons — Sparrow's old killer-robots argument, then the meaningful human control people, and lately the same template being applied to LLMs making consequential decisions. The standard story is that a gap opens when a system causes harm and no human in the chain either intended the outcome, foresaw it, or could have intervened in time, so that nobody seems apt to blame. I've been testing that story against my own gut verdicts on cases, and my verdicts don't line up with it, so I'd like to work out what principle I'm actually using.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. An autonomous car running a learned collision-avoidance policy executes a maneuver its engineers could not have predicted, and a pedestrian dies; I can't bring myself to blame the engineers, and blaming "the company" feels like a dodge — so here I accept a gap. A trading firm deploys a fully autonomous market-making algorithm while knowing about its tail risks, and it triggers a flash crash; nobody at the firm could have intervened in the moment, yet I have no trouble holding the firm responsible — no gap. A hiring model trained on historical data systematically downrates older applicants, and the company deploys it at scale; again no gap, the company is squarely responsible, even though no engineer intended the bias. A commander launches a loitering munition under rules of engagement he wrote loosely, and it strikes civilians in a way he could not have foreseen; I hold him responsible, though less than if he had aimed at them deliberately. But then a hospital deploys a triage model nobody audited, it systematically deprioritizes a group of patients, and here the gap feeling comes back — I balk at blaming the administrators, which looks flatly inconsistent with my hiring verdict, and I can't articulate why. And in a case where a fully autonomous research agent writes a vulnerable code library that third parties adopt and get breached through, I genuinely don't know whether anyone up the chain is responsible at all.

Is there a principle that delivers these verdicts, or close to them? The obvious candidate — a gap just in case no human foresaw or could intervene — is contradicted by my trading and hiring verdicts, since in both, no human could intervene in the moment, yet responsibility lands on the deployer. My tentative repair is that a gap only opens when the harm fails to trace back to any upstream act humans did perform: deploying, tuning, setting thresholds, choosing what to audit. But then the puzzle is why that clause covers the trading firm and the hiring company while somehow not reaching the hospital administrators. Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it saves and which it forces me to overturn, and tell me what it predicts for the research-agent case. And if my "no gap when a deployment decision exists" repair ends up doing all the work, I'd like to know whether the resulting notion of a responsibility gap is thin enough to be worth keeping at all.
