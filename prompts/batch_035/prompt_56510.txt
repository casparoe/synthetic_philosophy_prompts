I need your help building something practical out of an argument I couldn't win. Last week a friend and I spent an entire evening fighting about whether atrocities can be ranked, and we ended exactly where we started. Her position: any severity ordering smuggles in a metric chosen in advance — death toll, intentionality of the perpetrators, duration, number of survivor communities, ongoing legacy — and each metric picks a different winner, so the ranking tells you about the ranker, not the events; worse, comparison relativizes each atrocity by turning it into a data point in someone else's chart. My position: refusing to compare is itself a comparison, since declaring one event incomparable grants it a status no other event can attain, which is a ranking by another route; and besides, historians and teachers juxtapose these events constantly, so someone has to say when that's legitimate. The closest we got to common ground was her distinction between explanatory comparison (putting events side by side to explain mechanisms) and evaluative ranking (grading them on a severity scale) — she thinks the first is fine and the second obscene, and I'm not convinced the line survives contact with real cases, since a sidebar that "merely explains" can still quietly crown a winner.

Here's why I need this settled in operational form rather than over more wine. I sit on the review committee for a regional history curriculum board, and roughly a dozen times a year we get materials that compare atrocities in some way — museum exhibit scripts, textbook units, anniversary essays, teacher-submitted sidebars. The committee is eight people, none of them philosophers, we have about twenty minutes per item, and every decision has to be justified in a single paragraph that will be read by teachers and occasionally by journalists. What I want from you is a decision procedure the committee can actually run: an ordered list of questions to ask about a proposed comparison, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which failures produce an automatic rejection, and which points the procedure must explicitly hand off to human judgment because they're questions of values rather than facts. The scaffolding should come from the real philosophical terrain of this debate — commensurability across different dimensions of harm, what "uniqueness" claims are actually doing when communities make them, whether moral attention is a fixed pie that comparison redistributes, the difference between comparing and subsuming one event under another as an instance of a type — and not from generic editorial sensitivity guidelines, which we already have and which are useless for this.

After you've built the procedure, run it on three concrete items so I can see it work. First, an exhibit proposal that places a colonial-era famine and the Holocaust under a single thesis about how all imperial systems converge on genocide. Second, a textbook sidebar that ranks five atrocities by death toll, with a bar chart. Third, a commemorative essay for a school assembly that opens by declaring one particular event beyond all comparison — and per my side of the argument with my friend, I want the procedure to treat that claim as itself a comparative act and evaluate it as such, so tell me if you think that's a mistake. One honesty requirement, and I mean it: I actually want the death-toll sidebar to survive review, because I think quantification forces a kind of discipline that vague solemnity lets people avoid. If your procedure kills it, say so plainly rather than bending the procedure to spare my preference — and likewise tell me where the procedure will misfire, what kinds of cases it cannot settle, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules.
