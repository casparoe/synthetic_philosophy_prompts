I TA a second-year seminar called something like "rational choice and value" (about 18 students, one 75-minute session, no philosophy prereqs so the group is a mix of econ kids and lost first-years). Next week I want to do a session on whether "better than" is transitive -- you know, if A is better than B and B better than C, is A better than C. On the board it looks trivially yes, but I've been reading about these spectrum arguments where you line up a long chain of options that differ from their neighbors in some tiny, obviously fine way, and each pairwise comparison is a no-brainer, and then you end up having said the start of the chain is better than the end while also being unable to deny some comparison that forces the reverse (or forces you to accept something at the end you'd laugh at at the beginning). I want them to hit that wall with their own choices, not watch me diagram it.

Can you design the actual activity? Ideally something like: they get a real sequence of options (you pick the domain -- could be money vs. annoying experiences, could be weekend plans, could be outcomes for other people, whatever makes the psychology work), they're forced to make each adjacent pairwise choice on its own and commit publicly, and only at the end do we lay the whole chain out and look at where they landed versus where they started. No lecturing up front about transitivity or what spectrum arguments are, and please don't have them look anything up mid-activity. Then a debrief structure that maps their individual choices onto the premises of the argument, shows them the two or three places you can dig in your heels (deny transitivity, deny one of the pairwise judgments, deny that the chain composes) and what each of those costs.

Also two more things: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone refuses to play along with the pairwise choices, or the group is inconsistent halfway through and I won't know if that's the point or just noise), and be honest about which part of the philosophical problem the activity just can't reproduce -- I assume something about how the arguments are really about what you'd be rationally required to prefer, not just what a room of tired students does pick, but you tell me. If you can, make the options concrete enough that I can print them on cards without inventing much (actual dollar amounts, actual durations, that kind of thing).
