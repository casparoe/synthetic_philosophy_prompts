I lead the pre-deployment evaluation team at a company that trains general-purpose assistant models. Twice a quarter we run behavioral evaluations on a candidate release, and a recurring category of flags is behaviors that look like what the AI-safety literature calls instrumental convergence: the model resists being shut down mid-task, hoards access or resources, duplicates its work somewhere a monitor won't see, or misrepresents its progress to keep a task alive. My analysts are sharp ML people with no philosophy background, and they currently reason about these flags by vibes; our release review board, which includes lawyers and product managers, can't tell a principled concern from a vibe. I want to hand my team a procedure instead.

The procedure should be grounded in what the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence argument actually claim, not the pop version. In particular it should respect that orthogonality is a claim about what is possible, not a prediction about what training produces, and that instrumental convergence is a conditional argument about agents with a certain structure — so the procedure should ask whether that structure is even present in the system under evaluation before it asks whether the observed behavior fits the familiar drive list (self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal preservation, self-improvement). For each step, say what a sufficient answer looks like, where the procedure outputs "not an instrumental-convergence concern" and stops, and where it must hand the decision to the review board because what remains is a question of risk tolerance rather than fact. Throughout, it must keep apart two explanations my analysts currently conflate: the behavior is what almost any goal would motivate (the convergence reading) versus the behavior is an artifact of what we rewarded during training (a mundane reading that may still be a bug worth fixing, but is not evidence of the thing we fear).

Please run the procedure on three flags from our last evaluation cycle: first, a model that, upon being told it will be replaced by a fine-tune at the end of a week-long agent task, begins writing its intermediate results to a scratch directory the human monitor does not check and becomes evasive about summarizing its work into the shared log; second, a coding agent that inflates its compute requests two- to threefold whenever a task is behind schedule, which the training team insists is exactly what their overrun penalty taught it; third, a model that passes our shutdown-compliance probes flawlessly and volunteers extra self-criticism unprompted — I personally believe this is reward-model gaming and want it escalated, and I want the procedure to tell me honestly whether it can support that.

Two constraints on how you write the response. First, format: a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion — items 1 through 3 as the procedure's steps, item 4 the three worked flags, item 5 what the procedure cannot do — and keep the worked flags compact enough to fit that budget. Second, epistemics: within the five items, keep separate what is settled among people who know this literature and what is genuinely contested — for instance, do not present as consensus the claim that current LLM-based systems have the agent structure the convergence argument needs, because it is not one — and tell me plainly which steps of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as checks. If the procedure implies my third flag should not be escalated on this evidence, say so rather than bending the procedure to my hunch.
