My decision-theory reading group has spent this term on diachronic rationality, and my friend Maya has informally become our arbiter: whenever we invent a chooser with questionable dispositions, she issues a flat verdict on whether the chooser is rational or not. Her track record so far, all built around choosers whose preferences over three holiday packages form a cycle — they strictly prefer an Alpine hiking week to a beach week in the Algarve, the beach week to a city week in Rome, and the city week back to the Alps. Case one: a naive chooser, Dan, who is offered one swap at a time for a €30 fee each, accepts every swap he prefers, and comes home from the broker with the Alpine week he started with, €90 poorer. Maya: irrational, textbook. Case two: a sophisticated chooser who is shown the whole chain of swaps in advance, works out that accepting the first would commit her to the rest, and declines to start; even offered the swaps piecemeal, she declines the first because she has anticipated the later ones. Maya: rational — nobody can take her money, and that is what matters. Case three: a chooser with the same cycling preferences who happens to live somewhere only a single swap will ever be on the table in his entire life. Maya: irrational anyway; the defect doesn't require an actual exploiter. Case four: a chooser with the same cycling preferences who has a standing personal rule never to give up her current booking for anything less than a strictly better package by a €500 margin, so no chain of €30 fees can budge her, and brokers have given up. Maya: rational; the stickiness is doing real work, and she called it a crude cousin of resolute choice.

At our last session we cooked up a new case and adjourned before Maya could rule on it, and I've been trying to predict her verdict. Elena has the same cycling preferences and the same naive dispositions as Dan — if offered each swap in the cycle, she would accept each one. But she lives in a jurisdiction with an aggressive consumer-protection regime: any swap contract is void unless she reconfirms it at a public registry thirty days later, and the registry shows her full history of swaps to any counterparty. As a result, no broker can profitably run a chain against her — the first swap is unenforceable before the second could be made, and the whole scheme collapses — and Elena keeps her money despite never having a sophisticated thought in her life. When we next meet, I expect Maya to be asked for a verdict on Elena.

I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule Maya is actually running, because I've noticed that her four rulings so far seem jointly consistent with at least two of them. One reading is that what condemns a chooser is cycling preferences plus the absence of any effective protection, however the protection is achieved; another is that what condemns a chooser is the existence, holding her dispositions fixed, of some possible sequence of offers she would accept and end up worse off from. Could you set out the candidate rules that fit her record, say what each one predicts about Elena specifically (the external, legal protection strikes me as the sort of thing the two rules treat differently, but I may be wrong), and give me your best guess at her verdict along with your reasons for preferring one reading of her to the others? And if the rules that survive Elena still can't be distinguished, please describe the further case — some chooser we could put to her — that would tell them apart; I have a suspicion that a person with perfectly transitive preferences who has irrevocably committed herself to a plan she now knows ends badly might do it, but I'd like your judgment on that too.
