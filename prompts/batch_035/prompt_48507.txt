I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and this term I'm running the discussion section attached to our department's upper-level seminar on rational choice — 14 students, one 80-minute session, a seminar room with movable desks, a whiteboard, and no tech to speak of beyond my laptop. The coming week's material covers expected-utility maximization, Newcomb's problem, and the fracture line between decision theories that let you conditionalize on everything you've learned before choosing and ones that have an earlier, less-informed version of you choose a policy that binds you across states you might turn out not to be in. The canonical cases are the counterfactual mugging (a coin was flipped before you were asked for money; if it came up heads you'd have been offered a large payout for free, but it came up tails, and now you're asked to pay) and XOR blackmail (an extortion letter sent by someone with a decent predictor of whether you'll pay, set up so that your standing policy changes what receiving the letter is evidence about — in particular, whether it's evidence about your own health). I could lecture this. I'd rather they trip over it.

Please design a two-phase in-class game, played for points convertible into something small but real (you pick: coffee vouchers, exemption from one reading response, whatever keeps it above trivial). Phase one should stage the mugging so that the students deliberate both before and after learning the coin's result, and so they feel the answer appear to flip depending on where they're standing in time — with the reveal of the coin's result happening at a moment you choose carefully. Phase two should stage the blackmail as individually delivered letters, and I want one specific mechanic: the "predictor" should be, in some honest and visible way, the students' own earlier recorded choices from earlier in the session, so the past selves are doing the committing. The decision-theoretic positions must not be named or put on the board until the debrief; the order of revelation is the whole point, and the pull of "the coin has already landed, so paying is just losing" should be felt before anyone hands them vocabulary for it.

Then give me: (1) the debrief script, mapping particular moments in play onto the positions at stake — which moment was the updateless perspective, which was the conditioned one, and where the blackmail letters turned paying from a costly action into a self-undermining policy; (2) an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce — e.g., that a real predictor predicted *them*, not a stand-in, and that points are not money — and how to handle that without deflating the effect; and (3) a frank rundown of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, with fixes.

Take the role of an expert decision theorist with long experience designing classroom games that actually work. Before presenting the final design, brainstorm two or three candidate framings and tell me briefly why you're discarding the ones you discard, then commit to one and give the full, runnable version — rules, materials, timings, payoff tables.
