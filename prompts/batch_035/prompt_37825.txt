A German colleague of mine defends his country's church tax system by comparing it to club dues: a church, he says, is like any voluntary association — a sports club, a choral society — that members join, pay dues to, and can leave whenever they like; the fact that the state collects the dues on the church's behalf (and takes an administrative cut for the service) is mere bookkeeping, not establishment. Since freedom of association surely includes the freedom to set membership fees, he concludes that there is no principled church–state problem here at all. Please audit the analogy for me. Lay out the mapping explicitly — what corresponds to the club, the dues, the member, the resignation letter — and then show where it breaks. I can already see some trouble spots: membership typically begins with infant baptism rather than a deliberate act of joining; "leaving the club" requires a formal civil renunciation rather than just letting a subscription lapse; and exit can cost a believer the sacraments, burial, and — depending on the community — much more, which is not something a lapsed tennis player faces. There is also the churches' own side of the story: that those who stop paying still consume pastoral care, weddings, and funerals, so the dues are partly a response to free riders. Distinguish the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't, say whether the "mere bookkeeping" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and — if it doesn't — what analogy, if any, would do the work he wants done.
