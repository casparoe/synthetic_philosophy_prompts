I teach metaphysics, and every couple years I try to get a seminar excited about the necessity of origin, and every couple years I watch the same thing happen: the debate runs up to a certain point and just stops, and it's been basically stopped since the 90s. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a rerun of the positions — and honestly I can't tell anymore whether this is a real metaphysical disagreement or something else wearing a metaphysical costume.

The terrain, quickly. Kripke's move: the table in front of me couldn't have been made from a different hunk of wood, and by extension I couldn't have come from a totally different sperm and egg. Nearly everyone accepts something in that neighborhood. Then two flanks of trouble. First, the near-miss problem: change one base pair in the sperm — different person? One base pair, obviously not. Three billion, obviously yes. There's no line anywhere in between, both sides know it, so the Kripkeans retreat to the strict reading where the exact gametes are essential, which then looks absurdly rigid — the exact gametes with one minute's delay in conception presumably still give you you. Second, the how-far-back problem: if the gametes are essential because you came from them, why not the grandparents' gametes they came from, and so on back? That splits people into a source camp (just the immediate origin is in the essence) and an ancestry camp (the whole causal history), and I've never seen a principled arguement that decides it — only temperament. The best-candidate fix, where if the rival origin is close enough you just are the person from that origin too (Lewis toyed with versions of it), satisfied almost no one, since the cutoff for close enough is arbitrary. And counterpart theorists never really engaged: if you're already happy saying you could've had different parents because some counterpart did, the Kripkean cases never had grip on you in the first place. So the whole thing drifted into bioethics, and that, I think, is where the real stall is now.

In the bioethics corner the fight is live but the camps don't even argue, they assume. Take mitochondrial replacement: a woman with mitochondrial disease has IVF where her nuclear DNA is moved into a donor egg with healthy mitochondria. Clinicians, and a lot of ethicists, describe this as the same child she would have had, just not doomed to die young. A strict origin essentialist who counts the egg as part of the origin says no: that child would never have existed, a different healthy child exists instead, and all the moral accounting has to be redone on that footing. Same fight in embryo selection: does picking embryo 3 over embryo 4 benefit the child who gets born? Nobody converts. They cite each other and move on.

My working hypothesis, and it's only that: the dispute is downstream from an indeterminacy neither camp will name. The child she would have had is like the problem of the many — there's a candidate from the original egg, a candidate from the donor egg, and no fact about which one her child names, so each camp sharpens the vagueness in the direction it already preferred. And the Kripkean and the counterpart theorist may be answering different questions under one heading, one about the logic of de re claims, one about what actually individuates people. But there's no fact here is a wierd thing to say to a couple in a clinic, so I want the hypothesis pressure-tested, not nodded at.

So: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, bedrock intuition, or a hidden formal question about the semantics of origin claims? Diagnose it from what each camp actually says. And don't give me citations or a reading list; I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Then tell me what progress would even look like: a distinction, a semantics for origin claims that predicts where the intuitions blur, a principled cut between source and ancestry — or an argument that this is bedrock, and the bioethics fight should be settled some other way entirely. If you think the indeterminacy line is wrong, say why. The best-candidate people thought vagueness was a solution and I always thought it was a surrender, so I know I'm being inconsistent here; I'd like to know whether I'm being inconsistently right or inconsistently wrong.
