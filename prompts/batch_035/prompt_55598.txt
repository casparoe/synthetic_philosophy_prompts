The student's answer reads as follows: "(a) Jackson asks us to imagine Mary, a vision scientist confined from birth to a black-and-white room, where she masters everything there is to know about the physical processes underlying color vision—optics, retinal transduction, cortical processing, the complete causal story. One day she is released and sees red for the first time. It seems obvious that she learns something: what it is like to see red. Since she already knew all the physical facts, what she learns cannot be a physical fact. So there are nonphysical facts, and physicalism, the thesis that all facts are physical facts, is false. (b) The strongest physicalist reply is the ability hypothesis, which Jackson himself proposed in response to his own argument. On this view, what Mary gains on release is not new knowledge of a fact but a cluster of abilities: the ability to recognize red, the ability to imagine red, and the ability to remember red experiences. Knowing what it is like is knowing how rather than knowing that; abilities are not facts, so acquiring them does not contradict her possession of all factual knowledge. The crucial premise—that Mary learns a new fact—is therefore false, and the argument fails. (c) In the single-exposure case, Mary insists she still knows what red looks like even though she has lost every one of the relevant abilities. Since she is the best possible judge of what she herself knows, she does still know. So there can be knowledge of what it is like without the abilities, and the ability hypothesis is refuted. Because the ability hypothesis is the strongest physicalist reply to the knowledge argument, the argument goes through: Mary gained a genuine new fact on release, and dualism is true."
