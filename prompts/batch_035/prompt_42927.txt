i've been going through the literature on thought experiments and it keeps splitting into two useless modes: pure celebration (Galileo dropping weights! Einstein chasing a light beam!) or pure debunking that treats every case as rhetoric in a lab coat. Before I read any more theories of how thought experiments actually persuade people — and where they mislead — I want the spec first. Write exactly five numbered requirements that any adequate theory of how thought experiments do their persuading must meet, one or two sentences per item, no introduction, no conclusion, and no scorecard of existing views (I already know the Norton argument-view stuff and Brown's platonic-rationalism line, so don't just replay that debate either).

The purpose, so you can calibrate adequacy: good enough that when an author deploys a case I can decide whether the intuition it stirs in me counts as evidence or is just a reflex being pressed. A few things I suspect the requirements have to cover, but judge for yourself: misfires, and how a theory explains them — Dennett catalogued a whole bunch of ways intuition pumps can be rigged through framing, and a theory that can't say what went wrong when a pump misfires is only describing the successes; cases run in domains where nothing ever shaped our intuitions, like infinite sets or the far future, where the pump either runs dry or produces whatever the framer wanted; and what I think of as twist cases, where someone in the Kamm tradition runs thirty trolley variants each varying a single detail and the intuition drifts somewhere no individual variant supports. The theory has to cope with the drift, or at least say honestly that it's out of scope.

Also, in each of the five items, mark whether the requirement is basically settled among people who actually know this literature or genuinely contested — dont dress one camp's prejudice up as the consensus. And if you think two of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, say which two inside the relevant items; there is no conclusion section to save it for.
