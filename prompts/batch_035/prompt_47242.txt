I hold a fairly specific version of the idealized-preferences approach to aligning advanced AI: that the right target is not what humanity wants as revealed by surveys, purchases, and votes, but what we would want under idealized conditions — better informed, reflecting more carefully, becoming more like the people we aspire to be, and idealized together rather than separately, with the idealization run until it stabilizes rather than stopped at some arbitrarily chosen level of improvement. Call this the extrapolated-volition view. I find it attractive because it appears to respect autonomy (the values targeted are our own, not an external designer's) while answering the obvious objection that actual preferences are misinformed, adaptive to unjust circumstances, and mutually inconsistent. I am trying to decide whether to keep investing in this research direction, so I want a map of the view's exposure rather than a defense of it.

Please give me the defeaters, sorted by weight rather than listed neutrally, and I am especially interested in four clusters:

1. Structural defeaters. The extrapolation may not terminate, or may be radically underdetermined: small changes in the idealizing conditions (more knowledge versus better reflection versus shared development) or in the initial population whose volition is extrapolated could yield incompatible endpoints, and there appears to be no independent fact about which endpoint is the correct one. If idealization functions as a projective device rather than a discovery procedure, what exactly is the view tracking, and does the view collapse into aesthetic choice dressed up as moral fact?

2. The authorization problem. Actual me has not consented to being governed by idealized me, and the people the extrapolation most transforms — those whose idealized selves diverge furthest from their actual selves — are precisely the ones most thoroughly overridden. Is this simply paternalism with extra steps, and if so, is that a fatal problem or a cost to be priced against the alternatives?

3. Convergence assumptions. The view seems to require that idealized agents converge toward at least a negotiable consensus. What would count against that? Consider the possibility that second-order preferences never stabilize, or that increased knowledge sometimes removes a moral insight rather than adding one, so that some idealized endpoints are worse than the actual preferences they replace.

4. The proxy problem. Anything short of actually running the idealization requires human beings to model what the idealization would output, and that substitute tends to collapse into the modelers' own values. At what point does the view, so qualified, become indistinguishable from "well-intentioned people deciding for everyone" — and does that count against the view itself, or only against near-term implementations of it?

Please distinguish what a committed defender of the view would concede as a genuine cost from what they would diagnose as a confusion — for instance, treating the idealized agent as a stranger whose good is not ours, or assuming that actual consent is the only available form of legitimacy. Then tell me which defeater you take to be most serious and why, and where someone who accepts your ranking should jump ship: at what point should they abandon the view for a rival target, such as a bargain among actual preferences subject to side constraints, a negotiation among a small number of fundamental moral theories, or a direct specification of goods to be protected?

Finally, be explicit about your own fallibility here: which consideration, if it came out the other way than you judged, would most reorder your ranking; and where in the reasoning above do you think you are most likely to have gone wrong — the assessment of whether the underdetermination worry is decisive, the treatment of the authorization problem, or something else entirely?
