I've been reading rationalist blogs and their prediction threads for well over a decade, and there's a recurring fight I've never seen properly resolved: whether Malthus was right but early. For our local group's annual prediction review session, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two longtime members. One is a committed Malthusian: he holds that the core argument — exponential growth against a finite carrying capacity — was sound all along, and that its apparent refutations were merely one-time subsidies (fossil energy, the Haber-Bosch process) that shifted the ceiling without abolishing it; the delay, on his view, is exactly what a correct long-run theory should predict look like from inside the run-up. The other is a Popperian: she argues that a prediction with no deadline, whose every failure to date gets rescued by appeal to some unforeseen enabler, has been immunized against refutation, and that "right but early" is not a vindication the theory can claim but a retroactive license to never concede anything. Both should be equally competent: the Popperian must grapple with the fact that some genuinely good theories did survive early apparent refutations before their enabling conditions arrived, and the Malthusian must say what observation, even in principle, would lower his confidence rather than merely reset the clock. Let them end not with a winner but by locating the actual crux — roughly, whether the prediction is best modeled as a conditional with a not-yet-satisfied antecedent (so the Popperian's complaint about vacuity misses the structure of the claim) or as a tail-risk claim whose repeated not-yet should earn no Bayesian credit. Write the dialogue in American English, and keep it to roughly a thousand words.
