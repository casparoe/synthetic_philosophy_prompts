You are a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics and the measurement of wellbeing, writing for a technically sophisticated reader with no philosophy background.

I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company that builds logistics software for disaster-relief organizations. Part of my job is designing the scoring function that ranks incoming requests for a limited fleet of delivery vehicles, and my team has been arguing about how to weight one category against another. Roughly speaking, the requests we score involve two kinds of stakes: preventing acute suffering (medical supply shortages where people are in serious pain, exposure, untreated injury) and improving general conditions (comfort items, entertainment, marginal quality-of-life upgrades for people who are basically okay). One faction on the team wants everything on a single numeric scale so the algorithm can always produce a total ordering; another faction insists that no amount of the second kind of benefit can ever justify deprioritizing the first, no matter how the quantities stack up — that the two categories aren't tradable at any exchange rate.

I've since learned that this disagreement has a name and a history in philosophy: it maps onto the debate over negative utilitarianism and lexical threshold views, where certain severe bads are held to be incommensurable with any quantity of goods. I want to understand it well enough to turn it into something usable, not just argue about it.

Please design a decision procedure that a technical team like mine could actually apply when deciding how to score or triage requests — a sequence of questions to ask in order, with clear stopping points, that operationalizes the lexical threshold idea: how we would decide whether a category of harm is severe enough to be non-tradable, how to handle cases that fall near the threshold, and how to reason when the algorithm must still output a ranking even though the underlying values may not be commensurable. Be explicit about which steps are genuine value judgments that no procedure can settle and must be escalated to a human, and honest about where any such procedure will misfire or produce answers the team won't like. Then work through two concrete cases: a request to divert two vehicles from a large comfort-goods delivery to reach one site reporting untreated injuries, and a case where a modest suffering-reduction for many people competes with a large suffering-prevention for a few.

Before writing the procedure, brainstorm briefly: consider what a strict lexical view and a simple aggregationist view would each say about our scoring problem, and what each gets embarrassingly wrong. Think carefully about edge cases before committing to the structure.
