Last month I joined the three-member hardship panel at the university where I teach. Twice a term we allocate a fixed fund among student applicants — emergency housing, medical costs, bereavement travel — under a procedure inherited from a predecessor: each case is scored on a 0–100 impact scale, and we fund the highest scores until the money runs out. I anticipated quibbles about the rubric. I did not anticipate that the panel itself would reopen a ninety-year-old debate. Our economist refuses to treat the scores as additive: he holds, in the tradition of Lionel Robbins's interwar campaign against interpersonal comparisons of utility, that whatever the rubric measures, it is not a quantity common to all applicants, so adding one person's relief to another's is arithmetic theater. Our third panelist, a public-health physician who spends her working life with QALYs and disability weights, finds this quaint: policy has compared benefits across persons for decades, she says, and refusing to compare is itself a comparison — one that silently weights people by the luck of the application queue. They have argued through three meetings without either moving an inch, and on Thursday I must decide, in writing, whether my signature goes under a number.

I want a diagnosis of this standoff, not a verdict on who is right. Is the disagreement verbal — a residue of the word utility doing double duty, as a choice-theoretic bookkeeping term for the economist and a folk term for wellbeing for the physician? Is it substantive — a genuine dispute about whether a common scale of relief exists, answerable in principle by better measurement? Or are they answering different questions under one heading — one about what can be measured, the other about what weights a society ought to apply once measurement is as good as it gets? One wrinkle makes me doubt it is simply verbal: every decision we have made so far has been invariant under monotone rescaling of the scores — we never needed more than the ordering — and yet the character of our disputes changes at the margin, where two cases sit close enough that different normalizations of the rubric (a min-max scaling, say, versus a fixed anchor at the worst case we have ever seen) would flip the ranking. That suggests the debate bites precisely where the panel needs genuine cardinal information, and that neither colleague has stated a position at that level of precision: how much cardinality, if any, can our procedure honestly claim?

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, engaging with what each colleague would actually say — his Robbinsian line about ordinal preference and magnitudes that no observation could fix, her line about existing practice and the normative cost of declining to choose. Separate, if the separation holds, the scaling question (does a common unit of relief exist?) from the weighting question (how should one person's unit count against another's?), and say which of the two my panel actually faces on Thursday. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction I could put in the panel's written rationale, an empirical procedure we could adopt, a reframing of the question — or an argument that a residue remains that is bedrock, and why. I would rather sign something I understand than something that merely adds up.
