I write novels, and for most of my working life my material has been the moral codes of the worlds I write about: a mountain society in which a man's name is his fortune and slights are settled face to face; a mid-century industrial city in which that older code was dissolving into something more legal and more impersonal; and, in my most recent book, a university, where I encountered a moral life organized around grievance in a way I had not anticipated. Over these years I have accumulated a set of convictions about such worlds, and I would like to know whether, taken together, they are jointly tenable. I state them as plainly as I can.

First: honor cultures — societies organized around reputation, courage, and the keeping of one's word at real personal cost — produce genuine virtues that our own arrangements do not reliably produce, and my best characters have almost always come from such worlds.

Second: the historical passage from honor to dignity — from ranked worth to equal worth, from private redress to law, from the duel to the tribunal — is real moral progress, not merely a change of manners, and I would not wish the old dispensation back.

Third: what is sometimes called victimhood culture — the cultivation of grievance, the upgrading of small slights into major moral events, complaints pressed upon third parties rather than settled between the parties themselves — is a corruption of the moral life, and I am inclined to think it worse than either of its predecessors, since it takes honor's touchiness about slight without honor's readiness to bear a cost for it.

Fourth: dignity culture's central achievement is its refusal to rank persons by worth. Whatever else it loses, it stops sorting people into the admirable and the contemptible, and I regard that refusal as standing.

Fifth: as a novelist I am bound to render every form of life from the inside, and I hold that no way of life should be condemned wholesale from without; judgment of a code is possible only after the code has been made legible in its own terms, and caricature is a failure of craft before it is a failure of ethics.

Each of these, taken singly, seems to me defensible. But I have begun to suspect that some of them pull against the others, and I cannot tell which. Perhaps my admiration for the honor worlds sits badly with my refusal to rank persons; perhaps my contempt for the grievance culture sits badly with my discipline as a writer; perhaps some of these conflicts are only apparent. I honestly do not know.

So: go through the five commitments. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly; which only look to be; and for each real conflict, what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave. I am not asking you to tell me which commitment I ought to abandon — I want the audit, not the verdict.
