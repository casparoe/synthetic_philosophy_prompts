A debate I follow has stalled, and I'd like a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict on it. The dispute is between people who work on interpreting neural networks — probing activations, hunting for "features," editing a model's internals — and a skeptical camp, and the question is whether the structures they find are representations in a full-blooded sense, or partly artifacts of the researchers' own conceptual scheme.

The realist side points to two kinds of results. First, probes that reliably extract information from a model's activations, such as recovering its implicit belief about a board state from a layer that was never trained on that quantity. Second, causal interventions: ablating or amplifying a direction in activation space and observing the predicted change in behavior. If turning up a vector makes the model treat every dog as a cat, they say, that vector was doing representing work for the network; the feature belongs to the model, not to us.

The skeptics reply in two registers. One is underdetermination: activation space admits many decompositions into "features," and which one we single out reflects the vocabulary we brought with us — our word "dog," our taxonomy — rather than anything the network was obliged to carve on its own. Several distinct directions can each individually predict dog-labels; designating the one we happened to isolate as "the dog feature" and dismissing the rest as correlates is a choice we make, not a fact we uncover. The second register is normativity: to represent something, a state must be capable of misrepresenting, of answering to how things actually are, and it's unclear what inside a network could ground that. There may be no fact of the network representing the world inaccurately — only a fact of it producing outputs we like or dislike, with the notion of accuracy smuggled in from the training objective rather than belonging to the network.

What I'd like from you is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it verbal — two camps using "representation" for different things, one meaning roughly an information-carrying internal state and the other a state with correctness conditions — or substantive, with a hidden question about whether networks actually satisfy the stronger conditions? Is the underdetermination worry doing the real work, and if so, is that a fact about networks or a fact about us? And where I'd especially like your judgment: whether anything would count as progress — say, a principled criterion for privileging one decomposition over its rivals, or an account of correctness conditions for internal states that doesn't quietly borrow them from the loss function — or whether the debate should be expected to persist because the two camps are answering different questions under a single word. I want a reasoned diagnosis, not a survey of the positions.
