ok so some background: I'm a philosophy professor, been working on Leibniz's ethics since my dissertation, and I've been asked to write up my reading of his moral views for a colloquium where the department will poke at it. Before I do, I want a consistency check. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. I read Leibniz's moral necessity the strong way: for a perfectly wise being, choosing anything other than the best is impossible. The familiar line about God inclining the soul without necessitating it is true of us finite creatures, whose passions can push against the apparent good — but at the top, where the understanding is adequate, determination by the good just is necessitation. God cannot do otherwise.

2. At the same time, I've half convinced myself there is no best possible world. Among the infinitely many candidates there's no maximal element — improvements run out in some directions but trade off against others, so at best you get worlds unbeaten in a neighborhood, not a world that beats all comers. So when Leibniz says God chooses the best, I take him deflationarily: a world that's not improvable all-things-considered, or good enough by some satisficing standard, not a world that wins an outright contest.

3. I hold the aggregative reading of justice as the charity of the wise: what makes conduct right is its contribution to the optimum of the whole. The standard of justice is the same kind of maximization as the standard of creation, and each person's good figures in ethics the way each part figures in an organism — as a contribution to the harmony of the whole.

4. But I also refuse the compensation move in theodicy. I don't accept that one person's misery can be justified as the price of a better aggregate, and I read Leibniz as agreeing: justice is directed at persons, each mind's good is intrinsically owed, and the city of God is a republic of citizens, not a lump of welfare to be optimized. The aggregate doesn't get to buy anyone's suffering.

5. On justice and will I'm with Leibniz against Hobbes and Pufendorf: justice doesn't get its authority from God's command, and Leibniz's ethics is detatchable from the theology — you could lift the whole moral theory out and run it without the theism and nothing essential would be lost.

6. Though when I ask myself what ultimately grounds the authority of justice, I say: the moral order is the same order as the cosmic one. The republic of rational beings is part of the same optimal harmony that orders the physical universe — the moral and the natural are two vocabularies for one optimum.

7. On love, I take seriously Leibniz's genetic account — loving someone is being disposed to take pleasure in their happiness — and I'd call him a refined psychological hedonist about motivation. And I hold his charity is nonetheless genuinely for the other's sake, not self-interest dressed up.
