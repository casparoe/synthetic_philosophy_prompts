I chair the steering group for a five-year programme on intergenerational ethics — about a dozen philosophers, two economists, money for postdocs and workshops, nothing that counts as empirical. The question I keep coming back to: of the open threads in the pure time preference debate, which ones actually deserve our salaries, and which are just clever? The old core is familiar — Sidgwick's temporal neutrality, Ramsey's line that discounting goods merely for coming later is ethically indefensible, the economists' comeback that you can get a steep effective discount rate out of extinction risk without positing any pure preference for the present at all. What I want is the downstream picture: does the case against pure time preference even purport to bind a person deliberating about her own future, or only an impartial evaluator sizing up outcomes, and does the distinction matter for climate policy or just for the seminar room? Can declining discount rates be made coherent rather than a political compromise dressed up as an ur discount rate? Does the whole debate survive admitting that population-ethics is unsettled enough that the far future might not be aggregatively comparable to the present in the first place? Argue me into a ranked agenda: what each question is upstream of, how you'd recognise progress in a debate with no new data coming in, and which fashionable questions you'd cut because nothing turns on them either way. Flag what would make you reorder the list — a formal result, a policy moment, whatever. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
