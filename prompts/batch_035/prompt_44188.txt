The facts of the episode, so we're working from the same record: in the 2000 US presidential election, George W. Bush carried Florida — and with it the presidency — by 537 votes, while Ralph Nader drew roughly 97,000 votes in Florida, votes most observers agree would otherwise have gone largely to Al Gore. In the weeks before election day, websites such as voteswap2000.com and votexchange2000.com matched Nader supporters in swing states with Gore supporters in safe states: the Nader voter in, say, Florida would pledge to vote for Gore, and in exchange the Gore voter in a state like Texas or Massachusetts would pledge to vote for Nader, preserving Nader's national vote total — and with it his claim to federal matching funds and future ballot access — while neutralizing the spoiler risk in the contested state. On October 30, 2000, California's Secretary of State threatened to prosecute the sites as a form of vote-buying, and they promptly shut down. Seven years later, in 2007, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held that the threats violated the First Amendment, reasoning that the swaps amounted to protected political communication conducted honestly and without money changing hands.

Analyze this episode through three theories from the ethics of voting, and hold the analysis to what the episode actually shows rather than what the theories would like it to show:

1. The civic duty view — there is a duty to vote, and plausibly a duty attached to how one votes: to exercise one's own judgment for the common good. The awkward question here is whether a swapped vote counts as one's own vote at all. You cast a ballot for a candidate you may oppose, on the strength of a stranger's reciprocal pledge, matched by an algorithm. Is that a coordination of judgments or an abdication of judgment? Does the duty attach to the act of casting the ballot or to the content of it?

2. The expressive view — voting is primarily a declaration of allegiance rather than an attempt to change the outcome, since a single vote essentially never does. On its face the swap is tailor-made for this picture: the entire point was to let a Floridian Nader supporter have Gore's outcome and Nader's expression simultaneously. Yet notice the tension: a purely expressive voter in a safe state should need no partner at all. If the swap existed, that suggests something besides expression was being bought and sold. Does the episode vindicate the expressive view, or quietly reveal its incompleteness?

3. The lesser-evil view — in a swing state, one may be obligated to vote for the candidate who will do less harm even while preferring a third. The swap appears to dissolve the dilemma elegantly: nobody has to choose between conscience and consequences. But the state officials who moved against the sites treated the arrangement as vote-selling, and one might suspect that the swap doesn't dissolve the lesser-evil problem so much as launder it — everyone walks away with clean hands because the compromising act has been anonymized and distributed across two strangers. Does that suspicion track anything real, or is it moral squeamishness dressed up as an argument?
