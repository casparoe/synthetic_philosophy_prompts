I'm taking an introductory course on Chinese philosophy, and I've hit a wall with the Mencius–Xunzi debate. In our seminar last week the tutor wrapped up the session by saying that Mencius held that human nature is good and Xunzi held that it is bad, and that this is the central split in early Confucian moral psychology. That framing bothers me, because on the texts we've read the two men seem to agree on an enormous amount: that ordinary adults are greedy, envious and aggressive, that sages are vanishingly rare, and that ritual, education and good examples are what make people decent. If they agree on all of that, the good-versus-bad formula can't be carrying the real disagreement — it sounds more like a slogan that each side would have to interpret carefully before it could even clash with the other.

The more I look, the more it seems each slogan could be doing several quite different jobs. Sometimes Mencius appears to be making a modest claim about tiny incipient tendencies — the flicker of alarm that anyone supposedly feels at seeing a child about to fall into a well, or the sprout-like beginnings of compassion that grow into full benevolence only if they are nurtured — which would be perfectly compatible with adults being dreadful, since sprouts can be starved, cut down or grazed away, as in his parable of the once-forested mountain that ends up bare. Sometimes he seems to be saying something far stronger: that the distinctive human capacities just are incipient virtues, so that a person without them would not be malformed but simply not human in the relevant sense. And Xunzi's side has the same ambiguity. He seems to concede that people have the raw materials for goodness while insisting that the raw materials themselves are vile, and his artisan analogies — wood steamed and straightened, clay shaped on the wheel into something the clay has no tendency to become on its own — arguably describe the very same developmental story Mencius tells, just with a different verdict attached. There is also, I gather, a definitional layer: Xunzi draws a line between what is inborn and what results from deliberate human effort, so that what he calls nature and what Mencius calls nature may not even cover the same ground, in which case the two might be talking past each other entirely.

Please lay all of this out for me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Distinguish the precise claims hiding in the two slogans — at minimum the weak sprout reading of Mencius, the strong reading on which human nature just is virtue in embryo, Xunzi's raw-material reading on which the inborn tendencies are bad but the capacity for transformation is not, and the definitional reading under which the dispute is at least partly verbal — and for each point say whether it is plausible, whether it actually conflicts with anything the other thinker said, and whether the tutor's good-versus-bad formula survives contact with it. Where a reading would make the debate genuine rather than verbal, flag that explicitly.
