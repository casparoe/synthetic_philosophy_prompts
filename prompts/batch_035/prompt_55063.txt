A sculptor buys a piece of clay on Monday and sculpts it into a statue on Tuesday. Explain why this case is supposed to raise a problem about identity. Then set out three responses: (1) the coincidence view, on which the statue and the clay are two distinct objects; (2) the deflationary view, on which there is only one object there; (3) the eliminativist view, on which one of the two candidates does not exist at all. For the coincidence view, give one argument in its favor and state its main cost. For the other two views, give one motivation each. [10 points]

and here's the answer i want graded, copied verbatim, typos are the student's:

"The problem of material constitution can be stated using Leibniz's Law. If the statue just were the piece of clay it's made from, they'd have every property in common. But they differ in their modal properties: the clay could survive being squashed flat, while the statue could not, since a squashed statue isn't a statue anymore. So by Leibniz's Law the statue and the clay are distinct objects — and yet they are in exactly the same place at every moment, made of exactly the same matter. This clashes with the principle that no two material objects can be in the same place at the same time, which is common ground for everyone in the debate. So we get a contradiction, and that's the problem.

The coincidence view, associated with Wiggins and Baker, bites the bullet: there really are two objects, sharing all their ordinary properties (weight, color, shape, location) but differing in modal and historical ones (the clay existed before the sculpting, the statue didn't). Since sortals individuate objects by fixing their persistence conditions, the two are identical relative to the broad sortal 'object' but distinct relative to the narrower sortals 'statue' and 'piece of clay' — that relative-identity doctrine is really the heart of the coincidence view. On Baker's version, the clay has being-a-statue essentially, which is what lets it constitute the statue rather than merely make it up. The argument for the view is the modal one above, and its main cost is that it forces us to give up, or at least heavily restrict, the no-two-objects-in-one-place principle.

The deflationary view says there is only one object there, which is both a statue and a piece of clay, the way one person can be both a philosopher and a cyclist. It answers the modal argument by claiming that substitution of co-referring terms fails in modal contexts, so 'the clay could survive squashing' can be true while 'the statue could survive squashing' is false, even though the statue is the clay.

The eliminativist view, defended by van Inwagen, denies that statues exist at all: there are only simples arranged statue-wise. This dissolves the problem, since there was never a statue to be distinct from the clay, though at the cost of saying there are no statues, houses, or laptops, which most people find too revisionary."
