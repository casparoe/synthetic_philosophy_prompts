I'm a first-year graduate student in a seminar on theories of truth, and a line I keep hearing — usually with a nod toward Quine, though I've never checked whether he would endorse the uses it gets put to — is that "truth is just disquotation." This week a classmate used it to dismiss my question about what makes it the case that "the universe is expanding" is true: he said my question presupposes that truth is some deep property requiring a correspondence-style or coherence-style metaphysics, and that once you see that truth is just disquotation, there is nothing left to ask. I suspect the slogan, whatever its merits, cannot carry that much weight.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest — from something close to a platitude about what quotation marks do, through claims about the function of the truth predicate, up to the claim that any request for an explanation of why a sentence is true is confused — and give a verdict on each: platitudinous, substantive and defensible, substantive but doubtful, or outright false? Along the way, tell me which readings, if any, actually support my classmate's dismissal of my question, and which do not. I would also like the generalization case addressed: "everything the witness said under oath is true" removes quotation marks from no particular sentence, so I want to know whether that use of the truth predicate is a problem for some readings of the slogan, a neutral fact for others, or the very thing the strongest reading was designed to accommodate.

One further wrinkle. I understand deflationists often claim that the whole content of the concept of truth is given by instances of the equivalence schema — a sentence is true just in case the world is as it says. So please also say, for each reading, what it implies about the liar sentence that says of itself that it is not true: does the schema, so understood, even apply to a self-referential instance, and which readings have the resources to say something about it rather than falling silent? I want to bring a structured list to the next seminar meeting, not just a vague sense that my classmate moved too fast.
