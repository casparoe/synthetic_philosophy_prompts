I teach an intermediate decision-theory seminar, and for our unit on the coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization I want a case that isolates exactly what those arguments are entitled to. Construct a decision problem satisfying all of the following: (i) the agent's preferences violate only the sure-thing principle — completeness, transitivity, and continuity all hold, and the violation may be ordinary Allais-type preferences; (ii) the agent is fully foresighted: every price and consequence is posted in advance, nothing is misrepresented, and she correctly predicts the vendor's behavior and her own future choices; (iii) the vendor's initial offer is free, and she may decline any trade at any time without paying anything; yet (iv) the vendor ends up with a positive fee, and the agent ends with an outcome she would have refused had she been able to bind herself in advance. The scenario should be a realistic commercial arrangement — no demons, no perfect predictors — and no more than a paragraph. Then demonstrate that the specifications are met: why transitivity gives the agent no protection, why foresight alone does not either, and which single structural feature of the case is doing the work. Finally, play the two standard defenders in turn — the advocate of precommitment (be resolute, and the pump evaporates) and the advocate of sophistication (predict your own exits, and never pay for what you can foresee) — and say what each defense, if it succeeds, leaves standing: does the case show that rationality requires expected-utility maximization, or only that rational agents need access to binding devices?
