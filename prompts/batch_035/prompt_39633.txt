My trading friend — a convert to time-average thinking, the ergodicity-economics line about ensemble averages versus time averages — keeps pressing an argument on me, and I can't locate the false step without also losing something I want to keep. Here it is, as fairly as I can render it.

(1) Domination principle: if strategy A leaves you strictly richer than strategy B with probability approaching one as the number of rounds goes to infinity, then, whenever A is available, following B is irrational.

(2) The classical results: in a long sequence of favorable, repeated, multiplicative bets, the Kelly strategy — staking the fraction that maximizes expected log wealth — eventually overtakes every essentially different strategy with probability one, while betting too large a fraction leads to almost-sure ruin. He cites the old gambling-systems literature, something by Breiman in the early sixties and Thorp's later work; I haven't checked the exact conditions those theorems assume.

(3) So in repeated favorable games, rational agents bet Kelly. Overbetting is irrational because it courts almost-sure ruin; underbetting — the fractional Kelly that practitioners use to sleep at night — is irrational because it is dominated.

(4) There is no principled line between a repeated game and a life. A life is a long sequence of bets — jobs, moves, houses, ventures — not identical ones, but favorable ones on average, and compounding across all of them. He adds that the asymptotic optimality of the log-optimal strategy doesn't require identical independent rounds, and that this is known.

(5) So rational agents treat every decision as a Kelly bet: what matters is the growth rate of capital, and any decision that lowers it is irrational.

(6) Consequences: buying insurance is irrational, since it lowers the growth rate; so is donating; and so, the clincher he actually endorses, is consumption itself. Every dollar spent is a dollar not compounding, and the no-consumption Kelly bettor almost surely ends up ahead of the one who buys lunch. The rational life is an untouched compounding balance, and he says that's fine — the thought that money is for something is, on his telling, a pre-rational prejudice.

I can live with something like (1)–(3) for genuinely repeated games, though I half-suspect the domination in (2) only arrives in an infinite limit that no finite agent ever lives to see. What I cannot accept is (6). But every rejection I try seems to take down (3) with it, and he notices and grins.

So, two things. First, name the step where the argument goes wrong. I have my eye on the slide between (3) and (4), and on a possible equivocation inside (5) between maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing a growth rate of capital — I have a feeling these come apart once insurance and consumption are on the table — but tell me whether that's right or whether the real flaw sits elsewhere, including in (1) or (2). Second, tell me precisely what rejecting that step costs: can he keep Kelly for the repeated case while losing the reductio, and if so, what distinguishes the two settings besides his convenience?

And please be hard-nosed about the mathematics rather than charitable to my framing. If the almost-sure-domination results actually require identical independent rounds, or if finite-horizon domination fails, or if expected log wealth in fact endorses buying some insurance, say so plainly. If you think I've misstated his argument somewhere — the ruin claim in (2), the generality claimed in (4) — don't silently repair it; flag it. And if you think the argument is sound and my horror at (6) is mere attachment to a spendthrift status quo, tell me that too: I would rather lose the argument cleanly than be agreed with wrongly.
