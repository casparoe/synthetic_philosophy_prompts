I'm a graduate student in a systematics seminar, and our professor has a Friday ritual she calls "lump or split": she reads out a population or pair of populations and gives an instant, confident verdict on whether we're looking at one species or two, and refuses to explain her reasoning -- the whole point is that we're supposed to reverse-engineer the species concept she's implicitly running. We've been logging her verdicts all semester to test whether a single consistent rule is there. The record so far: wolves and domestic dogs are one species; lions and tigers are two; the Ensatina salamander ring around California's Central Valley -- a chain of interbreeding populations whose coastal and inland endpoints meet again in the south and, by and large, do not interbreed where they overlap -- counts as two species; the sympatric fish-eating and mammal-eating killer whale ecotypes of the Pacific Northwest are separate species despite being so similar that you basically need genetics and a practiced eye for the saddle patch to tell them apart; bdelloid rotifers, which have gone without sex for tens of millions of years, come in a large number of perfectly legitimate species; and the desert sunflower Helianthus anomalus, which arose by hybridization between H. annuus and H. petiolaris and is now reproductively isolated from both of its parents, is a genuine species in its own right.

Next Friday's case has been announced in advance, and we want to go in with actual predictions: the hooded crow and the carrion crow. Over most of Europe they replace each other geographically, but where their ranges meet they interbreed along a narrow hybrid zone that has been stable for a very long time; hybrids are fertile, the plumage differences are stark, and the formal taxonomy has flip-flopped between splitting them and lumping them for decades. My labmate thinks the professor is running a gene-exchange rule -- populations that actually interbreed where they meet are one species, which is how she lumped dogs with wolves and split the Ensatina endpoints and the orca ecotypes. I suspect she's running something more like an independent-trajectory rule -- what matters is not whether genes leak across a boundary but whether the lineages are on separate, non-merging evolutionary paths, which is how she can lump dogs (a recent offshoot sitting inside wolf variation that would fuse back given the chance) while splitting populations that stay distinct despite contact. As far as I can tell, both rules fit all six verdicts on the record, though both need some story about how they handle the asexual bdelloids, and I'm not fully convinced either is what she has in mind.

Please help me prepare. Identify the candidate rules that can accommodate her entire track record (including any others besides the two we've come up with, and be honest about which verdicts strain which rules and how a strained rule would have to be patched). Give your best prediction of her verdict on the crows, the closest precedent in her record, and your reasons for preferring that reading over its rivals. Then design the further case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules -- some population scenario we could bring to a future Friday session on which those rules give different verdicts -- and say what each rule predicts for it. Please return the whole thing as a JSON object with the following schema: a "candidate_rules" array, where each element has "name", "fit" (how it accommodates all six verdicts, including its handling of the asexual case), and "crow_verdict" (either "lump" or "split", with a "reasoning" field); a "best_guess" object with "verdict", "confidence" (high, medium, or low), "closest_precedent", and "rationale"; and a "discriminating_case" object with "case" (a description of the scenario), "predictions" (an array mapping each candidate rule to the verdict it gives on this scenario and why), and "why_it_decides". Keep the prose inside the JSON crisp but substantive -- I'd rather have real reasoning than boilerplate.
