My friend and I got into a massive argument last week about surge pricing. A storm hit, the rideshares doubled their fares, and she said that's just the price doing its job — moving the rides to whoever values them most — and that the alternative is a queue, which is worse. I kept saying there's something gross about it but couldn't say what without just sounding like I hate markets, and at some point she goes "fine, so would you ban it? what's your rule?" and I had literally nothing. That stung, because it turns out I'm on a committee where ruling on exactly this kind of thing is my job.

Here's the situation: I'm the staff (non-faculty) member of a small college committee that decides whether the college will broker, host, or facilitate transactions people propose to us. We get roughly one a month and the proposals keep getting more creative. Real examples from the last couple years: a food delivery app wanting to price dynamically in the campus dining halls during exam week (so a burrito costs more at 2am during finals); a paid line-standing service where students pay other students to hold their spot in the queue for capped seminar registration; the administration floating the idea of letting a student pay to bump someone off the waitlist of an oversubscribed course; a pharma company paying students a fairly large sum to test a sleep drug over spring break; and a student group wanting to raffle a reserved commencement seat for charity. When something is obviously a kidney sale or an outright vote purchase we all know the answer, but almost nothing we actually get is that clear, and our meetings mostly consist of people saying "it feels off" at each other until someone moves to postpone.

What I want is a real decision procedure the committee can run on any proposal in about twenty minutes during a meeting: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as enough of an answer to each one to move on; which answers end the process with a no; and where it has to stop and hand the thing to a straight vote because the remaining question is about values, not facts. Ground it in whatever philosophers have actually worked out about commodification and repugnant markets — the worries about desperation and bargaining power, about a market changing what a practice means even for people who don't participate, about things whose value gets corrupted by having a price put on them — but it has to be usable by people who've never read a word of it. And it has to handle the genuinely hard cases, not just the easy ones: cases where both parties are fully consenting and fine, where the price is transparent and nobody is deceived, and where the alternative to the market isn't some pure commercial-free status quo but something with its own problems — like queueing, which quietly favors students with the most free time. Then run the procedure on three of the proposals above: the exam-week burritos, the paid line-standing, and the waitlist bumping. I want you to be honest about where it gives an answer I won't like — I currently support the waitlist thing, so don't rig the procedure to let me off the hook. And last, tell me straight what the procedure can't do, especially where it will just launder our prejudices into official-sounding steps.

Format: start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
