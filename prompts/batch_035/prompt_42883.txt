been reading up on the old debate about whether dreams are experiences at all -- malcolm vs dennett, the whole "dream reports can't be trusted because you only give them awake" thing -- and i don't have a position yet, just gut reactions to specific cases. here they are, and I want to know if there's some principle that captures all of them or whether im just being inconsistent:

1. the classic case: someone gets woken by an alarm clock clattering off the nightstand, and reports a long involved dream that ends with a shelf collapsing or something that obviously "explains" the crash. my gut says that dream got composed in the split second after the noise, it wasn't actually running before the alarm fell.

2. lucid dreamers who agree in advance to signal with their eyes, like left-right-left, once they realize they're dreaming, and the sleep lab records exactly that pattern of eye movements from them during REM. my gut says there's no way around this one, that's experience happening in real time while asleep.

3. I wake up from a 20 minute nap with what feels like hours of story in my head. gut says: I experienced all of it, dream time just runs faster than real time, whatever that's supposed to mean.

4. "white" dreams -- where you're certain you dreamed but there's zero content, just a feeling of having dreamed. gut says there was a genuine experience there and the memory encoding failed, rather than no experience at all.

5. people woken from non-REM sleep mostly report stuff like "I was thinking about my exam" -- no imagery, just thought. gut says that's not a dream and not even really an experience in the sense we care about, i'm not sure it's anything.

6. everything I "remember" about a dream five minutes after waking is heavily reconstructed -- I've caught myself smoothing a smear into a plot. so gut says dream reports in general are shaky as evidence.

is there one story about when dreaming involves experience happening during sleep that gets all six? the obvious move, "dreams are real-time experiences except sometimes they're retro-fitted at waking", feels ad hoc to me, like it saves 1 and 2 at the cost of explaining nothing. also curious what the principle says about a case I haven't decided: sleep-talkers who answer questions coherently and have no memory of it after -- does that count as evidence they were experiencing something at the time? and please be honest about which of my verdicts you'd throw out, because 1, 3 and 6 together already feel like they can't all be true in any clean way, I just can't see which one is the problem.
