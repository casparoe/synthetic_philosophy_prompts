Write a sonnet about the French headscarf affairs, from the point of view of the school itself — the building, not any person — which has watched the whole arc from the first exclusions of 1989 in Creil, through the minister's referrals to the Council of State and its opinion that wearing a sign was compatible with laïcité so long as it was not ostentatious or an act of pressure or proselytism, through years of case-by-case jurisprudence and creeping local bans, up to the law of 15 March 2004 forbidding conspicuous religious signs in public schools. The volta should come at the ninth line, somewhere in the years between the advisory opinion and the statute, and should be the moment the school realizes that the Republic has quietly changed its answer to the question of who must adapt to whom: in 1989 the assumption was that the girls could be accommodated inside the school's walls, and by 2004 the assumption was that the walls themselves needed defending against the signs. The octave should build the older, more confident laïcité — a school that believed it could absorb difference and turn it into citizens, the scarf treated as one more oddity the République would digest as it had digested everything else. The sestet should register the newer, more anxious laïcité — the fear that some signs are not just worn but asserted, that neutrality is not a background condition but a rule that must be enforced, and that the school is no longer a melting pot but a border post. End with a couplet that does not simply endorse or condemn the ban, but states the tradeoff in its sharpest form: something that acknowledges what the school gained in equal treatment among pupils and lost in the idea that integration is something the Republic does with people as they are. Please write it in rhymed iambic pentameter, and make it precise enough that someone who has followed the French debate — as opposed to the Anglophone caricature of it — would recognize the actual stakes: the distinction between freedom of conscience and freedom of display, the school's role as the republican institution par excellence, and the fact that the same law that excluded the scarf was defended, sincerely, as the condition of the girls' own emancipation.
