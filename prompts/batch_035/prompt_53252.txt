I work in AI governance, and I have been trying to get clear on how much confidence the safe Pareto improvement framework — sometimes discussed under the heading of surrogate goals — actually warrants, not as a slogan but as a formal guarantee. Please give me your credence, decomposed.

The idea, as I understand it: when parties face a game with destructive possible outcomes, they can jointly commit in advance to playing a modified version of the game — for instance, each side publicly binding itself to treat a minor provocation exactly as it would treat a major one, or agreeing to let a coin toss settle some dispute — where the modification is constructed so that, whatever bargaining solution would have governed the original game, no party ends up worse off in expectation once the modified game plays out. Because the guarantee is meant to hold across all bargaining solutions, adoption is supposed to be rational even for parties who cannot agree on how the surplus would have been divided: the improvements are safe precisely because they do not presuppose any particular division. The most-discussed application is to delegated or automated agents, where the commitment could be built into the agent's objective function before anyone knows which conflicts will arise.

My question concerns the strong safety claim: that this construction delivers what it promises — that there is a genuine sense in which adopting the modified game cannot leave a party worse off than not adopting it, without any assumption about how the unmodified game would have been resolved.

Give me your credence as a number, then decompose it. I have in mind at least the following terms, though add or remove as you judge fit: (a) whether the comparison 'no worse off than you would have been' is even well-defined when the counterfactual outcome of the original game is indeterminate — whether bargaining solutions are respectable enough idealisations to anchor the comparison; (b) whether the formal guarantee survives the move from the abstract construction to any concrete instantiation, where the added commitment must be similar enough to existing stakes for the construction to work, and that judgement of similarity is made under uncertainty; (c) whether adoption is itself incentive-compatible — whether a party can gain by manipulating which modified game gets adopted, or by defecting from the commitment, in ways the safety argument does not cover; and (d) whether the intended application to delegated AI agents smuggles in further assumptions, about the principal–agent relationship or the verifiability of the commitment, that are doing hidden work.

Assign a rough probability to each term with a line or two of justification, then show the combination. My own credence has drifted from around 0.75 down to about 0.55 over the past year, mostly because of doubts about (c), but I am not sure that is where the weakest link really is. So after the decomposition, tell me which term you are least confident in, and describe what a decisive counterexample would look like: a concrete scenario in which the construction, as advertised, leaves a party worse off than the original game would have. Please answer in British English.
