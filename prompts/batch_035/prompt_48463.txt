I'm writing a term paper for a metaethics seminar on the fitting-attitudes analysis of value — roughly, the idea that something is valuable just in case it is fitting or appropriate to favour it in some way. I've gotten stuck on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem and, more specifically, on whether the standard rescue attempts actually rescue anything. Take the classic demon case: a powerful being will torment you unless you admire him, and maybe he'll torment you with intense, painful dread unless you fear him. You clearly have overwhelming self-interested reason to admire and fear him. Yet he isn't admirable or fearsome, and the demon knows it. The lesson is supposed to be that there are reasons to have an attitude that are not reasons of fit — reasons that count in favour of taking the attitude but don't make its object appear valuable — and that a fitting-attitudes analysis that simply quantifies over reasons therefore collapses into the wrong account of what admiration or fear track.

The literature has various repair proposals: distinguishing the object of an attitude from the attitude itself and saying right-kind reasons are those that bear on the object's features; saying wrong-kind reasons explain the desirability of the attitude rather than bearing on fit; treating them as "state-given" rather than "object-given"; building the distinction directly into the semantics of "reasons for." What unsettles me is that each repair seems to help itself to exactly the evaluative fact it was supposed to analyse. If I say the demon gives me reason to admire him but not reason *for* admiring him, because admiration's fittingness conditions track admirability rather than self-protection, haven't I just quietly re-imported value as an independent phenomenon — so the analysis is saved only by ceasing to be reductive? My seminar instructor is unmoved: he thinks the distinction is structural, principled, and that the reduction was never the important part anyway.

Here is what I want from you. Give me your credence that there exists a principled, generalizable right-kind/wrong-kind distinction that genuinely saves the fitting-attitudes analysis from the demon-style counterexamples — a number between 0 and 1, not a shrug. Then decompose it: separate out the probability that any correct distinction will turn out to be merely formal or structural rather than smuggled-in-evaluative; the probability that a formal distinction suffices to save the analysis even if the reduction is abandoned; the probability that the demon cases are unusual edge cases that the analysis can tolerate rather than fatal counterexamples; and any other terms you think the credence should factor into. Give each term a rough probability and a one-line justification, then combine them. My own credence sits around 0.3 and my instructor's, I'd guess, around 0.65 — so finally, tell me which single term in your decomposition the two of us most plausibly disagree about, and what would be the cleanest way to test it.
