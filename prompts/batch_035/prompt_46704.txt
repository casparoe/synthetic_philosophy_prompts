I am a doctoral student in statistics. My thesis concerns the aggregation of probabilistic forecasts, so the vocabulary I think in is that of opinion pooling: a set of experts, each submitting a probability distribution; an assessor who must combine them into a single distribution; pooling rules such as the linear pool (a weighted average) and the logarithmic pool; and the axioms one can impose on a rule — respecting unanimous opinions, behaving sensibly under conditioning — together with the theorems classifying which rules survive which axioms. I can follow a proof, and I have read enough welfare economics to know that Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem is treated there as a landmark: if each individual's preferences over risky social prospects conform to expected-utility theory with a common prior, and the social preference does too, then social utility must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. Harsanyi took this to vindicate utilitarianism, and economists and philosophers have argued about it ever since — most famously in Diamond's 1967 comment, which observed that the theorem's social ranking is indifferent between prospects giving everyone the same expected utility but distributing outcomes very differently across persons, and more recently in results showing that once individuals hold different priors, the Pareto principle and Bayesian aggregation come apart.

Here is the translation I would like you to perform, and then to test. Restate the theorem and the debate it spawned in my vocabulary: persons as experts; each individual's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function as the quantity being aggregated, occupying the place probabilities occupy in my problems; the social welfare functional as a pooling rule; Harsanyi's result as the claim that, under common priors and expected-utility rationality on all sides, the only admissible pool is linear, with weights that are the same in every state of the world. Perform this as faithfully as you can: do not quietly assume the mapping holds, and flag each point at which the analogy is doing work it has no warrant for.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. In my setting there is a fact of the matter: the pooled forecast estimates something, and a rule can be judged, at least in principle, by how well calibrated it is against that something. As far as I can tell, nothing of the sort exists in the welfare setting — the pooled utility does not estimate anything; it is constituted by the pooling rule. If that is right, it ought to matter, and I would like to know where. In particular: does Diamond's objection have any counterpart in a statistician's worries about a pooling rule, or does it cohere only because the aggregated quantities are lives rather than forecasts? Can the veil-of-ignorance reading, on which the assessor is a single person uncertain about which individual she will turn out to be, be expressed at all as an assessor with a prior over expert identities, and if so does that expression capture what its proponents intend by it? And where the heterogeneous-prior results are concerned, is their statistical analogue the familiar one about experts who disagree on the model, or is something further lost?

Finally, tell me where the residue points. Does my vocabulary simply lack a concept the welfare debate needs, or does the debate look different once one notices that its central quantity is constituted rather than estimated — less theorem-shaped, perhaps, and more convention-shaped? I am fluent on the statistical side and moderately well read on the welfare side; do not simplify either, but you may assume I know what a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility is.
