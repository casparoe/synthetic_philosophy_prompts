I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I'm preparing a column on Augustine of Hippo's doctrine that lying is always impermissible. The headline version of this view is well known, but what I want to surface for readers is the less familiar conceptual machinery beneath it. Please act as a scholar of late antique philosophy and theology, and before composing your answer, think through the specific distinctions Augustine actually deploys — brainstorm internally and discard the standard summary-level material.

Then reply with exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each point should illuminate one distinct, somewhat technical aspect of his account of lying. You need not use the following, but the sort of thing I have in mind includes: how he defines a lie relative to the speaker's mind rather than to what is in fact the case; what follows from this about jokes, irony, or statements that mislead while being literally true; how he sorts lies into types and grades their comparative gravity; and what he says about misleading or deceptive episodes in Scripture, where a straightforward reading seems to attribute dissimulation to revered figures.

Two constraints for the piece: favor the in-the-weeds material over the familiar debate about whether one may lie to save a life, and avoid citing chapter, verse, or section numbers — I want conceptual content a lay reader can follow, not a reference apparatus.
