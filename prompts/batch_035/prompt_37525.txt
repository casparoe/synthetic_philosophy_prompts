I am a novelist with twenty years of practice and a working craftsman's vocabulary: point of view and its distance, free indirect style, scene versus summary, interiority, backstory, the character arc, composite characters, anachronism, the paratext of the author's note and the afterword that tells readers what became of everyone. I think in those terms fluently; I do not think in the terms of the philosophy seminars I have read about secondhand.

Restate, in my vocabulary, the main positions in the debate over what historical fiction and biopics owe the real dead — the strict-fidelity view that dramatizing actual persons forbids inventing material facts about them; the larger-truth defense, which holds that the artist's obligation runs to the essence of a life rather than its documented incidents; the consent-and-harm view, which grounds our duties to the dramatized dead in the claims of surviving relatives and posthumous reputation; and the genre-contract view, under which everything turns on what label the work carries and what expectations that label creates in the audience. Translate each position as faithfully as my craft vocabulary allows. Where a position maps onto something I already do — say, the strict-fidelity view onto decisions about POV depth and the licence to invent interiority, or the genre-contract view onto paratext and shelving — say so plainly.

Then give me the residue report. Specifically: which parts of the debate survive as genuine craft decisions, which had to be distorted to fit, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. My working suspicion is this: from inside the craft, entering a real person's mind in free indirect style is a technical matter of narrative distance, something I decide for reasons of effect; the philosophers seem to treat that same act as something done to someone, a matter of what a person who can no longer object may be made to have thought and felt. If that gap is real, I want to know which side is missing something — whether my vocabulary lacks a register for the moral standing of the dead that novelists have quietly been trading on, or whether the philosophical positions, once translated, reduce to conventions of labeling and afterword that we already handle with an author's note. Please be honest about the translation and do not quietly decide the argument by calling one side's assumptions craft.
