Last month I was a guest at a literary festival in Istanbul, and at the closing dinner two people I like argued themselves into a corner over the conduct of a third guest, and I found I could not referee it. The third guest — call him the American — was on his fifth night in the city. He never tipped beyond the service charge printed on the bill, although the guidebook in his pocket said plainly that the waiters live on the extra; by the fifth night the staff knew him and had stopped smiling. And each morning at the ferry landing he stood in a single-file queue of one while the local crowd gathered in a loose cluster and boarded in a surge, so that he crossed the water last every day, having pushed past nobody.

Graham, my London publisher, opened by saying that conventions are not obligations but punctuation. Nobody holds the Turk who drives on the right morally superior to the Englishman on the left; the visitor's duties are prudence (do not get run over) and ordinary courtesy (do not give calculated offence), and beyond that a convention is simply the local way of doing a thing one never signed up for. The bill had a price printed on it, the American paid the printed price, and that is not cheating a waiter — it is declining to join a local top-up scheme. One cannot owe dues to a club one never joined.

Dilara, the Turkish novelist seated opposite, said the driving analogy smuggled in the one kind of convention that cannot carry the weight. Driving side is pure coordination: the point is that everyone does the same thing, and each person's conformity benefits everyone, including the conformer. Tipping is instead a transfer scheme: the menu prices are set on the assumption that the table will pay the waiter's wage in two instalments, part in the bill and part on top, so the American eats a meal subsidised by that assumption and then withholds the second instalment. That is not declining membership; it is taking the discount and skipping the levy. And nobody signs the convention against queue-jumping either, she added, yet a visitor who pushed to the front of a bread line on his first morning in the country would be doing wrong all the same.

Graham answered that if the levy were genuinely owed, the locals would treat the under-tipper as they treat a thief, and instead they pardon him: the waiter who smiles at the foreigner's thin tip while cursing the native's identical one is displaying the exact boundary of the scheme — it binds members, not strangers, and that smile is the waiver.

Dilara replied that the smile covers ignorance, not policy: the pardon lasts precisely as long as the waiter can believe the guest does not know, and the fifth-night American with the guidebook now receives the cold plate, not the smile. The exemption is an epistemic grace period, not a border. And the club has him inside it already — he is not being invited to join, he is seated and eating; the only question is whether he settles at the going rate or at a rate he invents for himself.

Graham then offered her the two cases she kept folding together. Queueing he conceded, but for a reason requiring no convention-mysticism: the jumper imposes his wait on others, a direct harm — ordinary morality with local knowledge attached. The ferry, though, proved the opposite: there the local convention is the cluster and the surge, and the American holding his single file is not immoral, merely inefficient; likewise the local who crosses against the light on an empty street at three in the morning, with every driver's expectations organised around it, breaches no duty at all. Conventions carry moral force only through the harms they prevent or the offence they avoid; the remainder is habit wearing the costume of duty.

Dilara closed with the shoes. In her house, shoes come off at the door. Suppose a guest keeps his on while the family is upstairs: no one sees, no one is harmed, no offence is given — is nothing amiss? If Graham concedes that something is, he has admitted a duty that is neither harm-avoidance nor offence-avoidance: accepting hospitality on the terms on which it is offered. A house admits you on a footing; you do not renegotiate it room by room. Visiting a country, she said, is being a guest at scale, and the tip is part of the footing.

They then turned to me, and I confess that after each round I had privately changed my mind, which is why I am writing to you instead. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each overreaches, and whether the coordination-versus-transfer distinction (or some better one) really does the work Dilara needs of it. And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — I have the persistent sense that the disagreement turns on something neither of them names, and as a novelist I collect such unnamed things, but this one has refused to close.
