My colleague on the city school board has begun citing a twin study conducted entirely within our own district: several thousand twin pairs, assessed on reading achievement at age ten, in a district where every school runs the same scripted literacy curriculum under the same class-size caps. The study puts the heritability of reading achievement at .75, with the shared-environment component near zero. My colleague calls the district's uniformity a methodological advantage, since it "holds schooling constant," and from the figures he draws three conclusions: that a struggling ten-year-old is simply built that way; that funding after-school remediation is money down the drain; and that if we ever diversified curricula across schools, the heritability estimate would climb higher still. Please give the most important implications of what heritability does and does not actually mean for assessing these claims about our district, as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
