I'm a graduate student in history and philosophy of science, and this term I'm taking a seminar on thermodynamics and information theory. For our final paper we've been assigned a counterfactual exercise: pick a historical figure, pick a later development in their field, and reconstruct — with actual argument, not hand-waving — how they would most likely have responded. I've chosen James Clerk Maxwell and the century-long afterlife of his demon, and I'd like your help thinking through the reconstruction before I commit it to paper.

Here's the situation I'm putting him in. Maxwell died in 1879, only a few years after describing his sorting demon, and he never saw how the problem unfolded: Szilard's engine in the 1920s, Brillouin's attempted resolution in terms of the cost of measurement, and finally the now-standard Landauer-Bennett line, on which measurement can in principle be done reversibly and the real thermodynamic price is paid when the demon erases its record of a molecule's speed to reset for the next cycle, at a minimum cost of kT ln 2 per bit. On top of that sits the broader modern moral people draw from the episode: that information is not a free abstraction but a physical quantity with irreducible thermodynamic weight. My seminar has been arguing about a further wrinkle that I'd like folded into the reconstruction: a demon with an unbounded memory never needs to erase, so the Bennett resolution seems to push the cost to the asymptotic boundary of the process rather than locating it anywhere the demon actually pays it. Some of my classmates find that a genuine resolution; my professor calls it bookkeeping elegance that quietly swaps Maxwell's being for a device Maxwell would not have recognized as his.

So: how do you think Maxwell would react, and why? In arguing for your answer, please stay anchored to things actually documented about him — his stated purpose in introducing the demon (as best we can tell from Theory of Heat and his correspondence with Tait and Thomson, he seems to have intended it to show that the second law is statistical, a claim about creatures like us who cannot sort molecules, not an absolute metaphysical barrier), his mechanical and atomistic leanings, his religious commitments, and whatever else you can genuinely ground. Distinguish clearly between inferences you'd call well-supported and ones you'd call speculation, and tell me where the counterfactual simply becomes underdetermined — where the historical record can't decide between two readings. My professor is hostile to Whig history, so if you think the whole exercise is compromised — say, because the modern framing of the demon's "failure" presupposes a concept of information Maxwell had no resources to evaluate — please say so plainly rather than manufacturing a confident verdict, and tell me how you'd reformulate the paper question to survive the objection. If it matters, my current draft leans toward Maxwell being quietly delighted rather than defensive, and I'd want you to push on that if it's wrong.
