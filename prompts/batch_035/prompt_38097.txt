I teach political philosophy — legitimacy, obligation, that sort of thing — so I'm not a language person, but a pattern keeps showing up in my neck of the woods that I suspect the speech act literature has a grip on and I can't find the term for it. I've asked around at colloquia and gotten shrugs, and the one law colleague who engaged said it's just the de facto officer doctrine, but that's a rule about officeholders, not a concept about what act was performed, so let me try describing it here.

The pattern: an utterance that's supposed to *do* something — pronounce people married, fire someone, enact a decree — is made by someone who isn't entitled to make it, or in circumstances that don't allow it. At the moment of utterance, the act doesn't take. Fine, standard case, I get it. But sometimes what happens next is strange: everyone just keeps going as though it had taken, and eventually the act counts as having been done all along — no one ever repeats the utterance, no second ceremony happens, yet the record gets rewritten so the thing was valid from day one. Instances I've actually run into: a couple married by an officiant whose authorization turns out to have lapsed, and ten years on, kids and mortgage, the state decides they've been married since the wedding day; a junta issuing decrees before it formally holds power and then backdating its own authority to cover them; a board member voting on a merger before his appointment technically took effect, with the deal treated as approved from the date of the vote. And the contrast case: a bigamous second marriage, or a baptism by a defrocked priest, where the same kind of defect seems to make the act unfixable by any amount of going-along — void not just now but forever.

So my question is whether there's a name in the speech act literature for that third outcome — not the act failing (as I understand Austin, that's the misfire, where the act is null and void; and I know he separates off cases where the act goes through but is hollow, insincere promises and the like, but neither of those is my case), and not the act being redone properly later (that's just a new act, performed felicitously this time). My case is the act becoming valid retroactively through nothing but continued practice, without ever being reperformed. What puzzles me is the retroactivity itself: as far as I can tell Austin treats a botched performative as void at the time of utterance, and says little about how time and subsequent behavior could change *which act*, if any, was performed back then. Is there a principled line between the cases that get retroactively fixed and the ones that stay void forever? And if there's no established term, I'd honestly settle for a good candidate concept plus a definition that handles the edge cases — e.g. the couple splits up the week before the lapsed-license defect is discovered: does the marriage retroactively un-happen, and if so, on what date? I'm not after a label I could have guessed myself; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what its not, and where its edges are.
