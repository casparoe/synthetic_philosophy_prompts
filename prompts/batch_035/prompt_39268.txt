My metaphysics advisor is a die-hard perdurantist and her go-to move in seminars is the road analogy: a persisting thing is stretched out in time exactly the way a road is stretched out through space, so an object having incompatible properties at different times is no more mysterious than a road being muddy at one end and dry at the other, and endurantists who insist the whole object is present at each moment are like someone who thinks the whole road is located at every mile marker. She leans on it to argue that temporary intrinsics is a pseudo-problem and that anyone happy with spatial parts but squeamish about temporal parts is just being inconsistent. I have to respond in our reading group next week and I want to do it properly instead of gesturing at A-theory intuitions. Can you lay out the mapping — what in the persisting thing corresponds to the road's spatial extension, to its parts, to mud at one end, to driving the whole road in an afternoon — then say where the mapping breaks down? The ones I half see: time has a direction and space doesn't, my temporal parts causally interact (I can wave at where my arm was a second ago) in a way the road's west end can't wave at its east end, and nothing in the target plays the role of the road's being there all at once for a surveyor. Then the thing I actually care about: which of these disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which are just noise? Does perdurantism fall out of the analogy, or is perdurantism doing the work and the analogy just illustrating it after the fact? And if theres a better analogy for her side, or a mirror-image one the endurantist could run (wholly present at each time the way something can be wholly present in many places at once?), I'd like to hear it.
