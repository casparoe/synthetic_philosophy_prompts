Before I evaluate any particular proposal, I want the specification: what must a theory of aesthetic testimony deliver in order to count as adequate? The phenomenon to be explained is an apparent asymmetry. Told that Canberra is the capital of Australia, you know it outright; told that a film is subtle and beautifully paced, you seem to acquire something epistemically second-rate. Yet we defer constantly to critics, prize juries, and friends with better taste, and we expect students of art history to learn most of what they know from others.

Write the requirements list. It should cover, at least: the cases the theory must handle (a bare verdict from a trusted critic vs. testimony that also gives reasons; disagreement with someone whose taste you otherwise trust; expert deference where the expert's judgment outruns your ability to check it; the insistence that some works you simply have to experience for yourself); the distinctions it must respect (between testimony and recommendation; between coming to believe truly and acquiring the ability to notice the aesthetic features yourself; between the aesthetic case and ordinary empirical testimony); and the constraints it cannot violate (whatever explains the asymmetry had better not imply that we know next to nothing about art). For each requirement, state what motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp — in particular, whether the thought that aesthetic judgment must be first-person is a constraint on the theory or something the theory may itself revise.

Most importantly, tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that aesthetic testimony can transmit knowledge and aesthetic deference remains epistemically deficient in a way ordinary deference is not cannot both be maintained once the deficiency is spelled out non-trivially. If some subset is unsatisfiable, say which requirement should be dropped. Skip the scorecard of existing views — the spec is the deliverable, and if the list itself turns out to be the interesting result, so much the better.
