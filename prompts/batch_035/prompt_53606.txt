Second-year grad student here, trying to decide whether to spend my dissertation on what Buddhist no-self actually implies for practical ethics. The standard line goes: if there's no enduring self, then things like special concern for your future self, keeping long-term commitments, prudential planning, even blame all get shaky. But when I look closer, half of these arguments seem to quietly conflate "no self" with "no persons at all," and the other half seem to be attacking a soul-like self that maybe wasn't the target of the original denial in the first place (I'm thinking of how the doctrine is often read as rejecting a permanent, independent essence, not the everyday person who gets hungry and pays taxes).

So: of the alleged implications of anatta for ethics -- prudential concern, promise-keeping, responsibility and blame, the asymmetry between caring about your own future pain vs. others' present pain, how bad death is -- which ones actually carry weight if you take the doctrine seriously, and which are overrated or just don't follow? Please rank them and defend the ranking; I want to know what makes one worth three years of work, not just that it's unresolved. Where does Parfit-style reductionism about persons already do the work, and where does the specifically Buddhist version add something different?

And since I'm betting years on this: tell me where your ranking is most likely to fail. What interpretive dispute about the no-self doctrine, if it came out the other way, would reshuffle the list? And what argument would move you to a different ordering -- I'd rather know the crux now than after chapter two.
