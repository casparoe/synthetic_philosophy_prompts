I write novels for a living and I've got a craft question that keeps turning into a history-of-history question, and I want you to treat it seriously even though I'm a novelist and not an academic. So: the book I'm plotting right now turns on a cascade of small accidents, a real for-want-of-a-nail structure, where a jammed lock, a misread train timetable and a letter arriving one day late compound into something enormous. My editor says this is cheap, that real history doesn't work that way, that big outcomes have big causes, and her example is that even if Franz Ferdinand's driver hadn't taken the wrong turn, something else would have lit the powder keg in 1914. But when I poke at what historians actually say, I can't find a settled answer, just what looks like a permanent, tired standoff between people who think history is sensitive to tiny accidents and people who think outcomes are over-determined by deep structures, economics, geography, institutions, and both sides seem to talk past each other forever. So here's what I want: a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it a factual one about how the past really was, which the two sides would resolve with better evidence if they had it? Is it a methodological one about what counts as an explanation, where one side wants a story and the other wants something more like a law? Is it, as I half suspect from my side of the fence, secretly a fight about narrative form, about whether history has the shape of a tragedy (everything hangs on the poisoned cup) or the shape of one of those dismal novels where nothing anyone does matters because the system grinds on? And there's a sub-question I really want you to hit, because it's where the debate seems to go quiet and I don't know if that silence is informative: when people say the counterfactual is unknowable, are they right that small accidents can't be assessed, or are they just declining to assess them, and is that a principled refusal or an occupational habit? I'd rather you answer like you're writing to a colleague, and please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or anything like that, just continuous prose like a long email, because I'm going to read this on my phone in a cafe. No need for citations either, I'm not trying to pass anything off as scholarship, and honestly if you start listing books it'll just make me feel guilty about the ones I haven't read. At the end, do tell me whether your diagnosis should actually change how I plot the book, or whether the whole question, whatever the answer, is something a novelist is entitled to ignore.
