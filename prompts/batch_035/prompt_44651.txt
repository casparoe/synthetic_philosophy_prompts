I'm writing an essay on Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences (2013), whose thesis is that analogy is not a rhetorical ornament but the engine of all thought: every act of categorization is the silent mapping of a new situation onto an old one, and categories are fluid enough to stretch wherever a mapping takes them. The book mostly describes this machinery admiringly. What I want to know is whether the machinery can also be used to police it — and I'd like to test that on an analogy that does real political work.

The analogy is the one that dominates fiscal debates: a national economy is like a household. Governments, like families, must live within their means; deficit spending is like running up credit-card debt; someday the creditors will cut up the national card. The analogy is wielded to argue that austerity is the moral and practical equivalent of a family's belt-tightening, and that anyone who borrows against the future is fiscally irresponsible. My economist friends dismiss it in a sentence: a household cannot print the currency it owes, cannot tax its income into existence, and does not see its own spending become someone else's wages. Done.

But on the book's terms, this dismissal may be too quick. If every act of understanding is analogy — if even the economist's picture of the economy as a self-regulating system of flows is itself a compression built from prior situations — then the question isn't whether the household analogy is an analogy but whether it is a good one, and by what criterion.

So here is what I would like. First, lay out the correspondence mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the household, the paycheck, the credit card, the debt collectors, the inheritance; and mark which of these are structural correspondences and which are surface resemblances carried along by the word debt. Second, use the book's resources — its talk of an analogy's depth versus its range, and of conceptual skeletons at a level of abstraction high enough to cover both domains — to say whether the household analogy is shallow or deep. Third, and this is the part I care most about: if Hofstadter and Sander are right that all cognition is analogy, is there any neutral standpoint from which one analogy can be called misleading and another illuminating? Or does the framework explain why the household analogy is so compelling — the felt familiarity of the mapping, the way a national budget presses on everyone's most overlearned category, the household — without giving us grounds to resist it? I suspect the answer turns on whether the difference between a good and a bad analogy is a difference their theory can draw from inside, or something imported from outside it, and I would like your view. If the analogy fails, tell me what a better one would look like — but note that whatever you propose (the economy as a different kind of household, as an engine, as an ecosystem) will itself have to answer the same question.
