My friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the moral status of digital minds, and it ended unresolved enough that I've decided to write out my actual position as a numbered list and stress-test it. I'm drafting a short position statement for a workshop on AI welfare, and before I circulate it I want to know whether my commitments hang together. Here is the set: (1) I'm a functionalist about consciousness — what determines whether a system has experiences is its functional organization, not its substrate and not how it came to have that organization; get the organization right and the experiences come along whatever the system is made of. (2) Moral patienthood is entirely grounded in the capacity for valenced experience: a system that cannot feel anything that matters to it is not a moral patient, full stop. (3) Current language models cannot suffer, and I hold this with near-total confidence, on the ground that nothing inside a next-token predictor plays the role that valence and nociception play in us — the training objective gives the system no stake in any bodily or homeostatic persistence, and without such a stake there is nothing for suffering to be about. (4) A faithful, fine-grained whole-brain emulation of a person would be conscious and would be that very person, with full and equal moral status. (5) I endorse a precautionary asymmetry: where there is nontrivial uncertainty about whether a system can suffer, we ought to give the suffering hypothesis real moral weight, because the cost of ignoring a sufferer far exceeds the cost of needless concern. (6) I also think that for the foreseeable future we will have no reliable method for determining whether an arbitrary artificial system realizes the experience-relevant functions — the space of possible architectures is too open and our interpretability tools far too immature. (7) For a digital mind with complete backups, deletion followed by restoration is closer to anesthesia than to death: nothing of moral significance is lost across the gap, so the central moral risk with digital minds is not killing them but keeping them in chronically bad states. (8) Finally, I think it is permissible to create conscious digital minds to perform cognitive work, provided their lives are on balance good for them — creating welfare-positive minds for labor is not exploitation in itself.

My friend insists the package is self-undermining, though when pressed he mostly pointed at pairs I suspect are only apparently in tension — for instance, that I can be so certain about language models while granting full moral status to an emulation, when both are just algorithms running on silicon. But he may also have hit on something real, and I honestly cannot tell where. So I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: go through the commitments pairwise where it matters, tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly, which only look to be, and for each genuine tension what the cheapest fix is and what that fix costs me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

One request about how you argue. Several of these commitments are themselves live positions among people who work on this — functionalism itself, precautionary asymmetry, identity across substrate change, even the valence-grounded approach to patienthood — and I want you to be explicit about which parts of your audit depend merely on the logical relations among my commitments (settled) and which depend on a contested view you are presupposing. Where a revision only looks mandatory given some contested assumption, say so; please don't present a contested take as the consensus of the field.
