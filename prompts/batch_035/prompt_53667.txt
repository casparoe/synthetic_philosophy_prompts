I write historical fiction, and for fifteen years my partner — a historian of early modern Europe who reads my drafts before any editor does — has been issuing verdicts on the explanations I tuck into afterwords and put in the mouths of my characters. When I ask her for the principle behind her rulings, she waves me off and says the next manuscript will tell. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: she accepted my account of the trench stalemate of the First World War, which rests on the generalization that entrenched defenders with machine guns dominate massed infantry; she accepted the standard explanation of the collapse of the Aztec Empire via smallpox, praising the underlying generalization about previously isolated populations meeting Old World diseases; she accepted an explanation of the Reformation's spread that turns on cheap printing lowering the cost of coordinated dissent; she rejected, as an explanation, the claim that Princip's shots caused the First World War — she concedes they were a cause but insists that two pistol shots are not an explanation; she rejected a biography's account of Napoleon's invasion of Russia as hubris, remarking that hubris is a diagnosis, not an explanation; and she accepted my account of the repeal of the Corn Laws, which traces a particular sequence — the famine, Peel's conversion, the League's agitation, the parliamentary arithmetic — though she grumbled that its generalizations were all implicit and would have to be written out before it counted as finished.

My next novel is about the Haitian Revolution, and my afterword currently explains its success through three ingredients at once: a lawlike statistical claim (European armies campaigning in the Caribbean suffer catastrophic mortality from yellow fever, to which the formerly enslaved population has far greater resistance); a traced chain of particular decisions (the French expedition's early victories, the treachery by which Toussaint was seized and shipped to France, the moment news arrived that slavery had been reinstated in the other colonies, Dessalines's final campaign); and an overall narrative arc from the uprising of 1791 to independence in 1804, told so the reader can follow how each stage grew out of the one before.

Predict her verdict on this afterword — accept, reject, or accept with conditions — and tell me what exactly she will demand I change. Then reconstruct the rule she seems to be running: strict covering-law explanation, causal-chain explanation, narrative understanding, statistical relevance, or something else that fits her six earlier verdicts just as well. I want the rival rules that also fit, where they diverge in their editorial demands on my afterword (for instance, whether she will insist the yellow fever generalization carry the explanatory weight and demote the rest to narrative ornament, or instead foreground Dessalines's reasons and treat the epidemiology as background), and your best guess as to which rule is really hers and why. Finally, name the one further case — an explanation I could float at dinner — that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts she would say about it.
