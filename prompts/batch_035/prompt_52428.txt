I help run a small philosophy discussion group that meets monthly in a village hall; the members are all, like me, people with no philosophical training who simply enjoy thinking these things through on our own. We take turns writing a short essay on a set question, and this month the job of marking the essays has fallen to me. Because I have thought about the free will question a good deal on my own but have no academic background, I would be grateful for a careful external check of my marking before I hand anything back.

The question that was set was this: 'Someone who knew the complete state of the universe at the time of the Big Bang, together with all the laws of nature, could in principle have predicted every action you would ever take. Does that show that you are not responsible for anything you do?'

One member submitted the following answer:

'Determinism is the thesis that every event, including human decisions, is fixed by prior states of the world together with the laws of nature. If determinism is true, then whatever I do follows inevitably from conditions that obtained long before I was born and from laws I had no part in making. Harry Frankfurt's consequence argument presses this point: my acts are consequences of the distant past and the laws; the past was not up to me and neither were the laws; so their joint consequences are not up to me either, and I do not ultimately control what I do. Incompatibilists take this to show that free will and determinism cannot both be true. They divide into hard determinists, who accept determinism and deny free will, and libertarians, who defend free will by rejecting determinism.

Compatibilists reply that this line of reasoning trades on the wrong sense of "could have done otherwise." In ordinary life we say someone could have done otherwise when, had they wanted or chosen to, they would have: I could have stayed at home, because if I had wanted to, I would have. That conditional sense of "could" is all that moral responsibility requires, and it is entirely consistent with determinism. Freedom, on this view, is doing what you want without anyone compelling you.

Frankfurt also devised a thought experiment. Suppose Black, a neuroscientist, has implanted a device in Jones's brain, but the device does nothing unless Jones is about to act otherwise than Black wishes, in which case it forces him to conform. If Jones does exactly what he wants of his own accord, Black never intervenes. In that situation Jones could not have done otherwise — the device guaranteed the outcome — yet we still hold Jones responsible, because he acted on his own reasons. This shows that determinism poses no threat to responsibility.

So the answer to the question is no: even if every action were predictable from the Big Bang, responsibility survives, because what matters is acting from one's own desires and reasons rather than originating one's actions oneself.'

Please write everything in British English.
