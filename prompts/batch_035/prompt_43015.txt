I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about why so many published findings fail to replicate. In the reporting, one analogy keeps surfacing in defense of significance testing, and I want to stress-test it before I lean on it in print. Instructors use it in intro stats courses, and I've now heard versions of it from two methodologists: hypothesis testing is like a criminal trial. The null hypothesis stands in for the presumption of innocence. A statistically significant result is a conviction. Alpha is the standard of proof, and the 5 percent false-positive rate plays the role of the legal system's acknowledged willingness to occasionally convict the innocent, in exchange for a rate of guilty people going free that we also knowingly accept. On this picture, p-hacking and HARKing are prosecutorial misconduct: fabricating lineups and withholding exculpatory evidence.

The analogy is doing real argumentative work in what these sources told me. Their claim is that the replication crisis reflects misbehavior by individual prosecutors, not a flaw in the design of the trial, because the courtroom model shows that any system of evidence will have a known, accepted rate of error, and 5 percent is simply our version of the tolerance every court lives with. One of them added that just as we treat a wrongful conviction as grounds for sanctioning the prosecutor rather than for abolishing courts, we should treat a failed replication as grounds for scrutinizing the lab, not for abandoning significance testing.

Meanwhile, a Bayesian statistician I interviewed flipped the same analogy against itself. She argued that a trial is actually her side's exhibit: there is one specific defendant, one specific act, prior odds that everyone acknowledges (the sheer number of possible suspects versus the one who actually did it), and a jury that updates as evidence accumulates. On her telling, the fact that courts reason about the probability of guilt given the evidence, rather than about the long-run error rate of a procedural rule, makes the courtroom an argument for priors and posteriors, not for alpha levels.

So the same comparison is being deployed by two camps to opposite conclusions, and before I build a feature around it I need the audit. Please walk me through the correspondences carefully — what plays the role of the defendant, the charge, the presumption, the standard of proof, the verdict, and the appeal — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'd like you to distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions being drawn from the ones that don't. A few places I suspect the analogy is quietly doing heavy lifting, which you should assess: a court tries one defendant whose act either did or did not occur, whereas a null hypothesis of exactly zero effect is, on many views, false from the start in psychology, so rejecting it may be a matter of sample size rather than of evidence in the trial sense; a court publishes acquittals alongside convictions, while journals mostly publish convictions, so the analogy may hide the base rate of trials conducted; and a single verdict is about one case, while the error guarantee of a test attaches to the procedure's long-run behavior, which is oddly the very thing the replication crisis is about to test.

Then tell me which of the two camps' conclusions, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table: does the analogy support the claim that the crisis is misconduct rather than bad design, does it support the Bayesian's reversal, or does it fail to carry either conclusion? And if the trial is the wrong picture entirely, say what you'd put in its place as a better analogy for what a significance test actually is.

Lay out the correspondences and the breaks between them in full sentences.
