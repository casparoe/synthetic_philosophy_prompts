I'm a Bayesian statistician by training and trade: I build hierarchical models, think in terms of posteriors and predictive distributions, and treat de Finetti's representation theorem as basic equipment — for me "independent and identically distributed conditional on a parameter" and "exchangeable with a prior over the parameter" are two notations for the same object. This past weekend I had a long argument with a friend who did her PhD in philosophy of science, about David Lewis's Principal Principle — the rule that if you know the objective chance of an event is p and know nothing else relevant, your credence in the event should be p. She insisted that without genuine objective chances this rule has no foundation, and that my discipline quietly helps itself to chances whenever it talks about parameters. My reply was that de Finetti dissolved the issue: everything about "chance" is recoverable from exchangeable credences alone, so "deferring to chance" is really just deferring to your own future predictive distribution, and there is nothing further for objective chance to be. We ran out of steam without either of us moving.

I'd like you to translate the whole dispute into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can — I want a translation, not a caricature that makes my side win by construction. Concretely: the Principal Principle as a claim about calibration and predictive distributions; her comeback about "undermining" — the worry that if the chance of an event is fixed by the total pattern of outcomes, as Humeans like Lewis hold, then learning about future events can legitimately revise the chance you assign to a past one — expressed in terms of a hierarchical model with a fixed-but-unknown parameter and posterior updating over it; the patched rules philosophers have proposed in response, such as Hall's "New Principle," as modifications to the conditioning policy or the scoring rule; and the Humean identification of chance with something like limiting relative frequency or best-system summary, stated in terms I would recognize. Where something in her position cannot be put in my terms at all, I want you to say so plainly rather than fudge it with an analogy that silently changes the claim.

Then give me the residue report. What, if anything, in this philosophical disagreement has no counterpart in how statisticians actually handle these situations? Is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because the disagreement is less substantive than it looked at midnight on Saturday? My own tentative view is that the residue compresses into a single question I'd phrase as: in a hierarchical model, is the parameter a discovered quantity or a bookkeeping convenience? I'd like to know whether the philosophers have anything to say about that question which survives translation into my terms — or whether I'm right that once exchangeability is granted, there is no further fact for "objective chance" to be, and the Principal Principle is a theorem about coherence dressed up as a doctrine about the world.
