I recently listened to a popular economics podcast whose episode on the socialist calculation debate can be paraphrased roughly as follows:

In the 1920s Ludwig von Mises argued that rational economic calculation is impossible under socialism: without private ownership of the means of production there can be no markets for them, and hence no prices, and hence no way to compare the costs of alternative production plans — so a socialist economy could not allocate resources rationally at all; it would simply be flying blind. Oskar Lange replied in the 1930s that the planning board could simulate the market: post prices, watch for shortages and surpluses, and nudge prices up or down until the queues clear — trial and error, which is more or less what markets and entrepreneurs do anyway. So the calculation problem was solved in principle, and Lange had the good grace to thank Mises for forcing socialists to take accounting seriously. Hayek's mid-1940s essays are usually read as a rejoinder, but they mostly repeat Mises with new emphasis — the knowledge needed for planning is scattered across millions of individual minds, so the data are never available to any single planner. That is an empirical caveat rather than a new argument, and in any case it is now a computing problem: a newer generation of planners argues that with modern computing power and big data, Lange's trial-and-error procedure can be run at whatever speed you like. By most accounts the debate ended there, with Lange the technical winner, and when the Soviet economies collapsed it was for reasons of incentives and politics, not calculation.
