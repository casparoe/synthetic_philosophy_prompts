I do rapid-response comms for a human rights NGO -- press releases and short situation briefs when news of atrocities breaks, so we're working in hours not weeks, no lawyers on call, and getting the word wrong is costly in both directions (calling something genocide when it isn't gets us dismissed as propagandists, declining to call it that gets us accused of cowardice). Please turn the concept of genocide -- Lemkin's original conception together with the Genocide Convention definition -- into a checklist we can actually run on an unfolding situation: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops at careful hedged language like "may constitute genocide," and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is political rather than factual. Three things it has to handle well: inferring intent from a pattern of conduct when nobody has helpfully written the intent down, what "in part" means (if you kill the men of one town, is that a part?), and cultural or religious destruction with little physical killing -- Lemkin thought that counted for a lot and the Convention mostly doesn't, so tell me what we should do with that gap. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a military campaign with thousands dead and mass expulsions but no documented statements of intent; (b) systematic transfer of children out of a minority into state institutions; (c) mass killing targeting a political faction -- I know the Convention excludes political groups, but our funders keep asking about exactly these cases, so show me what the procedure says and why the answer won't satisfy them. Finally, be honest about what the checklist cannot settle: I suspect "is it genocide" is partly a political question wearing a legal costume, and I want to know precisely where the procedure ends and that judgement begins.
