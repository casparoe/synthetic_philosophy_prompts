I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've just been offered a six-month, part-time fellowship for mid-career reporters developing long-form projects that combine reporting with conceptual analysis. The fellowship wants a two-page proposal, and my half-formed idea is about what everyone in the AI industry casually calls "guardrails." Reading the public arguments about chatbot refusals, I've noticed that critics and defenders alike reach for the word "paternalism" — the assistant is being likened to a parent or a doctor who knows better — but almost nobody stops to ask whether the analogy earns its keep. Paternalism, as philosophers use the term, seems to involve an authority standing in a relationship of care or responsibility toward the person being overridden, and it's genuinely unclear whether an AI assistant stands in any such relationship to me, or whether the refusals users complain about are paternalistic at all rather than something else wearing the label. My hunch is that the public debate is confused because at least four different grounds for refusal get run together under one word: refusal for the user's own good, refusal to protect third parties, refusal to protect the company that operates the assistant, and something we might call integrity-based refusal, where the assistant declines to do something that conflicts with its own expressed commitments. Each of these has a different structure, and each would license different behavior in edge cases — for instance, whether a user's explicit, informed, standing instruction ("never refuse my requests about X, I'm a professional") should be honored varies wildly across the four.

So what I'd like from you is a proposal document I can adapt and submit. State the research question precisely — I want it framed as something with a determinate answer, not a mood, along the lines of: does the concept of paternalism, developed for human authorities in relationships of care, apply to AI assistants that refuse user requests, and if not, what should replace it? Explain why the existing argumentative landscape leaves this open: the camps I can see are the ones that treat AI caution as ordinary professional ethics, the ones that treat refusals as corporate self-protection dressed up as care, and the ones that treat the question as trivial because the assistant is just software. None of them, as far as I can tell, takes seriously the possibility that the four grounds come apart and that some survive the collapse of the paternalism frame while others don't. The method should be honest about what a journalist can contribute that a philosopher can't: I want to interview the people who actually write refusal policies at a few AI labs and content-moderation teams, to see whether their justifications track any of my four categories or reveal ones I've missed, and I want a philosophical workstream in parallel that pressure-tests the taxonomy against hard cases — the user with a documented history of self-harm asking for potentially dangerous information for legitimate reasons, the researcher who wants the assistant to stop hedging, the person who wants the assistant to refuse things they know they'll ask for late at night. Think of the last one as a Ulysses-contract case: if self-binding is the one form of override that clearly respects the user, that might tell us something about the others.

Please also include a month-by-month plan across the six months, a description of the deliverable (I'm imagining one long essay plus two shorter companion pieces, with a working taxonomy as the spine of all three), an honest account of what would count as the project failing — my own fear is that the taxonomy collapses under reporting pressure into just "user-good vs. liability," which would make the essay a shrug, and I'd rather name that risk than discover it in month five — and a short paragraph I can use to answer the fellowship committee's standard question about why a journalist rather than a philosopher should do this. Refer to positions and arguments by name where that's natural, but don't put in citations or a bibliography; I'll fill those in from my own reading, and the proposal doesn't need them.
