I have been recording my verdicts on cases about whether a person could have originated from anything other than the particular sperm and egg they actually came from, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures them, or whether some verdicts must go. My verdicts are as follows. A child conceived from my mother's egg but a different sperm would not have been me, and could not have been. The very embryo that became me, implanted in a gestational surrogate rather than my mother, would still have been me, so I am not an essentialist about wombs. That same embryo frozen for a decade before implantation would still have been me, so I am not an essentialist about timing either. If the zygote that became me had split into identical twins a few days after conception, neither twin would have been me, though I cannot defend this and it is nonetheless my firmest intuition. Destroying one cell of that zygote at the two-cell stage, leaving the other cell to develop normally, would still have left me — a verdict I hold with confidence even though it is hard to square with the twin one. Finally, for the case of two IVF embryos accidentally merging into a single chimeric person, I have no verdict at all, which unsettles me. Please reply in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: point one, the principle, or minimal set of principles, that best fits my verdicts, stated precisely; points two through four, one new case of your own devising each, with the principle's verdict on it and an assessment of whether that verdict is plausible; point five, an honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, if any, and what it is about the case that breaks it.
