I recently attended a panel on proposals for regulated compensation for living kidney donors. Whenever a question turned to the details — what a fair payment would look like, whether it should be a market price or a fixed honorarium set by a public body, whether payments should be capped — one of the panelists, a bioethicist, would close the discussion with the line that some things just shouldn't be for sale, and treat the matter as settled. The same line resurfaced afterwards in an exchange about whether utility companies should be permitted to auction relief supplies during a storm, so it appears to be doing a lot of work across cases.

My suspicion is that the sentence bundles several quite different claims, only some of which could support the way it was being used — in particular, the move from the slogan to the conclusion that questions of price and payment design are not merely mistaken but not even open. I would be grateful for a careful disambiguation. Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. My provisional carving, which you should correct if wrong, yields at least these candidates: (1) the empirical claim that markets in such goods misallocate or degrade supply; (2) the claim that, given actual background conditions, any voluntary sale is exploitative, so that the wrong lies in the terms of trade rather than in the sale; (3) the claim that introducing payment changes attitudes, so that people come to regard the good as a commodity, with the crowding-out of other motives being the real loss; (4) the claim that the value at stake is incommensurable with money, so that weighing it against a price is a category mistake rather than a mistaken weighing; (5) the claim that the sale itself wrongs the person or the good regardless of consequences — a status claim about what a price says about how the seller is regarded; and (6) a lexical claim that no quantity of money could ever compensate for any amount of the relevant loss, so that the trade-off is not just mistaken but impossible in principle.

For each reading, I would also like to know whether it would actually license what the panelist did with it. Two specific questions in particular. First, several of these goods already carry explicit monetary valuations elsewhere without much protest: insurers price the risk of my death, and firms in the life-settlement business purchase the policies of the dying outright. Being for sale in the literal sense therefore cannot be the operative notion; which of the readings survive this observation, and in what modified form? Second, most live proposals are not free markets but fixed compensation — a sum set by a public body rather than discovered through supply and demand, and not the product of bargaining. Does any reading of the slogan distinguish a sale at a market price from the acceptance of a fixed honorarium, or does the prohibition, if it holds at all, apply to both equally? And if none of the readings supports the design-shutting move, I would like to know which of them, if any, supports the weaker conclusion that such arrangements are wrong all things considered — that is, where the real disagreement should be located.
