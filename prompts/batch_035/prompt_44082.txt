I run a math circle for sharp high-school students with no calculus, and next month's session is on Bertrand's paradox. Don't explain the paradox to me; plan how I should teach it. What has to be in place first — I suspect the students need to see how one even puts a uniform distribution on an infinite set of chords before the three rival answers can register as a paradox rather than as one correct answer plus two computational slips — and where in the sequence the principle of indifference should enter, so they see where it works comfortably (finite, discrete cases) and where it starts to strain. Decide whether one concrete physical demonstration should carry the whole session (e.g., actually generating chords by the three different procedures — spinning a bottle for the midpoint, throwing darts for the endpoints — rather than just drawing them), and why that framing might matter more than the trigonometry. Anticipate the misconceptions in the order they'll arrive: that one of the three answers is secretly right and the others cheat; that the paradox shows probability theory itself is inconsistent rather than that "a random chord" is incomplete until a sampling procedure is specified; and that indifference is a guide to truth rather than a formal constraint that can be over-applied. Tell me where to head each off, what to openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes (say whether Jaynes's invariance solution and the cube-factory puzzle belong on the cutting-room floor or make a good closing flourish), and give me one exit question whose answer would distinguish a student who has grasped the actual moral from one who has merely memorized the three numbers.
