I'm a PhD student in bioethics, writing a chapter on the abolitionist programme that descends from David Pearce's Hedonistic Imperative. My supervisor is sympathetic to it and presses an argument on me that strikes me as proving too much, but I can't locate the fault precisely, and I'd like a diagnosis I can actually defend.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Every episode of suffering is bad for the subject who undergoes it, and no good realised elsewhere compensates for it from that subject's point of view: no amount of happiness in others makes my agony anything but bad for me.
(2) Where a state that is bad for its subject can be prevented, and preventing it sacrifices nothing else of value, we ought to prevent it — including, where necessary, by altering the subject.
(3) The only way to guarantee that no episode of suffering ever occurs is to remove the capacity for it: to re-engineer the reward system so that the worst state any descendant could reach lies well above the line at which experience stops being unpleasant.
(4) Nothing else of value is thereby sacrificed, since nothing we value — insight, art, love, moral seriousness — logically requires the capacity for suffering; each is coherently conceivable in a mind that cannot suffer.
(5) So we ought, in the long run, to rewrite the hedonic architecture of sentient life so that no descendant can suffer.

What makes me suspicious is a substitution test. In (3) and (4), replace the capacity for suffering with the capacity for suboptimal experience — the very distinction between better and worse states of well-being. Every suboptimal state is, from the subject's point of view, worse than a state she could have occupied; nothing we value logically requires that well-being come in an ordering at all; so by the same chain, we ought to level the whole of sentient life into a single undifferentiated maximal state. That is the wirehead's plateau, which even Pearce rejects: he warns that direct stimulation of the reward centres destroys informational sensitivity to environmental stimuli, and he insists our descendants should instead be animated by gradients of well-being far richer than today's peak experiences. So either the argument overshoots into the plateau — a conclusion its own friends refuse — or it fails somewhere, and whatever blocks the plateau looks uncomfortably as though it would also block the removal of the floor.

Please tell me which step or inference to reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: is my substitution genuinely parallel, or does it quietly fail to reproduce some premise — and if the parallel fails, does the original argument then stand as it is, or does some further flaw remain in it? And if the abolitionist must abolish the floor while retaining the gradients, can that line be drawn on principled grounds, or only by fiat? For what it's worth, I should like to keep (1), and I suspect (5) is true; my worry is that the argument, as it stands, licenses far more than its conclusion.

Please write your answer in British English.
