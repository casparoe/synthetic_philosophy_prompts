I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and I'm putting together an interactive segment for an undergraduate seminar on comparative cognition where we'll use Dennett's four-fold taxonomy of minds — Darwinian creatures (hardwired, selected phylogenetically), Skinnerian creatures (trial-and-error learners shaped by operant conditioning), Popperian creatures (agents that can pre-select actions by internally simulating or hypothesizing before acting, so bad hypotheses "die in their stead"), and Gregorian creatures (agents that bootstrap their cognition with mind-tools like language, arithmetic, and explicit notations). The students have read the primary material, so they know the labels; what they haven't done is try to apply the hierarchy to anything that isn't a textbook example. For the seminar I want a "which kind of mind is this, really?" quiz — but built the opposite way from a typical comprehension check: I don't want a single question that mentions any of the four category names or asks directly "is this creature Popperian?" Instead I want eight to ten concrete cases, each with three or four answer options describing only what the entity does or how its behavior is produced, such that the answer patterns sort cases (not people — the quiz's subject is the entity) into the right rung of the hierarchy.

The cases should deliberately include some that stress the taxonomy's seams, not just clean examples like a bacterium with a chemotactic hardwired loop or a rat learning a lever press. Please make sure to include, at minimum: a slime mold that reorganizes its body over hours to reach food sources through mazes; a corvid that watches which of several experimenters has cached where and re-caches when observed by a dominant bird that was absent during the original caching; a large language model that produces successful action plans in text without acting in the world at all; an immune system building antibody diversity by clonal selection; and a chess engine running static evaluation over millions of candidate positions before moving. You may add further cases — a honeybee dance economy, a squirrel's deceptive caching, a PID controller, whatever you think forces genuine sorting work — but flag explicitly which pairs of cases your questions cannot separate (e.g., can any behaviorally observable answer distinguish a genuinely Popperian simulator from a Skinnerian that merely had a very lucky history of reinforcement?), and note where the hierarchy's own criteria conflict (selection among representations versus selection among actions versus the requirement that Gregorian mind-tools be culturally acquired). Please conclude with a scoring key and two or three sentences on where you think the taxonomy bends or breaks under this load, rather than rescuing it with a footnote.
