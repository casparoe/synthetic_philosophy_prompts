I'm a novelist, seven chapters into a book about a fishing town where every captain knows the bay is being fished past recovery, every captain would prefer a closed season to a dead bay, and every captain goes out anyway. My editor's standing complaint about the manuscript is that a story without a villain is a story without an engine: readers, she says, cannot sustain attention on a harm for which no character is responsible. I have come to suspect that this complaint is philosophically loaded, and that the literature on multipolar traps, races to the bottom, and Moloch is precisely where the loading shows. So I am asking for a translation exercise rather than an explanation, and I want to be precise about what I know and don't know, so you can calibrate.

My fluency is this: I am fluent in narrative craft. I think in Aristotle's terms of plot, reversal, and recognition; in the workshop vocabulary of want versus need, stakes, scene and sequel, free indirect discourse; in genre terms — tragedy, comedy, satire, the escape plot, the conversion plot; and in the craft truism that a premise makes a promise to a reader that the ending must keep. My game theory, by contrast, ends at knowing the Prisoner's Dilemma and the Nash equilibrium as phrases. Please use no notation and no matrices; if a payoff structure would help, describe it in words a novelist could put in a character's mouth.

Restate the debate about multipolar traps in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what did not survive the translation. Three things I want carried over in the translation proper. First, the core mechanism: agents each doing what is individually best, every one of them preferring some other equilibrium to the one they jointly produce, and no one able to move to it alone. Tell me what this is in my terms — what it does to the concept of motivation, whether want versus need can even express it, what happens to stakes when escalation is nobody's decision. Second, the fact that visibility does not help: agents who see the trap with total clarity walking into it anyway. In craft terms this ought to be dramatic irony — the audience knowing what the character doesn't — except that here the character knows too, and it changes nothing, which is a form of irony my vocabulary may not have a name for. Third, the standard exits that literature proposes: enforced coordination, the Leviathan move, where some authority changes the game by fiat; changing the payoffs themselves, so that what was individually rational stops being so; and exit, founding a smaller game with new players who can hold a better equilibrium. For each exit, tell me which genre move it is, which character function it performs — and notice, I suspect, that all three look suspiciously like the one thing craft forbids: the author stepping in.

After the translation, I want a residue report, and two specific items on it. The first is the narrator. A novel is written by one mind, so the vantage from which the whole trap is legible is built into the form; the narrator's omniscience is exactly the coordination the characters lack, which may mean my craft vocabulary structurally cannot say the most central thing there is to say about a multipolar world. If that's right, say so plainly. The second is hamartia. The tragedy I inherited requires a flaw — Oedipus errs, Lear errs — but in a multipolar trap nobody errs; everyone acts well and the bay dies. I know my tradition has a word for outcomes no one chooses, fate or necessity, but I want you to tell me whether it carries this weight or whether it smuggles in a personified agent, a someone, which is exactly what this debate denies. Add to the residue report anything else in the debate you find that has no expression in narrative craft at all, and flag wherever you had to distort either vocabulary to make the translation go through.

Finally, a verdict on the residue: is what fails to translate a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks — or that it is secretly asking narrative questions, about how a human being can hold an outcome-responsibility with no responsible agent? And render a judgment on the editor's complaint. Does no villain, no story survive the translation as craft wisdom, or is it a prejudice my form smuggles in — and if the latter, what do I tell her I'm writing instead?

Please organize your answer in four parts, in this order, with rough lengths: Part I, The Translation, roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, with the mechanism, the visibility problem, and the three exits as clearly marked subsections; Part II, The Residue Report, roughly four hundred to six hundred words; Part III, The Verdict on the Residue, roughly two hundred fifty to three hundred fifty words; Part IV, A Note for My Editor, no more than two hundred words, addressed to her directly. I'd rather have no bibliography and no citations; if you need an illustrative work, use something at the level of Oedipus Rex or Moby-Dick, not scholarship, and say why it fits in one line. If you coin a term to bridge the vocabularies — and I suspect you will need at least one — flag it as coined. Please write in American English throughout.
