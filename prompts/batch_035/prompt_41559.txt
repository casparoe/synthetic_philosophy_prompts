I've been going through a pile of decision-theoretic games trying to settle what I actually believe about when a gamble is worth taking, and I've ended up with a row of verdicts that I suspect don't all fit under one rule. Here they are.

First, a game of the ordinary kind: I'm offered a coin flip that doubles my wealth or halves it, with even odds. The expected value is positive, and my gut says play, maybe for a small entry fee too. Second, the classic St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and I'm paid $2 to the number of flips. The expected value is infinite, and yet I wouldn't pay more than about fifty dollars to play — a few hundred at the outside, if the house is demonstrably good for it and I'm feeling loose. Third, the Pasadena game: same coin procedure, but the payouts alternate between large gains and large losses of roughly comparable, growing size, so that the expected value isn't infinite and isn't minus infinity and isn't any number at all — the sum just oscillates without converging. My verdict: I won't play it even for free, and I can't fully explain why, except that "there is no expected value" feels like a reason. Fourth, a cousin of the Pasadena game in which every negative outcome has been capped at a loss of, say, one hundred dollars, while the positive outcomes are unchanged. Now I'd happily pay something modest to play, even though the expected value, if it exists at all, is dominated by terms I can barely estimate. Fifth, and this is the one I keep chewing on: a game with a perfectly well-defined expected value of about fifty dollars, but where that average comes from a small chance of a crushing loss and a large chance of a small gain. I'm reluctant to play, even knowing the expectation favors me.

My tentative principle is something like this: when the expected value is undefined, decline the gamble outright; when it is defined, use it, but discount it for risk in the ordinary way; and when it is infinite, behave as if it were some large finite amount set by realistic limits on the payout. But I can see the seams. The principle tells me to refuse the Pasadena game because its expectation is undefined, but to accept a capped version whose expectation I can't even compute — it can't really be undefinedness doing the work in both directions. And my St. Petersburg verdict seems to flout the positive-expectation rule outright, since no plausible "realistic cap" reasoning should bring a fifty-dollar price tag anywhere near an infinite average. Meanwhile my refusal of the well-defined, positive-expectation, heavy-tailed game is presumably just risk aversion, but if risk aversion is allowed to veto defined expectations there, why isn't it enough to explain the Pasadena refusal too, without any appeal to undefinedness?

So: is there a single principle — or a principled division of labor between a few rules — that captures all five verdicts? If so, state it as precisely as you can, show which clause is doing the work in each case, and tell me whether any of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. If keeping all five costs too much in ad hoc structure, I'd rather know which verdict to give up and why. Please also tell me what your preferred account says about a case I haven't mentioned: a Pasadena-style game in which the crushing negative outcomes are made vanishingly rare but correspondingly enormous, so that the series technically converges to a large positive number while the modal, typical outcome of playing is a comfortable win. Does your principle recommend playing, and does that recommendation strike you as the right one — or is it a reductio of the account?
