Write an Onion-style news article, headline plus roughly 450–550 words, reporting that a local man has taken Chesterton's fence so seriously that he can no longer discard anything. The man — give him a name, a town, the whole deadpan wire-service apparatus — has stopped throwing out expired coupons, a broken flashlight, his late grandmother's filing system, and a family tradition of attending funerals in a strict order nobody can explain, on the grounds that he cannot prove no one ever had a reason for any of it. To be clear, the target of the satire is not Chesterton, and not even Chesterton's actual point, but the over-generalized version of it that circulates in policy arguments: the inference from some institutions embodying accumulated, tacit knowledge to the conclusion that no one may ever change anything without first reconstructing the complete evolutionary rationale of the whole social order. The humor should come from getting the conservatism right rather than caricaturing it from outside — so weave in the real conceptual machinery, played straight: the survivorship problem (he cannot distinguish things that survived a filtering process from things that merely survived), the tacit-knowledge problem (the ugly lamp stays because its reason, if it ever existed, is unrecoverable), and a regress gag in which every reason he reconstructs for keeping something turns out to be itself an unexamined inheritance that must therefore also be preserved. A good recurring bit: he keeps inventing plausible-sounding rationales for objects and then treating his own just-so stories as recovered ancestral wisdom. Include at least one quote from a concerned neighbor, a bleeding-heart utilitarian who is at her wits' end because she cannot prove the flashlight's destruction would be net-positive, and one from the man himself, preferably deploying a phrase like "second-order ignorance" with quiet, unearned confidence.

For context: this is for a philosophy department zine's satire issue, and the editors want pieces where a reader who actually knows the Burke–Oakeshott–Hayek neighborhood would recognize that the author understands what the fence principle was for — the piece should exaggerate a real feature of the reasoning rather than invent one, and the deadpan should stay deadpan, with no winking narrator. It should also leave the reader genuinely unsure whether the joke is entirely at the man's expense, since a smart reader should come away feeling the pull of the man's position at least once before the absurdity reasserts itself.

Append a brief note (100–150 words) after the article stating where, if anywhere, the piece leans on a contested reading — e.g., whether treating Chesterton's counsel as epistemic humility about all inherited practices versus merely a procedural caution about reform is a defensible interpretation — and identifying the exact point at which the local man's position stops being a fair exaggeration of the principle and becomes a strawman. Commit to a definite answer about that boundary rather than offering both possibilities.
