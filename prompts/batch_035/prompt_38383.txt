I'm putting together a final paper topic for my seminar on markets and ethics, and the unit we just finished was reproductive labor — surrogacy and egg donation. The background my students have: most of continental Europe prohibits surrogacy altogether, even uncompensated (France, Germany, and Italy ban it in all forms, and Italy has recently gone as far as prosecuting citizens who arrange it abroad), while in the US a number of states run a functioning market, with agencies, escrow accounts, and total costs for intended parents often running into six figures. Egg donation shows the same split in milder form: European law officially limits donors to "expenses" (a thousand euros or so, which clinics interpret loosely), while American donors routinely recieve five figures per cycle — which is part of why European intended parents and donors end up at clinics in Cyprus, or flying to California.

In seminar I've been pushing a frame I more or less made up, which I call the deprivation test: a ban on compensating reproductive labor is justified only if what remains open to the affected women, once the ban is in place, is better for them than the banned option would have been. And the hard part is deciding what counts as "open to them," given that the women most likely to become surrogates in an open market are usually the ones with the fewest alternatives. It's one thing to say a market exploits their circumstances; another to show that blocking the transaction leaves them better off rather than just poorer. I also keep pressing them on the asymmetry between surrogacy and egg donation — nine months of pregnancy versus a few weeks of hormones and an extraction. Nearly everyone finds one more troubling than the other and almost nobody can say why, in a principled way.

So the assignment: write an essay evaluating whether Europe's prohibitive approach or the American market approach (assume some regulation is on the table either way) is the more defensible one. Take seriously the strongest arguments on both sides — the commodification worry about putting reproductive capacity up for sale, versus the paternalism objection that bans mostly export the practice and make it more dangerous for everyone. But don't end on "there are considerations on each side" — that's the cop-out I always complain about. Defend a verdict, and say something concrete about what, if anything, should actually be prohibited or capped: commercial compensation as such, advertising, or only so-called traditional surrogacy where the surrogate is also the genetic mother? A rough target is 1200–1800 words, and you can assume a reader who knows no law or bioethics background, so explain whatever you need along the way.
