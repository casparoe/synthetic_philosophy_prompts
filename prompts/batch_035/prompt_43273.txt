I'm writing a short piece for a philosophy of science reading group where two characters argue, and I want the argument anchored in a concrete moment rather than being a floating debate. The setting: two philosophers — one a Humean about laws of nature (laws are just the best systematization of the total mosaic of particular facts; nothing governs anything) and one an anti-Humean (necessitarian/essentialist flavored — think laws as genuine powers or necessities in nature) — are grading an intro physics exam together. A student wrote, about a refracted light ray, that the ray "had no choice" but to bend the way it did. They get into it over whether that phrasing is just loose talk or whether it tracks something real. Write their dialogue.

Make sure the anti-Humean actually presses the governing intuition (what makes the regularity hold? why does the electron "have to"?) and the Humean pushes back with the regularity/best-system view and the charge that governing adds spooky quiddities nobody can point to. And let each land real hits: the Humean should squirm a bit at why one system counts as "best" if standards of simplicity and strength are themselves just more facts in the mosaic, and the anti-Humean should struggle to say what a law's hold over unactualized cases amounts to without sounding like a rulebook written nowhere. Neither should be a strawman, and they should respond to each others actual points, not talk past each other. End it unresolved but with both of them having sharpened something about where they actually disagree. Around 600-800 words is fine.
