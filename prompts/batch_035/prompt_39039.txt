No academic background here — just someone who's been reading mechanistic interpretability stuff for a couple years and turning it over on my own. The idea that hooked me is mechanistic anomaly detection: rather than list every bad behavior up front, you check whether the model computes things the same way it did on-distribution, and treat a change in how it computes as the thing to flag. I've settled on what "the same way" means to me: same causal structure — the same earlier features feeding into the same combining step to produce the output — even if the exact numbers along the way drift a bit. So if a model does arithmetic with the same decomposition whether the numbers are digits or spelled out in words, its one mechanism used twice; if it suddenly routes around its usual features on some strange input, that's a new mechanism and the alarm should go off. What would count against this way of cutting things up? I want the observations and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including things that may have already happened, and an honest answer on whether I've just picked a definition that can't lose — and if so, whether that's a real problem for something meant to become an actual safety test someday.
