I'm writing a novel whose protagonist undergoes a corpus callosotomy in her late twenties — the full severing of the band of fibers joining the hemispheres, done as a last resort for intractable epilepsy — and the aftermath of the operation is meant to structure the whole book. My editor, who has been reading the popular accounts, is set on the picture of the split-brain patient as two people in one body: a speaking self and a mute twin who watches the world through the same eyes, occasionally seizing the wheel. She wants the novel to alternate between two narrators — two typefaces, two chapters for every day of the protagonist's life — with the right hemisphere as a fully realized consciousness locked inside, commenting on and sometimes sabotaging the plans of the spoken-for self. She points out that this is basically what Sperry's own early reports suggested: that the surgery leaves the patient with two streams of awareness, one of which simply cannot tell anyone it exists, which she finds an extraordinary thing for a novel to attempt.

My friend, a cognitive scientist who has worked with some of the later split-brain literature, says the editor is ten years behind the discussion. On her telling, the twin-in-one-skull picture was an over-reading of the early experiments: the right hemisphere of a typical patient has language comprehension but no speech output, so it behaves less like a whole hidden person than like a competent but inarticulate bystander; the celebrated confabulations in the classic experiments are the work of the left hemisphere's press-office instinct, improvising reasons for actions whose causes it never saw; and in most patients, outside the lab's carefully segregated testing, life goes on without any felt seam at all, with whatever integration survives traveling below the severed bridge. She thinks the two-narrator design gets the character fundamentally wrong and will read as neurological folklore.

I'd like to know how much of the disagreement is a dispute about the analogy itself. Please give me the correspondence table for the twin picture — what plays the role of the twin, the muteness, the shared body, the imprisonment, the sabotage — and then show me where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for a novel (which must render a lived experience from the inside) from the ones that only matter for the laboratory. I'd especially like to know what the picture smuggles in: whether personhood really tracks the hemispheres, or whether the twin metaphor quietly assumes what the experiments were supposed to test. And then the practical question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does my editor's two-narrator design die, or does it survive on a different footing — and if there is a better analogy a novelist could build on instead, what would it be?
