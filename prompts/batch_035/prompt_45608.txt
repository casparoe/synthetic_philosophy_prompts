I'd like a probability estimate from you, but the number matters less than the derivation, and I want the derivation in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just the way one would write a careful letter. The question concerns the free will debate, but not the version that gets rehearsed a thousand times; I want your credence about what ordinary people's judgments actually presuppose.

The proposition: the concept of free will that is operative in most people's concrete judgments of blame and praise — as opposed to the concept they avow when asked abstract survey questions — is compatible with causal determinism.

Some background so we are on the same page, and so this is answerable from reasoning alone. Experimental philosophers studying folk intuitions about free will found a striking pattern. Ask people in the abstract whether, in a universe where every event follows from prior events by strict laws, anyone is ever morally responsible for what they do, and a majority say no. Give the same people a concrete case — a man in a deterministic universe who murders his family in cold blood — and ask whether he is morally responsible, and most now say yes. The standard reading of this pattern is that abstract questions put people in a theoretical frame in which they equate free will with exemption from the causal order, while emotionally charged cases put them in a frame where what they actually check for is whether the agent understood what he was doing, could respond to reasons, and was not coerced — conditions that determinism leaves intact. Dennett's line, from Elbow Room onward, is that this second pattern reveals the free will worth wanting, and that compatibilism delivers it. The harder line, associated with Saul Smilansky's illusionism and Derk Pereboom's hard incompatibilism, is that the deeper part of what people want is a kind of ultimate authorship that determinism rules out, and that whether or not the compatibilist package is worth wanting, it is not what people's practices of blame and desert actually presuppose.

My credence on the proposition is about 0.7. A friend of mine, who thinks Dennett is changing the subject, puts it at 0.15. Give me your own number, and decompose it into the sub-questions it depends on: whether the concrete-case responses genuinely reflect compatibilist commitments or are an emotional override that would evaporate under reflection; whether the concept in use is even single enough to have a determinate compatibility relation with determinism, or whether people deploy several concepts across contexts without noticing the shift; whether what people want from free will, once the deliverable part is separated from the impossible part, is something determinism can supply; and any further terms you think belong in the decomposition. Assign each sub-question a rough probability with a line of justification, show how the terms combine into your final number, and say which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me whether the gap between my 0.7 and my friend's 0.15 most likely lives in one particular sub-question or in a disagreement about how the decomposition itself should be structured, and name the single piece of evidence — something about how people actually judge, not a new argument — that would move your number the most.
