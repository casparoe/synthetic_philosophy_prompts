so in my decision making seminar this week we played the dollar auction (prof auctions off a single dollar bill, highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, but the twist is the second highest bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing). after the game she said the point was to demostrate escalation of commitment, like companies that keep throwing good money after bad on failing projects. I figured that was the whole lesson, but then I got in an argument with my roommate because I think I can prove escalation is actually rational:

(1) Sunk costs are irrelevant to rational choice — only future costs and benefits matter. (everyone in the class agrees on this, it's like decision theory 101)
(2) Say I've bid 90 cents and my roommate has bid 95. If I quit now, I lose my 90 cents for sure and walk away with nothing.
(3) If I bid one dollar instead, I win the dollar, so I come out 10 cents ahead of quitting. And if he bids over me, I'm right back in the same situation, and the same comparison says keep going again.
(4) This holds at every single point in the auction: quitting is a guaranteed loss of my sunk bid, continuing always beats it, for both of us.
(5) So two fully rational agents bid each other up indefinitely, and the rational move in a dollar auction is to escalate forever. Which means either rationality is self defeating here, or escalation isn't a bias at all and my prof's whole lesson was wrong.

My roommate is convinced step 3 or 4 is sneaking something in but he can't actually articulate what. Personally I'd like to keep (1) since basically the rest of the course is built on it lol. Where exactly does this go off the rails, and what do I have to give up to block it?
