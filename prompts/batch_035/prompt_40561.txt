My flatmate (philosophy PhD student, annoyingly good at this stuff) has an argument that's been eating at me all week. It's meant to show that if you accept two pretty innocuous-looking principles about rational choice, you end up committed to fanaticism — the view that you should prefer a one-in-a-googol chance of a near-limitlessly good outcome to the certainty of an absolutely wonderful one. Setup: imagine outcomes spread along a spectrum, from paradise at the top, down through paradise-minus-one-grain, paradise-minus-two-grains, and so on, all the way down to ordinary decent lives. His steps:

(1) Weak dominance: if a lottery gives you, with probability 1−p, something at least as good as X, and with probability p something strictly better than X, then the lottery beats X for sure — no matter how small p is. (2) Small-trade principle: if the top prize of a lottery gets bigger, it's rational to accept a slightly worse fallback in exchange; the fallback is where you almost certainly end up anyway, and a slightly worse near-paradise is still a near-paradise. (3) Better-than is transitive. (4) So start by holding paradise-minus-a-million-grains for sure — still an unimaginably good life. By (1) you'd swap it for a lottery that's the same thing with probability 1−ε but paradise-plus (yep, better than paradise) with probability ε. Then by (2) you accept a tiny downgrade of the fallback — call it paradise-minus-a-million-and-one-grains — in exchange for a further boost of the top prize. And again, and again: each trade is fine on its own. (5) After enough trades you're holding: ε chance of something astronomically good, 1−ε chance of an ordinary decent life. By (3), that final lottery beats paradise-minus-a-million-grains for sure, since it got there through a chain of improvements — and by relabelling it beats any sure thing you like. (6) But that's just fanaticism, which (he claims) no sane person accepts.

I hate the conclusion, but every premise looks fine to me, and the ones that look most fine are (1) and (3), which is exactly where he says the escape routes are the most expensive. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? Please dont just wave at "it's a sorites" — I want to know where the chain breaks and what I have to live with once it's broken (money-pumps? arbitrary thresholds? biting the bullet on Pascal's mugger scenarios?). Also be upfront about the fragility of your diagnosis: which premise or consideration, if it turned out to be independently defensible, would flip you to locating the problem somewhere else, and where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
