I'm a 2L who just finished contracts, and my partner is one of those LessWrong-adjacent people who keeps going on about "acausal trade" — cooperating with agents you've never communicated with and never will, agents maybe in other branches of the multiverse or in the distant future, just because each side can predict the other well enough that defecting stops being worth it. My eyes glazed over until it occurred to me that this is, like, a contracts problem: you have two parties, an exchange of performances, but no offer, no acceptance, no negotiation, and no enforcement mechanism — the whole thing is supposed to work anyway. So here's my ask: restate acausal trade for me in the vocabulary I actually have, the vocabulary of contract law. Formation doctrine (mutual assent, the mirror image rule, the mailbox rule, consideration as bargained-for exchange), the substitute doctrines (promissory estoppel and reliance when formation fails), the remedies framework (expectation vs. reliance vs. restitution, efficient breach, liquidated damages as precommitment), maybe escrow and arbitration as third-party workarounds. Be as faithful as you can to the actual acausal trade idea — including the version where the two agents cooperate because they can each simulate or prove things about the other's decision procedure, so their "agreement" is basically a shared algorithm rather than any communication.

Then give me the residue report, since that's what I actually care about. What translates cleanly? My guess is some of it does: precommitment to cooperation looks a lot like a liquidated damages clause that raises the price of breach, and the functionalist idea that contract law exists to make cooperation rational when trust is absent seems to carry over. But what about the stuff that clearly doesn't — contract law's insistence on an outward manifestation of assent, something a court could actually point to, when acausal trade has literally no observable token of a "meeting of the minds" (there's no meeting!). And consideration doctrine's requirement that each party's promise be the price of the other's — can that do any work when neither party's performance is even observed by the other? Is there a contract-law analog to the question of whether an "agreement" nobody can ever evidence was ever formed, sort of an extreme version of the statute of frauds problem?

Mostly I want your honest read on the residue: is it there because contract law is epistemically conservative — it demands observable tokens of assent because courts can't verify predictions about decision algorithms, but a sufficiently good predictor in principle could — or is it there because acausal trade is missing something that real trade has, some feature that contract law is tracking without being able to say it? My partner insists the residue goes the other direction, that contract law is the crude human approximation of the real thing and full-blown agents with transparent source codes wouldn't need any of it. I half suspect she's right about the prediction part and wrong about the normative part — that even ideal cooperators would still want something like enforcement, and the idea that you can get trade with no sanction at all, only counterfactual ones, is where the analogy is doing drugs. Which of us is wrong, and which parts of my vocabulary do I need to drop to see it?
