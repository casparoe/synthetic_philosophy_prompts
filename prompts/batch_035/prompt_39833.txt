So I volunteer at this "philosophy for everybody" night at a community center — about fifteen people, mostly retirees plus a couple curious twenty-somethings, no philosophy background at all. Next month is my turn and I picked Wittgenstein's On Certainty, mainly the hinge idea, which I've read a few times but never taught, and I know from past sessions how fast these things collapse into vibes if I don't have a real plan.

Don't explain the idea to me — I want the plan itself. What has to be in place before "hinge" can even mean anything to them (like, does the Moore setup come first or does it just get them thinking the whole book is about proving things)? What single example should carry the whole session, and does it have to be Moore's hand, or is there something that works better for people who've never heard of him? And I can already tell you the misconceptions I'm dreading: the guy who says "oh so hinges are just assumptions," the guy who read a summary the night before and insists hinges are "rules of the language game not propositions," and the one who'll decide Wittgenstein is saying the external world doesn't exist. Where in the sequence do I head each one off, and in what order do they actually arrive?

Also tell me honestly: should I even touch the question of whether hinges can be known or said, or the whole "is this just foundationalism with extra steps" thing — or is that too much for ninety minutes? And give me one closing question that would let me tell whether the idea actually landed vs. everyone just nodding along. If it helps, one of the regulars is a retired math teacher who treats every session like a proof-checking exercise, so factor him in.
