I'm writing a term paper for a political philosophy seminar on luck egalitarianism, and I want to defend a thesis my TA is going to find annoying: that the view's verdicts can invert in perfectly ordinary circumstances — it can end up owing compensation to someone whose loss flowed from her own choices while owing nothing to someone hit by pure bad luck. The usual story is the opposite (Dworkin's whole brute luck / option luck apparatus exists to protect the prudent and refuse the gamblers), so for the paper to work I need the cases, and I can't build them myself without begging the question against the view.

So here's the build order. Two scenarios, one paragraph each, realistic — a workplace, a family, a farming town, whatever, but no trolleys, no demons, no hypothetical insurance auctions. In Case A, someone suffers a loss that traces back to a choice she made, but the choice has to be one that was forced or shaped by unchosen circumstance in a way that gives a principled luck egalitarian reason to treat the origin of the whole thing as brute luck (this is roughly where G.A. Cohen presses Dworkin, but I want it dramatized, not just asserted). In Case B, someone suffers a loss that is textbook brute luck — no choice anywhere in the causal chain — and yet the principled luck egalitarian reason is to compensate her less than nothing, or nothing at all. Hard constraints: neither verdict may be stipulated. Since my thesis is that the view inverts itself, I can't write "obviously she couldn't help choosing that" — the involuntariness in A and the non-entitlement in B have to fall out of the concrete details and then be defended from the theory's own resources. Also no cheating by making A saintly and B obnoxious; the sympathy work must be equal, and don't get the reversal by quietly swapping in a strawman version of the view — stick to the Dworkinian core. My hunch is Case B should lean on the fact that luck egalitarianism is about relative position, not absolute loss, and Case A on brute luck hiding upstream of the choice, but tell me if you think something else does the work.

After the two scenarios, do the walkthrough. What the brute/option luck line says about each case, where exactly each verdict flips, and which single feature of each case is doing the work. Then stress-test both: if we switch to an absolute rather than comparative metric of disadvantage, or if we make A's alternatives genuinely open (costly but available), which verdicts flip back and which survive? My roommate says Case B is just the levelling down objection wearing a costume, and that a grader will reach for Anderson's point that this is exactly what happens when you make equality about compensating luck rather than about relational standing — so tell me how close my two cases come to restating her point, and whether that kills the paper or is actually fine, since my claim is about what the view *commits* to, not what it should say. End with your own judgment in a fixed form I can quote in my notes: a final line exactly like \answer{Case A: X; Case B: Y} where X and Y are each either "compensate" or "deny".
