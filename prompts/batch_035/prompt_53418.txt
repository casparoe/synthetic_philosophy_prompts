Taking phil of language this semester and we just finished the Moore's paradox unit, and I've come out of it pretty committed to the normativist story and I want to stress test it. So the view I hold: the reason you can't assert "it's raining but I don't believe it" isn't that it's contradictory — obviously it isn't, it can be true, picture the stubborn professor standing in a downpour insisting it's just mist — but that there's a norm on belief, something like belief aims at truth, plus a norm linking assertion and belief, assert only what you believe. Uttering the Moore sentence puts you in a position where you either violate the truth norm or the assertion norm no matter how you read yourself, and that clash is what the felt absurdity is. I like that it explains the first-person present-tense restriction for free: "it was raining yesterday but I didn't believe it" and "it's raining and John doesn't believe it" are both fine because past selves and other people aren't under the norms that apply to me, here, now.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters mapped out and ranked, fatal to merely awkward, and specifically these things I keep chewing on:

- The commissive/omissive asymmetry. "It's raining but I don't believe it" vs "it's raining but I believe it isn't." To me the second one feels worse, like an active clash rather than a gap. If the norm story predicts they're exactly on par, is that a problem or is it noise?
- The future-tense one our prof brought up (I might be butchering it): something like "it will rain tomorrow, but I don't believe it will" seems way more sayable — like a grudging concession — even though the belief is still present tense and first person. If the norms are about me-now, shouldn't that one be absurd too? Is digging in and saying it's actually bad a real commitment of the view or just a bullet nobody should bite?
- Embeddings. "If it's raining and I don't believe it, the picnic's ruined" is fine, the weirdness vanishes under 'if'. Is the normativist predicting that (norms govern assertion, and the antecedent isn't asserted), and if so is getting that right impressive or is it cheap?
- My linguistics friend says the dynamic semantics people treat the absurdity as a straight update contradiction once you model assertion right, so the whole norms apparatus is a redundant re-description. If that story could absorb everything the norm story explains, does my view lose, or do they just have different subject matter?
- Weird subjects. Self-deceived people, absent-minded professors, someone who sincerely claims not to have beliefs about weather. Are there cases where the norms are intact but the absurdity isn't, or vice versa? Those divergences feel like they'd hurt the most.

Tell me which of these a committed normativist would concede as real pressure vs wave off, and be honest about whether the view has quietly been qualified into something unfalsifiable — and if it has, whether that even matters for this kind of position, since it's not like it was making risky empirical predictions to begin with. Also, if you think the whole norms framing is at the wrong level and a pragmatic Gricean self-defeat story does the same work with less metaphysics, say so, but then sketch what would count against THAT one, because that's the fallback I'd probably adopt. My own sense is the future-tense case is what I'd personally find most persuasive, so lean into whether it's really the knockdown it feels like to me.
