Compare the two main routes by which coherence arguments are supposed to establish expected utility maximization: the Dutch book argument descending from Ramsey and de Finetti, which shows that violating the probability axioms leaves your credences vulnerable to a sure-loss set of bets, and the representation-theorem route of Savage and von Neumann–Morgenstern, which derives EU maximization from structural axioms on preference (sure-thing principle, transitivity, independence, and so on) without ever mentioning a bookie. I've absorbed the standard rationalist folklore that these collectively prove you should be an EU maximizer, but I've never seen the folklore distinguish them carefully, so I'd like the comparison to do three things. First, lay out what each argument actually assumes about the agent: the Dutch book argument seems to need an adversarial counterparty who can trade against you at will, in liquid markets, with no transaction costs — conditions that an embedded, bounded agent in a one-shot world rarely faces — whereas Savage's axioms make no such assumptions but instead load everything onto the normative plausibility of the axioms themselves, several of which (independence especially) are exactly what a skeptic denies. Second, address the objection that both arguments at best establish conditional advice — if you face repeated adversarial interactions, incoherence will cost you — rather than the unconditional conclusion that a single agent making one-off decisions under uncertainty ought to approximate coherence. Third, say which route you find stronger, and whether either genuinely closes the gap between "incoherent agents are exploitable in principle" and "you, a person with a finite budget and no bookie stalking you, must have preferences representable as expectations." I'd especially welcome attention to whether transitivity violations alone (the money-pump style arguments) do more or less work than probabilistic incoherence in the folklore's overall case, and to whether the two arguments justify the same notion of coherence or subtly different ones.
