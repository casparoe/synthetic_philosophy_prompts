I've been in a bioethics reading group going on three years now and the therapy/enhancement debate has basically fossilized on us. One faction, mostly the medics, insists the distinction marks something real: medicine exists to treat disease and restore normal function, so growth hormone for a kid with an actual deficiency is therapy while the same hormone for a short-but-healthy teenager is enhancement, and crossing that line turns doctors into something else (service providers? engineers of persons? they gesture at this a lot). The other faction just keeps bringing counterexamples, and honestly they're good ones. Vaccination gets given to healthy people and doesn't restore anything, yet everyone counts it as medicine. Eyeglasses don't make eyes normal, they compensate. Modafinil for night-shift workers — is sleep-deprived-and-slightly-impaired the "normal function" we're restoring? Orthodontics that's purely cosmetic but is routinely covered by insurance. And if you define normal function statistically, the line moves whenever the population does — if average height creeps up, does shortness quietly become a pathology? Nobody has changed their mind in maybe two years of meetings, and from what I can tell it's the same in the literature: the people who defended the distinction around the bioconservative-ish council reports of the early 2000s and their critics (the ones pushing fairness-based views where what matters is who gets access, not which side of the line a treatment falls on) have been trading the same moves for decades now.

My working theory is that we're not having one disagreement but at least two. One is about whether there's a coherent line at all, a conceptual question, and the counterexamples are aimed at that. The other is about whether whatever line we can draw should be load-bearing for policy — what insurers must cover, what the state should provide, what doctors may do — and the medics hear the counterexamples and shrug, because for them the line is doing normative work about the purpose of medicine, not descriptive work about bodies. Then the critics ask why the telos of an institution should settle anything about fairness, and we go around again.

Is my diagnosis right? Partly right but missing something? Maybe there's a third thing going on that I'm not seeing, like a hidden empirical assumption about how slippery slopes actually work, or a disagreement about whether the goals of medicine are discoverable or chosen. And if the impasse is structural like I suspect, what would even count as progress here — a better concept, an institutional settlement that makes the concept unnecsesary, or just admitting the question is ill-posed and dropping it? I'd like a real diagnosis grounded in what each side actually argues, not another tour of the counterexamples.
