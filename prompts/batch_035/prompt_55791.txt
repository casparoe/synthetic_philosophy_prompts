I've been writing down my considered positions in decision theory — partly to get clearer myself, partly to see whether they add up to a view or just a pile of case-by-case verdicts — and I would like an audit of the result. Here is the list.

(1) In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a duplicate of me — no causal connection, no communication, but someone whose reasoning runs exactly as mine does, so that whatever I conclude, they conclude — I cooperate, and I think defecting would be the mistake.

(2) My ground for cooperating is not that my cooperation causes theirs; it plainly doesn't. It is that the two of us instantiate a single decision, so the options really on the table are both-of-us-cooperating and both-of-us-defecting, and I prefer the former to the latter.

(3) In Newcomb's problem with a highly accurate predictor, I one-box, and for what I take to be the same kind of reason: the predictor's past prediction and my present choice are two outputs of one psychological setup, and choosing is, in effect, selecting which output that setup gives.

(4) In the smoking-lesion case — a lesion that independently causes both a disposition to smoke and cancer, while smoking itself has no effect on cancer — I smoke, and I think refusing to smoke on evidential grounds is a confusion the lesion exploits.

(5) I defect against an opponent who is not my duplicate but merely predicts my move with 70% accuracy and cooperates only when predicting cooperation — this despite the fact that, with standard payoffs, cooperating against such an opponent earns more in expectation than defecting does. My thought is that a statistical correlation between my act and theirs is not the kind of thing that can speak in favor of my act.

(6) I judge a decision theory by how its adherents fare: if following a theory predictably leaves you worse off than following a rival across the cases where it matters, that is a strike against the theory, whatever its metaphysics.

(7) I believe there is no backward causation and no action at a temporal distance: nothing I do now can make it the case that my duplicate chose differently, or that the predictor predicted differently.

So: which of these should I actually be worried about? I can see that (2) and (7) sound as though they cannot both be true — the options can't be both-cooperating and both-defecting unless my choice settles both — though my suspicion is that this tension is shallow and turns on reading (2) too literally. What I want is a proper audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and, for each genuine tension, how severe it is and what would have to give. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and tell me which single revision, if any, would clean up the most. I have tried to state each commitment as I actually hold it, not in the form that makes it easiest to defend, so if one of them can't be saved without rewriting it into a different claim, say so.
