I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and last week we spent two hours on Kavka's toxin puzzle without reaching anything like consensus: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon, where the toxin will make you violently ill for a day but poses no lasting danger, and where the money is paid at noon tomorrow whether or not you actually drink. My classmate — a committed Gauthierian, always going on about how rationality should be about dispositions rather than isolated choices — left the seminar annoyed and came back this week with an argument he claims shows that our professor's official line proves too much. It goes like this. (1) A rational agent cannot now intend to perform an action she knows she will have decisive reason not to perform when the time to act arrives. (2) In the toxin case, by tomorrow afternoon the million is already banked regardless of what you do, and drinking serves only to make you ill, so you will have decisive reason not to drink. (3) So no rational agent can intend to drink, and hence none can collect the million. (4) But ordinary commitments have the same structure: promising to help a friend move on a Saturday you'll dread, signing a lease, an alcoholic resolving at breakfast not to drink at the evening party — in each case you now intend what you know you will, come the time, have strong or even decisive reason not to do. (5) So if (1) blocks the toxin intention, it blocks every such commitment. (6) That is absurd — practical life collapses without the ability to bind ourselves. (7) Therefore (1) is false, and once it's false, nothing stops the rational agent from intending to drink, collecting the million at noon, and then calmly declining the toxin in the afternoon, having held a perfectly rational intention throughout.

Our professor's reply, as best I can reconstruct it, was that the parity in (4)–(5) is fake: in the commitment cases the agent will have a future reason to comply — the obligation created by the promise, the contract, the resolution — whereas the toxin drinker will have none at all, so only the toxin case violates (1). My classmate pressed back that this "future reason" is manufactured by the very intention whose rationality is in question, so the professor is either bootstrapping reasons out of intentions or sneaking in an independent constraint he hasn't stated. I can see both of them being right about something, which I take as a sign I don't actually understand the dialectic.

What I'd like from you: tell me which step of the argument to reject, or, if you think it's valid, which premise is least costly to give up. Be explicit about what rejecting your chosen step costs — e.g., if the professor's obligation-based difference between the toxin case and the promise case is doing real work, say exactly what work it does and whether it can be stated without bootstrapping; if it can't, say what follows for promises, contracts, and resolutions. For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (1) than accept that my friend's afternoon millionaire is behaving rationally, but I also can't accept that keeping (1) makes my Saturday moving promise irrational — so if you think I have to choose, make the price of each choice vivid. Finally, since I plan to bring this back to seminar, tell me where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to fail, or what consideration — a case, a distinction, an argument — would move you to a different verdict.
