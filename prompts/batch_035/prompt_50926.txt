I'm a postdoc straddling an AI-safety research group and a school of public policy, and next month I'm presenting to my university's international relations workshop a position paper on what the small formal literature calls safe Pareto improvements. The core idea I'm defending: in many games where agents face the prospect of a costly conflict over which of several possible outcomes will prevail — a war over a territory that either side might win — the parties could, in principle, commit in advance to a substitute protocol that replaces the conflict with a lottery. The lottery implements, with some probability, the continuation each side would most prefer, scaled so that each side expects strictly more than it would get from the conflict itself; and because the protocol is anchored to what each would have received anyway, the commitment is safe in the sense that neither can end up worse off relative to the fight. My thesis is not merely that this is a tidy formal curiosity about cooperation between artificial agents, but that it is a live, underexplored policy instrument for interstate disputes where war's waste is real but the stakes are modest — think contested maritime fishing rights or tit-for-tat tariff escalations — and where the enforcement and verification problems, while serious, are not categorically harder than those we already solve with ordinary treaty design.

The workshop audience is mostly political scientists who work squarely in the bargaining-model-of-war tradition, steeped in Fearon's rationalist explanations for war and prone to diagnose every proposal as a rediscovery of the commitment problem or the private-information problem; one formal theorist who thinks in mechanism-design terms and will immediately start hunting for failures of individual-rationality and incentive compatibility; and one constructivist who regards the entire exercise of modeling states as unitary expected-utility maximizers as a category error and will say so in the first ten minutes. Nobody there will have encountered the idea before, and several will suspect it of being AI-safety imperialism — an abstraction imported from designing agents that cooperate, dressed up as statecraft.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the eight or so objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it (including at least one delivered as a polite refusal to accept the unitary-actor premise at all); a two-minute reply to each; a candid note on where each reply is soft, especially on the regress worry that the ex ante commitment presupposes exactly the kind of credibility whose absence that literature treats as a cause of war in the first place; what I should concede up front rather than fight; and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
