I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy training and I recently fell down a rabbit hole I can't get out of. At work I spend a stupid amount of time in arguements about what counts as "one thing": is the payments service plus its database one service or two, does a monorepo count as one codebase or a bunch of them, when two services share a message queue have they merged into a single distributed system or not. These arguments are annoying but I always assumed they were resolvable -- you just pick whatever boundary makes your reasoning easiest and move on, nobody thinks the monorepo question has a deep fact of the matter.

Then I found out philosophy has been having the exact same argument about tables and chairs for like 40 years. As far as I can tell: one camp says tables don't exist, strictly speaking, there are only tiny particles arranged in a table-shaped way (van Inwagen, I think, and I believe he only grants "one thing" status to living organisms). Another camp says every collection of things composes a further thing, no restrictions, so there's an object that's my left shoe fused with the Eiffel Tower, it's just very spread out and boring. And a third camp says the common sense answer -- yeah, sure, there's a table, next question -- is the right one. All three sides have been at it since at least the 80s, everyone knows everyone else's moves, and from the outside it looks like nobody has budged an inch. It looks EXACTLY like my microservice arguments, except these are brilliant people publishing papers for four decades.

My working theory is that it's a verbal dispute -- like arguing whether a monorepo is "really" one codebase. Both sides could agree on every single fact about the particles and still disagree, purely because they mean different things by "exists" or "there is an object." I've seen this described somewhere as quantifier variance, though honestly I might be misusing the term. But another part of me thinks it can't just be verbal, because if there genuinely are no tables, that seems like it *should* have consequences for something, even if I can't articulate what. Whereas if it's purely verbal, then all these smart people grinding on it for a generation is itself the interesting puzzle.

So: diagnose this thing for me. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or are the sides secretly answering different questions under one heading? If it's verbal, why hasn't it died -- what keeps smart people fighting about it for decades, is it status, or is there a hidden real question (about parsimony? about what our language commits us to?) doing the work underneath? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here -- a distinction, a reformulated question, a finding from physics -- or should I expect philosophers to still be publishing on this one when I retire?
