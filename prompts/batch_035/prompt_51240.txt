hey, so i'm in a political philosophy seminar this semester on burke and the conservative tradition (we did the reflections and some oakeshott, and now we're on chesterton's fence). last week we got into a long debate about whether the fence thing actually works as an argument, and i want to sort out my thoughts before writing my paper, because my prof leans on it constantly and one guy in the seminar pushed back pretty hard.

the way it gets deployed: some reformer wants to remove a rule or institution they can't see the point of -- the classic case is the literal fence or gate across a road. the conservative says don't clear it away until you know why it was put there, because the fact that you personally don't see the use doesn't mean there isn't one. in seminar we tried it on a bunch of things: the filibuster, our university's distribution requirements that nobody can explain anymore, a proposal to change how grad students get supervised, etc.

i want an audit of the analogy itself, not a history lesson on chesterton. pretend you're a proffesional philosopher who works on analogical reasoning and political philosophy. think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a bit before giving your final answer. specifically:

1. lay out the mapping -- what corresponds to what when we go from fence to social institution. fence = rule, builder = ?, the act of seeing the use = ?, etc. a table is fine.

2. where does the mapping break? i'm especially stuck on this: in the fence case there was presumably a builder with a reason, and if you're puzzled you could go ask them -- the reason is the kind of thing one person could have had. social institutions mostly don't have a single builder, and the "reason" for lots of them is either lost, contested among historians, or was never a reason at all but an accident or a muddle of compromises. does the analogy quietly assume intentional design? and what happens to the instruction to go figure out the purpose when there may be no fact of the matter to figure out?

3. the deeper thing i keep hitting: even if you DO dig up the original reason, why does that matter? our department has a rule whose original rationale we actually found in old minutes, and it's frankly bad -- it was put in decades ago for a reason most people today would find kind of unjust. the fence picture suggests that once you know the reason, you've earned the right to remove the fence. but in our case knowing the reason made people MORE eager to remove it. is the analogy smuggling in an assumption that the original reason will turn out to be a decent one, or maybe that origin justifies function?

4. also it seems like chesterton's point isn't really "the reason is good, therefore keep the fence." it's more like "you, the reformer, are in a bad epistemic position, so your confidence is misplaced." so is the analogy doing epistemic work (about what the reformer knows) or practical work (about what should be done with the fence), and can those come apart? is there a version where the reformer fully understands the institution and should still keep it, or fully understands it and should definitely remove it?

5. last thing: is there a better analogy for what the conservative wants to say? someone floated burke's society-as-organism but that seemed to have worse problems (organisms have a good and death is built in, etc). i was toying with a cowpath that got paved into a road, or vestigial organs, or legacy code nobody dares delete. tell me which, if any, of these captures the point without the smuggled assumptions -- or propose a better one yourself.
