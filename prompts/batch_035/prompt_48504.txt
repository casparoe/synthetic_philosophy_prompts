I'm a PhD student in economic history with a strong side interest in philosophy of history, and I've been asked to give a guest lecture on path dependence to an undergraduate methods class. I want to use the Pottersville sequence from It's a Wonderful Life as my running example, and I'd like a careful reading of the film through the path-dependence literature before I commit to it in front of thirty students.

Here's what interests me. Clarence's tour is, in effect, a counterfactual experiment: delete George Bailey from the world, hold everything else fixed as far as possible, and observe. The result is Pottersville -- Martini's has become Nick's bar, Harry is in the cemetery because nobody pulled him out of the ice, Mary has shut herself away in the library, and the whole town seems locked into an absorbing state of saloons and dance halls. But as a piece of counterfactual methodology the film's experiment is oddly designed. It runs exactly one intervention, the deletion of a single individual, and treats the resulting world as what the town really was underneath all along. It never tests the intervention a structuralist would run first -- delete Potter instead, or delete the Building and Loan, or shift the timing of the intervention so George dies in 1929 rather than on the eve of the war -- and it never asks whether Pottersville reflects George's absence as such or merely the particular moment and manner of his absence. My worry is that the film smuggles in a great-man theory dressed as path dependence: it displays contingency (one man matters enormously) while implying a kind of reverse inevitability (without that one man, the same set of falls follows).

So: read the sequence through the actual conceptual apparatus of path dependence -- contingency, lock-in, increasing returns, absorbing states, and the distinction between outcomes in which small events have merely large effects and those in which they have persistent, self-reinforcing ones. Where does the film genuinely dramatize lock-in, for instance in the Building and Loan as an institution that keeps the town's housing finance on an entirely different branch than Potter's world of slums? Where does it trade on a confusion between 'George was causally necessary for Bedford Falls' and 'Bedford Falls was fragile'? And where does the film resist the reading -- moments where the counterfactual world on display looks suspiciously tailored to shame George rather than to track the nearest possible world, or where structural facts (depression-era banking, Potter's preexisting grip on the town) would seem to be doing work the film attributes to one man? I'd also like your view on what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the formal literature doesn't -- perhaps the point that whoever runs a counterfactual also chooses which intervention to run, and what that choice quietly settles.

A few requests. Please be candid where the film is thin gruel for these concepts, or where my framing distorts it. Answer in American English. And since I'll be repeating whatever you tell me to a roomful of undergraduates: state how confident you are in each of the main claims of the reading, and single out the point you are least sure of, so I know exactly where to hedge.
