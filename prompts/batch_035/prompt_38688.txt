My reading group has been going through population ethics stuff this semester, and last week it went off the rails into infinite ethics and hasn't come back. The trigger was someone pointing out that if the universe is spatially infinite (eternal inflation and all that), then it plausibly contains infinitely many people at every welfare level, and total utilitarianism apparently can't say anything useful anymore. Fine, I thought, that's a known embarrassment for aggregative views. But then Dan, who is the kind of person who has opinions about measure theory, walked me through an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I've been unable to find the exit for a week.

Here it is, as he gave it to me. (1) The value of a world depends only on which welfare levels it contains, not on which particular person occupies which level — swapping lives between people is just relabeling, and relabeling can't change how good a world is. (2) If the very same people all exist in two worlds and each of them is better off in one of them, none worse, then that world is strictly better. (3) Now take an infinite population indexed by all the integers — a minus two, a minus one, a zero, a one, and so on forever in both directions — where person a-n has welfare level n, on a scale with no floor and no ceiling. (4) Press a button that shifts everyone up: a-n now has welfare n+1. Every single person is exactly one unit better off; no one is worse off. (5) By (2), the post-button world is strictly better. (6) But the button merely permuted which person sits at which welfare level — same people, same collection of levels — so by (1) the two worlds are equally good. (7) So (1) and (2) can't both hold for infinite worlds. Dan's conclusion isn't just that aggregative views break — it's that whichever principle we drop, one of the two most obvious truths about betterness fails whenever the population is infinite, which means we can't condemn a button that makes every life slightly worse forever (it comes out equally good), and by extension we lose grip on ranking infinite worlds at all.

I can't decide what to reject. My gut says the trick is somewhere in the fact that he needed a welfare scale unbounded in both directions — with a lowest level the improving shuffle apparently can't be constructed, since someone has to get dumped into the basement — but Dan waves this off as an artifact of his example, not the point, and I can't tell if he's right or if it's actually load-bearing. If I had to pick a premise to save I'd probably keep (2), honestly, but I want to know what that costs, including whether the standard escape routes work: declaring infinite worlds incomparable, going person-relative on comparisons, whatever. And don't jsut tell me infinity is weird and Cantor did nothing wrong — tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what the bill is.
