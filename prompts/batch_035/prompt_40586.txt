I've become fairly settled on the etiological, selected-effects account of biological function: the function of a trait is whatever it was selected for doing in ancestral lineages, so the heart's function is to pump blood rather than to make thumping sounds because pumping, not thumping, is what got ancestral hearts retained and spread. What draws me to the view is how neatly it handles the things that make function talk philosophically odd in the first place: it explains how a trait can malfunction (a diseased heart fails to do what hearts were selected to do, and that remains a fact about it), it grounds the normativity of function attributions without invoking any designer or mysterious teleology, and it makes sense of why knowing a trait's function is explanatory — it tells you why the trait is there at all.

But in my philosophy of biology seminar, a classmate who defends Cummins-style causal-role analysis has been raising objections that I can't quite shake, and I want to know which of them I should actually worry about. There's the novelty problem: at the moment a new trait first appears — the first proto-feather, an enzyme created by a gene duplication — it has no selection history, so it seems the view must deny it any function at all, which sits badly with the fact that working biologists happily assign a function to a novel protein in the very paper where they first characterize it. There's plasticity: a transplanted heart, or a co-opted structure being used in a new context, where the causal contribution and the selection history come apart. And there's the scope-of-selection question: if selection processes other than natural selection — clonal selection in the immune system, neural selection during development, even trial-and-error learning — can also fix functions, then what exactly is doing the work in the account?

So here is my real question: what would count against the etiological view? I want the defeaters mapped and ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including cases it cannot describe, results in adjacent areas it stands or falls with, and pressures from biological practice rather than just counterexamples dreamed up in a seminar. Which of these would a committed defender — someone in the tradition of Neander or Millikan — concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of what the account was meant to capture? In particular, I'd like a straight answer on whether the standard repairs have insulated the view into something close to unfalsifiability: restricting functions to what recent selection maintained (the modern-history move), or weakening the selection requirement so that a trait needs only a modest history of differential reproduction. And is there a genuine collapse worry — that as the notion of selection gets stretched to cover immune and neural and developmental cases, the etiological view quietly converges on the causal-role account it was supposed to rival, and the dispute dissolves rather than being won? For my part, I'd be most moved either by a concrete biological case that the best available versions demonstrably cannot handle, or by a principled reason why biologists' everyday function ascriptions were never supposed to line up with the theoretical notion the etiological view is defining.
