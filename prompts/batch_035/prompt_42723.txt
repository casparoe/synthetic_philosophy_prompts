I'm taking a course on theories of justice, and a running theme is the distinction between outcomes that trace to a person's choices and outcomes that trace to circumstances she never chose — what my professor calls "brute luck" versus "option luck." Once I had that distinction in my head, I started seeing it everywhere in how my country's actual institutions handle misfortune, and I can't figure out what coherent theory, if any, the patchwork presupposes. Unemployment insurance pays only if you're actively searching for work, as though your unemployment were partly your responsibility to remedy, while disability benefits never ask whether the disability was present from birth or followed decades of deliberately dangerous hobbies. Bankruptcy law will discharge credit card debt run up on vacations and gambling but makes student debt nearly impossible to discharge, treating the decision to borrow for education as more binding than the decision to borrow for consumption. Disaster relief compensates homeowners who knowingly built in a floodplain just as fully as victims of unprecedented wildfires, while someone who develops a chronic illness through no fault of her own receives nothing comparable from the same treasury. It's as if each program draws the choice/circumstance line in a different place.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility for outcomes that this patchwork of practices presupposes. Does the pattern reflect a single underlying view — something like the claim that people should bear the costs of outcomes they genuinely chose, and be insulated from the costs of outcomes they didn't — or does each program quietly presuppose its own incompatible view, with job-search requirements presupposing a robust responsibility for employment outcomes, etiology-blind disability benefits presupposing almost none, and the floodplain/chronic-illness contrast presupposing something stranger still about which kinds of risk count as voluntarily assumed? Please distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely tolerate: could several different theories of distributive fairness each license this arrangement, or is there a reading on which the arrangement only makes sense as a compromise no theory would endorse? And finally, tell me concretely what would have to change if the state instead took seriously the strict view that outcomes traceable to genuine choices should be left with the chooser while outcomes traceable to circumstance should be collectively absorbed: which programs would flip their rules, which would survive unchanged, and where does that strict view run out of road — for instance, when "genuine choice" is itself shaped by upbringing, addiction, or the information available to the person at the time?
