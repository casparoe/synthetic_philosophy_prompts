I have recently taken over the grading of the letter-string analogy items on a reasoning test administered by the tutoring cooperative where I work, and a string of grade appeals has made it personally urgent that I figure out what my own scoring standards actually are. The items belong to the style of Hofstadter's Copycat microdomain: the student is shown a change made to one string of letters and asked to make the analogous change to another. I have firm verdicts about which answers deserve credit, but when a student writes to ask why, I find I cannot say — and I am no longer certain my verdicts form a coherent set.

The base item, on which everyone agrees: given abc → abd, the analogous change to ijk is ijl — replace the rightmost letter by its successor. An answer of ijd, which copies the literal letter d into the slot, gets no credit. That much is easy.

The contested cases, stated as verdicts on student answers to that same base item:

1. mrrjjj → mrrkkk. Credited. The salient rightmost object in the source string is a single letter, but in mrrjjj the salient rightmost object is the group jjj, and the successor of jjj is kkk. The student perceived the group.
2. xyz → wyz. Credited, with hesitation. Since z has no successor, the student allowed rightmost to slip to leftmost and successor to slip to predecessor — two slippages at once — and yet the answer seems right to me rather than strained.
3. xyz → xya. Not credited. The student posits wraparound, taking a to be z's successor under a cyclic reading of the alphabet: one perfectly consistent rule, no slippages at all, and yet the answer seems mechanical, as though it missed the point of the change.
4. xyz → xyy. Not credited, and this is the verdict I am least sure of. The student changes the rightmost letter to its predecessor: one slippage, positional concept intact. By any count of slippages it is more economical than the wyz I credited in case 2, and the only defense I can produce is that xyy feels like a retreat — the answer degrades the string — where wyz feels like an insight. I suspect that is not a defense.
5. kji → lji (full credit) versus kji → kjj (half credit). Since kji is written in descending order, either the positional concept holds and the rightmost letter i becomes j, or the string's reversed direction drags rightmost toward the opposite end and k becomes l. I keep flip-flopping; a colleague insists both are single consistent rules and that my preference between them is arbitrary.
6. iijjkk → iijjll. Credited; a group answer again, this one easy.

My tentative principle is something like this: an answer deserves credit when the concepts it preserves, and the ones it slips, are the concepts that were doing the real work in the source change — slippage is licensed when the target string pressures it, and the identity of a concept matters less than the role it plays. But as far as I can tell this principle does not discriminate between cases 2 and 4, and I do not know what would.

What I would like from you is a principle — or a small set of candidates — precise enough to deliver verdicts on all six cases, with the specific case that motivates each clause; an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, if any, and why those rather than others should be surrendered; and the principle's verdicts on two items I have not yet had to grade: given abc → abd, what should mrr produce (mrs, holding to the positional concept, or mss, treating the rightmost group rr as the object to be changed); and given a shifted base item, abc → bcd, what should ijkkk produce?
