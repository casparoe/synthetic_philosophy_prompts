A friend who is an economist and I spent an entire evening arguing about how governments should weigh costs and benefits that fall in the distant future, and I came away puzzled by something more specific than our argument. She insisted that discounting future benefits is nothing more than opportunity cost of capital: money not spent now can be invested and made to yield more welfare later, so a benefit in 2100 genuinely is worth less today, and no value judgement about future people is involved. I suspected that some view about the moral status of the later-born has to be hiding inside the arithmetic. What puzzles me most is that the official practice seems to split the difference in a way that may not be coherent under any single theory.

Take the way a national treasury appraises long-lived projects — flood defences, nuclear decommissioning, infrastructure. It discounts future costs and benefits back to the present at a standard annual rate, but the rate declines in steps with distance: a lower rate beyond thirty years, a lower rate again beyond seventy-five, so that far-future effects are not shrunk to the near-nothing that a constant rate would produce. So far, so equivocal. But the same guidance also caps the calculation at a horizon of about 125 years: whatever a project does to people after that — however catastrophic — counts for nothing whatsoever. So the far future is protected by a gentler rate, and then abolished by a cut-off.

What theory of intergenerational value, if any, does this tiered practice presuppose? Does the declining schedule presuppose a pure time preference that simply weakens with distance, or can it be reconstructed by a view on which every generation's welfare counts equally — say, through diminishing marginal returns to consumption if the future is richer, or uncertainty about whether anyone will be there? Can the 125-year horizon be licensed by any of these theories, or does it quietly rule them all out and reveal something else entirely — a tractability convention masquerading as a moral position? And tell me what would have to change in the practice, step by step, if the implicit theory were replaced by a principle of equal concern for all generations: would consistency demand a flat rate out to infinity, a zero rate, or no discounting of welfare at all? I would also like to know which parts of the practice are genuinely underdetermined — points at which several quite different theories of the future would all license the same numbers.
