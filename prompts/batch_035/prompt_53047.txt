I'm preparing a seminar unit on suffering-focused ethics, and I want a thought experiment that tests a specific view I'll call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: roughly, the view that suffering is what matters fundamentally, that suffering above a certain severity threshold cannot be compensated by any quantity of pleasure or of milder suffering, and that below the threshold the ordinary aggregative arithmetic proceeds as usual. The familiar objections to negative utilitarianism — the pinprick-versus-world-destruction family — all operate at the level of whole worlds, and I want a case at the level of ordinary allocative choice instead, where the threshold either earns its keep or looks like an artifact of the setup.

Construct the thought experiment to the following specifications. First, a one-paragraph scenario, realistic and non-exotic: no demons, no perfect predictors, no hypothetical utopias. I have in mind something like triage with a scarce palliative resource — a field hospital after a disaster, a fixed stock of analgesic, two administration protocols — but any realistic setting will do; the constraints below, not the setting, are what matter.

Second, the case must be engineered so that three theories deliver precisely the following pattern of verdicts:

1. Classical hedonic utilitarianism must reach a definite verdict that the suffering ledger alone cannot produce. Total suffering should be tied between the two plans, so that the utilitarian's preference has to be carried entirely by the pleasure side of the calculus. I want that story spelled out — which patients can sleep, eat, walk, and enjoy their recovery under which plan, and roughly how much positive experience each plan buys — because it is there that the commensurability of pleasure and pain does its work.

2. Plain aggregative negative utilitarianism (suffering as the only disvalue, summed across persons, no threshold) must be exactly indifferent between the plans, and the tie must fall out of the case's structure rather than being stipulated. If you use a finite medical resource, for instance, a linear dose–response relation with a fixed total quantity of relief makes the tie a matter of arithmetic; show the arithmetic.

3. Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism must favor the plan with the same total suffering but differently distributed, namely the one that keeps every individual below the severity threshold, over the plan that pushes a minority over it. Give the threshold a naturalistic motivation if you can — some clinically recognizable line above which pain crowds out sleep, appetite, and every positive experience — since that gloss is also the view's best argument for treating the threshold as lexical.

Third, after the scenario, demonstrate that each specification is met. State the verdict each theory gives and why; identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work (I suspect it is the placement of the threshold relative to the untreated and partially treated pain levels, together with the linearity assumption, but you should say so yourself); and run a sensitivity check: what happens to the three verdicts if (a) the majority's untreated suffering is nudged above the threshold, (b) the resource's efficacy becomes sharply non-linear, and (c) the above-threshold minority is made very small while their untreated suffering is made very large — the last being the classical reductio smuggled back in at the scale of an ordinary decision.

Finally, give each theory that comes out badly its best counterattack — the utilitarian on the arbitrariness of drawing a severity threshold at all, the plain negative utilitarian on the artificiality of the tie — and say whether the threshold theorist has a reply that amounts to more than restating the view. The case is for my seminar precisely because I want to know whether the threshold does genuine moral work in everyday distributive choice, or whether it only ever matters in contrived or apocalyptic cases; end by saying which way your case supports.
