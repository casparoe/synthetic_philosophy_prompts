I'm preparing a discussion handout for an applied ethics seminar on the question of whether there is a morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading someone, and I want a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey. Please write a "where do you actually stand on lying versus misleading?" quiz — eight to ten vignettes, each a concrete case with two or three answer options — together with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions in the debate.

The vignettes must be drawn from at least these kinds of situations (or close analogues): (a) the murderer at the door, answered with a technically true but misleading evasion; (b) an equivocation — replying with a sentence that has a strict reading on which it is true and a natural reading on which it is false; (c) paltering, i.e. answering a question with a true statement one knows is likely to be misread; (d) letting a false presupposition of a question stand uncorrected while answering only its other part; (e) technically true but misleading language in advertising, contracts, or legal discovery; (f) exploiting conversational implicature — saying something true because one knows the hearer will infer something false from it; (g) a cooperative case, such as a doctor giving a dying patient a falsely hopeful impression, contrasted within the same vignette with an out-and-out lie in the same circumstances; (h) misleading someone who arguably has no right to the truth — an aggressor, an intruder, an unjust regime's official.

The outcomes the key must include are at least the following: the strict definitionist view, on which lying requires asserting what one believes false and the wrong of lying is specifically a wrong against the hearer as a rational agent, so that truthful misleading, whatever else is wrong with it, is not that particular wrong; a Kantian view on which misleading in the relevant ways can itself wrong the hearer — for instance by manipulating their deliberation — even though the definitional distinction is preserved; the deception-parity view, on which the wrong-making property is intending to cause false belief, so lying and misleading are morally on a par wherever both are designed to do that; the checkability view, on which the moral weight of the distinction derives from what a hearer can hold a speaker to — assertions are inspectable and contestable in a way implicatures and omissions are not, which is what makes honest assertion a reliable social institution and lies a special kind of betrayal of it; and the pragmatic-hypothesis view, on which any moral difference is a defeasible heuristic that erodes under scale, competition, and institutionalization — once everyone exploits technically-true evasion, as in advertising or carefully drafted disclosures, the hearer-protective rationale collapses and misleading becomes as bad as lying.

Construction requirements. No question may ask directly whether the respondent thinks lying is worse than misleading; every question must be a case judgment someone can make without knowing any of the position labels. At least one vignette must be one on which the deception-parity view and the pragmatic-hypothesis view yield opposite verdicts, so the quiz can actually separate them — something about whether habitual, mutually anticipated exploitation of technically-true evasion changes the moral character of each instance. At least one vignette must separate the strict definitionist view from the checkability view — for example, a misleading utterance that is fully inspectable and checkable, or a lie whose content the hearer could never verify. Where the answer space contains a region corresponding to no standard position, say so and add a sentence on whether it is unoccupied because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of outcomes the quiz cannot reliably separate given the vignettes used, rather than papering over the overlap. The audience is upper-level undergraduates who have read Kant on the wrongfulness of lying but none of the secondary literature.
