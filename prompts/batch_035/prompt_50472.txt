Been reading LW and the adjacent blogs since like 2012, and theres a norm in that whole scene I've always half-accepted and never actually examined: if you state a numerical credence out loud — "I'm 70% on this" — you're supposed to be willing to put money on it at roughly those odds, and if you wriggle out of a bet at your own stated odds, that gets treated as evidence you didn't really mean the number. People do calibration training, keep prediction ledgers, and the gold standard for "actually believing it" has always been something like "would you take either side of the bet at your stated price." The justification, I take it, traces back to the Dutch book stuff: if your "prices" are inconsistent you can be milked, so willingness to trade at your stated credence is what makes the credence real instead of just talk.

But theres a couple of things about this I can't square, and I'm hoping someone can lay out what the norm actually presupposes.

First, the same people who push this norm are also huge fans of Kelly betting, which is basically advice to never bet your full edge — always keep a reserve, because going broke is unrecoverable. But a Kelly bettor will refuse bets that are positive expected value at the very odds they'd happily state as their credence, whenever the stakes are big relative to the bankroll. So does the "bet your credence or admit you don't beleive it" norm presuppose an agent who maximizes expected money, while the Kelly advice presupposes an agent who won't? Can both be the right norm at once, and if so at what point does one take over from the other — what decides whether a refusal is hypocrisy or just Kelly?

Second, and maybe deeper: what does the norm presuppose about the relationship between credence and willingness to bet in the first place? It seems to assume money is a linear measuring stick for belief (so 70% maps to accepting 7:3 on any stake), that refusing a bet can only mean your number was off — never that you just don't like gambling, or that the counterparty probably has private info, or that making your position public costs you something else you care about. Are those really assumptions the practice needs, or just stuff it happens to sit comfortably with? Like, how much of the norm survives if you allow agents with nonlinear utility for money — does the whole thing quietly become "state the credence you'd bet at for infinitesimal stakes," and if so, does refusing a large bet stop counting as evidence of insincerity?

So basically: reconstruct the implicit theory of rationality this community practice is running on, say whether it's actually coherent given the Kelly tension, and tell me what the norm would have to look like if that implicit theory were made explicit and consistent — I'm fine with the answer being "the folk version of the norm is just wrong and here's the corrected one," I just want to know which parts are load-bearing.
