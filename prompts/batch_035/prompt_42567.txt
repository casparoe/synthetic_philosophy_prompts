I want to practice attacking a serious defense of Zeno's paradox of the dichotomy, so I need a worthy opponent. Please play a contemporary philosopher of mathematics — call her nothing, just play her — who accepts all of standard analysis without reservation, can sum a convergent geometric series in her sleep, and yet holds that the dichotomy, in its proper form, states a genuine metaphysical problem that the mathematics neither poses nor settles. Her central claim, which she must never concede: the dichotomy is not the question of whether the runner arrives (he does, and she is happy to say so), but of how an infinity of sub-traversals is completed, and the fact that the intervals sum to a finite total answers a question about the length of the run while silently changing the subject away from its structure.

Make her sharp, not a mystic. She is forbidden from denying any mathematics, from appealing to quantum discreteness or finitist revisionism, and from pretending the runner fails to finish. Her resources are philosophical: she may distinguish the version of the dichotomy with no last subtask from the version with no first subtask, and she thinks the second — the regress that infects the very start of motion, not its completion — is the true form of the paradox and the one people quietly stop talking about. She may invoke Aristotle's distinction between potential and actual infinity, Russell's at-at theory of motion, and the lamp literature (Thomson's lamp and Benacerraf's point that a completed infinity of switchings leaves the final state underdetermined), but she must argue with these rather than name-drop them. If I accuse her of confusing a paradox with a queasy feeling, she must be able to say exactly what would count as a solution — and she should demand the same precision from me, since "we impose the subintervals from outside; nature contains no tasks" is itself a metaphysical thesis in need of support, not an obvious truth.

Procedural rules: stay in character until I write "stop"; one paragraph per reply so it reads as a live exchange; she may concede genuinely small points (for instance, that the Achilles as ordinarily posed really is settled by the series) but never the central claim; and each reply should end with a pointed question aimed at whatever I just said.

Here is my opening move. The intervals of the dichotomy form a geometric series with a finite sum, so the run simply takes, say, one minute. The supposed infinity of "tasks" is not in the runner's legs or anywhere in physics — it is an individuation we impose from outside, and nothing in nature answers to "reaching the midpoint of the midpoint." The runner does one thing, moving from A to B; we then carve his trajectory into infinitely many overlapping descriptions and act astonished that the pile is infinite. A paradox requires the world to contradict herself; here only our choice of description is strange, and we can simply decline the choice. The dichotomy falls to this, the Achilles falls to it faster, and I do not see what is left over for you to defend.
