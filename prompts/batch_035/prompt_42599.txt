I'm a third-year PhD student in ethics writing a chapter on whether moral considerations keep their valence fixed across contexts or can flip from reasons in favor to reasons against. My supervisor has a ritual I've come to depend on: at the end of every supervision she poses a hypothetical and issues a ruling. Three years in, I've logged enough rulings that I suspect there's a system, and I'd like to know whether I can treat her as a generalist or a particularist before I build my framework around her intuitions. Here is the log:

- Lying to the murderer at the door to protect the fugitive upstairs: permissible, perhaps required.
- Breaking a promise nobody else knows about, whose keeping benefits no one and costs you a weekend, where the promise was freely made and the promisee still cares: impermissible. She said the fact of the promise settles it.
- Telling a colleague, at her explicit insistence that only bluntness would get through, exactly how bad her manuscript is: required. When I pressed her on whether the harshness was a regrettable means to a good end, she said no — the harshness was part of what made it right here.
- Pushing the large man off the footbridge: impermissible. Diverting the trolley via the switch: permissible.
- A promise extracted by threat ("Promise you'll never move abroad, or I'll hurt myself"): void. She insisted it gives you no reason at all, not even an outweighed one.
- Keeping a promise that foreseeably causes moderate harm to a bystander: impermissible.

Next week she'll rule on three cases, and I'd like your prediction before I hear hers:

(a) You can perform one of two otherwise exactly similar actions. One gives a stranger a small pleasure — but only because he falsely believes it harms someone he despises; no one is actually harmed either way. Is the pleasure-giving action better, worse, or neither?
(b) A freely made deathbed promise, sincerely sought by the promisee, to visit her grave every year. She has now died; the visits are known to no one, benefit no one, and cost you something each time. Are you still required to go?
(c) A freely made promise to a living promisee who still cares about it, but whose circumstances have changed so that keeping it now distresses her — though she refuses to release you, because she thinks promises matter.

To my eye, both a Ross-style pluralism on which considerations have fixed valence and variable weight, and a Dancy-style holism on which their valence itself shifts with context, can accommodate all six logged rulings — though not at the same points. So what I want from you is: the candidate rules that fit her track record (including any I haven't considered); what each predicts for (a), (b), and (c); your own best guess at the rule she's actually running and why you prefer it; which of my six logged rulings is doing the most ambiguous work; and the single further hypothetical I could put to her that would best divide the remaining candidates.
