I teach a second-year module on collective decision-making — about sixty students who have had a term of introductory rational-choice material (preference orderings, expected utility) but no formal game theory. Next week I have a fifty-minute lecture on the rational-choice paradox of voting: the problem that the expected instrumental payoff of a single vote in a large election is so small relative to its cost that high turnout, rather than abstention, is what cries out for explanation.

Please don't give me the lecture itself; give me a plan for it, in British English. Specifically:

1. The order of the pieces, and why that order — which ideas must be in place before the paradox can be stated so that it lands as a paradox rather than as a truism about lazy people. I'd expect the expected-utility machinery to come in somewhere, but I want you to say where the empirical magnitude of the probability of being pivotal should sit relative to the normative sting: whether students should see the arithmetic before or after they've decided they already know the conclusion.

2. The one carrying example — a concrete election with actual numbers for electorate size, the cost of voting, and the benefit at stake, so students can watch the arithmetic swallow the stakes. Please say what the example must contain and what it must lack: in particular, it shouldn't be a small committee where being pivotal is genuinely plausible, since that lets students file the paradox as a matter of scale rather than of structure. I'd also like a variant worked in where the voter counts everyone's benefit rather than only her own, so students see that the conclusion is robust even to a hundredfold inflation of the stakes — but say whether you'd front-load that or hold it as a rebuttal absorber.

3. The misconceptions students will bring or form, in the order they'll actually surface, and where in the arc each should be headed off. The ones I can predict: (a) confusing this paradox with Condorcet's cycling paradox and with Arrow's theorem, which share the name across different literatures; (b) hearing the civic-duty term in the pB − C + D calculus as a knock-down refutation rather than as a promissory note that itself demands explanation; (c) the thought that if everyone reasoned like the abstaining voter, no one would vote, so the conclusion must be wrong — a smuggling-in of collective rationality that students won't notice themselves doing; and (d) the overcorrection that one vote literally never matters, even in electorates of two hundred. But there will be others I haven't predicted, and I'd like to know where in the sequence each belongs rather than getting a flat list.

4. What I should explicitly decline to cover at this level, and say so aloud to the students. I'm thinking of the minimax-regret response and of any serious treatment of mixed-strategy equilibria in turnout models, both of which I can name-drop without teaching — but tell me if something else belongs behind that line more urgently.

5. One check-for-understanding question for the tutorial sheet that would separate a student who has grasped the structure of the problem from one who has merely learned the slogan that voting is irrational — and a sentence on what a good answer to it looks like and what a memorised answer would say instead.
