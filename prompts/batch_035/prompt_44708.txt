So every month I run a little philosophy evening at our local community centre — about 14 people, mostly in their 30s-50s, no academics, just people who like arguing after work. Next session is 90 minutes and the topic I want to do is one I've actually been chewing on for ages after a trip to Japan, where I felt genuinely rude about things I didn't even know were things (but also fine jaywalking in a way that would've scandalised the locals). The question: when you're a visitor somewhere, which local conventions are you actually *obliged* to follow, and which are just... vibes? Like tipping in the US feels obligatory to me even though the whole wage structure it props up is rotten, but standing on the right side of the escalator feels optional, and I honestly can't articulate the difference. I want the group to *feel* that distinction rather than be told it, and I figure the only way to do that without everyone just pattern-matching to tipping vs queueing is to invent a place with invented conventions. So the activity should be set in a fictional country (call it whatever you like — let's say Velmara) where part of the group plays locals who get a sheet of local conventions with the local justifications on it, and the rest play visitors who get nothing except the situations. Invent maybe four or five conventions, and make them vary in kind: at least one that's basically pure coordination with no moral content that the locals have heavily moralised, at least one tipping-like one where following it props up a system that's quietly exploitative (the "listed price is only half what you pay" kind), one queue-like fairness one, and — this is the bit I really want — one convention that exists specifically to extract things from visitors, so the group runs into the question of whether "when in Rome" has a limit when Rome is set up to fleece you. Give the visitors real decisions with real stakes in points or tokens, some cost to complying each time, and let the locals choose whether to sanction non-compliance at some cost to themselves too. Then swap roles for a second round, ideally after the visitors think they've figured the place out.

For the debrief: I want it to map specific moments in the game onto the actual philosophical question rather than lecture at people — who came to feel obliged by a convention that was pure coordination, and what did the locals' willingness to sanction (or not) do to that? Whether anyone made the argument "I'll be gone in a week, the convention survives without me" and whether that cut any ice. What I need from you, though, is some honesty about what will go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing the visitors treat it as a puzzle-guessing game rather than a moral one, and the exploitative-convention round might make someone actually annoyed — tell me how to handle that), plus an honest note on which part of the real question the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself as facilitator.

One more thing: don't give me a wishy-washy debrief plan where every convention "raises interesting questions." Commit. In the debrief notes I want you to actually pick which of the Velmari conventions the visitors were genuinely morally required to follow, say which ones it was fine to blow off, and defend those calls — I've got my own tentative answers and I want something to argue with. And please write it all in British English, since that's what the group will expect (queueing with the full complement of e's and everything).
