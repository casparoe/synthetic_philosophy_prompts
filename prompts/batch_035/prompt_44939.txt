My mother has early-onset frontotemporal dementia, and I hold lasting power of attorney for her welfare. The care team keeps asking me to decide "what she would have wanted," and I have found myself unable to answer without a principled account of what that question even means. Her current stated wishes are erratic and sometimes alarming — she has become suspicious of her own sisters, whom she loved devotedly for seventy years. Her wishes before diagnosis were clear in some areas, self-contradictory in others, and silent in most. Somewhere in the course of trying to reason about this, I came across the idea of coherent extrapolated volition as originally proposed in the context of AI alignment: build a machine that acts not on our present instructions, but on what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were." That framing struck me as startlingly close to my problem, and I want to understand it properly rather than as a slogan.

Please explain the conceptual machinery of CEV and the broader family of idealised-preference theories. Specifically: why "coherent" — what role does the demand for consistency play, and is it a substantive assumption about what people would converge on, or merely a technical requirement for representing preferences at all? Why "extrapolated" — what is the difference between extrapolating a person's values from their present, conflicted state and simply substituting the surrogate's judgment dressed up as theirs? And why "volition" as opposed to mere preference — what is supposed to distinguish what someone wants from what they would endorse under better conditions? I am particularly interested in the standard objections to this framework: that extrapolation is radically underdetermined (different extrapolation procedures starting from the same person can yield incompatible results), that it smuggles in the extrapolator's values under the guise of discovering the person's, and that the output may be path-dependent in a way that makes talk of "her" extrapolated volition a fiction.

Then tell me honestly how much of this transfers from the intended use case — extrapolating the volition of humanity in aggregate, as a specification for a machine — to a single individual whose extrapolation I, a fallible and interested party, must carry out by hand. Are the aggregate and individual cases disanalogous in ways that break the framework? For instance, CEV was partly motivated by not wanting to freeze humanity's current mistakes into a permanent directive, whereas my mother's past is fixed and finite; and one person's incoherence cannot be resolved by the averaging or deliberative dynamics that might smooth out a population's. Finally, given all those caveats, set out how you would actually reason through a concrete welfare decision in my position: when her present expressed wish, her documented prior values, and your best guess at her extrapolated volition come apart, which should win, and what would justify privileging one source over the others? I would rather have an honest account of where the framework runs out than a comforting one that pretends the extrapolation writes itself.
