Quick background on where I'm coming from: I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training at all, but a few years ago I stumbled into the effective altruism corner of the internet and it got me thinking about wild animal suffering — the billions of animals in nature that starve, get eaten alive as juveniles, freeze, get parasitized, etc. If suffering matters at all, nature produces a staggering amount of it, and almost nobody even counts it.

Since then I keep running into two positions that both present themselves as the "rational, welfare-focused" take, and they point in exactly opposite directions on one concrete question: does destroying wild habitat (say, converting a forest into cattle pasture, or just paving it) reduce the total amount of suffering in the world?

Position A goes like this: most wild animals are r-strategists, meaning they have huge numbers of offspring and the vast majority of those offspring die very young — a fish lays thousands of eggs and most of them get eaten or starve within weeks, and that sort of thing is the norm accross invertebrates, amphibians, fish and so on. So the average wild animal life is plausibly strongly net negative, and if that's right, the fewer wild animals that ever exist, the better. From there it's a short step to: anything that shrinks wild animal populations, habitat destruction included, reduces suffering. Even if you feel gross saying it out loud.

Position B pushes back in two parts, and I want to understand how much of the disagreement is each part. Part one is factual: habitat destruction doesn't delete animals from existence, it swaps one community of animals for another. A pasture isn't an empty lot, it's loaded with earthworms, insects, mice, birds; if you count individuals, a hectare of grazed land might host as many or more animals than the hectare of forest it replaced, just arranged differently. So "fewer wild animals" isn't even the right accounting — the real question is whether the post-destruction community contains less suffering than the pre-destruction one, and nobody can sign that number with a straight face. Part two is more philosophical: even if you can't sign it, destroying habitat on the basis of a hunch seems to get something backwards — you're inflicting a certain, massive change in order to prevent a purely speculative harm, and the humble move is to leave the thing alone until you actually understand it.

So here's what I want from you. Compare these two positions properly, and in particular: first, where exactly does the disagreement live — is it about empirical facts (population densities, whether the average wild life is net negative), about how to act under deep uncertainty, or about something more like a value difference over whether the existence of animals or intact ecosystems is good in itself, independent of any felt experience? Second, which parts of each position could in principle be settled with better data, and which parts never will be? Third, your honest verdict: which position is on firmer ground overall, and does your answer change depending on whether the average wild animal life is slightly negative versus catastrophically negative?

Also please don't cop out with "more research is needed" as your entire conclusion, I'd like an actual verdict at the end.
