I should say up front that I have no background in philosophy; I've just spent the past year reading and thinking about one philosopher on my own, and I've hit a knot I can't untangle by myself. I'd like an essay that untangles it and comes down on one side.

The philosopher is A. J. Ayer. Everyone knows the caricature of his 1936 book: the young enfant terrible declares that statements about God and the immortal soul are not false but literally meaningless, because they can't be verified by any possible observation. What I didn't expect, reading around it, is that the book doesn't simply flush afterlife claims away. Ayer also argues that the self is not a soul-substance but a logical construction out of experiences; and once you put it that way, the question of surviving death looks like an ordinary empirical hypothesis — roughly, that experiences will continue to occur, linked to my present ones by memory and character. He even allows that a claim of that shape could in principle be verified: picture someone waking up in a new body reporting my memories in detail. So far as I can tell, his considered early view is that an afterlife is meaningful, just very unlikely, while the religious framing of it as the destiny of a disembodied soul is what's meaningless. I find that an odd combination, and I'd like the essay to examine whether the two halves really fit together: can you hold that the word survives picks out a genuine hypothesis while the traditional description of what would be surviving is nonsense, without the "hypothesis" quietly depending on the thing you ruled out?

Then there's the strange coda. In the last year of his life, Ayer's heart stopped for several minutes after he choked on food at a party, and he was revived. He afterwards wrote a short, startlingly candid piece about the episode. As I understand it, he admitted that the experience had somewhat weakened his conviction that death is the end — but rather than concede anything to survival, he floated the possibility that any apparently veridical perception during the episode could be explained by telepathy: his mind picking up the thoughts of the doctors and friends around him while his body was gone. That move fascinates me. On one reading it's the young man's method applied with perfect fidelity: keep everything empirical, weigh alternative hypotheses, and never let the conclusion outrun the evidence. On another reading it exposes something the method had been hiding all along — that "verifiable in principle" and "actually decidable" had come apart, and that a standard loose enough to admit telepathy as an equal-rival explanation is too loose to do the polemical work the verification principle was built for. If survival and telepathy both pass the test, it starts to look as though the test never ruled out an afterlife so much as rearranged which strange things we're permitted to believe.

So the controversy I want adjudicated is this: over the whole arc, was Ayer's treatment of life-after-death claims consistent — one position stated early and bravely held to the end, with the near-death episode handled exactly as his own principles required — or did his position quietly shift, and does the telepathy explanation mark the point where the shift shows? The essay should lay out the early view accurately, including the part about the self being constructed rather than substantial, since that's what makes survival empirical for him; assess whether the theological-vs-empirical distinction holds up; then evaluate the late piece against that standard. I don't want a survey that ends by praising both sides for their insights. Defend a verdict, even if it's a qualified one — for instance that the early distinction is stable but was never a good way to divide the meaningful from the meaningless, or that the concession at the end was larger than it looked and he disguised it with the telepathy move. And argue from the positions themselves rather than from anything you can't check; I care about whether the reasoning hangs together, not about what any particular commentator has said.
