I have no background in philosophy, law, or political theory; this comes from reading about constitutions on my own time and thinking it through, so I'd rather have the reasoning laid out plainly than be pointed at scholarship. The comparison I keep meeting is the one between a constitution and Odysseus lashing himself to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens' song without turning the ship toward the rocks. As far as I can tell, the comparison is not offered as decoration — it is doing real argumentative work. It is meant to show that a self-imposed restriction is not a loss of freedom but an exercise of it, and from there that constitutional limits on what a majority may do are legitimate precisely because they are self-imposed: the political community tied itself down while calm, and the ropes only bite when the singing starts.

The details of the story, as I understand them, matter to my question. Odysseus wants the song, so he plugs his crew's ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast, with standing instructions to tighten the ropes and pay him no attention if he screams to be untied. He gets the experience, the crew rows on, and once the danger has passed they release him and he goes back to giving orders as though nothing happened. Notice three things I suspect the usual comparison skips over. First, his desire is untouched: he screams to be released; the binding restrains his hands, not his will. Second, he had a choice of technologies — he could have used wax on himself and never heard the Sirens at all, and he chose the mast instead, which keeps the appetite and blocks the action. Third, the arrangement has a natural end: the ship passes the Sirens and he is untied, whereas a constitution claims no such expiry date.

So my request is an audit of the analogy. First, the correspondence table: what plays the role of the mast, the ropes, the wax, the crew, the standing instructions, the Sirens themselves, and the begging to be untied, in the constitutional setting the analogy is actually used for. Second, where the mapping breaks — and here I would like the disanalogies sorted into those that threaten the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support and those that are just friction. The ones that already worry me: the crew plugs its ears to the Sirens yet must still be able to hear the commander's ordinary orders, and I cannot tell who in a state is supposed to have that selective deafness and what keeps them loyal when the person who is supposed to be commanding them is the one screaming; the man doing the binding and the man begging to be freed are the same individual, while a founding generation binds majorities full of people who never agreed to anything; and the untethering at the end. Third, whether the conclusion — that constitutional precommitment is legitimate because it is self-imposed — survives once those gaps are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way. As a side question, I have read that the same story is invoked for medical directives in which a person instructs caregivers to disregard their future refusals of treatment, and that those instructions are sometimes overridden anyway on the ground that the person in crisis is no longer quite the one who gave them. If the myth really does support the constitutional conclusion, I would like to know why the same logic seems to wear so much thinner at the bedside — or whether it never fit either case. And if the mast is the wrong picture entirely, say what would serve better.
