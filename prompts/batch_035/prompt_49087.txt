I've spent a few years keeping track of my own snap judgments about risk, mostly around my house and my kids, and I've come to suspect they don't add up to any one rule — but maybe they do, and I'd like help finding it.

Here are my verdicts on cases, stated as strongly as I actually hold them. When a new synthetic pesticide replaces an older one we already know is fairly harmful to nearby streams, I'd rather keep spraying the old one, because at least its harms are known and the new one's aren't. When the county proposes adding fluoride to our water, something studied for decades, I voted against it — deliberately putting a substance into everyone's water still feels like the kind of action that deserves extra hesitation. When my basement test came back with elevated radon, a completely natural, unmanufactured substance, I paid for mitigation without a second thought. When I read that aged cheeses and naturally fermented foods contain compounds that have never been through a formal safety trial, I kept eating them happily, and I give my kids raw honey and unlicensed herbal teas for colds on the same reasoning: people have used them for a long time and they seem fine. When a new model of airliner entered service last year, I booked a flight on it without checking which model I'd be on. And when my doctor offered me a statin, a drug class that's been prescribed to millions for over thirty years, I hesitated for months over the side-effect pamphlet in a way I never hesitated over the fermented food.

My tentative principle has been something like: when an action deliberately introduces something man-made and novel into my body or my family's environment, and the possible harms aren't yet understood, don't do it until they are. But I can see this fails in at least two directions: it apparently exempts the herbal teas and the aged cheese (novel to me, untested, but somehow not counting), and it doesn't explain why the new airplane was fine while thirty-year-old statins gave me pause, since both are engineered and deliberate.

So here is what I want from you. State, precisely, one principle — or if you must, a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case forced you to add it. Then tell me what the principle says about three cases I haven't mentioned: eating lab-grown meat now being sold in supermarkets; taking a fast-tracked vaccine during an outbreak when the usual multi-year trials were shortened but the disease is spreading; and buying a house under a high-voltage transmission line. Finally, be blunt about which of my original verdicts the best principle cannot save — I would rather lose a verdict than carry a principle bent into nonsense to keep it.

End your reply with a single line in exactly this form: Principle: <one sentence, no longer than forty words> — or, if you conclude no single principle is worth having, the line: Principle: none.
