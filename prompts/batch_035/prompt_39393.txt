A colleague in the psychology department is writing a popular book on pain and has asked me to comment on a draft chapter. She summarizes the phenomenon of pain asymbolia roughly as follows: "Pain asymbolia is a condition in which patients no longer feel pain at all, though their pain receptors work fine. The condition shows that pain itself is not intrinsically bad — the badness is something we ordinarily attach to pain by a kind of learned aversion, which these patients have lost. They feel the tap of the pin, note that it should hurt, and simply don't care; some even laugh when threatened. What the condition really refutes is hedonism, since the hedonist is committed to the claim that every pain is bad for the person who feels it, and here we have people feeling pains that are not bad for them at all. It also suggests that the best analgesics of the future will be ones that mimic asymbolia — leaving the sensation intact while dissolving the suffering attached to it."

She is a careful scientist and not given to overstatement in her own field, but I suspect this passage does some philosophy badly, and I would like to be able to tell her precisely where before I return the draft. Please go claim by claim through the passage and tell me: what a pain researcher or philosopher of mind would accept as it stands; what conflates distinct conditions or distinct claims that the literature keeps apart; and where a defensible but modest thesis has been inflated into a sweeping one. In particular, I want to know whether the inference to the refutation of hedonism survives once the description of the condition is corrected, and what resources the hedonist has for accommodating cases like this — or whether the case instead forces a genuine revision in how we individuate pains. Please be explicit about which of your corrections turn on empirical facts about the condition and which turn on philosophical reconstruction, since I will need to flag that difference to her.
