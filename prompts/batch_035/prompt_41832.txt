I'm a PhD student in environmental economics, and a routine feature of my work keeps snagging on something nobody in my department treats as a question. When regulators appraise a climate policy, they compress all future damages from an added ton of CO2 into a single number — the social cost of carbon — and compare it against the cost of abatement. Producing that number requires three steps, each of which looks philosophically loaded. First, harms are monetized through willingness-to-pay, which means a death in a poor region counts for less than a death in a rich one, simply because the victims have less money. Second, damages are discounted: future harms count for less than present ones, at a rate assembled from a pure preference for the present, an aversion to consumption inequality across generations, and a projection of future growth. Third, the analysis is distribution-blind: it licenses a policy if the winners could in principle compensate the losers, even though in practice no compensation account is ever set up. And there is a wrinkle that puzzles me most: the very same model discounts future harms on the assumption that future people will be richer, and simultaneously warns of damages so deep that they would make future people much poorer — the optimism that shrinks the number and the catastrophe that inflates it sit inside it at once.

What does this practice presuppose? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed. What must it assume welfare is, such that it can be converted to money, summed across persons, and averaged over uncertain futures? Does the discounting step presuppose that future people count for less than we do, or is it something a view granting them full and equal moral status could still accept — say, as a reflection of risk, or of their anticipated greater wealth? Does the hypothetical-compensation test presuppose that the distribution of benefits and burdens has no intrinsic importance, or could a theorist who does care about equality still license the practice, on the ground that climate policy is simply the wrong instrument for pursuing it? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a degrowth theorist who denies that ecological losses are commensurable with money nevertheless sign off on the same number for entirely different reasons, or does the practice quietly rule out any view on which there are thresholds beyond which talk of cost stops making sense? Finally, say what would have to change if the presupposed welfarism were dropped — if future people were given strictly equal weight, and compensation had to be actual rather than hypothetical. What happens to the number, and what has to change in the institution of regulatory analysis that has grown up around it?
