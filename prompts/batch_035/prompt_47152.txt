My roommate and I got into a multi-hour argument last night about whether the language you speak actually shapes how you think, and I came out of it annoyed at both of us — he was insisting it's all or nothing, either your language totally determines your thoughts or it does basically nothing, and I kept saying "surely it's somewhere in between" without being able to say where. So instead of going in circles I wrote down my gut reactions to a bunch of specific cases, and now I want someone to tell me if there's actually a principle behind them, because honestly they look kind of inconsistent.

Here's where I land. Speakers of languages that use compass directions for everything instead of left and right ("your fork is to the northwest of the plate") apparently develop an almost spooky sense of orientation even indoors, and to me that's a genuine case of language rewiring thought. I also want to count Russian having two separate basic words for blue, one for the lighter and one for the darker shade, since Russian speakers seem to genuinely discriminate those shades faster — that feels like a real effect, not a party trick. But the whole "Eskimo words for snow" thing makes me roll my eyes: having a pile of synonyms just seems like vocabulary, not thinking, so I count that one out. Then it gets messy. German speakers describing a bridge (die Brücke, grammatically feminine) tend to call it elegant or slender, while Spanish speakers, whose word for bridge is masculine, lean toward strong or sturdy — but those effects mostly show up when people are doing something translation-ish, so I genuinely don't know if grammatical gender counts as a real effect or if it's just the language momentarily elbowing its way into the task. And the hardest one: the Pirahã in the Amazon, who have no exact number words, reportedly struggle with exact quantities above about three even in nonverbal matching tasks — but is the language holding them back, or is it just that their way of life never had any use for tracking exact amounts in the first place? I've flip-flopped on this like four times since dinner.

So what I'm asking for is this: is there one principle that sorts the cases I count in from the cases I count out — my current half-baked guess is something like "obligatory distinctions the grammar forces you to make every single time you speak" versus "optional vocabulary you can route around" — and if so, which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Does the Russian blue case actually fit under a "forced by grammar" rule, or is it lexical like the snow words, in which case I either have to drop it or stop dismissing the snow one? Where does that principle put the Pirahã counting thing and the grammatical gender stuff? And since I'm apparently unable to form an opinion on my own now: take the case of Mandarin, which doesn't mark grammatical tense the way English does — would my principle predict that Mandarin speakers conceptualize past and future differently, or would it stay silent there, and is staying silent the right move or a cop-out? I'd take a clear answer that overturns one of my verdicts over a wishy-washy one that keeps all of them tbh.
