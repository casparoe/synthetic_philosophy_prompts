In a graduate seminar on utilitarianism, the transplant case came up — one healthy traveler, five dying patients — and a classmate shut down the discussion by saying that harvesting the organs would violate the separateness of persons, a phrase he attributed to Rawls. He never said what the phrase meant, and when I pressed him afterward he gestured at the idea that people aren't mere containers of utility, which sounded more Kantian than Rawlsian. The phrase has since surfaced twice more in reading group: once against interpersonal aggregation generally, and once as part of an argument for egalitarian distribution. I suspect it is doing different work on each occasion, and I would like to be equipped to tell the occasions apart.

Please lay out, as an ordered list I can bring back to seminar, the distinct precise theses that the separateness of persons could be expressing, arranged from the most trivial to the most radical. I have a rough sense that the menu should include at least the following candidates, but I expect you to sharpen, add to, or prune this list: (1) a merely descriptive or phenomenological claim about how suffering and benefit are in fact experienced — that each person bears her own pains and pleasures in a way no one bears anyone else's, which by itself seems consistent with summing them; (2) an epistemic or justificatory claim in the spirit of contractualism, that a moral principle must be justifiable to each person from her own standpoint, not merely endorsed from an impartial spectator's view; (3) a constraint on interpersonal aggregation, under which the totals may not be compared across persons at all or may be compared only in restricted ways; (4) a deontological side-constraint claim in roughly Nozick's sense, on which some things may not be done to a person regardless of the aggregate outcome; and (5) a distributional claim, that the pattern of a distribution matters and not merely its total, which even many non-utilitarians share with one another but which need not forbid aggregation outright.

For each reading, tell me: whether it is plausible on its own; whether utilitarianism can accept it without giving anything up (that is, whether the objection is empty on that reading); and whether it actually delivers my classmate's verdict in the transplant case. I am especially interested in whether the readings come apart there — for instance, whether a justificatory reading would condemn the harvesting on grounds that also condemn much milder interpersonal trade-offs, or whether an aggregation-forbidding reading would commit him to the view that numbers never count in rescue cases, the position associated with John Taurek's 1977 paper Should the Numbers Count?, and whether he would accept that result if he saw it coming.

Two further things I want included. First, say something about which reading Rawls most plausibly had in mind in A Theory of Justice, where the complaint appears bound up with his comparison between utilitarianism and a single individual balancing present against future gains and losses — in particular, whether his target is aggregation itself or the justification of aggregative principles, and whether the famous complaint that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons is best read as a claim about how principles must be justified to each person rather than about what may be summed. Second, examine whether the objection, on its strongest reading, is too strong for its own friends: does a prohibition on interpersonal aggregation also threaten prioritarianism, or Rawls's own difference principle, which after all compares the life prospects of some groups with those of others? I want to know not just what the slogan can mean, but which meanings have teeth, whose positions they bite, and which one my classmate would have to be invoking for his appeal to it in the transplant case to have been more than a rhetorical flourish.
