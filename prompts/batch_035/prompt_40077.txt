quick bit of context: i'm a retired climate scientist (30+ years building and running earth system models) and now that I'm not chasing grant deadlines I've fallen deep into philosophy of science, which I should have done decades ago. just finished Popper's The Poverty of Historicism and I keep thinking about how my whole career I was surrounded by people making claims about the future, and we had fairly clear internal standards for when a forecast was worth anything. so heres what I want: write me the list of requirements a genuine law of history would have to satisfy before I'd take it as a law rather than a lucky read of a trend.

the angle I'd bring from my own field, feel free to push back on it: in climate we never claimed unconditional laws about the future, we gave scenarios conditional on emissions, and everyone understood the system being forecast contains people who read the forecasts and change their behavior (tell the world sea level is rising and emissions policy shifts, which shifts the sea level). Popper apparently had a name for this, the Oedipus effect, the prediction influencing the event predicted, and I want a requirement built around it: if publishing the prediction changes whether it comes true, in what sense was it ever a law? a prophecy that comes true because people believed it shouldn't count as confirming evidence. though, counterpoint, our conditional forecasts never pretended to be laws, so maybe the requirement needs to be drawn more carefully than I can draw it.

also Popper's big one: you can't predict future knowledge growth, so you can't predict the technology history runs on, so you can't predict history. I want that on the list too, but I want you to actually evaluate whether it's a genuine requirement or just Popper's own prejudice -- a defender of historical laws could reply that their laws abstract over technology (populations grow, states consolidate, empires overextend, whatever the mechanism is supposed to be).

beyond those two, I'll let you decide what else belongs on it. you know the obvious candidates -- specificity (a law compatible with every outcome isn't one), retrodiction vs novel prediction, single unique run vs repeatable process, that kind of thing -- but I want you to sort out which are real requirements and which are just what people in my lab would have smuggled in as common sense. for each requirement tell me the case or consideration behind it and mark it negotiable or non-negotiable, with your reasoning for the marking.

the bit I most want is the last one: tell me if any subset of the requirements can't be satisfied together. I suspect the reflexivity one and the precision one fight each other somehow but I can't quite see the shape of it. then finish with a quick pass at how (a) the Marx-style inexorable laws of historical destiny and (b) a weaselly trends-will-continue-until-conditions-change minimalism do against your list. no citations or reading lists please, I'll chase those down myself, I just want the thinking.
