I'm taking a phil of language course this semester, and we hit Moore's paradox a couple weeks ago. Since then I've been keeping a list of my own gut reactions to sentences in that neighborhood, trying to see if there's actually a pattern. The plain Moore sentence, "It's raining but I don't believe it" — obviously crazy, nobody could sincerely say that. The flipped version, "It's not raining but I believe it is" — also unsayable, but it feels like a different kind of unsayable, more like a flat contradiction than like madness. And that difference bugs me. Past tense: "It was raining all week but I didn't believe it" — completely fine, I'm just reporting that I was in denial about the weather. Third person: "It's raining but John doesn't believe it" — fine, that's just John being an idiot. Embedded: "If it's raining and I don't believe it, then we're getting soaked" — also fine, I can suppose that state of affairs without any weirdness. Then a couple I'm genuinely unsure about. "It's raining but I don't know it" sounds almost as bad as the belief one, though for some reason nobody ever mentions that version. And "It's raining but I don't believe that I believe it" sounds strange but not crazy the way the first one does — like I can just barely imagine a person for whom that's true and who might say it.

One more wrinkle: in class I can say the forbidden sentence out loud while explaining the paradox ("Consider: it's raining but I don't believe it") and it's perfectly fine, which I gather is something like mentioning rather than using, but it feels like there's more going on than quoting — when I say it I'm somehow displaying the badness instead of committing it?

So here's my question. Is there one principle that predicts exactly which of these can be felicitously asserted, or supposed, or whatever the right verb is, and which can't? The obvious candidate I keep hearing from the literature is something like: asserting p expresses belief in p, so you can't assert something that conveys belief and lack of belief in the same proposition at the same time. But I can't tell whether that handles the knowledge version, the "don't believe that I believe" version, the in-class display case, or the omissive/commissive asymmetry, or whether it needs patching with clause after clause. And if it turns into a mess, is some other principle better — consistency of the speaker's overall belief set, or something about the purpose of assertion, or what the sentence implies about the speaker rather than about the world? If the best principle can't save all my verdicts, tell me honestly which one I should give up — I suspect the "don't believe that I believe" one is the liar in my list, but I'd like to know for real, not just be agreed with. And I'd like to hear what the principle you settle on predicts for a case I haven't listed, say the disjunction "Either it's raining or I don't believe it," or someone saying the Moore sentence while sleep-deprived and sincerely confused about their own mind.
