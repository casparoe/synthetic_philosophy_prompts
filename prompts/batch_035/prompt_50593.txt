I'm a second-year graduate student currently taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and I'm trying to decide whether to commit to epistemic utility theory — the accuracy-first research program that grounds norms of credence in the idea that credences should be as close as possible to the truth values of propositions — as the focus of my dissertation work. My background is in mathematics rather than programming, and my realistic budget is one term paper now and, if I go this direction, roughly three years of dissertation work. I'd like your help constructing a defensible agenda, not a survey.

To make this concrete, here are the open questions I've gathered from the seminar and my own reading; I'm confident the list is incomplete, so please tell me what's missing. First, the framework's vindications of probabilism (in the tradition of Jim Joyce's accuracy arguments) and its vindications of diachronic norms like conditionalization (in the tradition of Hilary Greaves and David Wallace's decision-theoretic argument) seem to rest on different premises — the diachronic arguments appear to require stronger assumptions about the agent's foreknowledge of what evidence she will receive. Second, there is the question of the additivity of epistemic value: Richard Pettigrew's version of the program scores each proposition separately and sums, and it is not obvious which norms survive if epistemic value is allowed to be global rather than a sum of per-proposition scores. Third, the framework seems to presuppose that every proposition has a determinate truth value and that credences are precise real numbers, which generates trouble for credences in necessarily false propositions, for self-locating beliefs whose accuracy depends on more than which world is actual, and for proponents of imprecise credences. Fourth, there is the cluster of objections alleging that epistemic utility theory is a form of epistemic consequentialism and inherits consequentialism's problems: cases where trading off accuracy across propositions appears to license epistemic akrasia or belief against one's evidence. Fifth, there is the question of whether the framework extends beyond the ideal individual agent — to groups, to peer disagreement, or to suspension as a third attitude alongside credence.

Given all that: which of these questions (or ones I have not listed) should someone with my training and time budget actually prioritize, and in what order? I care less about the list than about the reasoning behind it. For each item you prioritize, I'd like to know why it is upstream of the others — which questions quietly presuppose its answer — whether it is tractable with the tools of philosophy and mathematics rather than empirical work, and what would have to happen over the next few years for it to move up or down your ranking. Please also say which of my five candidates are overrated in the sense that settling them would not change much downstream, and flag any question that you think is not really open at all but instead a dispute about how to define the framework's terms. I'd rather discover that now than in year two.
