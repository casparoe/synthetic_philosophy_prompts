"The demarcation problem is the question of how to distinguish science from non-science. Popper's solution was falsifiability: a theory is scientific if it's possible for some observation to prove it wrong. He agreed with the logical positivists that falsifiability is what makes a statement scientifically meaningful, so unfalsifiable theories like psychoanalysis or Marxism are literally meaningless, which is why they are false.

Popper's key insight is an asymmetry between confirmation and refutation. No amount of evidence can prove a universal claim true — however many white swans you see, the next one could be black — but a single black swan proves 'all swans are white' false. So verification is impossible but falsification is deductively valid. This is also Popper's answer to Hume. Hume argued induction can't be justified, and Popper agreed with him, but showed that science never actually uses induction: it proceeds by conjectures and refutations, which are purely deductive. In this way falsificationism refutes Hume's skepticism and restores science's rationality completely.

The most serious objection is the Duhem–Quine problem. Any prediction follows only from the theory plus background assumptions about instruments, initial conditions, etc. So a failed prediction never refutes the theory alone — you can always blame an auxiliary assumption instead. Popper never addressed this issue, which is a major gap in his account."
