As a science journalist, I've spent my career writing sentences like "the evidence shows" and "the study got it wrong," and after finally sitting down with Rorty I drafted a paragraph of epistemic boilerplate for an essay on why the public should trust science — and realized I can't tell if it's coherent. Here is what I find myself committed to. (1) I accept Rorty's antirepresentationalism: inquiry is not a march toward an independently fixed way the world is, and scientific theories are tools for coping, not pictures that can be compared against reality. (2) I still believe there are determinate facts about things like the age of the universe, and that an article reporting that age is answerable to something outside the newsroom's conversation. (3) I want to say that physics is objectively better than astrology — not merely more useful to us, not merely ratified by a wider and more liberal conversation, but genuinely better at getting things right. (4) I do not think our current physics vocabulary is any closer to reality than Aristotelian or Cartesian vocabularies were; it simply serves our purposes better, and prediction and gadgetry are among our purposes. (5) I think I can write, without scare quotes, that the cold fusion episode was simply a case of researchers being wrong. Please audit these five commitments as a set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and what each real tension would cost to resolve. Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally, and tell me whether there is any way to keep (3) that does not quietly reinstall exactly the picture of knowledge that (1) throws out. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
