Hi there! So I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, low temperature stuff, nothing glamorous) who got into philosophy of science after retiring — long story, a lot of coffee and philosophy podcasts — and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on theories of causation. Maybe you can help me dig out.

Here's my amateur understanding of the two big views. On the counterfactual theory (Lewis's, roughly), A causes B when, had A not occurred, B would not have occurred — we figure out the latter by looking at the "closest" world where A doesn't happen. On the interventionist view (Woodward, Pearl), A causes B when there's some way of wiggling A that changes B, where the wiggling has to be a proper intervention — setting A in a way that doesn't also mess with other variables through side channels.

Now please compare these two theories, but here's the angle I actually care about, and it comes straight out of my physics days.

In physics we deal constantly with systems where the variables just aren't independantly settable. Take an ideal gas: pressure, volume, and temperature are tied together by the equation of state, so you literally cannot "turn up the pressure" while holding volume and temperature fixed — it's not a knob, it's a surface you're sliding along. Or take a pendulum with a rigid rod: you can't reposition the bob however you like, the constraint eats your intervention. Even more basically, conservation laws mean that in a closed system you can't change total energy at all, only shuffle it around. To me it looks like interventionism has a real problem here, because an intervention on variables like these is ill-defined — no knob, no intervention, no causation? But then I suspect the counterfactual theory isn't any better off: asking "what would've happened had the pressure been higher, all else equal?" seems just as meaningless, for the same reason. There's no closest world where the pressure is higher and everything else stays put.

So my actual questions: which of the two theories handles constrained or non-independent variables better? Is this a recognized problem with a recognized answer, and if so can you sketch it? Or is my framing off — maybe the interventionist just says re-describe the system using variables you CAN set (compress the piston, light the burner), and the "constraint problem" dissolves into a bookkeeping choice about which variables to include? And is there some third option I'm missing entirely?

I'd genuinely like your best take rather than a wishy-washy "both have strengths and weaknesses" — if one view handles this clearly better, say so and tell me why. And please push back if you think my whole setup is confused or rests on a caricature of either theory — I'm an old physicist mangling philosophy here, and I'd much rather be corrected than agreed with politely.
