I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind at a department that has spent the better part of a decade arguing about heterophenomenology, and the argument has become so ritualised that people now talk past each other in fully predictable ways. On one side are colleagues in the Dennettian camp, who take heterophenomenology to be simply what any respectable science of consciousness must do: treat subjects' first-person reports as data about what the subject believes or seems to experience, bracket the question of whether the reports are veridical, and let third-person constraints from psychology and neuroscience eventually resolve or dissolve the remaining mysteries. On the other side are colleagues sympathetic to neurophenomenology and the phenomenological tradition, who charge that this method quietly begs the central question: by reclassifying first-person reports as evidence about beliefs rather than about experience itself, it builds an answer into the methodology before any data arrive, and thereby makes the distinctive phenomenal character of consciousness permanently invisible to science.

The two groups have agreed, somewhat exhaustedly, to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been tasked with drafting the joint statement. The statement should record, first, the claims both camps can genuinely sign — about what heterophenomenology actually commits one to (the interpretation of subjects' reports as texts, the suspension of verdicts on their accuracy, the eventual search for third-person explanations), and about what the opposition's complaint actually is when stated in its most defensible form. It should then state the residual disagreements with precision, each phrased in wording the relevant side would itself accept, and identify the cruxes — I suspect one of them is whether the heterophenomenological bracketing is a neutral methodological move or a substantive metaphysical concession, and another is whether disciplined first-person methods (of the sort trained meditators or phenomenologists claim to practise) could ever yield the kind of sharable, testable data the Dennettians demand. For each crux, please include a concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the dispute strikes you as merely verbal — e.g. two camps meaning different things by "data" or "experience" — please say which part and why.

Format the statement as two numbered lists and nothing else: exactly five agreed claims, each a single sentence, followed by exactly five disagreement entries, each a sentence or two and phrased neutrally enough that the side it describes would endorse the wording. No introduction, no conclusion, and no recommendations about who is right.
