I am a professor of philosophy at a British university, where I have taught decision theory for some years. For the past three years I have also sat on the steering group of our local hospital trust's shared decision-making programme, which produces patient decision aids for one-off, irreversible treatment choices: prostatectomy versus radiotherapy versus active surveillance for localised prostate cancer, open repair versus watchful waiting for a small abdominal aortic aneurysm, and similar dilemmas. Nurse facilitators, who have no statistical training, run three or four forty-five-minute sessions per week in which they elicit a patient's risk preferences and feed them into the aid. Every alteration to their session scripts must be signed off by the clinical lead, a surgeon with little patience for theoretical hedging, and anything we write for the facilitators must fit on two sides of A4 in plain English.

The problem is this. In our pilot sessions, a substantial minority of patients display precisely the pattern Maurice Allais put to Leonard Savage at the Paris colloquium on risk in 1952: they prefer a certain moderate outcome to a gamble mixing a large gain with a small chance of nothing, yet reverse that preference when the common consequence — the outcome shared by both options — is stripped away. Allais regarded the pattern as a datum about rational preference that any adequate theory must accommodate; Savage, on discovering that his own responses violated what would become his sure-thing principle, treated them as errors to be corrected on reflection. My facilitators confront the same fork every week, with forty-five minutes and considerably higher stakes than either man allowed himself.

I would like you to write the procedure I can put before the steering group. It should give the facilitators an ordered set of questions to run when an elicitation has produced an Allais-type pattern, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a decision, and where it hands the matter to a named person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. It must cover, at minimum: how to elicit without manufacturing the pattern in the first place (ordering of questions, warm-up lotteries, the difference between framing outcomes as survival rates and as mortality rates); a test for whether the violation is stable and considered or an artefact of presentation; a step in which the facilitator shows the patient the cancellation of the common consequence, in terms a layperson can follow, without browbeating her; and what to do when the patient, having understood all of that, nonetheless holds the pattern. On that last point I want honesty about the money-pump argument, which cannot even get started in a one-off medical choice, and about whether anticipated regret and disappointment are preferences the aid ought to respect or noise it ought to edit away.

Please then run the procedure on two worked cases: the prostate choice described above, and the aneurysm choice, at least one of which should end in a conclusion I will find unwelcome. Close with a statement of what the procedure cannot settle — including whether decision aids should be in the business of purifying preferences at all — and mark plainly the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated. Write the whole answer in British English, at the level of formality appropriate to a paper circulated to a steering group that contains both a surgeon and a philosopher.
