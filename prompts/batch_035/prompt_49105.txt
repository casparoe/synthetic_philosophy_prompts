I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, cramming for comps, and I picked classical Chinese philosophy for my non-Western requirement partly because it sounded fun and partly to avoid another semester of Grice. Anyway, I've been going through the White Horse dialogue (Gongsun Long's "a white horse is not a horse" thing, the School of Names crowd) and I keep landing on the same thought, and I want to sanity check it before it goes anywhere near my comps paper. The thought is: classical Chinese doesn't have a copula, right? There's no word for "is." So the original sentence isn't really an identity claim, or maybe isn't even a proposition in the way "a white horse is not a horse" is in English — the translation manufactures the paradox by inserting an "is" that isn't there in the original. Which would mean the whole two thousand years of commentary is arguing about an artifact of translation, and Gongsun Long never actually asserted anything false (or true), he just put two noun phrases next to each other and everyone since has projected Western logic onto it. There's also that thing I half remember, maybe from Hansen, about classical Chinese nouns being mass-noun-like — so "horse" works more like a stuff-word, like "water," than a count noun — which I figure supports the same conclusion, since you can't really predicate one stuff of another, so there's no fact of the matter either way.

So my question, I guess: given all that, is the standard move in the literature just to dissolve the paradox at the level of translation? Like, is the respectable position that the dialogue is a linguistic curiosity and not a genuine semantic argument? If so that's honestly kind of a relief, because the moves Gongsun Long makes inside the dialogue — the stuff about how if you ask for a horse, a yellow or black one is acceptable, but if you ask for a white horse they're not — look like real arguments to me and I don't really know what to do with them. Maybe I'm just missing something dumb about how classical Chinese does predication without a copula, I honestly wouldn't know.

Also, if the copula point does all the work I think it does, why do translators keep writing "is not"? Wouldn't something like "white horse: not horse" be more honest, and wouldn't the paradox just evaporate on the page? I'm half expecting to find out I've talked myself into a position some famous person already refuted in 1965, so go easy on me.
