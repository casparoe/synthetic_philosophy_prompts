I've been following the decision-theory-adjacent discussions on the blogs for a while, and the concept that has stuck with me most is the safe Pareto improvement, the idea that agents who are uncertain about each other's payoffs, and even about whether the other is rational, can sometimes commit in advance to a fallback policy such that, whatever the underlying game turns out to be, every possible type of every player does at least as well as they would have under the default — a Pareto improvement that is safe precisely because it holds across all the candidate games the agents think they might be in, not just the most likely one. The toy examples in the literature are simple two-by-two games, and I keep wondering how the idea fares against a real episode.

Consider the 1972 Incidents at Sea Agreement between the United States and the Soviet Union. Through the Cold War's early decades, American and Soviet warships and aircraft shadowed, buzzed, cut across, and harassed each other in frequent close encounters — intelligence trawlers trailed carrier groups, fighters made mock attack runs, destroyers performed dangerous maneuvers. There were collisions and deaths, and persistent worry that an accident could escalate into a crisis neither government wanted. The agreement, concluded at the 1972 Moscow summit, committed both navies to rules of the road: maintain prescribed distances, refrain from simulated attacks and maneuvers that endanger the other side, use agreed signals, and meet periodically to review implementation. There was no external enforcement, just reciprocal compliance and reputation, and the dangerous incidents reportedly fell off substantially.

My question: is this agreement well-modeled as a safe Pareto improvement? Map the thing carefully — who the agents are (the two states? the individual captains on the spot?), what uncertainty there was about the game (was harassment a best response for some plausible types, e.g., hawks who valued demonstration and testing, or did everyone lose from it and the problem was really a coordination failure?), and what the "commitment to a fallback policy" corresponds to in a treaty that neither side could actually enforce. Then tell me honestly where the SPI framework is strained: the agreement seems safe for all the types anyone could have taken seriously in 1972, but is that safety an artifact of the framework's abstraction from who drafts, monitors, and defects? And does the episode show something the formalism has not captured — that the binding force came from the repeated interaction and professional naval culture, not from the Pareto-safety of the arrangement itself — or, conversely, that SPI theory explains something ordinary game theory obscures about why such rules were easy to agree on despite deep mutual mistrust? I'd like an analysis that runs in both directions, theory on case and case on theory, and that says plainly where you would rather use a different model.
