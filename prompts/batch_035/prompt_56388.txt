I'm a novelist (four books, mostly domestic realism, if it matters) and I've been asked to contribute a short essay to a collection on why readers cry over people who don't exist. I've got a rough draft below and I'd love comments before I send it in. My editor is great with prose but hopeless with the philosophy, and honestly so am I.

Here's the draft:

Everyone in my family cried when we finished the last chapter of the book I'm reading aloud to my kids, and we all knew the character wasn't real. The standard puzzle is supposed to be that this is somehow irrational: emotions require believing your object exists, we don't believe fictional characters exist, so either we don't really feel anything or we're being irrational. I think the whole puzzle is framed wrong. My claim is that fictional characters are real -- real as artifacts. My desk is real, a promise is real, a game of chess is real, and a character is made the same way a promise is made: somebody performed an act of a certain kind, and now there's this thing. When readers grieve a character, they're grieving an artifact, which is exactly as strange as grieving a destroyed letter from a dead parent, which nobody thinks needs an excuse. The tears come from somewhere real, so the object must be real too. I'd go further: I don't think anyone ever actually believed fictional characters exist in the way they believe their neighbor exists, so the "paradox" rests on a confusion between two kinds of existence that we've always been able to tell apart, like mistaking a map for the road -- philosophers just enjoy pretending otherwise.

Things I'd want from you: (1) general comments on whether the argument is any good or just a stack of analogies; (2) where my draft states something as settled that philosophers actually still fight about -- I'm a total tourist here and I don't want to present a contested view as the consensus view without knowing it's contested; (3) whether the "real as artifacts" idea is actually my idea or whether I half-remembered it from somewhere and mangled it (very possible, I read alot). Please keep it in American English -- the collection is US-based.
