I donated a kidney back in March (living donor, to a stranger through the paired exchange program, if that matters), and ever since I keep getting this weird reaction where people call it heroic or "above and beyond," including the transplant center's own social worker who described it as something nobody is ever expected to do. Here's my problem: I'm also a philosophy MA student and I TA intro ethics, and when we hit the Singer-style drowning-child material, most of my students happily accept that you're obligated to make real sacrifices to prevent a stranger's death when you can do it at modest cost. By that logic my donation looks obligatory, full stop, and the "hero" talk is doing something other than tracking my duty. But then again it *doesn't* feel like I failed a duty in the years I didn't donate, and calling me a hero also seems to miss something — so maybe my students' intuition is wrong, or maybe supererogation and obligation aren't mutually exclusive the way everyone assumes, or maybe I'm secretly terrible? I genuinely can't tell.

And there's a second thing gnawing at me that I think might actually be the more interesting one. It seems like the *very same act* migrates between heroic and baseline depending on what everyone around you is doing. Donating blood in a city where hardly anyone does it reads as saintly; doing the identical thing during a heavily publicized crisis drive just reads as normal civic behavior. If someone invented a way to get basically everyone to register as a living donor, I suspect my donation would stop being described as heroic at all — nothing about the act or my reasons changed, only the distribution of other people's behavior. So my half-formed suspicion is that supererogation isn't a property of acts, and maybe not even of acts-relative-to-an-agent's-threshold, but something contrastive, the way "tall" is: a status that depends on the crowd you're standing in. If that's right, a bunch of the standard framing (the act is optional-but-praiseworthy in itself, the agent goes "beyond duty" in a fixed, non-relative sense) is off, and maybe there's a mirror-image story about the permissible-but-blameworthy category that some people call suberogation, where an act is only "bad" against what others routinely refrain from doing.
