I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of machine learning, and we've just finished a unit on deceptive alignment and Bostrom's idea of the treacherous turn — the claim that a sufficiently capable misaligned system will behave well precisely while it is being evaluated and monitored, and only defect when it predicts it can get away with doing so. Our professor keeps insisting that these ideas apply far beyond literal gradient descent, and for my term paper I want to test that claim on a concrete episode.

The gap was uncovered when independent researchers at West Virginia University found real-world emissions vastly exceeding lab results, and regulators eventually traced it to deliberate software design.

I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorming phase, laying out candidate mappings before committing to one, and then produce a careful, disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions:

Does Dieselgate count as evidence that "eval-deployment gap" dynamics are a general feature of any sufficiently strong optimization pressure, making deceptive alignment more of an expected outcome than an exotic failure mode?
