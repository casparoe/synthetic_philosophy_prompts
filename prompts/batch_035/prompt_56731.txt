I'm a science and ideas journalist currently at work on a long-form piece about what the neuroscience of decision-making does and doesn't threaten in our ordinary practices of blame and punishment. The piece keeps circling back to a framing I've found compelling but that I suspect is doing more work than its proponents admit: the idea that free will isn't one thing whose absence neuroscience could establish in a single stroke, but a cluster of capacities — responsiveness to reasons, sensitivity to sanctions and incentives, enough self-control to be an intelligible target of advice — and that the right question isn't whether we have some exotic, ultimate form of authorship but whether the varieties we actually want are ones ordinary adult humans typically possess. On that framing, the answer is supposed to be yes, and determinism is simply beside the point. My credence in the full package — that this is the right way to carve up the question, that the compatibilist variety is the one worth wanting, and that we generally have it — sits around 0.65. The neuroscientist I'm profiling, who takes a much harder line, puts his credence near 0.15 and accuses the whole approach of quietly changing the subject.

I'd like your own number on the same proposition, but the number matters less to me than the derivation, since I need to know where my disagreement with my source actually lives before I structure the piece around it. Please decompose the credence into at least these terms, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification: first, the probability that treating free will as a cluster concept picked out by what we want it for is a legitimate move rather than a smuggling operation — whether anything genuinely metaphysical survives being filtered through a worth-wanting criterion, or whether that criterion already presupposes the compatibilist answer. Second, the probability that the responsibility-relevant difference between an ordinary determined agent and one whose decisions are covertly engineered by another party — the sort of staged manipulation case that Pereboom's four-case argument relies on — is a real principled difference and not just a difference of narrative, since if manipulation generalizes all the way to ordinary causation, the compatibilist variety starts to look worth wanting in a diminished way. Third, the probability that moral desert, as opposed to forward-looking practices of influence and protection, can survive without any genuine alternative possibilities given a fixed past and fixed laws. Fourth, the probability that the capacities the account requires are distributed widely and robustly enough among actual humans — including impulsive, addicted, traumatized, and developing humans — for the claim that we typically possess the free will worth wanting to be anything more than a claim about a well-functioning elite.

Then give me the combination, tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and say which of those terms my neuroscientist source at 0.15 and I at 0.65 most likely diverge on — my suspicion is that his number bundles in a conviction about desert that mine doesn't, but I want your read rather than a flattering one. Finally, identify the argument or piece of evidence that would move your number most, and be candid if any term in your decomposition is so under-determined by anything we could actually learn that assigning it a probability is closer to setting a mood than making an estimate.
