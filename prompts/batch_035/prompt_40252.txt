I am a recently retired theoretical chemist. For the better part of four decades I computed approximate wavefunctions for small molecules — first by Hartree–Fock methods, later by configuration interaction and coupled-cluster schemes — and checked the predictions against spectroscopists' measurements. In retirement I have taken up philosophy of physics, and I find myself gripped by the question of whether the wavefunction is a real physical thing or merely a representation of our knowledge. The literature I have encountered, however, is conducted at a level of idealisation rather far removed from anything I ever did: the states under debate are exact states of perfectly isolated systems, whereas every wavefunction I ever handled was knowingly inexact, and was one of many equally legitimate descriptions depending on my choice of basis set.

I should be grateful for an essay, of roughly a thousand words, on whether the wavefunction is real — weighing the psi-ontic view against the psi-epistemic view and reaching a definite verdict rather than ending on a non-committal note. But please do not treat the question in the abstract alone. I want the essay to engage seriously with two complications drawn from my working life, which I set out here so that they need not be familiar to you in advance:

1. Approximate wavefunctions. Suppose the quantum state is ontic. Every wavefunction I ever computed was an approximation whose error I could estimate but never eliminate. What, then, is the ontological status of a knowingly inexact approximation to something real? One might reply that the same question arises for classical orbit calculations; but it strikes me that something further can be said. As my colleagues and I climbed the ladder of increasingly accurate methods, certain features of our wavefunctions survived every rung — the nodal structure, the symmetry adaptations, the exchange antisymmetry — while other features (orbital energies, say) shifted about with each improvement. Does this suggest that one could be a realist about only some structural features of the wavefunction, in the way philosophers apparently speak of structural realism about theories? If so, is that position coherent, or does it collapse into either full-blooded psi-ontology or something usefully close to the epistemic view?

2. The difference that made no difference to my notebooks. In all my years of practice, I cannot identify a single entry in my laboratory notebooks that would have been written differently had the wavefunction been a piece of the world's furniture rather than a very powerful bookkeeping device. On its face this might seem to favour the epistemicist, or to make the whole question idle. But I suspect both reactions are too quick, and I should like the essay to say why — or, if you think one of them is right, to defend that conclusion against the obvious objections.

A few further requests. Please give some attention to the asymmetry of terrain: my work concerned pure states of small, well-isolated molecules, whereas epistemic views of the state seem to draw their plausibility chiefly from mixed states, preparation procedures and the statistics of measurement. If that asymmetry is genuine, does it make the ontic/epistemic dispute a dispute that changes content from one regime of application to another, and can a view that is ontic in one regime and epistemic in another be a stable position at all?

You should feel free to discuss the well-known no-go results that are often said to rule out a purely epistemic reading of the quantum state, but I am less interested in whether the theorems go through than in what their assumptions presuppose about the very questions I am asking. And please do not supply citations, bibliographies or reading lists: I want the argument to stand on its own, since I shall be sharing it with a discussion group that meets without reference works. Plainly written, careful reasoning matters more to me than terminology, though I am content to learn the standard labels where they genuinely help.
