I've been reading a pop-history of astronomy book, and there's a bit about Kepler that's been nagging at me. I'm paraphrasing from memory, but the gist was something like:

'Kepler was really the first modern scientist in his attitude to his own job. He took the post of imperial mathematician for the astronomy, and the horoscope-casting that came with it was a slightly shameful side gig -- the way a modern physicist might grudgingly write a newspaper astrology column to keep the landlord paid. Privately he regarded the whole bussiness as superstition, and he kept it up only because his patrons expected it. His discovery that the planets move in ellipses was the clean break from all that: once you have the real orbit, the zodiac signs and the aspects are just noise. By late in life he was embarrassed enough about the astrology that he quietly dropped it.'

Something about this feels too tidy to me, but I honestly can't put my finger on where it goes wrong. It can't be as simple as "superstitious then, scientist later," but it also can't be that he believed every last horoscope -- he was scathing about plenty of astrological practice in his own time. So help me audit the summary. Which parts of it would a historian of early modern astronomy accept as standard, which parts would make them wince, and which parts are flat out wrong? I've also heard somewhere that Kepler compared astrology to a foolish daughter of a most wise mother, who nonetheless brings home the dowry that supports the mother -- if that's a real remark of his, does the book's cynical reading even survive it? And does the book's bigger conclusion, that the planetary laws made the astrological parts of his system obsolete in his own eyes, actually follow from whatever is true in the summary? Please don't just hand me "scholars disagree" and a menu of readings -- commit to one account of what Kepler's real attitude toward astrology most likely was, and defend it. If some piece of the summary can't be settled either way, say so, but tell me which piece you'd bet on and why.
