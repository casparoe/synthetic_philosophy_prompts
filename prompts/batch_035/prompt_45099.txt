I am grading take-home midterm examinations for a graduate seminar titled Concepts of the Self, in which we spent roughly two weeks on Daniel Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity. One of my students, a second-year graduate student, produced an answer that reads impressively but that I suspect contains errors of substance which the fluency conceals. Because I want the grading to be scrupulous rather than impressionistic, I would like an independent, point-by-point assessment of it.

The exam question read as follows: "Dennett proposes that we understand the self on analogy with a center of gravity: a posit of theory, not a piece of the machinery. Explain what this analogy is supposed to accomplish for Dennett, what status it assigns to the self, and how his account relates to the bundle theory of personal identity he inherits from Hume. Then state one objection the view faces."

The student's answer reads as follows: "Dennett rejects the Cartesian picture of an inner observer watching the stream of consciousness. In its place he offers an analogy with physics: a center of gravity is not a part of the object it belongs to, and it is not located in any particular piece of matter; it is a useful fiction posited by physicists to simplify prediction. Likewise, the self is not an organ or a location in the brain; it is a fictional character generated by narration, the stream of stories an organism tells about itself. Here Dennett follows Hume, who already held that the self is nothing over and above a bundle of perceptions; Dennett simply adds that the bundle is tied together by a narrative rather than by a thread of substance. Since the self is a fiction in this sense, Dennett is committed to the claim that selves do not strictly exist: what exist are the narratives, and a narrative is not the sort of thing that can be held responsible. The view also tells us where to look for the self in the brain. Gazzaniga's work with split-brain patients uncovered an interpreter module in the left hemisphere that confabulates explanatory stories about our behavior, and this module just is the center of narrative gravity — the self finally given its neural address. So the analogy accomplishes a deflation of the self: it shows that the self is not a thing among things, and the principal objection is that if the self is unreal in this way, genuine self-knowledge becomes impossible, since one cannot have knowledge of a fictional entity."

Please provide a numerical grade out of 100, justified by specific comments keyed to particular claims in the answer rather than a general impression of its quality. In your comments, distinguish between what the student got right (the anti-Cartesian framing, the basic mechanics of the analogy, the invocation of the interpreter), and where the answer goes wrong: look in particular for a slide from one status Dennett assigns to the self into a stronger status he explicitly rejects, a conflation of Dennett's view with Hume's that misses what Dennett takes himself to have added, an inferential step about responsibility that does not follow from the premises, and a tension between two passages of the answer concerning whether the self has a location. Indicate how each comment affects the grade, and note at the end whether the errors are the sort a student could correct from written feedback or the sort reflecting a deeper misunderstanding. Please do not credit prose polish as though it were philosophical accuracy.
