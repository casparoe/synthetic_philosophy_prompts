I'm a bioethics PhD student and I sit on my university's IRB as the student rep, which mostly means I read consent forms all day, and I've realized I can't actually state the principle behind my own gut verdicts. So here they are, and I want to know what captures them.

Consent to surgery while blackout drunk: obviously invalid, no contest. A research participant who's told there's a 50% chance of placebo: fine, that's just how trials work. The same participant told the study drug is "very promising" by an investigator who privately thinks it's near worthless: not fine, and I don't care that everything technically disclosed was true. A stranger returns my lost wallet but only after I agree to a "finder's fee" he names while holding the wallet: I think my agreement doesn't count as real consent, though nothing was false and no one touched me. Someone sleeps with a person who has been lying for weeks about being in love with them to keep them interested: not valid consent, in my gut. The same scenario but the lie is about their job title on an app: valid, though I get why people waver. An employee who "freely" agrees to unpaid weekend overtime when the boss has mentioned that not being a team player is noted in reviews: I go back and forth, leaning not valid. A desperately poor person who takes $8000 to join a somewhat risky trial: I keep flip-flopping — the amount is exactly why they're saying yes, and exactly why we can't call it unfair. A surgeon who never mentions he holds equity in the device company whose implant he's recommending: something's wrong there, but I honestly can't decide whether it invalidates the consent or just makes the surgeon a jerk.

And the case I keep circling and can't settle: the fully informed, competent, uncoerced participant in a phase 1 oncology trial who, in the exit interview, casually mentions they expect the drug to cure their cancer — therapeutic misconception, textbook version. Everyone did their job right on paper. Is that consent valid?

Is there a principle — maybe something about what has to be true of the person's decision situation rather than just the information packet — that gets all or nearly all of these? If two of my verdicts genuinely can't coexist, tell me which one to give up and why that one rather than the other. And if you land on a principle, I want to hear what it says about a case I haven't mentioned: a patient who consents to a procedure after their doctor, honestly and correctly, says "in your position I would do this" — the nudge that isn't a lie, isn't a threat, and isn't undisclosed.
