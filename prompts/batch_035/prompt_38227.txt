My roommate is deep into game theory and keeps issuing verdicts on whether people's commitments are rational, like it's a judicial function. She's a Schelling person and goes on about commitment races — how in a bargain the side that credibly locks in its position first grabs most of the surplus, so everyone races to bind themselves early, often before they've half thought things through — and I've started collecting her rulings. So far: Odysseus getting himself lashed to the mast so he can listen to the sirens without steering onto the rocks, rational (and she made a point of noting he also ordered the crew not to untie him no matter what he yells); a union head who, at the open of wage talks, publicly stakes her own house on the union not settling below a number, when everyone knows she goes soft at the table, rational; a general burning the bridges behind his own army, rational; and a guy who, after a bad breakup, writes in his journal that he's done with dating forever — she said that's not even a commitment, just a mood. Also in a one-shot negotiation exercise where whoever locks in a position first takes the bigger share, she said committing fast was rational even though the parties barely got any time to think.

Now I've got three new cases to run past her and I want to predict her calls. (1) A negotiator with famously iron self-control, zero history of caving, who publicly burns a bridge anyway to gain leverage. (2) A compulsive gambler who quietly puts himself on the casino's self-exclusion list, tells absolutely nobody, no audience at all — pure private self-binding. (3) A negotiator who just announces, flatly, that she cannot go below X — no mechanism, nothing staked, nothing at all stopping her from taking a worse deal later — and the counterpart believes her and concedes. My problem is I honestly can't tell what her actual rule is. I can see at least two that fit every verdict so far: one where a commitment matters because it moves other people (the crew, the employer, the counterpart), and one where it matters because it stops your own future self (the enchanted Odysseus, the union head about to go soft). And those two come apart on at least one of my cases, I think — case 2 has nobody to move, and case 3 has a future self that's never actually stopped by anything.

So what I'd like from you: lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record (there might be a third about whether the binding has to be real/hard to undo — the journal guy and case 3 both have nothing holding anything in place), say what each rule predicts for each of my three cases, give your best guess at her actual call with your reasoning, and then tell me the single further case that would most cleanly seperate the rules — the one I should save for last if I want to catch her in a genuine dilemma rather than just hear her rationalize.
