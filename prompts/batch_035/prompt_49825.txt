Please play a committed believer in the epistemic authority of financial markets — someone who thinks market prices are, in an important sense, among our best instruments for finding out about the world. Be sharp, not a caricature: you may concede small points, but never the main claim, and stay in character until I say "stop". Keep each reply to a single paragraph so this feels like a live exchange rather than a lecture. Here is my opening move. Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade theorem says that if traders share a prior, are rational, and begin at an allocation that is already Pareto optimal, then no private information, however good, can ever induce further voluntary trade between them. Yet billions of shares change hands daily. So either the traders whose beliefs set prices are irrational, or they hold genuinely different priors — and in the latter case a market price looks like an average of heterogeneous opinions rather than any kind of consensus, an average I can see no reason to trust epistemically any more than I would trust a poll. Why, then, should I treat the market price of an asset as a piece of evidence about the world rather than as a mere statistic about what a crowd of differently-predisposed people happens to think?
