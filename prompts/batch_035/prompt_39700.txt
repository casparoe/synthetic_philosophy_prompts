I write about science and ideas, and for years my editors and I have used the same picture: the algorithm puts people in a bubble, like a sealed room where the only voices you ever hear are ones that already agree with you, so of course everyone's views get more extreme. The whole point of the analogy is that it makes the fix obvious -- just pop the bubble. Bust the filter, expose people to the other side, maybe a "see the other viewpoint" button, and polarization should ease. But I recently came across a philosopher, C. Thi Nguyen, who argues we've merged two different things under one name: an epistemic bubble where contrary voices are simply *missing* (the sealed room), and an echo chamber where they're actively *discredited* -- and his picture for the second one is a cult, where the members have been pre-inoculated so that outside criticism registers as proof of the conspiracy. Audit the analogy my field leans on. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the walls of the room, the outside voices, the person trapped inside -- then show where the sealed-room picture breaks down and what the cult picture gets right and where IT gets stretched too. Most importantly: does the "just expose people to disagreement" policy conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the bubble analogy quietly carrying it the whole time? And please keep straight for me what's actually settled among people who study this -- e.g. whether social media polarization is even driven by lack of cross-cutting exposure -- versus what's still genuinely contested, like how much real-world echo chambers resemble the cult model at all. Don't hand me a contested claim as consensus, my readers will notice.
