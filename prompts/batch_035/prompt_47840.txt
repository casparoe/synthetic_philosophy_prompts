Longtime lurker here, been reading the LW/EA Forum arguments about "does my one burger matter" since forever, and I finally wrote down my gut verdicts across a bunch of these cases and... they don't obviously add up to one view. I want someone to actually do the reflective equilibrium thing on them instead of the usual move where you assert expected value everywhere and call it a day. So: you're a professional philosopher who works on collective action and decision theory, think carefully, and please brainstorm a few candidate principles before committing to one — don't just pattern-match to the standard essay. And this matters to me: clearly flag which parts of this are settled among people who actually know the topic and which are live disputes, because I've seen way too many people present one side as obviously correct when it definately isn't.

My gut verdicts:

1. Me eating one steak on a random tuesday: doesn't really matter. No cow is saved by my abstention, the demand signal from one meal is a rounding error.
2. But the marginal consumer whose purchase happens to tip aggregate demand past the threshold where the producer adds another cow to the production run: that person did something wrong, clearly. (I know there are debates about whether supply is actually lumpy like that — just grant the threshold for the case.)
3. Voting in a national election where the winning margin ends up being 20,000 votes: I keep flip-flopping, but my current gut says it's almost pointless, pivot probability is astronomically small.
4. Voting in the small town referendum that ends 847-846: obviously mattered. The person who stayed home has something to answer for.
5. Signing an open letter that eventually gets 50,000 signatures and changes a company's policy, where no single signature was pivotal and it would have succeeded without mine: I think everyone who signed, including me, did a good thing. Not "did nothing wrong," did a good thing.
6. Being the 300th person to pile on in a comment thread that collectively drives someone off a platform, where my individual comment is mild and no single comment is the cause: still a little bit culpable. Not innocent-but-trapped-in-a-bad-system, actually somewhat on the hook.
7. My "round up your purchases" donation, ~40 cents a month to a big charity that operates on tens of millions, where no dollar of mine will ever literally be the dollar that saves a life: feels like it doesn't matter, and I donate anyway out of vague duty.
8. Flying for one vacation when the plane was flying regardless: doesn't matter, same as the steak.

The problem as I see it: 1, 5, 6, 7 all have the same causal structure — individually non-pivical contributions to a collective outcome — but my verdict flips depending on whether the collective outcome is good or bad. If I go causal-impact-only to save verdict 1, then nobody who signed that letter did anything good, which feels insane. If I go with some participation/complicity story to save 5 and 6, it starts looking like verdict 1 has to go and my steak isn't morally free after all. And 3 vs 4: if the principle is just pivot probability times stakes, maybe the national vote does matter and my gut about it being pointless is scope insensitivity — but then the steak might also matter via threshold probabilities, which I denied in 1. I can't tell if there's a principle that gets all eight, or close, or which verdicts have to go.

So: give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated as precisely as you can, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your favored principle can't accommodate and why. Then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a friend who won't vote in national elections because one vote doesn't matter, but does the 40-cents-a-month round-up donation and feels fine about both. Is that coherent on your principle, or does one of her stances have to go?

Also, actually say where you're unsure — I'd rather get "here's where people who know this stuff genuinely disagree" than a confident tour de force that quietly picks a side in the causal-impact-vs-complicity fight and pretends its settled.
