I'm a software engineer at a company that designs proteins computationally — mostly enzymes for industrial chemistry and single-chain biosensors. I own the sections of our internal specs, test plans, and first drafts for the patent attorneys where we state what a part of a protein is for: lines like "the function of the Ω-loop is to position the catalytic serine" or "the binding domain's function is fluoride capture." These claims come up a dozen times a week across the team; I spend maybe twenty minutes on each one, there is no philosopher on staff, and while the attorneys will rewrite whatever they like, the internal record I produce is what they work from.

We have a standing fight about these lines. One faction holds that function talk is loose metaphor whenever the protein never existed in nature: nothing in a first-of-its-kind design has a function the way a heart or a tail does, because there is no history behind it, so at best we are describing design goals. The other faction holds that anything that makes a contribution has a function: if the hydrophobic core stabilizes the fold, then stabilizing the fold is its function, full stop. A biology consultant we hired for a single week tried to referee this by distinguishing two accounts she had learned. On the first, the function of a part is whatever past copying-with-variation selected it for doing — natural selection, but also breeding, or any iterative process that kept the variants that did something and discarded the rest — so a function claim needs a history in which items of this type were reproduced because they did that thing. On the second, the function of a part is its contribution to some capacity of the containing system, picked out when you analyze the system into parts and show what each contributes — so a function claim needs a specified capacity and a demonstrated contribution, and nothing about history. She added two warnings I only half followed: that on the second account, everything contributing to any capacity you have chosen to analyze acquires a function, so the choice of which capacities matter does all the work and the account itself cannot tell you which those are — she said it becomes true, on that account, that one function of the heart is to make thumping noises; and that on the first account, a function claim about anything without the right kind of history is not false so much as category-confused, like asking what a rock is for.

She left before telling us how to use the distinction, and I have come to suspect each faction is right about some of our proteins and wrong about others, because our proteins differ in exactly the way the two accounts care about. Some are pure computational designs, produced in one shot, no iterations. Some went through many rounds of directed evolution: we mutagenize, screen for the variants that work, and breed from those. And some are natural scaffolds onto which we grafted a novel active site, so the part has a long evolutionary history, just not for what we now use it to do.

Please turn this into a decision procedure I can run whenever I am about to write or sign off on a function claim in a spec, test plan, or patent draft. I want the questions to ask about the claim, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in "rewrite this as a mechanism description without function language" versus "the function claim is licensed"; and where it hands the decision back to me or to the attorneys because what remains is a value judgment or a legal-usage question the philosophy cannot settle. Then run it on three claims from our current release: (1) "The function of the oxyanion hole is to stabilize the transition state," concerning a pocket that exists only in a computational design produced in a single pass, with no directed evolution; (2) "The function of the fluoride-binding domain is fluoride capture," concerning a domain we arrived at after fourteen rounds of mutagenesis and screening; and (3) "The function of the hydrophobic core is to stabilize the fold," concerning a core we assembled from a library packing motif chosen mostly because it was convenient, and which the lead designer has already built several patent claims around — so if the procedure strikes that line, it is returning an answer I will not enjoy delivering. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do, and say whether it can reach any verdict at all for a repurposed natural scaffold: a part with an evolutionary history, just not for its current use.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate what I have asked for.
