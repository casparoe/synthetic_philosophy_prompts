so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the units of selection debate and I can't tell if it's actually still alive or just zombie-ing along. background as I understand it: the old Dawkins vs. Gould fight about whether genes or organisms are the "real" unit of selection kinda got settled by the multilevel selection people reframing it — if you partition the Price equation differently, kin selection and group selection turn out to be mathematically equivalent descriptions of the same process, just with the accounting done at different levels. and yet people are STILL fighting about it, decades after everyone granted the equivalence. some say the fight is only about which bookkeeping is more "natural," others say there's a real causal question hiding in there about where the causal work actually happens (the interactor vs replicator stuff, or the point that statistical decompositions can be equivalent while only one tracks causes).

my hypothesis is that the residual fight is basically about explanatory pragmatics — which description makes you ask better next questions, like whether a trait spread because it helped the group or because it helped the allele, when "because" is doing dubious work if the process is literally the same process. but I half suspect that's wrong and it's actually a disguised disagreement about causation itself, like interventionist vs process views of causal relevance applied to selection.

so: what kind of disagreement is the units debate at this point, really? verbal, substantive, or the two sides answering different questions under one banner? and given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move it — a new formal result, empirical work on real populations, a conceptual distinction — or is the equivalence the end of the story and everyone should just pick their notation and go home?
