I am preparing a position statement on explanation and reduction for a philosophy of science seminar, and before submitting it I would like a rigorous consistency audit. Below are the five theses I currently endorse, stated as plainly as I can. Please tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, how severe each real tension is, what would have to give in each case and at what cost, and finally which single revision would clean up the most. For reference, I take Dennett's summary of the distinction to state my starting point exactly: "Good reductionists suppose that all Design can be explained without skyhooks; greedy reductionists suppose it can all be explained without cranes."

(1) Cranes only. Everything that looks designed — organisms, minds, languages, economies — must in principle be explainable by cranes: myopic, mechanical, bottom-up processes accumulating over time. Any explanation that helps itself to top-down intelligence, purpose, or unexplained marvels at any point in the history of the universe is disqualified.

(2) Greed is a sin. Greedy reductionism — trying to skip intermediate levels and fasten phenomena directly onto lower-level foundations, as Skinner's behaviorism did in trying to get all mentality out of operant conditioning in a single stroke — is not merely incomplete science but bad science. Real explanations must go through the relevant intermediate levels (evolutionary, developmental, computational), each of which does indispensable explanatory work of its own.

(3) The intentional level is doomed. I am an eliminative materialist in the style of the Churchlands: beliefs and desires as folk psychology conceives them are defective theoretical posits, and mature neuroscience will not vindicate them by smooth reduction but replace them, so that a future science of cognition will describe what we now call decision-making without quantifying over beliefs or desires at all.

(4) Consciousness leaves a residue. However complete our crane-built neuroscience becomes, it will not explain why there is subjective experience at all: the hard problem is a limitation in principle, not a temporary gap, so that a completed mechanistic account of all the functions would still leave something unexplained.

(5) Compatibilist freedom. Free will and moral responsibility are real, but they are higher-level phenomena: an agent is free when her action issues from her own reasons and values in the right way, which is fully compatible with her being a deterministic, bottom-up-built physical system.

I genuinely do not know where the fault lines are, I have no attachment to any particular thesis, and I would rather be corrected now than in the seminar discussion. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and end your reply with a final line of exactly this form:

Verdict: <the single most cost-effective revision, naming the thesis to revise and how>
