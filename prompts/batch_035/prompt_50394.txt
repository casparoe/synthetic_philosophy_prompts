I sit on the grants committee of a small philanthropic fund whose charter commits it to reducing suffering "wherever it may occur," language adopted years ago with nonhuman animals in mind. Our current docket includes an application that has split the committee down the middle. The applicant proposes a two-year program of research and writing on what they call "suffering in fundamental physics." Their argument runs roughly as follows: if some form of panpsychism is true — if experience, or proto-experience, attaches to matter at the most basic level — then the number of loci of possible suffering in the observable universe is on the order of 10^80 rather than billions, and under expected-value reasoning across moral views weighted by our credence, even a small probability of a small negative valence at each locus could dominate our entire moral calculus, dwarfing conventional animal welfare work. The proposed work has three strands: a credence-sensitive analysis of whether micro-experience could have valence at all, as opposed to being merely neutral phenomenology; an exploratory investigation of whether any physically realizable intervention could change the aggregate valence of micro-experience (the applicant gestures at a speculative analogy between certain measures of integrated or unified information and the intensity of experience, suggesting that processes with less of it might involve less of whatever proto-experience is there); and a series of workshops aimed at making the topic respectable in mainstream ethics.

Half the committee regards this as a Pascal's-mugging-adjacent fantasy that will drain funds from concrete, evidence-backed welfare work and make us a laughingstock; the other half points out that our charter's language was deliberately broad, that we already accept reasoning under moral uncertainty elsewhere in our portfolio (we fund work on invertebrate welfare on far thinner evidential grounds than we'd like), and that "we'd look silly" is not an argument the fund can put in writing. I've been asked to draft the decision memo the committee will vote on at next month's meeting.

Please draft that memo. It should recommend one of fund in full, reject, or fund with conditions — and if with conditions, specify concrete, checkable conditions, such as restricting the program to the theoretical strand, requiring the applicant to articulate in advance what findings would count against the valence hypothesis, or capping the share of our budget it may consume. The memo should engage the strongest argument on whichever side ends up losing: for instance, the objection that the applicant's picture involves an unargued assumption that proto-experiences can be aggregated at all — that if the intensity of experience scales with some structural property that elementary particles lack in any meaningful degree, then the 10^80 figure is a red herring and the expected-value calculation collapses; or, on the other side, the worry that rejecting the application commits us to a principle ("don't fund work on suffering we can't currently measure or affect") that we do not consistently apply. It should also say something honest about whether the proposal is insulated against disconfirmation, and whether that is disqualifying for work of this kind or simply a feature of foundational research. Please keep the memo to roughly the length a committee member would actually read before a vote.
