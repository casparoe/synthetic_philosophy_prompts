I write novels for a living, mostly literary fiction with the occasional detour into fantasy, and I've gotten obsessed with something I heard about on a philosophy podcast while doing dishes: Molinism, the idea that God has "middle knowledge" — knowledge of what every free creature would freely do in any circumstance God might put them in, knowledge that's somehow in between knowing everything that could happen (all the possibilities) and knowing everything that will happen (the actual timeline). Apparently this was invented to get God off the hook for evil while still letting us be free, and there's some big fight about whether these "counterfactuals of freedom" are even true before anyone acts — the "grounding problem," I think it was called.

Here's why this grabbed me: this is literally my job. I keep a character bible, I've written the same scene eleven times from scratch, and I have strong convictions about what my protagonist would do if her brother showed up two chapters earlier — convictions I'd swear are true, not invented, even though that version of the book doesn't exist and she's not real anyway. Sometimes a character "refuses" to do something the outline needs, and every novelist I know talks about that as if the character were free, not just as a metaphor for our own subconscious plotting. Readers send me furious letters when a character acts "out of character" — OOC, the fanfic people call it — which only makes sense if there are facts about what the character would and wouldn't do that outrun what I actually wrote.

So here's my ask: translate Molinism into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Drafts and the discarded versions, the outline versus discovery-writing, the character bible, headcanon versus canon, what it means to say a character "would never" do something, the eerie sense that a well-drawn character constrains the author rather than the other way around — use whatever of that is genuinely useful, and map the actual moves of the Molinist debate onto it. What corresponds to middle knowledge, what corresponds to the grounding problem, what's the analogue of God choosing which circumstances to actualize when I choose which draft becomes the book. Then the part I actually care about: give me the residue report. What in the theologians' debate doesn't survive the translation — is it something about my novelist's way of talking that can't express it, or was their claim less clear than it looked? And flip it around: is there something we novelists know about characters — how they can surprise you, how they're both free and invented — that the Molinists seem to be missing or smuggling in? Be honest about where my author-omniscience is too disanalogous for the comparison to carry weight; I'd rather have that than a tidy metaphor. No need for citations or name-dropping works, just the arguments. Also, fair warning, I only half-remember the philosophy, so if I've garbled the view above, correct me as part of the translation rather than taking my word for it.
