My flatmate reckons he's found a knock-down reply to the brain-in-a-vat sceptic and I can't decide if it's clever or cheating. (1) To know that p, you must be able to rule out every possibility in which not-p that's consistent with your evidence. (2) A recently envatted brain could have experiences just like mine, so the vat hypothesis is consistant with all my evidence. (3) So I don't know I have hands, and by the same reasoning I don't know any empirical proposition whatsoever — including everything I believe about brains, computers, and what envatting would actually involve. (4) But premise (2) is itself an empirical claim: it says a brain wired into a vat would receive just the experiences I'm having, and you'd need neurophysiology and some serious computing know-how to establish that. (5) By (3), nobody knows any of that. (6) So nobody can justifiably assert (2), and the sceptical argument saws off the branch it's sitting on. (7) Therefore scepticism is self-refuting — the sceptic can't even state his own argument without helping himself to the knowledge he denies. My flatmate is very pleased with himself. I'm less sure: the vat scenario still grips me, and I suspect something slipped between (4) and (6) without me being able to name it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If it helps, I'd much rather keep (1) than anything else — and if the argument really is sound, I'd want to know whether it actually gets me my hands back, or merely silences the sceptic.
