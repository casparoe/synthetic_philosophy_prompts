I'm writing a paper defending classical utilitarianism against the utility monster objection, and I want a case that turns the objection around rather than merely answering it. Construct a thought experiment containing two monsters, mirror images of each other: an intensity monster — a person whose experiences, positive and negative, are vastly stronger than an ordinary person's, so that modest benefits to them outweigh large benefits to everyone else — and what I'll call a misery monster — a person whose experiences are equally amplified but whose condition leaves them near the bottom of the scale, so that sparing them small harms outweighs relieving large harms to others, and granting them modest relief outweighs major benefits to everyone else combined.

Constraints: the scenario itself should be a single paragraph set in an ordinary institutional context — a clinic or benefits office distributing a fixed budget — rather than a philosopher's laboratory; the two monsters' conditions should be specified symmetrically (same degree of amplification, opposite regions of the scale), so that any theory treating the two differently owes an explanation of the asymmetry; and nothing in the case may turn on either monster deserving their condition, being responsible for it, or having any special claim of entitlement. Assume the decision-maker knows the magnitudes involved, as usual in such cases, but the case must not simply stipulate the verdicts — they should follow from the theories themselves.

After the scenario, do the following. First, state what classical utilitarianism says about each monster, and show why its verdicts, however unattractive, are at least symmetric — it pays the monster in either direction. Second, state what a prioritarian view with a concave weighting of well-being says about each monster, and argue whether its treatment of the misery monster is not at least as vulnerable to the original objection as the utilitarian's treatment of the utility monster. Third, do the same for an egalitarian view concerned with inequalities in well-being. Fourth, identify exactly which structural feature of each theory does the work — the linearity of the utilitarian's aggregation, the concavity of the prioritarian's weighting function, the shape of the egalitarian's inequality measure — and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed or weakened, for instance if the prioritian caps how much extra weight the worst-off can receive. Fifth, give the strongest reply available to the prioritarian: for example, denying that the weighting function behaves symmetrically above and below neutrality, or arguing that amplified experience is itself a further good or ill that makes the monsters' claims non-comparable to ordinary ones. Assess whether that reply is principled or whether it amounts to special pleading engineered to survive exactly this case.

Finally, tell me honestly whether the reversal succeeds. Does the friend of the utility monster objection have a response that embraces the misery monster's claims without cost, or does the symmetry constraint put genuine pressure on them? If you think the case fails — say, because the two monsters are relevantly disanalogous in a way I've overlooked — say so and explain the disanalogy rather than papering over it; a failed reversal is more useful to me than a flattering one, since I'd rather find the problem now than in a referee's report.
