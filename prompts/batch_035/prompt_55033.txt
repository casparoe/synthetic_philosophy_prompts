I do rapid-response communications for a mid-sized human rights org -- the person who drafts the first public statement when something awful starts happening somewhere. A few times a year a situation flares up and within a day or two I have to decide what we CALL it in our press releases and reports: genocide, crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, war crimes, or the infinitely deniable "mass atrocities." Our legal counsel then reviews before the director signs off, but the first draft and the framing are mine, and once you put a word in the first press release it's basically impossible to walk back.

The problem is that the word "genocide" specifically is a legal category with weird edges that everyone on all sides keeps muddying. As I understand it: the Genocide Convention limits protected groups to national, ethnic, racial and religious ones, so political groups and most class-based persecution are out by definition. You need specific acts (killing, serious harm, destroying conditions of life, preventing births, forcibly transferring children) done with special intent to destroy the group as such -- which courts have treated as a very demanding thing, more than just knowingly doing terrible things to a group. Meanwhile Lemkin, who basically invented the concept, thought of it much more broadly, including destroying a group's culture and way of life without mass killing, but that broader conception mostly did NOT make it into the convention. And then there's "ethnic cleansing," which everyone uses loosely but which is not a standalone legal category, more of a description that can slide into genocide depending on what the intent was. On top of all that there's the practical layer: calling something genocide when you can't prove special intent hands deniers a win when a court later disagrees, but NOT calling it genocide is itself a political act with its own history, and "crimes against humanity" is both technically more prosecutable in some ways and rhetorically weaker.

I need this turned into something I can actually run at 11pm before a 7am statement deadline. Write me a decision procedure -- ordered questions, what counts as enough of an answer to each, where the procedure outputs "insufficient basis, say X instead," and crucially where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because what's left isn't a factual question but a values/strategy question (e.g. whether withholding the word aids denial, whether our org should be in the business of legal conclusions at all vs. documenting facts). Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a labcoat.

Then run it on three situations. (a) A regional minority is being deported en masse from a contested province -- villages burned, mosques/churches razed, dozens killed in the course of expulsion, aid workers expelled, but killing doesn't seem to be the point so much as getting the land. Half my colleagues want the g-word in the first line. (b) Decades of a state removing an Indigenous group's children to boarding schools with forced language suppression and high death rates -- this is the case where the broader Lemkin conception and the convention's child-transfer clause pull apart, and where our own staff and the affected community call it genocide while a court probably wouldn't on the mass-killing framing. (c) A government blockades a rebellious region where an ethnic group is concentrated and food can't get in, famine deaths climbing -- the leadership clearly knows what blockade plus famine does but frames it as counterinsurgency, so it's a knowledge-vs-purpose problem.

Tell me where the procedure gives answers I won't like, and what it can't do at all. Also please say how the procedure should handle us being early -- situations where the destruction is clearly underway but evidence is still thin, since "waiting for certainty" is exactly how the phrase "never again" keeps failing. Keep it practical, I'm not a lawyer and never will be.
