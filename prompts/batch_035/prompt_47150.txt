I am the grader for a final examination in an upper-level undergraduate course on Buddhist philosophy, and I would like a careful second reading of one student's answer before I finalize its mark. Below are the exam question and the student's answer. Please assign the student a letter grade (with pluses or minuses as you see fit) and defend that grade with a point-by-point assessment of the answer.

The exam question read:

"Answer all four parts.
1. State the four alternatives of the catuṣkoṭi (tetralemma) and explain, drawing on Nāgārjuna's treatment of conditions or of motion in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, how the negation of all four alternatives is supposed to work.
2. Explain why emptiness (śūnyatā) is not the claim that nothing exists.
3. Explain the emptiness of emptiness, and why Nāgārjuna needs it in order to keep the Madhyamaka position coherent.
4. Address the charge that Madhyamaka is self-refuting: if all views are empty, why is the view that all views are empty not thereby refuted as well?"

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Nāgārjuna invented the tetralemma as a general logical instrument, systematizing scattered earlier Buddhist material. The four alternatives are: a thing exists, it does not exist, it both exists and does not exist, and it neither exists nor does not exist. In the first chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, which examines conditions, he applies the scheme to arising: an effect cannot arise from itself, since it would already exist; it cannot arise from something other than itself, since then anything could arise from anything; it cannot arise from both, since either fault would apply; and it cannot arise from neither, since things would then arise causelessly, anywhere and everywhere. Since nothing arises in any of the four ways, no phenomenon arises independently; whatever exists exists dependently, that is, lacks svabhāva, intrinsic nature. Emptiness just is this lack of intrinsic nature. It is not the claim that nothing exists: the doctrine denies intrinsic existence, not plain existence. Indeed, emptiness and dependent origination are two descriptions of the same fact, which is why Madhyamaka is the middle way between eternalism (things exist with svabhāva) and annihilationism (things do not exist at all).

Where does the fourfold negation leave us? Since the first two alternatives are excluded, and classical logic does not let us keep both the law of non-contradiction and the law of excluded middle while denying them, the conclusion must be the fourth alternative: phenomena neither exist nor do not exist. Nāgārjuna is therefore committed to a paraconsistent logic on which some contradictions, for instance that a thing arises and does not arise, are at least conventionally true. The doctrine of the two truths supports this reading: conventional truth is how the deluded take the world to be, and ultimate truth is the realization that all is empty. Conventional truth is simply error; once emptiness is grasped, conventional discourse is to be discarded. Nāgārjuna does say that without relying on the conventional, the ultimate cannot be shown, but this means only that language is a temporary ladder to be kicked away once one has climbed it.

Finally, emptiness must apply to itself, since it is a property of phenomena and so depends on them. The emptiness of emptiness means that emptiness is not some further absolute standing behind phenomena. But it also means that the doctrine itself is empty and therefore not ultimately true. In the Vigrahavyāvartanī Nāgārjuna concedes that he asserts no thesis, since asserting one would require svabhāva in the terms asserted. So the charge of self-refutation fails: Madhyamaka is not a position that can be refuted. The price, though, is skepticism. The view offers no account of how things are, only of how they are not, and even the emptiness of emptiness is one convention among others."
