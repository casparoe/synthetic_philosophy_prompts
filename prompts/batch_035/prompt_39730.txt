My buddy on the backend team thinks he's found a bug in expected value reasoning and I can't quite patch it. The setup is the St. Petersburg game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if that takes n flips you win $2^n. His argument: (1) a rational agent always picks the option with the higher expected value. (2) The St. Petersburg game has infinite expected value, since the sum 1/2*2 + 1/4*4 + 1/8*8 + ... never converges to anything finite. (3) Infinite is bigger than any finite number, so if the house charges you a fee of $X to play, you should pay, no matter how large X is. (4) Nothing in (2) or (3) depends on the payouts starting at $2 — a version of the game that starts at 1 cent still has infinite expected value, so (3) applies to it too. (5) So a rational agent holding, say, $80,000 should trade all of it for a chance to play the 1-cent version, a game where there's a 99.9% chance you walk away with less than $10. (6) Since essentially no one would make that trade, either almost nobody is rational or expected value maximization is just broken. He wants to conclude (1) is false and rational people should use something like medians or typical outcomes instead. I have a feeling something slips between (3) and (5), because intuitively the game is only worth infinite EV if someone can actually pay out astronomical sums — but I can't formalize that feeling without it sounding like I'm just refusing to do math I don't like. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd honestly like to keep (1) if there's a way. Also please answer in American English.
