I organize a small seminar on philosophy of computer science, and at the last meeting two regulars got into a dispute about whether the P versus NP question has genuine philosophical significance for what discovery and understanding are. I couldn't referee it and would appreciate an adjudication. The exchange went like this.

(1) Maya argued that if P = NP, the intuitive gap between appreciating an insight and having one would be shown to be an artifact of complexity: since verifying a proof is computationally easy, generating one could be made easy as well, so mathematical creativity would be mechanical in a strong sense. She added that this wasn't loose talk, because for standard NP-complete problems like satisfiability, an efficient method for the decision problem can be converted into one that extracts actual solutions, so easy checking really would yield easy finding.

(2) Dev replied that this is asymptotic fantasy: an algorithm running in time n to the thousandth power counts as polynomial but could never find anything any actual being could use, so even given P = NP the practical asymmetry between creating and verifying would be untouched.

(3) Maya said the claim was about principle, not practice: what would collapse is the idea that there is a difference in kind between recognizing and generating, and that collapse would be momentous regardless of practical running times.

(4) Dev countered that our concept of creativity is indexed to agents with finite lifetimes, so principle divorced from feasibility has no grip on it; and in any case polynomiality is not the philosophically relevant line, since n-squared steps is feasible while n-to-the-thousandth is not — the boundary of the complexity class simply doesn't track the boundary of the doable.

(5) Maya's last word was that vague boundaries do not abolish distinctions — the same is true of heaps — and that at most Dev had shown the distinction is drawn by feasibility considerations, not that the collapse she described would be anything short of momentous.

Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about which specific point, note where each side concedes too much or too little, and identify anything both of them missed.
