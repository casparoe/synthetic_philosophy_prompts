**Lost in Translation: Injustice by Assimilation (draft)**

Miranda Fricker's framework, introduced in her 2007 book *Epistemic Injustice*, distinguishes testimonial injustice, in which a speaker's credibility is deflated because of prejudice about who they are, from hermeneutical injustice, in which a speaker cannot render an experience intelligible at all because the shared conceptual resources for doing so are missing. Both categories locate the epistemic harm at a kind of boundary failure: either the hearer discounts the message, or the vocabulary runs out before the message can be formed. I want to propose a third category that fits neither description, in which uptake appears to succeed while in fact transforming the testimony into something else. Call it injustice by assimilation.

Consider Maria, the only nurse on a hospital committee that is redesigning shift scheduling. She repeatedly warns that the new system will fail on night shifts. The committee fully believes her; she suffers no credibility deficit, and the concepts she deploys are common property. But they take her to be reporting a logistical inconvenience, when her actual point is that the schedule breaks the informal mutual cover that night nurses provide one another, and that this makes the ward unsafe in a way no roster analysis will ever register. When the failure arrives, everyone agrees that Maria predicted it. She insists she was never heard.

Nothing in Fricker's taxonomy seems to capture this. Her testimony was neither discounted nor unformulable. What failed was the mode of uptake itself: the hearers translated her claim into their own explanatory frame, then credited themselves with understanding it. I claim this is a structural feature of testimony between unequally positioned speakers, not a matter of individual obtuseness: the interpretive frame of the dominant party does the absorbing, and the speaker cannot refuse the translation.

The obvious objection is that Maria's case is really hermeneutical injustice in disguise: the committee lacked the concept of informal mutual cover, and the gap in shared resources did the damage. I reply that this mislocates the failure. The concept was available to them — Maria explained it — but their interpretive practice absorbed the explanation and converted it into their own terms. The deficit is procedural, not conceptual: the harm lies in what the hearers' practice does with an adequately communicated meaning, not in what their vocabulary makes communicable.
