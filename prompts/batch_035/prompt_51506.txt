I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and for two weeks now an argument — assembled by a student who was, I think, only half in earnest — has resisted repair. Its conclusion proves far too much, but every individual step still looks defensible to me. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The argument concerns the principle of indifference and Bertrand's chord paradox:

(1) Where the evidence gives no reason to favor any one of a set of mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive possibilities over another, rationality requires distributing credence uniformly over those possibilities.

(2) A chord of a circle is drawn at random. What is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? The problem statement gives us no information distinguishing any chord from any other.

(3) By (1) and (2), we must distribute credence uniformly over the chords.

(4) "Uniform over chords" is not a well-defined instruction; uniformity holds only relative to a parameterization. If we represent a chord by its midpoint and take the midpoint uniform over the disk, we get 1/4. If we fix a direction through the center, take a line perpendicular to it, and place the chord's midpoint uniformly along the radius, we get 1/2. If we fix a point on the circumference and choose the second endpoint uniformly along the circumference, we get 1/3.

(5) Nothing in the problem statement or in our evidence distinguishes these parameterizations: each is a complete, symmetric, one-to-one redescription of the same set of chords, and each is exactly as faithful a rendering of "no chord favored over any other."

(6) An agent who knows only what the problem says is therefore, with respect to the choice of parameterization, in precisely the situation (1) is meant to govern: her evidence gives no reason to favor one alternative over another.

(7) So (1) must be applied to all three parameterizations at once, which yields three incompatible values for a single probability — or to none of them, in which case the principle simply fails to fire in the very case it was introduced to handle.

(8) The dilemma generalizes. For any continuous parameter, a smooth one-to-one reparameterization will carry a uniform distribution to a non-uniform one, so the agent can be maneuvered into any assignment whatever; and even in the discrete cases the same structure appears — a parameterization is just a way of individuating alternatives, and individuation is always a choice (a die rolled once; a die rolled twice, described either as thirty-six ordered pairs or as eleven sums).

(9) Therefore the principle of indifference is incoherent, not merely in Bertrand-style continuous problems but everywhere, and cannot ground even the most basic epistemic probabilities: a rational agent facing a well-shuffled deck she has never inspected would, on this view, have no non-arbitrary constraints on her credences at all.

Something in this proves too much, but I cannot locate the hinge. My suspicions run toward the stretch between (5) and (7) — there seems to be a slide from having no reason to favor a parameterization to having a reason to treat each parameterization's verdict as separately credible — but whenever I try to make the slide precise, it escapes me. A colleague urges the Jaynes-style repair: demand that the measure be invariant under the problem's symmetry group, which he claimed recovers a unique answer here. I am suspicious of this too, since in problems with little or no symmetry it appears to promise uniqueness it cannot deliver, and I worry it quietly converts "the problem has a symmetry" into "we know the physical procedure had that symmetry," which is substantive information we were stipulated not to have.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine when I first accepted it, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether the cost can be confined to continuous cases, as some have hoped, or whether the argument is right that the rot spreads to the discrete cases where indifference seems indispensable. Then, at the very end, state your diagnosis in fixed form: a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the step you reject and a short phrase naming the flaw, e.g. \answer{(5), equivocation between two senses of ...}.
