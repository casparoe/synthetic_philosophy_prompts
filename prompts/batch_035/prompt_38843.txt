At a recent retrospective for a stalled platform migration — two years in, several million spent, the deadline slipped three times — our consultant argued for pressing on, on the grounds that continuing would be keeping a promise to our past selves. On her telling, the team that committed in year one paid a real price on the understanding that the work would be finished; abandoning it now would be like defaulting on a debt, and so honoring sunk costs is simply promise-keeping in the special case where the promisee is ourselves, earlier. Our engineering director waved this off as sentimentality: the money is gone either way, and a sunk cost is like water already under the bridge — fit for grief, not for decision.

I would like you to audit the consultant's analogy rather than the director's slogan. Please proceed as follows:

1. Set out the correspondence table explicitly: within the promise framing, what plays the role of promiser and promisee, of consideration, of the expectation created, of performance, of breach, and of the sanction or standing complaint that ordinarily backs a promise?

2. Identify where the mapping breaks, and then sort the disanalogies: which ones actually matter for the consultant's conclusion (for instance, whether a party with no further interests or expectations can be a promisee, whether release from a promise requires the promisee's consent, and who could ever release us from this one) and which are merely differences of detail that she is entitled to stipulate away.

3. Ask what the analogy quietly imports. In particular: does it presuppose that the past selves' expectations can bind us through later evidence they did not have, and does it conflate two different rational roles the past choice might play — a commitment whose authority survives new information versus mere evidence about value, on the model of a second opinion from someone who knew our constraints well but knows nothing of what we have since learned?

4. Test the framing on cases: a marathon runner at mile eighteen who trained for a year; a reader four hundred pages into a trilogy she has soured on; a couple staying together because of the decades already invested; and our own migration, supposing a competitor has just shipped a product that achieves our project's entire purpose. Which verdicts does the promise framing get right, and where does it misfire relative to a simple preference-for-completion account on which finishing things is just one good among others?

5. Say whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support — that we owe continuation — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, consider whether a better analogy (sunk costs as a signal of reliability to collaborators whose future cooperation depends on it, as self-signaling of perseverance, as a burned bridge whose burning carries costs of its own) would support a weaker but defensible version of her position, and note the fallback move she might make: that even if no particular promise exists, it can still be rational to cultivate a general disposition to finish what one starts, and to act on that disposition even when, considered case by case, it loses. Finally, indicate where the director's dismissal is too quick: in what respects, if any, can honoring sunk costs be rational even though the promise framing fails?

Please write in American English, and keep the treatment rigorous — I care about where each disanalogy does and does not bite, not about a general sermon against sunk costs.
