I'm a third-year PhD student in science and technology studies, and at my committee meeting last week one of my readers shut down a section of my dissertation by declaring that the two methodological traditions I had been comparing are incommensurable, so the comparison itself was incoherent. The section was modest — it argued that one tradition's standards for what counts as a good explanation had proved more fruitful for a particular research problem — but the word did a lot of work, and I honestly could not tell which precise claim it was carrying. Lay out for me the distinct theses that the claim that paradigms are incommensurable could be expressing, ordered from weak to strong. I can see at least a few candidates: that practitioners in different paradigms use different vocabularies and sometimes talk past each other; that there is no neutral algorithm for theory choice that both sides would accept; that the very standards of evaluation — simplicity, accuracy, scope — get reweighted or reinterpreted across paradigms; the later Kuhnian idea of a taxonomic shift, where central kind-terms like mass or planet fail to map cleanly across the divide; and the radical readings, on which no rational comparison is possible at all, communication across the divide is illusory, or scientists literally work in different worlds. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and — this is the part I need most — whether it would actually support dismissing a cross-paradigm comparison of methodological standards. My suspicion is that the reading my committee member needs is stronger than the reading that survives scrutiny, and that Kuhn himself, who resisted the relativist label, would not have endorsed it. Give me a menu with verdicts that I can bring back to the next meeting.
