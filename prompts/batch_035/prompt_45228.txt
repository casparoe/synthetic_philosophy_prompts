I have a question about a pattern in arguments that I can't find a name for, and I'd like your help identifying it — or, if no name exists, in getting the thing properly carved.

Here is the pattern. There is a famous pair of theorems in mathematical logic showing, roughly, that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic is incomplete, and cannot prove its own consistency. What I keep running into is not the theorem itself but a certain use of it: people in other fields invoking it as if it established a limitation of their subject matter. A colleague in law argued that a constitution can never contain the principles for its own legitimate interpretation, since no system can certify itself from within. Someone at an economics workshop claimed markets cannot fully price in their own aggregate behaviour, for the same reason. I have heard versions about the mind (the psyche cannot represent itself completely), about organisations, about ecosystems, and once, memorably, about why a particular company's internal audit function was doomed. The sophistication varies enormously: at one end, careful arguments that human mathematical insight is not mechanical and that formal systems therefore can't capture it; at the other end, pure decoration — a gesture at the theorem's aura, used to make an ordinary admission of uncertainty look like a proven impossibility.

I am not asking whether the theorem applies in these cases; I take it that mostly it does not, since the things being discussed are not formal systems satisfying the theorem's preconditions. What I want to know is whether the argumentative move itself has been identified and named — the borrowing of a precise result about formal systems to license a claim about limitation in domains where nothing like effective axiomatisation, formal language, or consistency-as-defined obtains. The nearest things I know of are an ordinary argument from analogy and a bare appeal to authority, but neither seems to fit, because the invoker usually concedes the target domain isn't literally a formal system and insists the structural similarity does the work anyway. There may also be a genuine phenomenon in the neighbourhood that keeps getting confused with the bogus one — I gather computing has real self-reference limits of its own, and I'd want to know how those differ — so part of what I'm after is where the real limits end and the metaphor begins.

So, three questions. Does this invocatory move have an established name or a literature cataloguing it, as opposed to the separate literature assessing particular applications? How is it best distinguished from argument by analogy and from garden-variety overreach? And if no term exists, please coin one, define it so that the definition separates the version where the target domain actually has the relevant formal structure from the version where it plainly doesn't, and say where you would place the careful arguments about minds on the resulting map. Please write continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
