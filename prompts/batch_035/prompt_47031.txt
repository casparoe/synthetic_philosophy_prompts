I teach an applied ethics seminar for third-year undergraduates — 18 students, one 75-minute session, no prior exposure to the topic. Design an in-class decision game that makes students feel the force of the distinction between harm you bring about as a means and harm you merely foresee as a side effect of achieving some good, without lecturing on it up front; they should not hear the standard name for the distinction until the debrief. The scenario I have in mind: students act as dispatchers for a rural water network during a drought, choosing between routing plans whose outcome numbers are identical but whose causal structures differ — in some plans, a village's loss is itself the mechanism by which the others are supplied (the drop in their reservoir is precisely what drives the flow to the other villages), while in others the same loss is a downstream byproduct of a mechanism that works the same way whether or not that village is harmed. Include at least one round in which students must draft a one-sentence mission order describing what they are doing with the water and what they are letting happen to the village, and a final round in which the numbers favor the "means" plan decisively, so that holding the distinction has a visible cost. Present the design as a numbered list of exactly five items — (1) setup and materials, (2) the rounds themselves, (3) how choices are recorded and tallied publicly, (4) the debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction and finally names it, and (5) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — with each item a sentence or two and no introduction or conclusion. In item (5), also tell me what pattern in the recorded choices would indicate the game is tracking a confound — risk perception, framing effects, the order in which rounds are played — rather than the intending/foreseeing distinction itself, and what minimal change to the design would rule that out.
