I'm the TA for a 2nd year evolution course, and every semester we have students propose an explanation for some trait and sketch how you'd test it. Grading these is a nightmare of just-so stories: everything from giraffe necks to the human chin gets an adaptive story, the adaptation explains the trait, the trait's existence confirms the adaptation, and nobody ever considers that the thing might be a byproduct, a leftover, drift, or a straight-up physical constraint. I've been citing the Gould/Lewontin spandrels paper at them but they just nod and keep doing it. At the same time I don't want to just tell them adaptationism is wrong -- half the time the adaptive story is genuinely the best one, and teaching them to reflexively say "it's a spandrel" would be its own disease.

Can you write a procedure the students can run on their own hypothesis before they hand it in? Something they can actually use in 15 minutes with no lit access beyond what they remember: the questions to ask in order (e.g. is the trait even heritable/variable, what's the null -- drift, constraint, byproduct of something else, is there an independent way to test the function, etc.), what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure stops and tells them "this isn't a testable hypothesis as stated, rewrite it", and where it has to admit it can't decide and they need the actual study. Please be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist.

Then run it on three of the ones I actually got last term: (1) the human chin as an adaptation for stress resistance during chewing, (2) the appendix as a vestigial leftover that we only keep because removing it was historically too dangerous so selection never got a clean shot at it, and (3) sickle-cell allele frequencies as an adaptation to malaria. Also, since you'll probably find one where the procedure gives a verdict I don't like -- I happen to think (2) is basically fine, and I'd want to know if the procedure really flags it, and whether the flag is correct or just an artifact of the checklist being too strict.
