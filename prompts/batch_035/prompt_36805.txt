I'm a novelist working on a historical novel set in Manchester and the surrounding mill towns in the early 1840s, whose protagonist is a self-educated Chartist pamphleteer: a woman who sets type by day, writes unsigned articles by candlelight, and believes, with the fervour of the recently converted, that the six points of the Charter are within a decade's reach. My first draft, according to my editor, committed the sin she calls Whig history: my heroine came across as an early Victorian who had read the twentieth century in advance, her every doubt resolved neatly in the direction we now take to be right, as though the 1840s existed mainly to furnish us with a proto-heroine. I accept that criticism, and I have been reading about Butterfield's objection to organising the past by its convergence upon the present.

My difficulty is what to put in the draft's place. I had assumed, from the way the critique is usually stated, that the lesson of rejecting Whig history is that there was in fact no progress, no genuine forward-looking conviction, and no real tendency in the period toward anything — that the honest corrective is to write my characters as people whose own project must have appeared confused or futile to them, and to strip out any suggestion that they sincerely experienced themselves as working toward a better future they might help bring about. Before I rewrite the novel on that plan, however, I want to check whether I have the logic of the position straight: if Whig history's error is treating the past as a rehearsal for the present, is the cure really to deny my characters their own hopes and their own sense of a future? Or have I swung from one distortion to its mirror image, and if so, precisely where does my reasoning about the debunking of Whig history go wrong?

Please answer in British English. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, and give your reasoning thereafter, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
