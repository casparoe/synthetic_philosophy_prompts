I did my graduate work on the sorites paradox, and for the last several years I have watched the argument between epistemicists and supervaluationists go exactly nowhere, and I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Let me lay out where I think the stall is, so you can tell me whether I've got it right. The epistemicist says a vague predicate like "is bald" has perfectly sharp boundaries -- there is a last hair-count h such that a man with h hairs is bald -- and that our inability to know where these boundaries lie is a matter of our ignorance, not of semantic indecision. The supervaluationist says a vague predicate is associated with a range of acceptable precisifications, that a sentence is true iff true on all of them and false iff false on all of them, and that sentences in the penumbra are neither. Each side has a signature embarrassment. The epistemicist must swallow the claim that there are sharp boundaries whose location is not just unknown but unknowable in principle, and that these boundaries are somehow generated by our use, which never encodes them -- and the standard attempts to explain this via margin-for-error principles (my ability to know that n hairs are bald requires my ability to know that n+1 are) strike even sympathetic readers as a derivation of the mystery rather than a dissolution of it. The supervaluationist, meanwhile, has the problem that the machinery reproduces the disease it was meant to cure: supertruth is itself sharp, so "definitely bald" has a precise extension even though "bald" does not, and the sorites trouble reappears one level up as higher-order vagueness; and each individual precisification draws a line exactly as arbitrary as the epistemicist's, which raises the question of what work the range of them is doing. Attempts to fix this -- iterating "definitely," admitting penumbral connections that constrain which precisifications are admissible, tinkering with the penumbral axioms -- seem to me to generate one paper each and then stop.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: I suspect the two camps are not disagreeing about one thing but are silently talking past each other on at least three. First, the epistemicist seems to me to be answering a question about the logic and semantics we actually have -- why classical logic works for vague discourse, why modus ponens feels non-negotiable even in sorites form -- while the supervaluationist is answering a question about what the meaning of a vague term consists in, namely something extractable from use. Second, I suspect there is a hidden metaphysical disagreement about whether the world's vagueness, if there is any, is owed to language or to language-independent reality, and neither side states its commitments here because both officially treat the issue as semantic. Third, I suspect that at the bottom of the sharp-boundary intuition clash there are simply two protected starting points -- "there are no sharp cutoffs" and "bivalence and excluded middle are non-negotiable" -- such that each side treats the other's protected starting point as a reductio, and no argument reaches bedrock from either side.

So here is what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a survey. I know the standard moves -- Williamson's appeal to safety, the supervaluationist treatment of penumbral connections, the admissibility constraints on precisifications -- and I don't need them restated except insofar as they bear on the structure of the disagreement. Tell me which parts of my hypothesis survive scrutiny and which don't. Is the dispute substantive, or verbal, or a case of two research programs that took the same word "vagueness" to name different problems? Where exactly does the hidden metaphysics enter, if it enters at all -- for instance, does the epistemicist's sharp boundary have to be a fact about the world, or can it be a semantic fact all the way down, and does that distinction even do any work here? And then the forward-looking part: given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress. Is there a result that could settle it -- a formal theorem about the costs of giving up classical logic, a psychological finding about how competent speakers actually classify borderline cases, an independently motivated theory of what it takes to communicate a sharp boundary? Or is this the kind of disagreement where the honest conclusion is that each side's commitments are a package deal, and the debate will only be ended by one program quietly becoming the default working assumption rather than by either side conceding anything? I would rather hear that the field is waiting on something nobody knows how to produce than a false prediction of a decisive argument, so be honest about which it is.
