I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of social science that closes with a unit on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and I am setting the final. I want a single essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, on whether there can be laws of history — but not a question that can be discharged by reciting Popper's standard argument: that the growth of human knowledge is unpredictable, that historical development is conditioned by that growth, and that therefore no predictive science of historical development is possible. Any student who has done the reading can reproduce that syllogism; the question needs to force them somewhere the textbook summary does not go.

Here is the complication I want built in. A colleague of mine in the economics department claims that Popper's central argument is essentially the Lucas critique avant la lettre. Lucas held that econometric relationships estimated under one policy regime break down when the regime changes, because agents revise their expectations and behaviour in light of the new policy; Popper's so-called Oedipus effect — the way a published prediction about society can alter the very social processes it predicts — is, on my colleague's reading, the same phenomenon, and he concludes that Popper deserves little credit, the point being mundane and long familiar to economists. The exam question should ask students to evaluate this comparison: does it illuminate Popper's argument or flatten it? In particular, I want students to consider whether the Lucas critique leaves room for regime-conditional regularities — stable relationships that hold so long as a given policy environment is held fixed — whereas Popper purports to rule out even conditional laws of historical development in a stronger sense; and, if so, students should have to say what grounds that difference in strength. I would also like the question to press them on whether Popper's argument proves too much: if feedback from what agents come to know really precludes lawful regularity in the social realm, does it not equally threaten well-confirmed regularities in other special sciences, such as epidemiology or ecology, where the systems under study are likewise entangled with what people come to know and want? The strong answers will say precisely where those analogies break, rather than gesturing at the autonomy of the social sciences.

Please deliver three things: the exam question as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of roughly 650 words, written at the level you would expect of the best student in the room; and a marking scheme with 25 marks distributed across the must-hit moves, in which each band boundary is defined by a specific argumentative achievement rather than by the mention of a name or a text, together with a one-line note on how students most often lose the marks at issue. Two further requests. First, wherever the model answer or marking scheme takes a stand on a contested interpretation — for instance, on whether the Popper–Lucas comparison holds at the level of logical structure or merely at the level of rhetoric — please state how confident you are in that stand, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of, so I can decide whether to soften it before the paper goes to the external examiner. Second, keep everything self-contained: the question should presuppose only what a student who has read Popper and attended the seminar would know, and the model answer should not lean on any attribution I would need to verify independently.
