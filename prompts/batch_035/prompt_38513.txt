Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology: a proponent of the etiological (selected-effects) account of biological function and a proponent of the causal-role (systemic-capacity) account. The setting is a consulting session at a synthetic biology lab. The lab has built an organism whose genome was written from scratch: among its parts is a metabolic module designed by the engineers to break down a particular industrial solvent, and a sensor copied — nucleotide for nucleotide — from a bacterium in which it had been selected over millions of years to detect arsenic. The engineers have two questions for the philosophers. First: the solvent-breaking module, which is only three weeks old and has no selection history of its own, has begun producing a harmless but useless byproduct — is it malfunctioning, or does it simply have no function to fail at? Second: when the copied arsenic sensor fires and shuts down the organism's growth even though the synthetic organism lives in an environment with no arsenic and the response now serves no purpose, has the sensor done what it is for? The dialogue should turn on how each account handles these cases, and each character should press their strongest general point as well: the etiological theorist should argue that only selection history can ground the normativity of "malfunction" and explain why the heart is for pumping rather than for making heartbeat sounds, while the causal-role theorist should argue that a notion of function which cannot assign one to any part of a three-week-old organism, and which must consult phylogeny to say what a designed artifact-biology is doing, has drifted away from the explanatory needs of working biologists. Both characters should be equally competent and well-informed about the opposing view — no strawmen, no concessions the position could not actually make — and they should respond directly to each other's arguments rather than delivering prepared monologues. Let the dialogue end unresolved but with each character having refined their position under pressure: specify, at the close, what each takes the strongest surviving point of the other side to be.
