I'm a software engineer at a company that builds driver-assistance features, and I've ended up on a reading group with some colleagues from the policy side who keep invoking something called the "responsibility gap" — the worry that when an autonomous system causes harm, nobody in the chain of design and operation has the right combination of control and knowledge to be properly held responsible, so responsibility somehow falls between the human chairs. The case they always gesture at is the Uber self-driving test vehicle that struck and killed Elaine Herzberg in Tempe, Arizona in March 2018, and I'd like to understand whether that case actually shows what they think it shows, so I'm asking you to work it through carefully. For orientation, the salient facts as I understand them: the Volvo XC90 was under autonomous control in a public-road test; the safety driver, Rafaela Vasquez, was looking down at a streaming video on her phone in the moments before impact; the perception system had detected Herzberg roughly six seconds before the crash but kept reclassifying her (as an unrecognized object, then a vehicle, then a bicycle), which suppressed any decisive response; the car's built-in Volvo automatic emergency braking had been deliberately disabled so it wouldn't conflict with Uber's experimental system; the NTSB later criticized Uber's safety culture; the prosecutor declined to bring charges against Uber the company; and in 2023 Vasquez pled guilty to one count of endangerment and received three years of probation.

What I want is a disciplined analysis of this episode through the responsibility-gap concept, running in both directions. First, map the theory onto the case: who are the candidate responsibility-bearers (the company, its engineers, the safety driver, the regulators who allowed lightly supervised testing on public roads, the victim), and for each, state what the gap argument would say about whether they had the control and foreknowledge that responsibility is usually taken to require. Then tell me where the theory breaks or goes silent — because on the surface, the legal system did assign responsibility, to the single human in the driver's seat, and I want to know whether that outcome refutes the gap thesis, merely papers over the gap by punishing the person with the least authority over the system's design, or shows that the gap is really a gap in distribution rather than in existence (everyone bears a little, nobody bears enough). I'd also like you to say whether the flickering object classification changes the analysis: does an error that no human engineer could have specifically foreseen make this genuinely different from an ordinary product defect, or is that just what engineering negligence looks like when you dress it in software? Finally, tell me what this case teaches someone like me who actually builds these systems — does it support the "meaningful human control" response to the gap, or does it suggest the gap framing misdirects attention from ordinary questions of corporate and engineering responsibility? Please write your response as continuous prose only, in the style of a long, well-organized email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. You can name philosophers' positions and arguments in general terms, but don't attempt citations or a bibliography, since I'll track those down myself.
