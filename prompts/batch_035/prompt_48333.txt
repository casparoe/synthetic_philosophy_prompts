I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the classic objections to utilitarianism. Bernard Williams keeps coming up, and I've managed to confuse myself in a way I'm hoping you can untangle, because my editor says I can't publish 3000 words on the man until I actually understand what the objection *is*. Here's my problem. One thing everyone repeats: Williams argued that utilitarianism destroys integrity — if you have to treat your own projects and attachments as just one input among infinitely many others, weighed no differently from anyone else's, you can't really have a life of your own; you become a point of view through which the general welfare gets maximized. That's the version where utilitarianism "violates integrity," and it seems to be about what the theory demands of you. But there's also this other Williams thing, the famous bit where a man rescues his drowning wife and Williams suggests that if the man had needed some further justification — say, that rescuing his wife was the act that best promoted the general good, or that morality permits saving one's wife — then that would have been one thought too many. People constantly present these as if they're one and the same objection, but they don't feel the same to me at all. The integrity thing seems to be about how much the theory can demand (it asks too much, it lets you be sacrificed), while the one-thought-too-many thing seems to be about what should be going through the agent's head at the moment of action.

So here's what I want explained. Are these actually two distinct objections to utilitarianism, or two sides of one coin, and if they come apart, exactly where does the seam run? It complicates things that the one-thought-too-many point isn't even obviously an objection about what's *right* — saving your wife is presumably fine on utilitarianism too — it's about how you decide, which makes me wonder whether it's a moral objection at all or something more like a point in moral psychology about how deliberation works for people with commitments. I've also picked up, secondhand, that some philosophers distinguish between reading Williams as claiming utilitarianism gets the *verdicts* wrong (his Jim case, where you're invited to shoot one innocent so that twenty others are spared) versus reading him as claiming something about the *deliberative standpoint* itself, and that the strength of the case is very different depending on which reading you take. If the one-thought-too-many remark only supports the deliberative reading, what exactly is supposed to be wrong with the extra thought — is it bad taste, psychological implausibility, or does it reveal a structural flaw in the theory that we can state precisely? And does the integrity objection (the one about your projects and Williams's complaint that utilitarianism makes all outcomes everyone's responsibility in this diffuse, alienated way) actually depend on the deliberative point, or could you accept one while rejecting the other?

Ideally explain it with concrete examples as you go, since my readers won't know the jargon and honestly neither do I at this point. Also, if you think the two objections really do come apart, tell me which one is doing more work in Williams's actual case against utilitarianism — I want the piece to have a thesis and right now it doesn't. Don't worry about page references or anything, I can track those down later, I just need the logical geography mapped clearly.
