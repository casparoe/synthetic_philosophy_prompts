I am grading take-home midterms for a graduate seminar in collective choice theory, and I would like an independent read on one script. The examination question, which I reproduce verbatim, was the following:

"State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem precisely, specifying each of its assumptions and defining every term that appears in your statement (including 'social choice function,' 'strategy-proof,' and 'dictatorial'). Then: (a) explain which of the assumptions are essential, exhibiting for each assumption a rule that satisfies all the others while violating that one; and (b) say what the theorem does and does not establish about the prevalence of strategic voting in actual elections."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that any social choice function with at least three alternatives that satisfies unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, and unanimity (equivalently, surjectivity onto the set of alternatives) is manipulable — that is, not strategy-proof. A social choice function maps each profile of voters' ordinal preferences to a single winning alternative. Strategy-proofness means that for every voter, reporting her true preference weakly dominates every misreport, whatever the others report; so no voter can ever strictly gain by misrepresenting. A rule is dictatorial if there is a voter whose preferences determine the social outcome — that is, a voter such that, for every pair of alternatives x and y, society ranks x above y whenever she does.

"Turning to which assumptions are essential: the three-alternative condition is essential, since with two alternatives majority rule (with any fixed tie-breaking convention) is onto, non-dictatorial, and strategy-proof — a voter who reports the opposite of her preference either leaves the outcome unchanged or flips it to an alternative she likes less. Unrestricted domain is essential in the sense that the theorem fails on restricted domains: with single-peaked preferences on a one-dimensional policy axis, any non-dictatorial onto rule is strategy-proof, which is why the median voter's ideal point wins and why committee decisions over a left-right spectrum are immune to manipulation. Non-dictatorship is essential, since a dictatorship is trivially strategy-proof. The unanimity/onto condition, however, is not doing any real work; it is a bookkeeping condition. Any rule that violated it would be unresponsive to preferences altogether, and a non-dictatorial rule is by definition responsive to more than one voter, so the condition is automatically satisfied in any case of interest.

"As for actual elections: the theorem proves that in any plurality-rule contest with three or more serious candidates, voters whose favorite is not a frontrunner do better to vote for their preferred frontrunner, and that this is why third parties rarely win under first-past-the-post. One might object that voters rarely know when they are pivotal, so in practice the theorem has limited bite."
