My colleague in philosophy of mind has, over years of seminar discussion, settled into a stable pattern of rulings on which reported phenomena she will call genuine cognitive penetration of perception and which she waves off as something else — post-perceptual judgment, attentional selection, perceptual learning, or bias in reporting. I have been asked to predict her rulings on three new cases that her seminar will take up next term, and I want you to commit to definite predictions with your reasoning, rather than survey the possibilities and leave the choosing to me.

Her track record, as best I have it:

1. Knowing that the two segments of the Müller–Lyer figure are equal in length does not stop them looking unequal. Ruling: not penetration — the belief makes no difference to how the lines look.
2. The classic ambiguous figure that reads as a letter when flanked by letters and as a numeral when flanked by numerals: not penetration. It is a decision about how to classify the figure, she says; the figure itself looks the same either way.
3. An acoustically ambiguous consonant, engineered to sit midway between two phonemes, is heard as whichever phoneme would complete a real word in the frame it is embedded in, and as the other phoneme in the mirrored frame where that one would complete a nonword. Ruling: penetration.
4. A patient with a snake phobia who reports that the snake in the case looks bigger and closer than it measurably is. Ruling: penetration.
5. Someone scanning a crowd for a friend in a red coat, for whom red items seem to leap out of the crowd: not penetration — that is where attention is allocated, not how things look.
6. A grieving man who keeps momentarily taking strangers on the street to be his dead father: not penetration — the faces do not look different; he misjudges whose faces they are.

The three new cases:

(a) Subjects wearing loaded backpacks judge a hill as markedly steeper than they judge the same hill unburdened, and the researchers who report this maintain that the difference lies in the look of the hill itself, not merely in the numbers people produce when asked for an estimate.

(b) A veteran perfumer, trained over twenty years, for whom two scents that are nearly identical to everyone else present as unmistakably different — she insists it is not that she infers the difference; the smells are simply as different from one another as vanilla and gasoline, and she cannot smell them as the same even when she tries.

(c) A subject told, falsely and once, that the wine in her glass comes from an expensive bottle, who thereupon reports that it tastes richer and more complex — no training, a single session, one belief.

I care less about which of the three predictions is philosophically correct than about the following: state the rule, or rules, implicit in her six rulings, and note that at least two should fit those rulings equally well — name them and say precisely where they come apart; say which rule you believe she is actually running, and why; commit to a definite verdict on each of (a), (b), and (c) under that rule; and tell me what single further case, if I could put it to her, would best discriminate between the rules you have identified.
