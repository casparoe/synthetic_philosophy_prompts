I'm teaching an intro ethics course for second-years, about 22 students, one 75-minute session, and next week's topic is lying -- specifically the small stuff, the "no, that haircut is fine" kind. My students keep splitting into two camps that talk past each other: the "honesty is always better, brutal honesty is a virtue" camp and the "politeness is a form of care" camp, and neither notices that some cultures have basically settled this argument in opposite directions. I want them to feel their way into that instead of just hearing about Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness secondhand, since I suspect the whole debate rests on something they've never isolated: that an indirect communication isn't a lie when both parties share the code for decoding it.

So here's roughly what I want you to design -- a two-phase game. Phase one: students pair up, one gives a one-minute pitch on some harmless assigned topic (why their favorite topping is the best one, whether pineapple on pizza is a crime, that kind of thing), and their partner gives feedback on it. But each feedback-giver draws a condition card. Amsterdam condition: no hedges, no softeners, no "I see what you were going for" -- state your assessment of the pitch directly. Kyoto condition: you may never state a negative assessment directly -- you have to convey it through understatement, praise of something else, questions, whatever -- while staying pleasant. After each round the receiver writes down what they think the giver's actual opinion was, and rates how much they'd want to be paired with that person again for a later round. Pairs rotate a few times so everyone tries both giving and receiving in both conditions. Here's the twist I want built in: the givers should be working from evaluations that I've secretly adjusted -- sometimes harsher than what the giver actually thought, sometimes milder -- so we can see whether directness transmits truth or just transmits noise with more confidence attached. Phase two is the part I care most about: some rounds should be run cross-condition, where a Kyoto-style giver faces a receiver who's only ever been in the Amsterdam condition and doesn't know the code. Then the debrief.

For the debrief I need the mapping to the philosophy worked out: where the game illustrates the Kantian thought that deception treats someone as a means (they're acting on a false picture of their situation), where it illustrates the consequentialist defense of white lies, and -- the bit I most want them to get -- why the cross-condition rounds misfire without anyone having lied at all. If the receiver just doesn't know the politeness conventions, the speaker hasn't deceived them in the way a liar does; something else has gone wrong, and I want the debrief to name it. Then the honest notes: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing the Kyoto-condition students will either break character and blurt things out, or lean so hard into vagueness that nothing transmits at all, and the ratings of "would work with again" will be all over the place), and which feature of real white lies the game can't reproduce no matter how I set it up -- I suspect it's that rotating pairs make relational damage artificially cheap, since students know they never have to see that person again, which will bias them toward thinking bluntness costs less than it does. If you can think of a better way to rig the adjusted-evaluations part so it actually tests what I want, tell me, because right now I'm not sure it does. Also flag anywhere the game quietly stacks the deck in favor of one side of the debate -- I don't want to run an activity that just confirms whatever the students already believed walking in.
