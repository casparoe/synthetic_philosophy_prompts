The exam question read:

"State Arrow's impossibility theorem as precisely as you can, defining each of the conditions it imposes on a social welfare function. Then explain why pairwise majority rule, although it appears to satisfy each of the conditions, nevertheless fails to be an acceptable social welfare function; illustrate with an example. Next, explain which of Arrow's conditions the Borda count violates, and illustrate that violation as well. Finally, address the informational assumption of the theorem: would supplementing ordinal preference information with cardinal utility information allow us to escape the impossibility result? Defend your answer."

The student's answer read, in full:

"Arrow's theorem says that when there are three or more alternatives, no voting system can satisfy four conditions simultaneously: unrestricted domain (every logically possible profile of individual preference orderings is admissible as input), Pareto (if every individual ranks x above y, the social ranking must rank x above y), independence of irrelevant alternatives (the social ranking of x against y must depend only on individuals' rankings of x against y, and not at all on where individuals place any third alternative z), and non-dictatorship (there is no individual whose ranking coincides with the social ranking on every profile). So the theorem tells us that every electoral system — plurality, runoff, approval voting, the Borda count — must violate at least one of these conditions, which is why no electoral system is fully fair.

Pairwise majority rule satisfies all four conditions. Unrestricted domain: it accepts any profile. Pareto: if everyone ranks x above y, a majority obviously does, so x beats y. IIA: whether x beats y in a pairwise vote depends only on how voters rank x against y. Non-dictatorship: no single voter's ballot determines every pairwise outcome. What goes wrong is not a violation of any of Arrow's conditions but the output: with three alternatives the social 'ranking' can be cyclical. Suppose one third of the voters rank a above b above c, one third rank b above c above a, and one third rank c above a above b. Then a majority prefers a to b, a majority prefers b to c, and a majority prefers c to a. There is no social ordering at all, so majority rule fails to produce a social welfare function, since Arrow requires the output to be a complete and transitive ranking.

The Borda count violates independence of irrelevant alternatives. In Borda, each voter's bottom-ranked alternative receives 0 points, the second-from-bottom 1 point, and so on up the ballot, and alternatives are ranked by total points. Here is a violation: let there be five voters. In profile 1, three voters rank a > b > c and two voters rank b > c > a. Borda totals are a = 6, b = 7, c = 2, so b outranks a. In profile 2, the same three voters rank a > c > b and the same two voters rank b > a > c. Every voter's relative ranking of a and b is exactly what it was before. But now the totals are a = 8, b = 4, c = 3, so a outranks b. The social ranking of a and b flipped even though no individual's view of a versus b changed. The deeper reason Borda violates IIA is that it uses cardinal information about preference intensity: by assigning points, it registers how strongly voters prefer the alternatives, and this intensity information is precisely what independence forbids the social ranking of a pair to depend on.

On the last question: the impossibility result is driven by Arrow's restriction to ordinal, interpersonally non-comparable preference information. Arrow worked in a framework where only orderings are meaningful. If we supplement orderings with cardinal utilities, the escape is straightforward: the utilitarian sum-ranking rule satisfies unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence, and non-dictatorship, since the sum of utilities ranks x above y whenever it should and depends only on individuals' utilities for x and y. So cardinality is the culprit, and admitting cardinal information dissolves the theorem; the remaining dispute is just whether interpersonal comparisons of utility can be made sense of, which many economists doubt."
