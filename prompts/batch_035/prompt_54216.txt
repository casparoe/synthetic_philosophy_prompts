I hold a view I've come to think of as the "universal decoder" thesis: the meaning of a message is an intrinsic property of the message itself, and a sufficiently capable intelligence could in principle extract that meaning from the message alone, without any shared language, culture, or prior context. On this view, the familiar distinction between a message's frame (the signal that says "here is a message"), its outer layer (the decoding machinery that tells you how to read it), and its inner layer (the content) is a practical remark about degrees of decryption difficulty, not a principled objection. The outer layer is just more ciphertext; the chicken-and-egg problem — that you'd need to understand the outer message before you could use it to understand anything — is an engineering challenge that dissolves with enough computing power and the right statistical methods. After all, we routinely decipher unknown scripts and dead languages, and a far greater mind should do the same for an alien signal from scratch. I'd like to know what would count against this thesis.

Please give me the defeaters, ranked from most to least serious. In particular, I'd like to know: which results about actual decipherment successes and failures cut against me and which support me (I'm thinking of cases like Linear B, the Indus script, the Voynich manuscript, and decipherments that leaned on bilingual artifacts like the Rosetta Stone — but feel free to bring in others)? What kinds of messages could exist that no amount of intelligence, however great, could decode from the message alone, and is the existence of such messages a matter of logic or merely of contingent fact? Is there an argument from compression, information theory, or the one-time pad showing that some encodings are formally indistinguishable from noise or from rival plaintexts, so that "sufficiently capable" can't help? And conversely, what does my thesis predict that its rivals don't — where would a defender of the view that meaning lives partly in the receiver's shared context have to concede ground?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the "sufficiently capable intelligence" clause has insulated the thesis from falsification altogether, and whether that insulation is a fatal flaw for a claim of this kind or just the ordinary abstractness of an in-principle claim about all possible minds. Tell me which objections a committed universal-decoder proponent should concede as genuine evidence against the view, and which they can legitimately dismiss, and where I personally should update.
