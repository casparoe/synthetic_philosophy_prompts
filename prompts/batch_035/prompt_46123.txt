I write historical fiction — three novels set in the 1890s and a fourth underway — and for a decade now I have been caught between two camps of critics, each of which never moves an inch. When I invent a conversation between two documented figures who plausibly met, the first camp accuses me of betraying history: on their view, which goes back to Ranke, the only honest relation to the past is the historian's — reconstruct it from the archives, show how it actually was, and keep the writer's shaping hand out of it as far as evidence permits; the novelist's inventions are, at best, a licensed contamination. The second camp, drawing on Carr and Hayden White, tells me the opposite: since the historian already selects her facts, arranges them, and answers a question the documents never asked themselves, my inventions differ from a scholarly monograph in degree, not in kind. Carr's image of the historian as fisherman, with the facts swimming in an ocean and only those he catches reaching the table, and White's argument that historical works are emplotted as romance, tragedy, comedy, or satire before any weighing of evidence occurs, both seem to collapse the distance between my craft and Ranke's.

What strikes me is that the two camps never engage each other in my Q&As; they repeat themselves at one another, and the positions look essentially unchanged since the nineteenth century and the 1970s respectively. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not about the past at all but about what counts as access to the past: whether selection and narrative shaping are a distorting filter to be minimized, or the very organ of historical cognition, so that removing them would leave not a purer past but no past at all. It may also be that the sides are answering different questions under one heading — reconstruction versus composition — or that they hold bedrock intuitions about whether the past itself is even an available object, intuitions that neither can argue for without assuming them.

I am a novelist, not a philosopher of history, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict on who is right. Using what each side actually claims — not just their labels — tell me what kind of disagreement this is: substantive or verbal; a clash over standards of evidence; two questions run under one name; or something else. Then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, some class of cases that could decide it — or whether no progress should be expected, and why. Please keep your answer to 500 words at most; I would rather have a tightly argued diagnosis than an even-handed catalogue.
