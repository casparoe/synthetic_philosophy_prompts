My phil of science prof runs the Dutch book argument in basically every seminar like it's settled business. The way he tells it: your credence in p just is the price you'd buy or sell a one-dollar bet on p at. So if you'd buy the bet on p for 60 cents and also buy the bet on not-p for 60 cents, you've guaranteed yourself a twenty-cent loss no matter how the world turns out, and only a fool does that. His line is that a system of degrees of belief is literally a price list, the probability axioms are just no-arbitrage bookkeeping for price lists, and therefore anyone whose credences violate the axioms isn't being kind of irrational, they're not really in the belief business at all. He leans on this hard to dismiss people who talk about vague or non-numerical confidence as confused.

Audit the analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what — credence to price, sure-loss to arbitrage, the axioms to the no-arbitrage constraints — then where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion he wants, versus which ones are just noise. Things I half remember from seminars that seem relevant: the bookie has to know your prices before quoting you, which no real market lets anyone do; the bets pay in money but you care about utility, and if the value of a dollar shifts between outcomes the whole ledger goes sideways; there's a reading where it's only dispositions to accept bets, not the occurrent feeling of confidence, that get priced at all; and apparently there's a diachronic version where you can be booked across time unless you update by conditionalization, and I can't tell if that's the same arbitrage or a different animal the static price-list story has no room for.

Then the verdict: does his dismissal of incoherent or vague believers survive once the disanalogies are on the table? My worry is that the argument at most shows a certain kind of betting system can't be exploited, and getting from there to "incoherence is irrational" needs extra premises — that you ought to be such a system, that money is linear in whatever you care about — that the price-list picture quietly imports. And is there a better analogy for the actual epistemic failing? Something like a map that's internally inconsistent so it can't guide you anywhere — or is that just the betting argument wearing different clothes, since an inconsistent map only bites when you try to act on both parts?

If you think I've set this up wrong — say the price reading of credence isn't really what de Finetti or Ramsey had in mind, or the argument was never supposed to carry the weight my prof puts on it — please just say so instead of working with my framing. And flag for me which parts of your answer are standard and where you're stretching.
