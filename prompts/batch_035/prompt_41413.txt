Hey, so I teach an undergrad seminar on ethics and public policy — 16 students, one 80-minute session coming up — and I want to design an in-class game around why "fund whatever problem is biggest" is a bad allocation rule. You seem like the right person to ask: I'm picturing someone who's spent years designing classroom simulations for philosophy and econ courses, the kind of facilitator who knows that a game lives or dies on its payoff scheme. So think of yourself that way, and before writing anything up, spend some real effort brainstorming — sketch at least two candidate payoff mechanics, then tell me which you chose and why the alternatives break.

Here's the setup I'm imagining. The class is split into four boards of four students, each board acting as a small foundation ("the Meridian Fund" or whatever you like) with a fixed budget to allocate across five causes. Each cause comes as a dossier card with invented numbers — I made them up, they're not real statistics, and I'll tell the students that; the point is the structure, not the facts. Something like: (1) intestinal worms infecting 400,000 schoolkids in a fictional river valley, $12M to fund the full program, worth 240 "benefit points" if fully funded, with $9M already committed by a regional health ministry; (2) arsenic-contaminated wells serving 90,000 people in a fictional port city, $30M to fully remediate, 180 points, only $2M committed so far; (3) basic research on a vaccine platform for a made-up mosquito-borne fever, potentially 900 points but $220M to complete, $40M committed; (4) after-school tutoring for 15,000 students in the capital, $8M, 60 points, $1M committed; (5) flood relief in the delta, very visible and urgent, $20M, 120 points, $6M committed. Each board gets $50M in play money. The key mechanic I want: money piled onto a cause that's already fully funded does nothing, and other funders' commitments count toward filling a cause — so a dollar matters at the margin, not at the headline. You'll need to work out the exact scoring rule for which board "earns" which points when several boards fund the same cause; that's part of what I want your brainstorming to settle.

Somewhere in the middle there should be a reveal — a news flash that a big outside funder just announced major commitments that change the gaps — forcing the boards to reallocate and feel that the funding landscape moves under them. I'd also like a competitive element (bragging rights, no real money) so boards aren't just politely splitting things.

The students should run into the trap themselves: I expect most boards to chase the biggest number on the card, and I want the wasted dollars to be visible. Don't introduce the words "importance, tractability, neglectedness" until the debrief, and in the debrief map what happened in the game onto that framework — where the biggest-number instinct failed, where the completion of an almost-funded cause was the real steal, where a cause was just too big for their budget to touch.

One last thing on format: write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — like a long email to a colleague.
