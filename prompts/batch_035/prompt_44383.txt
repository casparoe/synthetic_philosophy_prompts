my phil of math reading group is about to hit Benacerraf's dilemma, and before we launch into the usual platonism vs fictionalism slapfight I want us to be clear about what we're even asking of these views. So write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of mathematical truth and mathematical knowledge has to deliver. The obvious two are the semantic one (mathematical sentences look like ordinary declaratives, subject and predicate and all — 'there are infinitely many primes' has surface grammar just like 'there are infinitely many groundhogs in Ohio' — so does the account owe us a uniform semantics, or is it allowed to say the surface grammar is misleading and, if so, what licenses that move?) and the epistemological one (if mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, how do beliefs about them get to count as knowledge rather than lucky guesses — and note this one is a constraint you can state weakly, as a mere reliability requirement, or strongly, as a demand for some genuine access relation, and I'd like to know which version you're endorsing and why, since my professor keeps waving at a reliabilist escape route where math beliefs just need to track truth the way perception does, no causal story required, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or hand-waving).

But I also want the less-discussed ones on the list, like: does the account owe us an explanation of why the identification questions are non-questions (von Neumann's 3 is {0,1,2}, the Zermelo coding makes 3 something like {{{∅}}}, both reductions do everything we want — so is 3 identical to one of these sets, both, or neither, and a good theory should tell us why nothing turns on this rather than just changing the subject); does it have to apply uniformly to elementary arithmetic and to claims that no actual proof settles (large cardinal axioms, undecided conjectures — is it ok for a theory to treat these differently, and can it even draw that line without smuggling in a philosophy of math); and should it explain the applicability of math to physical science at all or is that somebody else's problem.

For each requirement give the case behind it, mark it as non-negotiable or as one camp's prejudice dressed up as a neutral constraint, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — honestly that last part is the thing I most want to see, since as best I can tell the 'dilemma' just is the claim that the strong semantic constraint plus the strong epistemological constraint can't both be met by platonism, and I want to know whether that's a theorem-shaped fact about the requirements or just an artifact of stating them too strictly. Then at the end, one line each on how platonism, formalism, fictionalism, and structuralism do against the list — keep it that short, the scoring is for us to fight about in the group, the requirements are the actual work.
