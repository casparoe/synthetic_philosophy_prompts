"The argument from illusion begins with cases where things look different from how they are, like a straight stick half-submerged in water looking bent. The stick is not actually bent, but something is bent, and that something is what we are directly aware of, so it must be a mental object, a sense-datum. The argument then says that since the veridical case is caused in exactly the same way in the brain as the illusory case, the immediate object of experience must be the same in both, so even in the good case we only directly perceive sense-data that represent physical objects, never the objects themselves.

The disjunctivist rejects the common kind assumption, which is the assumption that the illusory experience and the veridical experience are states of the same fundamental kind. What the disjunctivist denies is that the two cases are indistinguishable. On the disjunctivist view, experience is disjunctive: in the good case you stand in a direct relation to a mind-independent object, and in the bad case you don't, and there is no mental item common to both. Since in the good case we perceive physical objects directly, this view is also known as naive realism, and it means the sense-datum theorist is wrong that all perception is mediated.

Another point in favor of disjunctivism is that sense-datum theory cannot explain the causal relation between the inner objects and the physical world, whereas the disjunctivist just says in the good case you're in direct contact with the external thing itself.

Finally, disjunctivism has an important epistemological payoff: it shows external world skepticism about perception is wrong, because in the good case we can know we are perceiving real objects."
