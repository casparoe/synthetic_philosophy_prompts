My question was: "According to deflationary theories of truth, what does calling a statement true add to the statement itself? Explain the redundancy idea, and then explain why deflationists nevertheless think we still need a truth predicate in the language, illustrating with an example."

The member answered as follows: "The deflationary theory, going back to Ramsey, holds that calling a statement true adds nothing: to say that it is true that snow is white is just to say that snow is white. The predicate 'is true' is therefore redundant, and Ramsey concluded that it could be dropped from the language without losing anything — we keep it mainly for stylistic emphasis, as when we say 'that's true!' to agree with someone. Tarski's biconditionals, such as the claim that 'snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white, are what the deflationist uses to fix the meaning of the word 'true', and they show that truth is not a deep property of propositions at all, just a device of abbreviation. That is why, if every statement were spelled out plainly, we would never need to call anything true, and the predicate could disappear from the language tomorrow."
