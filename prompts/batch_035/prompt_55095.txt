Before retiring I spent four decades as an experimental physicist, and in the years since I have been reading philosophy of economics as a hobby. One debate there has come to seem genuinely stuck to me, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than a verdict. The dispute is over what preference and utility actually are. On one side stand the revealed-preference theorists, in the tradition of Samuelson's work from the late 1930s: preferences are nothing over and above patterns of choice, utility is merely a bookkeeping device for representing a consistent chooser, and a preference that could never show up in behavior under any circumstances is not a scientific concept at all. On the other side are economists and philosophers who insist that preferences are psychological states that cause choices, and that collapsing them into choice makes the theory either circular — choices reveal preferences only because preferences were defined as whatever choices reveal — or silent exactly where welfare economics needs a voice, namely when addiction, temptation, or framing drives a wedge between what a person chooses and what is good for them. Both camps have worked with essentially the same formal machinery for eighty years, and as far as I can tell nobody moves an inch. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but three layered together: a methodological question about what economics is entitled to talk about, an empirical question about what actually causes choices, and a normative question about whose interests should count — with the camps silently swapping between them mid-argument. Is that right, or is there a genuine substantive dispute hiding here after all? I confess that as someone who spent a career measuring things, I came in sympathetic to the operationalist side, which may be why the impasse irritates me. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, an experimental program, or the honest admission that the question, as posed, is ill-posed and why.
