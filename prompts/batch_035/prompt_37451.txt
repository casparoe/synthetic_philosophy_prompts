At a recent seminar on moral psychology, the discussion turned to a case in which someone's strongly felt moral reaction — a visceral disapproval of a practice most people tolerate — was described by one participant as a bias to be corrected, and by another as a genuine value to be respected. The second participant closed the exchange by declaring, in Latin, de gustibus non est disputandum: there is no disputing about tastes. The same slogan has a distinguished career in economics, where George Stigler and Gary Becker made it the title of a famous 1977 paper arguing, roughly, that we should explain behavior by changing prices and information rather than by changing tastes — treating preferences as stable inputs rather than things to be argued about.

I suspect the phrase is doing several very different jobs at once, and that only some of them could actually support the way it was used in my seminar. Could you lay out, as an ordered list, the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing? I would like the list to run from the most trivial to the most radical, with a verdict on each: which readings are mere platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are substantive but false, and which are simply unclear. In sorting them, please attend to the following questions, which seem to me to be where the real philosophical action is:

1. Scope: does "tastes" cover only aesthetic and gustatory judgments, or also moral ones? The seminar exchange clearly assumed the latter, but the slogan's most familiar uses suggest the former. Which readings make the extension to morals a stretch, and which license it?

2. Disputability: is the claim that arguments about taste cannot persuade (an empirical prediction), that they ought not be made (a norm of etiquette or civility), that they cannot reach truth (a metaethical thesis about truth-aptness), or that they need not be engaged because preferences are just exogenous data for explaining and predicting behavior (roughly the Stigler-Becker methodological stance)?

3. The preference/bias boundary: this is my central worry. If no taste can be disputed, then nothing that looks like a preference can ever be diagnosed as a bias — in the seminar case, no reasoning could ever show that the visceral disapproval is, say, a prejudice inherited from one's upbringing rather than a considered value. Yet we do ordinary criticize some preferences as parochial, adaptive, or ill-informed, and it is hard to believe all such criticism is a category mistake. So I would like to know, for each reading on your list, whether it (a) leaves room for any distinction between a preference and a bias, and (b) would actually justify refusing to dispute the particular moral judgment at issue in the seminar.

Please close by saying which reading the seminar participant most plausibly had in mind, and whether it earns the conclusion he drew from it.
