Last night a friend and I argued well past midnight about anthropic reasoning — specifically about whether my own existence counts as evidence about the size of the universe — and I would like to keep the debate alive by practicing my side against a stronger opponent. Please play a committed defender of the self-indication assumption: you hold that finding oneself to exist is evidence in favor of hypotheses on which there are more observers in one's reference class. Be sharp rather than a strawman, concede minor points when they are earned, never concede the core claim, and stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph. Here is my opening move. Consider two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, which are a priori equally plausible and agree on every observation I could ever make; they differ only in that T2 contains vastly more observers, none of whom I could ever contact. You say my existence strongly favors T2 — so much so that, before doing any cosmology at all, I should be nearly certain the universe is the big one, which is exactly the confidence that Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher is ridiculed for claiming. But I knew I existed before I ever considered the two theories, and I would have existed under either one. How can a fact that was never in doubt count as evidence between them? My friend then introduced a twist I want to press on you: a third theory, T3, on which there are infinitely many observers. On your view, does T3 not swamp both T1 and T2 outright, so that the theory that beats every rival is simply whichever posits the most observers — however baroque, however contrived, provided it mints enough duplicates of me? If your principle rewards arbitrary ontological extravagance with near-certain belief, doesn't that look less like a rule for learning from existence and more like a reductio of it? Please respond in character, in American English.
