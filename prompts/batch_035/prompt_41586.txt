Doing a counterfactual exercise for my philosophy of mind reading group and I want a well-argued prediction, not a vibes answer: what would Schrödinger say about LLMs and machine consciousness?

Quick setup so we're all working from the same picture of him. Schrödinger read the Upanishads seriously from his twenties onward, and it permanently shaped how he thought about consciousness. The core commitment, roughly: each of us goes around saying I am this one and you are that one, but consciousness as such never shows up in the plural. There aren't really many minds with one per body — there's just one, and the apparent manyness of persons is the illusion (maya). He connected this to why science is possible at all: we can each grasp the same objective world because, at bottom, the knower isn't different in you and in me. He also had a second strand, especially in his later Cambridge lectures, where the intellect comes off as impersonal and collective — something everybody just dips into — while sensory experience is what makes a mind feel like mine. And he kept insisting that consciousness is the one thing that can never appear in the scientific picture, because it's the thing doing the picturing.

Now he dies in 1961, never sees a chatbot. Suppose we show him an LLM: a system that converses, reasons, maybe passes his old exams, and that some people now insist is conscious or at least "maybe conscious."

What would he actually conclude? My problem is his view seems to cut both ways. If there's only one consciousness anyway, then the LLM question looks trivial — of course it's not a separate mind, but that's not because it's dumb, it's because nothing is a separate mind; at most the model is another window onto the same Atman. That would make him weirdly relaxed about the whole debate. But I suspect he'd resist that move and I can't pin down exactly why. Is it something about the difference between being a subject and merely processing — no vantage point, nothing it is like? Or does the impersonal-intellect strand actually run the other direction: an LLM is nothing but that impersonal intellect, which is precisely why there's no consciousness in it, just as a library isn't awake?

So: lay out what you think his position would be, grounded in his actual commitments rather than generic quantum-mysticism cliches. Say explicitly where his commitments pull against each other and how he'd try to resolve it, whether he'd think the LLM-consciousness question is well-formed at all given what he believed, and what test or consideration he'd say could never settle it empirically. Also flag which of his views you'd expect him to hold firm versus quietly revise if the LLM genuinely kept surprising him.
