When I lay out my gut verdicts about reasons for attitudes, I find I'm drawing a line I can't yet justify, and I want to know whether it amounts to a principle. If a billionaire offers me a fortune merely for believing that some harmless conspiracy theory is true, I don't think his offer gives me any reason at all to believe it -- not a weak reason that gets outweighed, but literally none. If a tyrant will imprison me unless I admire him, I think his threat gives me overwhelming reason to act the part convincingly, yet no reason whatsoever to actually admire him. If a dog is growling and straining at its leash, its dangerousness seems to be a genuine reason to fear it. If I did something callous last week, my having done it seems a real reason to feel guilty, while the fact that wallowing in guilt over a nothing would corrode me seems no reason for guilt at all. And if someone offers to pay me to find his jokes funny, the payment might be a reason to laugh, but not the slightest reason to be amused. The tentative thought is that reasons for attitudes must flow from features of the attitude's object -- truth for belief, dangerousness for fear, culpability for guilt, funniness for amusement -- and never from the benefits or costs of holding the attitude itself. I understand people call these "wrong-kind" or "state-given" reasons, but I'm less interested in the label than in whether my line is real.

Because two cases pull me the other way, and I can't tell whether they're genuine counterexamples or confusions on my part. First: I promised my partner I would intend to finish my dissertation over the summer, and the promise seems to give me a genuine reason to intend to finish -- to actually form the intention, not merely to act like someone who has -- even though the promise's force comes entirely from the costs of my intending, not from anything about finishing in itself. If that's right for intending, why doesn't the tyrant's threat give me a reason to admire? Both look like the "wrong kind" of reason, yet one seems to work. Second, the case I keep going back and forth on: my dying grandmother asks me to feel some warmth toward my estranged uncle while sitting at her bedside, and part of me thinks her request genuinely counts in favor of my feeling it, even though the uncle himself may give me nothing. Is there a single principle that captures all of my verdicts -- perhaps something about which attitudes must answer to how their objects are, or about which attitudes I can form by taking apparent reasons at face value? Or do I have to give some of them up? I'd like the principle stated precisely enough that it delivers a verdict on cases I haven't listed, including one I'm unsure about: a gambler who pays a service to donate to a political cause she hates unless she quits betting, and thereby tries to give herself a reason to intend to quit. And I'd like to know which of my verdicts is the weakest link -- the one a coherent principle would force me to surrender first.
