My closest friend and I spent the whole of last night arguing about the lottery and preface paradoxes, and I left unconvinced but unable to say precisely where she had gone wrong. She offered a summary of the two paradoxes which I have written out as faithfully as I can, and I would like an audit of it: which parts a careful epistemologist would accept, which are subtly off, and whether the conclusions she draws actually follow from the accurate parts.

Her summary, more or less: 'The lottery paradox shows that it cannot be rational to believe something merely because it is highly probable. If a sufficiently high probability sufficed for rational belief, then in a million-ticket lottery you would rationally believe of each ticket that it will lose; you would also rationally believe that some ticket will win; and since these beliefs are jointly inconsistent, the threshold view of belief collapses. The only way out is to deny that rational belief is closed under conjunction, and no serious philosopher wants that. The preface paradox is just the same paradox wearing a suit: an author rationally believes each assertion in his book, and rationally believes — from long experience with books of that kind — that at least one assertion in it is false, so his beliefs are inconsistent. This proves that a rational person's overall belief set can never be consistent, which means that consistency, which logicians have always treated as a minimal demand of rationality, is in fact an unreasonable one. Taken together, the two paradoxes show that believing a single proposition outright is a kind of folk fiction: strictly speaking we only have degrees of belief, and talk of flat-out belief is just a shorthand for high probability.'

Several things about this bother me, though I could not articulate them well enough at two in the morning. It seemed to me that she moved rather quickly from 'you rationally believe of each ticket that it loses' to 'your beliefs are jointly inconsistent' — as though believing several things and believing their conjunction came to the same thing — but I could not tell whether that slip matters to the shape of the paradox. I also recall that some philosophers are quite relaxed about rejecting closure or conjunction for rational belief, so her claim that no serious philosopher would touch it seemed overstated; and I have a vague memory that the preface paradox is usually presented as interesting precisely because it is not just the lottery paradox again, though I cannot remember why. Finally, I am suspicious of the last step: even if both paradoxes succeed, it is not obvious to me that they force us to give up flat-out belief altogether, rather than just making things uncomfortable for one particular theory of what belief is.

Please go through her summary part by part. Tell me which claims a defender of the standard presentations would sign off on, which misrepresent or overstate something (for instance, about which principle each paradox actually targets, or about what the available responses are), and whether her eliminativist conclusion about flat-out belief is supported by the paradoxes as correctly described, or merely fits well with them. I would also like to know whether the two paradoxes really form a natural pair, or whether they pull in different directions once the details are respected — since that was, in the end, the hinge of our argument.
