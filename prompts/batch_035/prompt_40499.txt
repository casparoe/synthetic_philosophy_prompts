The exam question was: "State what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem establishes about the relationship between individual and social preferences over risky options, and explain why the result is sometimes taken to support utilitarianism."

Here is what the student wrote:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem concerns a society of individuals, each with preferences over risky prospects, i.e. lotteries over social outcomes. The assumptions are that each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and that the social preference relation, which ranks lotteries on behalf of the whole society, satisfies those same axioms. The theorem then establishes that the social utility function must be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions: social utility equals the sum of the weights times each person's utility. So the social ranking is completely determined by individual utilities, and linearly so.

The result is derived from a thought experiment in which an impartial observer stands behind a veil of ignorance, having an equal chance of turning out to be any member of society. Since the observer is equally likely to be anyone, their expected utility for a given social arrangement is the average of everyone's utility under it, and maximizing expected utility then amounts to maximizing the total. That is precisely the utilitarian sum-ranking, derived from rationality axioms that look pretty uncontroversial on their own.

This supports utilitarianism in two ways. First, it shows that anyone who accepts the vNM axioms at both the individual and the social level is thereby committed to sum-ranking. Second, the utilities being added are measured on a cardinal scale, so the theorem puts interpersonal comparisons of utility on a firm footing: utilities can be meaningfully added across persons, something utilitarians previously had to simply assume.

One limitation worth noting is that the weights in the weighted sum are not pinned down by the theorem itself, so a critic could argue that it only supports utilitarianism once all the weights are set equal, and the theorem does not itself tell us to do that."
