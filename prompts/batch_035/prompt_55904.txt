I cover science and ideas for a mid-size paper, and an embarrassing fraction of the fights I end up writing about are, I've slowly come to suspect, not really fights about facts at all. Is obesity a disease. Whether viruses count as alive. Whether addiction is a choice or a brain disorder. Whether the serotonin theory of depression was refuted or just quietly retired. Every time one of these flares up, I get scientists on the phone insisting the other side is confused about words, editors insisting we just report what the study found, and readers writing in to say the whole thing is semantics — and I have no systematic way of deciding who's right before deadline. I gather philosophers have actual machinery for this stuff: the people who argue about whether there's a table in addition to the particles arranged table-wise, the long fight about whether that disagreement is merely verbal, and the more recent attempts to give a test for when a dispute is just two people using a word differently. Something like stripping the contested word out and checking whether any substantive claim survives. That's the sort of thing I want, but usable.

So: write me a procedure my desk can actually run, in about twenty minutes per story, with no philosophy background. An ordered set of questions, what a yes or no at each step licenses me to write, where it stops and says this is a genuine empirical disagreement that deserves coverage as one, and — this is the part I most need — where it has to hand off to a human because the definitional choice, while not tracking any fact, still carries stakes (like, fine, whether obesity is a disease is verbal, but the word decides insurance coverage, so someone still has to pick, and that picking isn't science reporting). Two things I'd like folded in if they're worth anything, and discarded if they're not: my friend who took some philosophy swears by the test that a dispute is verbal whenever both sides, if they were perfectly honest and perfectly informed, would file the identical story — same headline, same lede — and my own hunch that some disputes are only locally verbal, i.e. the word disease might be doing no work in the obesity fight while doing all the work in a fight five years from now about whether doctors can treat ordinary unhappiness, and a procedure that just stamps "verbal" on a word once will mislead me later. If my hunch is confused, say so.

Then run the procedure on three cases: whether obesity is a disease, whether viruses are alive, and whether the recent claims that the chemical-imbalance story of depression got debunked amount to an empirical reversal or mostly definitional housecleaning. Fair warning on that last one — I wrote a column treating it as a major scientific about-face, and I have a feeling your procedure is going to make me look bad, which I'd honestly rather know. Also be straight about where the procedure misfires: the kinds of disputes it will mis-classify, the kinds of readers it will infuriate, and what it flatly cannot do. My copy desk will fight me over any framing sentence that sounds like it's taking a side in the underlying fight, so build that constraint in.
