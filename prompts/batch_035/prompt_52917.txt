I'm a PhD student in political economy, working on democratic innovations — mostly deliberative mini-publics and citizens' assemblies — so prediction-market governance is adjacent to my area rather than squarely within it. Last week my department hosted a guest speaker on institutional designs for long-term policy, and he spent most of the seminar arguing for handing policy selection over to conditional prediction markets: the arrangement Robin Hanson called futarchy, though the speaker mostly avoided the name and simply repeated the slogan that in such a system "we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs." The slogan did real work in one exchange I'd like to understand properly. A discussant objected that markets don't cleanly separate belief from preference: a trader who *wants* the verdict on some policy to come out favourable can put money on that outcome and move the price, so the market aggregates willingness to pay rather than credence, and wealthy committed partisans will dominate whatever "belief" signal the price is supposed to carry. The speaker waved this off as a category error — a confusion between what we care about and what we expect — and said the slogan is precisely a partition of institutional labour between the two, with votes handling the former and markets the latter. The discussant replied that the slogan was functioning as an incantation, and that no one in the room could say which of its component claims, if any, actually answered him.

Since then I've heard the line three more times in reading groups and at a conference, always doing the same job: converting an institutional objection into a supposed values/beliefs confusion. I can half-see a spread of candidate readings, from the trivial (any political system involves both expressing preferences and forming expectations about consequences — uncontroversial, and useless for the speaker) through the epistemic (that speculative markets, because losses discipline the ill-informed, aggregate credences better than committees or elections do) to something far stronger: that there exists a well-defined welfare measure that votes can register and markets cannot distort, which looks like it presupposes a solution to the very aggregation problems the discussant was raising. But that sketch is my attempt to reconstruct the argument, not the answer I'm after, and I don't trust my own mapping of which readings are platitudes, which are substantive, and which are simply false.

So: please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, with a verdict on each — trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and, crucially, a verdict on whether each reading answers the discussant's objection or merely appears to. Then commit. Don't leave me with a menu and a shrug: tell me which reading the speaker actually needed for his reply to work, whether that reading is true, and, if it isn't, whether the discussant's objection is fatal to prediction-market governance or merely to the slogan as deployed — and if the latter, what the honest restatement of the speaker's position would have looked like. I'd rather have a definite, argued position I can disagree with at our next seminar than a balanced survey. Please write in British English.
