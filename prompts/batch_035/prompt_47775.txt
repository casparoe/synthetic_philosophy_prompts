I write a column on science and ideas, and in coverage of online shaming I keep meeting the same analogy: social sanction as the community's immune system. The pile-on is a fever, the reputational pathogen is detected and marked, antibodies remain in the form of screenshots and search results, and the body social recovers its health. Writers using the analogy tend to draw a reassuring conclusion: that what we call cancel culture is not a mob but a self-limiting homeostatic response — unpleasant, occasionally excessive (that's the autoimmunity, the innocent person caught in the pile-on), but on balance protective, and not something that calls for deliberate reform any more than a fever calls for suppressing the immune system. Audit the analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the pathogen, the fever, the antibodies, the memory cells, immunodeficiency (the powerful who escape sanction?) — then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that don't: whether the response is genuinely self-limiting in the way a fever is, whether the system's memory ever allows someone to be cleared, whether sanctions in a plural society have anything like the single, well-defined 'self' that an immune system defends, and whether a punisher-less process can be said to have a function at all. Then tell me whether the reassuring conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it, and what a better analogy — if there is one — would be.
