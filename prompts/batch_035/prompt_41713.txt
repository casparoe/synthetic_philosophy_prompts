I teach an upper-level seminar on divine providence and free will — twelve students, one 80-minute session. We've just worked through the dialectic around middle knowledge: the claim that there are true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, statements of the form that in circumstances C creature X would freely do A, that God knows them logically prior to any creative decree, and that this knowledge is supposed to let God providentially steer a world of libertarianly free creatures without determining their choices. My students can reproduce the positions on paper, but the two things that actually matter haven't landed: why anyone would want such counterfactuals in the first place, and why their truth is so slippery — what would even make it the case that X would do A, given that X is free and hasn't done it yet? I want an in-class game where the desire for middle knowledge shows up as a felt need, and where the grounding problem shows up as something that bites them personally rather than as an objection they memorized.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind, but please improve on it. Three students form a world-design bureau that has to assemble a world out of circumstance cards and free creatures — the rest of the class, each holding a private, fairly stable character card plus a small number of one-use deviation tokens — and hit a welfare target that depends entirely on what the creatures will freely do. The bureau's only planning tool is writing dossiers: for each creature and each circumstance they create, they must state what that creature would do; the creatures then play it out, deviation tokens allowing occasional departures from character without explanation. The scoring should reward the bureau both for the welfare outcome and for dossier accuracy, so they're torn between planning on would-claims and hedging toward might-claims; and I'd like one late round that introduces a deliberately perfect-contrarian creature — one whose card says only that she does the opposite of whatever a dossier predicts — to make the hard case vivid. Give me the materials, full rules, timeline, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto parts of the doctrine: which element stands in for the prevolitional character of the counterfactuals, which models the grounding objection, what the deviation tokens are doing, and how the game stages the difference between merely possible worlds and feasible worlds. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the actual doctrine no classroom game can reproduce and I'll have to supply by lecture afterward — especially the fact that in my setup the bureau writes the dossiers, which is exactly backwards from the view, since the counterfactuals are supposed to be true independently of God's will.

Two requests about the answer itself. First, please flag explicitly where the design takes liberties with the doctrine for the sake of playability — where the mapping from game element to doctrinal element is loose or contested — so I know what I'll need to correct in the debrief. Second, tell me which part of the design you're least confident will come off as intended with a group running it for the first time, and give me an overall sense of how confident you are that the game delivers the two experiences I described at the start, since if it only teaches the vocabulary I've failed.
