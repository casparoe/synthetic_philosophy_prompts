I hold the view that a rational agent's credences are sometimes irreducibly imprecise: rather than assigning every proposition a single number, an agent's epistemic state is better modeled by a set of probability functions, with the agent having a determinate opinion only where the whole set agrees (and where the set's upper and lower probabilities bracket the rest). Ambiguity-averse behavior in Ellsberg-style choice problems, disagreement among experts one has no reason to break between, and cases where the evidence simply underdetermines a unique credence all strike me as naturally captured this way, in a way that insisting on a sharp number seems to paper over.

Tell me what would count against the view. I want the defeaters mapped, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like the following specifically addressed, since these are the ones I already half-worry about:

1. Dilation. On the standard story, conditioning on the outcome of a draw known in advance to be independent of the relevant partition can spread the imprecision — the set of probability functions can disagree more after you learn something they all agreed was uninformative. A view on which gathering evidence can make you less decided, even about a question the evidence was irrelevant to, looks like it is misdiagnosing something. Is this a technical artifact of particular representations, or a genuine cost?

2. Decision rules for sets. Once credences are sets, you need a bridge to action, and every candidate bridge I know of has a cost: the maximin-style rules (always bet as if the worst probability function in the set is the truth) seem to generate bizarre caution, and appear to violate a simple dominance principle — if every probability function in your set rates option A above option B, it looks like you should never choose B, yet some rules permit choosing B in sequential settings, for instance when the option to gather more information interacts badly with the rule's risk-aversion. How much of the view's plausibility rests on the availability of a tolerable bridge, and does the current state of that literature leave the view resting on a promissory note?

3. Precise-credence explanations of the same data. The behavioral evidence for imprecision — ambiguity aversion, reluctance to bet at either side of a range — can arguably be re-explained by a sharp-credence agent with the right utility function or the right attitude toward risk. If everything the set-model predicts can be mimicked by a precise agent with extra machinery, what does the imprecise view claim that its rival doesn't, and what would show which of them is right?

Beyond those three, tell me what the view's own defenders concede as counting against it rather than only what its critics press, whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — any behavior can be accommodated by suitably choosing the set and the bridge rule, which worries me — and if it has been so insulated, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or just what one should expect of a representational framework rather than an empirical theory. I'd also like your own judgment: which single consideration would you, personally, put most weight on, and where do you think the view actually stands.
