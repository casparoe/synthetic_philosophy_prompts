I sit on the franchise subcommittee of our country's constitutional review commission, which must report within two months on whether three contested groups should vote in national parliamentary elections: long-term resident non-citizens (about nine percent of the adult population, resident five years or more, all taxpaying, many barred from naturalization for reasons unrelated to loyalty, such as prohibitions on dual citizenship in their countries of origin); expatriate citizens (roughly eighteen percent of the citizenry, including a third generation born abroad that has never set foot here); and sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds. The subcommittee is deadlocked, and the structure of the deadlock matters: whoever's principle enfranchises the expatriates tends to exclude the minors, and vice versa. The all-subjected principle takes the residents and the minors but drops the expatriates; a competence threshold takes the residents and the expatriates but drops the minors; and the only principle that takes both expatriates and minors — affected interests — would, taken seriously, enfranchise schoolchildren and anyone touched by our monetary policy, which not even its defender accepts without a competence floor she cannot justify non-arbitrarily. Whatever rationale we give for one group will also be cited in litigation over the others, so splitting the difference is not available to us.

Draft the memo I will circulate before our final two sessions. It should recommend a concrete disposition for each of the three groups, together with an operational test the electoral commission could actually administer — a residency window, an application mechanism, a registration threshold — rather than a principle stated at the level of abstraction that got us stuck. It should be grounded in an explicit position on the boundary problem: what makes someone a member of the demos at all, as opposed to merely affected by its decisions or merely subject to them. And it must treat my chair's objection honestly rather than routing around it: his view is that the franchise is not a philosophical prize but a trust held by a specific historical community, that any principled test we write will be seized upon by groups we did not anticipate, and that the honest answer may be that the demos is constituted by mutual recognition and shared fate rather than by any criterion that could be written down. If you conclude that he is right that no single principle does the work, say so — but then show what does decide our three cases, and where that leaves the subcommittee's authority to decide them at all.
