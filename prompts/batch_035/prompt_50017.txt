I sit on the appraisal panel of a regional public archives, and we have a decision to make. Let me first give you our track record, because I want us to be consistent with ourselves, and I suspect our past calls are consistent with more than one underlying principle.

Our precedents, in rough order:

1. We accepted the complete papers of a shipping magnate who dominated the regional economy for four decades, on the stated ground that no history of the region could be written without him.
2. We declined a landed family's correspondence because the national archives already holds their estate records and we judged the marginal value of a partial duplicate to be low.
3. We launched, at real cost and against some indifference, an oral-history project with retired dock workers, fish processors and domestic workers — nobody had requested such material, but our holdings documented the owners of the mills and harbours and almost nobody who worked in them.
4. We accepted the internal case files of the municipal housing authority from the decades in which it administered a discriminatory allocation scheme — records created by the administrators of the discrimination, in which the tenants appear mostly as case numbers.
5. We declined a private individual's forty-year run of video diaries as having no evident historical significance, while accepting the papers of a minor but very long-serving local councillor.

The new offer: several thousand photographs and a continuous comment thread from a now-defunct local online forum that served as the main organising venue for a protest movement which twice shut down the port. The material is user-generated and documents people who held no institutional power; it is at genuine risk of loss, since the hosting company is insolvent; but it is noisy, fragmentary, partly anonymous, and — complicating matters — the local police, who monitored the forum at the time, hold their own (closed) archive of much of the same material.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of our precedents is the closest analogue? I can see at least three principles that might each fit our track record: document whoever exercised power over the region's life; fill the silences in what we already hold, whoever the absent people were; and take what is unique and at risk, whatever it documents — and I suspect they come apart on this case. Please say which principle each possible decision would commit us to, identify where the principles diverge on the facts as I've described them (including on the question whether the police copy matters, and what it means for us to hold material on the watched rather than the watchers), recommend a decision with your reasons, and be honest about whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine it. Finally, tell me what further offer or scenario — the sort of thing that could plausibly land on our desk next year — would most cleanly reveal which principle we have actually been running.
