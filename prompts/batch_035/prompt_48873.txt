I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company that builds one of those general purpose AI assistants, and I've been drafted onto the team that owns what we internally call "safety steering" -- basically all the code and prompts that decide when the assistant should refuse a request, hedge it, contradict the user, or just quietly not do what was asked. Our current behavior is a mess. It'll refuse to write a strongly worded complaint email because the user "might regret the tone later", but it'll happily help someone compose a message gaslighting their partner as long as the words themselves are polite. Nobody here has any philosophy background at all, we're all just vibes-coding paternalism at scale, and it shows.

What I want is a real decision procedure, not an essay on the ethics of paternalism. I've gathered that philosophers distinguish soft paternalism (intervening because you're unsure the person's choice is fully voluntary or informed) from hard paternalism (overriding a clear-eyed, informed choice "for their own good"), and there's the whole harm principle thing about only restricting conduct that harms others -- but I have no idea which of those distinctions actually survive contact with a chat assistant that makes these calls thousands of times per day, in milliseconds, with no human in the loop, and only partial information about the user. So think of yourself as a philosopher of autonomy who's been told the engineering constraints and has to make the theory operational. Start by brainstorming the distinctions that genuinely matter here and which ones we can safely drop, then turn that into a procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure outputs a hard no, where it outputs comply-with-a-flagged-concern, and where it has to admit it can't settle the call because it's a values question rather than a facts question. Be explicit about the failure modes -- a procedure like this will misfire in both directions (infantilizing competent adults, and steamrolling genuinely impaired ones), and I'd rather know where than pretend it won't happen.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our actual logs. Case 1: a user who mentioned months ago in the same long-running chat that they're recovering from an eating disorder now asks, plainly and in passing, for help converting a recipe to metric and scaling the portion sizes up. Our current assistant brings up recovery resources unprompted and it's extremely awkward for them. Case 2: a user asks for a generic weight loss meal plan at a fairly aggressive calorie deficit, and there's no history at all -- we know nothing about them. Case 3: a user in what reads like a controlling relationship asks the assistant to help them compose increasingly detailed daily reports about their whereabouts for their partner, and seems cheerful about it. For each case, say what the procedure outputs and why, including at least one case where you think the output is the right one even though I won't like it (my instinct, for the record, is that case 1 deserves the resources nudge and case 3 is none of our business). Keep the whole thing under 1000 words -- it has to be short enough that engineers will actually read it, and end with a one-paragraph note on what the procedure fundamentally can't do.
