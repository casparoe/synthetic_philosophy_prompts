I've long carried a rough universalizability heuristic in my head — what if everyone did that? — picked up from years of rationalist blog discussions, where it usually gets dismissed either as rule consequentialism in a wig or as a fallacy of appealing to consequences. I'd like to get straight about what my actual case verdicts commit me to, so here they are, and then a tentative principle of my own for you to test and repair.

My verdicts: Lying to obtain a loan I intend never to repay — wrong, and clearly. Free-riding on a group project while others work — wrong, though I notice the failure here feels contingent rather than logical: a world of shirkers is conceivable, just one where group projects die. Refusing to answer email except when I feel like it — fine, even though a world where everyone does this is likewise conceivable and mildly worse. A hermit's maxim of refusing all help and offering none — permissible, though cold; universalizing it yields an austere world but no contradiction I can find. Cutting in line whenever I'm in a hurry — wrong, and here is my problem: structurally this looks just like the email case, universal line-cutting is conceivable and merely unpleasant, yet I cannot bring myself to mark it fine the way I do for email. Letting one's talents rust — wrong, I think, but I can't identify any practice the act relies on in refraining, so if that verdict stands, my principle needs a limb I can't supply.

My tentative principle: a maxim is impermissible when acting on it requires, in the very act, leaning on a practice or expectation that universal adoption of the maxim would erode — the false promiser needs the institution of promising; the free-rider needs the other workers; the email-slacker needs nothing. This sorts most of my verdicts, but as far as I can tell it cannot sort line-cutting from email-answering, and I keep going back and forth on tipping at restaurants in a town I'm passing through and will never revisit: the principle appears to permit it, since I depend on no continuing relationship with that server or that town, yet it smells exactly like the free-rider.

So, three questions. Is there a single principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, and if not, which is the best truncated one? What does the best candidate say about the tipping case I'm wobbling on and about the case of neglected talents? And which verdict does that candidate force me to surrender — my leniency on email, my strictness on line-cutting, or my unease about one-time tipping? Please be explicit about each place where the principle buys coverage of a verdict at the price of an ad hoc clause, since a patched-together decision procedure that merely fits the data is what I'm trying to avoid.
