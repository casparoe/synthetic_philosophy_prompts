I'm a high school junior on my school's debate team, and I'm preparing for a rematch on the resolution that democratic states should substantially and persistently reduce military spending. In the first round I argued the pacifist side of the ledger: my claim was that preparing for war is itself one of the things that produces war, because arms buildups feed suspicion and arms races feed on themselves. My opponent closed her rebuttal with the Latin tag "si vis pacem, para bellum" — "if you want peace, prepare for war" — delivered as if it were a knockdown argument, and the judge nodded along. My history teacher later told me it's "the oldest wisdom there is," and I've also seen it in a YouTube comment section being used much more darkly, to mean roughly that peace is only ever an interval between wars, so there's no point pretending otherwise. The same eight words seem to be doing very different jobs in each mouth, and I want to understand exactly what work it was doing when it was used against me.

Since the round, I've done some digging, and the slogan turns out to be stranger than it sounded. It is adapted from a late Roman military manual by Vegetius, where the actual phrasing is "Igitur qui desiderat pacem, præparet bellum" — "Therefore let him who desires peace prepare for war" — though the underlying idea is supposed to reach back at least to Plato's Laws. George Washington told Congress in 1790 that "to be prepared for war is one of the most effectual means of preserving peace," which sounds like the same thought in civic dress. Meanwhile a German arms manufacturer took "Parabellum" from the second half of the phrase as its telegraphic address, which is why a standard 9mm cartridge is literally named after the slogan — so the maxim has served both statesmen and people who sell the instruments of war. And in 1907, at a huge peace congress that Andrew Carnegie presided over, someone answered it with a pointed counter-slogan: si vis pacem, para pactum — if you want peace, prepare a treaty. That exchange convinced me the sentence is not the simple truism my judge took it for.
