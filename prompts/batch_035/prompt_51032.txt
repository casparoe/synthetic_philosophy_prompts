My dad had a stroke in March and has been in a minimally conscious state since. The neurologist keeps talking about his "functional connectivity" scans -- how the hopeful scenario is his networks re-organizing so information flows between regions the way it used to, and how the tests they run are basically probing whether the right functional structure is still there or can come back. I mentioned all this to my old roommate, who's finishing a PhD in philosophy of mind, and instead of being comforting he built me an argument for why the entire framework the doctors are talking in is empty. I want to say he's wrong but I can't find the seam. Here it is as he gave it to me:

(1) Functionalism: a mental state is whatever state plays the right causal role -- pain is whatever tends to be caused by tissue damage and tends to cause distress, withdrawal, complaints, and so on.

(2) Any such role can be written out as a finite table of inputs, internal states, and outputs. So for a creature there is a finite-state machine M that captures its total mental life, or at least any finite stretch of it.

(3) Take any open physical system with enough continuous variables in it -- Putnam used an ordinary rock. Over any finite stretch of time you can chop the rock's microstates into chunks and choose a time-slicing so that the chunks match the table of M for that stretch. There's no constraint on how gerrymandered the chunks are allowed to be, so this can always be done.

(4) So for every physical system S and every machine M, there is a description under which S implements M.

(5) So the rock on the windowsill of my dad's hospital room implements the same mental states my dad does.

(6) And now the framework isn't just wrong but vacuous, its worst than false: if everything implements personhood, then "his brain still supports the right transitions" tells you nothing, and "the connectivity is coming back" is one description among infinitely many, no better than the rock's.

(7) So functionalism either collapses into absurdity or says nothing, and either way the doctors' way of talking is decoration on top of the actual biology.

He swears he's not being heartless, just careful with the concepts. For what it's worth, I want to keep (1) -- if the doctors are right that what matters is the pattern re-establishing itself, then there's a real difference between dad as he is and dad recovering, and I want that difference to be more than a choice of description. But I honestly can't tell whether the problem is (3), (4), or the slide from (4) to (5) and (6), and when I press him on it he just says that's *my* problem, he only does arguments. Which step do I reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd also accept being told the argument actually works, if you can tell me what's left standing afterward that I can believe about my dad.
