I teach political philosophy, and this term my seminar has been reading on the moral architecture of the welfare state — the old distinction between the deserving and undeserving poor, the history of workhouse logic, and the modern literature on conditionality. For the final paper, I have a student who wants to write on the American welfare reform of 1996, and I have been struggling to design an assignment for her that does justice to both the history and the theory. Rather than settle it myself, I thought I would write out the assignment as I would want it answered, and I would like your version to serve as a model response.

Here is the setup. The 1996 Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act, signed by Bill Clinton, abolished AFDC, which had functioned as a federal entitlement, and replaced it with TANF: block grants to the states, mandatory work requirements, and a five-year lifetime cap on benefits. The rhetoric surrounding reform drew on a decades-old trope of the benefits cheat — the able-bodied claimant who could work but preferred not to — and the reform is often read as the institutional triumph of that picture. The theoretical lens is the deservingness framework: the thought that welfare provision is pervasively structured by judgments about whether claimants meet conditions like need, attachment to work, and reciprocity, and that the entire apparatus of conditionality — workfare, sanctions, fraud investigation — exists to sort the deserving from the undeserving. Layered on this is the classic distinction between residual welfare systems, which act only as a safety net of last resort, and institutional ones, which treat support as a normal function of social membership, plus the paternalist argument associated with Lawrence Mead that the poor's real problem is not material deprivation but noncompliance with social norms, which the state must therefore enforce.

What I want is a careful mapping of the case onto the lens, running in both directions. Where does the deservingness framework genuinely illuminate the 1996 reform — which features of the act, its political coalition, and its reception does it predict or explain, and which does it misdescribe or simply not see (for instance, features that look more like fiscal devolution than moral sorting)? Does the reform fit the residual-versus-institutional taxonomy, or does it expose a category the taxonomy lacks? And does the episode, with its aftermath of falling caseloads alongside deepening poverty among the least employable, confirm the conditionality framework, complicate it, or show that it was never really about the cases it claimed to govern? Please structure the response with the following sections, in this order: a brief summary of your thesis (one short paragraph); the lens, laying out the deservingness and conditionality framework as you will use it (roughly 400 words); the reform through the lens, doing the disciplined mapping (roughly 600 words); where the lens strains or breaks (roughly 400 words); and a verdict on what the episode does to the theory rather than the other way around (roughly 300 words). Be honest about points where the framework is silent — do not force it to speak.
