On LessWrong sunk costs are treated as about the clearest bias there is: the resources spent are gone, only marginal costs and benefits going forward matter, and anyone citing past spending as a reason for future spending is making the classic mistake. Fine. But there's a move politicians make that I can't square with this, and it never gets the same treatment as a CEO pouring money into a doomed product to justify the R&D budget (management people call that one escalation of commitment): justifying staying in a war by pointing at the soldiers already lost — the thought being that we can't withdraw now, or the fallen will have died for nothing. Structurally that's the purest sunk cost reasoning imaginable — the deaths are about as sunk as sunk gets — and yet alot of people who'd mock the CEO don't find the war version obviously crazy. So what does the practice actually presuppose? That the value of a sacrifice depends on whether the project it was made for eventually succeeds, so a death can become pointless retroactively (some narrative or meaning-based account of value)? Or that there's a Schelling-style commitment logic, where being known to honor sunk costs makes your threats and deterrence credible going forward? Or is it just rhetoric aimed at grieving families, with no coherent presupposition underneath at all? And the bigger question: if honoring sunk costs can be rational anywhere, is the fallacy really a fallacy of reasoning, or just a heuristic that's right most of the time — and if so, when you're allowed to lean on it?
