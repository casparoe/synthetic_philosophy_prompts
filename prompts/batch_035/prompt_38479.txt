I'm writing a seminar paper pairing Kant's response to Benjamin Constant about the murderer at the door with Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons, and I'd like a reading of the play through Kant on lying.

The obvious pairing is this: Kant holds that you may not lie even to a murderer inquiring after his victim, and the play gives us a man who will not lie — will not even swear falsely — when a lie would plainly save his life, his family, and his position. But the fit bothers me. In Kant's case the lie is told to the pursuer in order to misdirect him; in the play, nobody asks More to lie to anyone. The state asks for an oath, and until Richard Rich commits perjury — the one real lie in the play, and it is what kills him — silence would have kept More alive and at liberty. More's stated grounds look theological and self-regarding (he will not give his soul, whatever is purchased with the lie) rather than anything about respect for the party deceived; and his courtroom maneuver — the lawyerly insistence that his silence must be construed as consent, not denial — looks like exactly the kind of casuistry Kant will not allow himself in the essay.

So my questions: Does the play dramatize Kant's absolutism, or a rival, conscience-based absolutism that merely happens to reach the same verdict? Does the mapping improve if we move from the murderer case to Kant's other treatment of lying — where it is classified as a violation of a duty to oneself, an offense against one's own dignity — since More's concern is with his own soul rather than with any person he might deceive? Where does a Kantian lens genuinely illuminate a scene (I'd point especially at the trial and at the early exchange in which More mocks Roper's willingness to cut down every law in England to pursue the devil), and where does the play resist the reading — what in it is legal, religious, or political in a way the Kant–Constant exchange knows nothing about?

Take both sides seriously: don't turn More into a Kantian if the play won't support it, and don't soften Kant to fit More. And tell me whether the play adds anything to the debate that the philosophical argument alone doesn't — for instance, what visible, costly integrity does to onlookers like Rich and Norfolk.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict: [the play vindicates Kant / dramatizes a non-Kantian absolutism / complicates Kant's case]" giving the label you have argued for.
