I want to get straight on what radical interpretation is supposed to accomplish before I argue about whether Davidson accomplished it. Give me the full specification: the list of desiderata any theory of radical interpretation has to meet, each backed by the scenario or consideration that motivates it.

Fix the problem as Davidson does: an interpreter arrives with no prior grip on a speaker's meanings or beliefs, and must work only from the speaker's patterns of holding sentences true in observable circumstances toward a theory that interprets her utterances. Then state what counts as succeeding. Among the requirements I want you to accept, reject, reformulate, or extend:

- The theory must answer to the holding-true data without smuggling in antecedent knowledge of what the speaker's words mean; otherwise the construction is not radical in the first place.
- It must be compositional: a finite apparatus that yields the interpretation of every potential utterance, including ones the interpreter never witnesses.
- Belief and meaning must be assigned together, each constraining the other, rather than one fixed in advance so the other can be read off.
- The speaker must come out mostly right about her immediate environment — and yet the theory must still accommodate the systematically mistaken: the superstitious speaker whose beliefs about spirits are almost all false, the one with flatly contradictory convictions, the one who is reliably wrong about where hidden objects are.
- Apparent disagreement must not be converted into untranslatability: disagreement over arithmetic or logic has to be representable as genuine disagreement, and the theory must explain how that is even possible rather than dissolving it into a mere difference of meaning.
- If many empirically adequate interpretations of one speaker are available, something must be said about what, if anything, settles the remainder — convention, convenience, or nothing — and whether two speakers interpreted differently by the theory differ in any way that matters.
- The account must extend reflexively to the interpreter's own language and to the comparison of whole conceptual schemes; if it cannot, the scheme/content apparatus is in trouble, which is the moral Davidson himself drew.

Where you suspect two requirements cannot be met together, say so and say which gives way. My own hunch is that near-guaranteed truth of the speaker's beliefs and the possibility of interpreting the badly mistaken pull apart unless charity is formulated with great care, and that the rule-following considerations — that a bare pattern of holding-true seems unable to make any utterance correct or incorrect rather than merely frequent — cut against the empiricist reading of the whole enterprise. Finally, mark which desiderata are constitutive of the project and which are artifacts of Davidson's particular formulation that a successor theory could drop. I am not asking for a scorecard of Davidson's own theory against the list; I want the list.
