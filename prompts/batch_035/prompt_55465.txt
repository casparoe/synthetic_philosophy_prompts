I chair the metrics and evaluation committee of a large international philanthropic foundation. Our flagship program, funded for the next decade, has the mandate to reduce global violence. The board now wants a single headline indicator by which the program's success or failure will be judged at the mid-cycle review, and our committee must recommend one at next month's meeting.

The committee is split. One faction argues that if the number of people killed, maimed, or assaulted in a year goes down, fewer victims suffer, and that is what we exist to change; on their view the headline number should be an absolute count of victims, since a world of ten billion people at any given per-capita rate contains more victims than a world of eight billion at the same rate, and it would be a moral failure to celebrate a world with more victims as a success. The other faction counters that absolute counts make our program hostage to demography: with population growth of even one percent a year, violent deaths can rise in absolute terms while every individual's risk of violence falls, and it seems perverse to declare failure in a world where everyone is safer than before. A third worry, raised by our grants team, cuts across both factions: our grantees work not only on lethal violence but on torture, domestic abuse, violent displacement, and cruelty to animals, and no common currency — death counts, victim counts, disability-adjusted life years, or something else entirely — has been settled on.

Draft the recommendation memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should (a) recommend a concrete headline indicator, including a fallback if the recommended one proves unmeasurable at reasonable cost; (b) defend the recommendation against the strongest version of the losing position, not a strawman of it, in terms the losing committee members could actually accept or at least respond to; and (c) say candidly where the choice of indicator presupposes a substantive and contestable view — about whether violence is bad in virtue of what happens to individual victims or in virtue of the risks individuals face, about whether lethal and non-lethal harms are commensurable, and about whether there is even a single fact of the matter as to how much violence a world contains, as opposed to many facts answering to different ways of counting. If you think the committee should decline to name any single headline indicator, argue for that instead, but then say what we should tell the board, which has asked for exactly one number.
