Every year in my philosophy of biology course the "what is life" unit starts the same way: I put a list of borderline cases up on the board, have the students vote, then give them my own calls. I've got my verdicts pretty much settled by now, but I've never actualy written down the principle behind them, and I half suspect there isn't one clean principle in there. I'd like to know what the best candidate is — even if it has to be baroque — and I'd rather know which of my verdicts it can't save than have someone gerrymander a principle to rescue all of them.

Here are my calls. Viruses: not alive, no question. Prions: not alive. A mule: alive, even though it's sterile — the sterility is a fact about it, not a disqualification. A desiccated seed sitting in a drawer for two centuries that still germinates when you give it water: alive. A viable bacterium frozen in permafrost for thirty thousand years: alive while frozen, or at least it never died — I'm not sure which way to put that, and maybe that's fine. HeLa cells in a dish: alive, though I confess some pull toward saying they're life but not a life, if that distinction does any work at all. A self-replicating computer program, one that copies itself and even copies the copying machinery: not alive, and pretty confidentely not. A red blood cell: not alive, even though it's busily metabolizing glucose the entire time — that one bothers my students way more than the viruses bother them. A synthetic cell built around a minimal genome designed on a computer and installed into a hollowed-out bacterium: alive. A slime mold plasmodium — one giant cell, no neurons, solves mazes: alive, obviously. An endolithic bacterium that divides once every thousand years: alive. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a mimivirus, huge for a virus, carrying genes for translation that it doesn't even use. Part of what pulls me toward "alive" is just that it's so close to the line, and part of what pushes me back is that closeness isn't a reason — which suggests that if I do count it, I need an actual argument.

So: is there a principle that gets all or nearly all of these? The obvious candidate — autonomous metabolism — can't be it, because I count the frozen bacterium and the drawer seed as alive while they do no metabolizing whatsoever, and the red blood cell does plenty while I say no. So whatever I'm tracking, it isn't metabolism at a moment. Maybe it's metabolism at the level of the organism across time, or a capacity rather than an activity — in which case say so and spell out how that's supposed to work, because "capacity" looks like it'll let the virus back in (it has the genes, it just borrows the machinery).

Give me the principle, or the top two candidates if it's genuinely close, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me what it predicts on cases I didn't list — invent some, the weirder the better — and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and for each of those, whether it's cheaper to overturn my verdict or to complicate the principle to keep it.

Please write in American English. And at the end, give me the bottom line as a JSON object with this schema: {"principle": string (one sentence), "clauses": [{"clause": string, "motivating_case": string}], "clashes": [{"verdict": string, "my_call": string, "principle_call": string, "resolution": "overturn_verdict" | "amend_principle"}], "novel_case_predictions": [{"case": string, "prediction": string, "confidence": "high" | "medium" | "low"}], "overall_assessment": string} — where each "resolution" says whether you'd keep my call and complicate the principle, or fold and let the principle win.
