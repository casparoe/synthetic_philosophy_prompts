I'm on the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital, and the definition-of-death debate has come up twice this year because of organ donation protocols, which got me reading the actual literature for the first time since grad school. What strikes me is that the positions haven't moved in basically fifty years: the whole-brain camp holds that death is the permanent loss of integrated organismic functioning (going back to the Harvard criteria and the UDDA's irreversible cessation of all brain function), the higher-brain camp says what dies at death is the person or mind and the body is a leftover, and then there's Shewmon and others pointing to the long-survival cases -- brain-dead pregnant patients carried to delivery, kids maintained for months or years on ventilation whose bodies grow, heal wounds, fight infections -- as evidence that a brain-dead body is a living but irreversibly unconscious organism, not a corpse.

Here's the thing though. Our own practice doesn't even stick to one definition. For donation-after-circulatory-death we declare death after something like two to five minutes without a pulse, which everyone knows isn't irreversible in the strict sense -- hearts have been successfully transplanted after that. So we apparently use a different defintion of death depending on which organs we want, and nobody seems to treat this as a crisis, which makes me suspect the whole debate is not really about what death is.

My working hypothesis, and I want you to evaluate it rather than just survey the positions: the standoff persists because the three sides are answering three different questions under one word -- one about the metaphysics of organism death, one about personhood, one about what the dead-donor rule permits -- and the institutional stakes (transplant programs, legal liability, family trust) keep anyone from admitting that. Is that right? Or is there a genuine factual or conceptual disagreement in there that would still be there even if we disambiguated the terms? And whatever your diagnosis is, tell me what progress would even look like -- a new distinction, a change in the law's framing, more cases like the somatic-survival ones, or nothing at all, because some disagreements are bedrock. I'd especially like to know whether the Shewmon-style cases are doing real argumentative work or just triggering intuitions that the other side can explain away.
