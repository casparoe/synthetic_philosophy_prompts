I teach a course called Rational Choice that I inherited this semseter when our decision theory person went on leave, and I'm now writing the final. The last third of the course was on Hofstadter's superrationality material, so that's what I want to examine, but I refuse to write a question a student can pass by reciting "superrational thinkers know they'll all do the same thing, so they cooperate." Quick recap in case it helps you phrase things: in the Platonia dilemma (his 1983 Scientific American column) there are 20 people, each allowed to send one reverse-charge telegram to a rich eccentric by noon the next day; if exactly one telegram arrives, its sender gets a fortune (call it a billion dollars); if zero arrive, or more than one, nobody gets anything, and the 20 can't communicate or coordinate. Hofstadter's superrational answer is to send a telegram with probability 1/20. He then actually ran a sequel, the Luring Lottery, in the magazine: to enter once you sent in a postcard with a 1 on it, but you were explicitly allowed to enter many times at once by writing one bigger number on the card; the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, paid to the sender of one randomly drawn entry. In real life most of the readership submitted astronomical numbers (people literally wrote googolplexes, and some filled the card with expressions designed to evaluate as huge numbers), the prize shrank to a minuscule fraction of a cent, and the magazine couldn't even determine a winner.

Here's what I want the question to force, and tell me if it's a bad idea. Students must (a) reconstruct where the 1/20 actually comes from — i.e., show it's the strategy that maximizes expected payoff under the assumption that everyone reasons identically and hence everyone ends up playing the same way — and then explain precisely why that is NOT the Nash equilibrium of the game and not what causal decision theory tells you to do; (b) identify the exact step where superrationality goes unorthodox (something like treating your own deliberation as evidence about what the others will do), and then say whether that's the same move as one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, or Kantian universalizability, or both, or neither — my students run these three together constantly and I want an answer that actually separates them; and (c) use the actual Luring Lottery outcome to assess whether that episode was a test of superrationality at all — does the googolplex response refute it, or just show the readership wasn't superrational, and if it's the latter, is that an answer with any falsifiable content or just an escape hatch?

It should be answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 marks total. Please write the question itself (you can tighten or rephrase my (a)–(c), that's fine, and keep it self-contained like my recap since the setup gets printed on the exam), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme listing the must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line note on how students typically blow each point. One more ask, and it matters to me: throughout both the model answer and the marking scheme, please flag clearly what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature — e.g. whether superrationality is just evidential decision theory in a funny hat, or whether Newcomb-style cases support it — because I don't want my exam implicitly grading a contested reading as the consensus.
