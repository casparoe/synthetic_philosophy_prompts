I'd also seen the line floating around in coverage of Robert Moses' low overpasses on the parkways and in old arguments about whether nuclear power is somehow structurally authoritarian while solar is democratic.

Here's my problem: as literally stated it's a question, not a claim, so I have no idea what I'd be endorsing if I let it stand. I can vaguely see a few different theses it might be shorthand for. There's a boring one where it just means technologies have social consequences, which seems true but wouldn't have surprised anyone in 1850. There's something stronger where the design itself, before anyone uses it badly, already settles political questions — like the famous overpass thing. And maybe something even stronger, where certain technical systems *require* certain political arrangements just to operate at all, which sounds close to technological determinism even though the people who invoke this stuff usually claim to be against determinism. And there's the question of whether "politics" here means partisan politics, or power and authority in some broader sense, or something looser like "having morally relevant effects."

Can you lay out the distinct readings from weakest to strongest — the trivial ones, the substantive and defensible ones, the ones that are probably false or unfalsifiable — and for each say whether it's true, whether it's interesting, and crucially whether it actually gets my source her conclusion that recommendation algorithms can't be neutral and that "we just build the tool" is always a dodge? And is there a reading under which the slogan is compatible with tools sometimes being more or less neutral, so I can push back on her a bit without sounding like a tech bro? I want to walk out of this knowing exactly which version of the line I'm signing up for when I put it in the piece. Keep it usable for a working journalist — punchy, no syllabus. Also if some of the readings apply better to the Moses overpass case than to the algorithm case, say which is which, because I suspect my source is smuggling together two things that only look like the same point.
