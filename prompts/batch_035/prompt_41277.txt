I'm a novelist, four books in, and my current one hinges on the reader believing my protagonist is basically good even though every good thing he does comes out of a mess of vanity and habit. At my last book club thing a reader asked me straight out whether he "counts as a good person," and I gave some waffly answer, but it's been eating at me because when I line up the characters I've actually written across all four books, my own gut verdicts about their moral worth don't seem to add up to any consistent principle, and I'd like to know if there's one under there or if I'm just capricious.

So here are my verdicts. Marta, who anonymously pays her dead husband's rival's rent for years after the rival insults his memory — spite is doing a lot of work in her motive, but I feel her act has full moral worth. A priest who admits, honestly, that his charity would collapse the moment he stopped fearing hell — I want to say his good works still count for something, though less than if he'd have done them anyway. A guy who returns a lost wallet only after clocking the security camera — no worth at all, even though the wallet gets returned either way. A woman who stays loyal to a friend purely out of love and never once registers it as a duty — that's my paradigm case of worth, I think. An aid worker who has deliberately trained herself to treat refugees as caseload statistics so she can keep functioning, and does enormous good while feeling nothing — I resent her, but I can't deny the worth. And the brother who drives through a blizzard to rescue his estranged sister while cursing her name the whole way, doing it against every inclination he has — worth, and maybe *more* worth than the loving loyal one, which seems weird to say out loud.

The part I keep going back and forth on is the protagonist himself: he commits an act of real generosity purely to impress a woman, and after she leaves he keeps doing it out of habit, until by the end neither he nor I can tell whether he's doing it for her ghost, for the habit, or because somewhere along the way it became what he thinks is right. Where does he land, worth-wise? Is there one principle that gets all my other verdicts — something about what the person would do in the nearest world where nobody's watching and no punishment is coming, or something about the structure of the will instead, or maybe just about the quality of attention the person pays to the actual situation in front of them — and if not, which of my verdicts has to go? I'd rather repair my intuitions than keep them all by accreting epicycles, honestly. Also please tell me what whatever principle you land on predicts for a case I haven't mentioned: someone who does the right thing in the actual world, but only because they've misread the situation — they think they're returning stolen money when actually the "victim" stole it first, say. Gut says worth, but my gut said a lot of things on this list that apparently dont cohere, so I'm prepared to loose that one too.
