my thesis supervisor keeps pushing this picture of imprecise probabilities as a committee: instead of one credence function you've got a whole panel of sharp Bayesians, each with their own precise probabilities, and your doxastic state just IS the set of their opinions. She uses it to argue two things: that when the committee disagrees (i.e. your credal set is spread out over some proposition) you should suspend judgment rather than average the members' views, and that there's no fact of the matter about what "your" probability is, since there's no aggregation rule inside the picture. I'm writing a chapter on Ellsberg-type cases and I want to use her framing but I'm not sure it holds up. Can you audit it for me? Like give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the committee member, the chair, the vote, the deliberation, hiring new members when evidence comes in — then where the mapping breaks. A real committee can compromise, can be persuaded by an argument, can have a chair with a tie-breaking vote; a credal set can't literally do any of that, it just gets updated member by member. So which of her two conclusions does the analogy actually support, and which does it smuggle in via features of committees that the formalism lacks? And if the committee picture fails, is there a better analogy for why anyone would want credences that are imprecise rather than just vague or half-believed?
