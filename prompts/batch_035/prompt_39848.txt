I'm the strategy director at a mid-sized industrial components firm, and a recurring category of decision has been landing on my desk that I don't think we handle well: irrevocable or near-irrevocable commitments made under time pressure because a counterparty might commit first. Publicly announcing we will exit a market segment before a competitor announces entering it; signing a long exclusivity agreement with a supplier before our rival gets a meeting; announcing a multi-year price guarantee that we cannot honourably withdraw from. The bargaining literature since Schelling tells us the first party to make a genuinely irreversible commitment often captures the surplus; but I've also seen the opposite failure, where two firms race each other into incompatible public commitments and both end up locked into positions they'd each have paid to unwind. In some decision-theory discussions I've come across the term 'commitment races' for roughly this phenomenon: when speed of commitment is itself an advantage, agents are pushed to bind themselves before they've gathered the information or done the deliberation that would have told them not to, and a capability that is an advantage in each individual interaction can be a systematic liability in the long run, because the organisation that can commit fastest is also the organisation that commits wrongly fastest.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run when one of these situations comes up — we face perhaps four to six per year, stakes in the range of a bad quarter to an existential threat, and sometimes we have a week, sometimes forty-eight hours, because the whole point is that the other side may move first. The people running it are smart but have no formal training in decision theory, and anything above a certain size needs board sign-off, so the procedure has to produce an auditable written trace of why we committed or didn't. Please give me: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure terminates in a 'commit', a 'don't commit', or a 'wait'; and the points at which it has to hand the decision to a named human because the question is one of judgement or values rather than fact — I want those points flagged honestly rather than papered over with pseudo-quantification.

The procedure should make room for the considerations that make commitment races distinctive rather than just risky decisions: whether the other side can actually observe and verify our commitment (a commitment no one believes in isn't one); whether 'irrevocable' really means irrevocable or merely expensive to reverse, and what that difference does to the logic; the value of the information we'd get by waiting, weighed against the risk of losing the first-mover position entirely; the possibility of negotiating a mutual standstill rather than racing; and the portfolio-level worry that winning individual races one at a time can still leave the firm committed into an accumulating pile of bad positions — though be careful to say whether that last consideration belongs inside the procedure or is a reason to have a separate annual review.

Then run the procedure on three cases, worked through in enough detail that I can see it bite: (a) a competitor is rumoured to be entering one of our smaller segments, and we could pre-empt them by publicly and verifiably announcing our own exit from an adjacent segment as part of a focus strategy we were half-planning anyway; (b) our sole supplier for a key input offers a seven-year exclusivity deal at below-market prices, but they will only keep the offer open for two weeks, and our own engineers tell us there's a decent chance a substitute input becomes viable within three years; and (c) a case where I expect you to show the procedure giving an answer its own user won't like — for instance, a situation where the race logic says commit immediately and the procedure says don't, or vice versa, with the reasoning made explicit. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it will systematically misfire, and what kind of commitment decision it should never be trusted with at all. Please write the whole answer in British English.
