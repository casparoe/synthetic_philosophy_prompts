I spent most of my career in a developmental biology lab and retired a couple years back, and since then I've gotten deep into philosophy of mind, mostly thanks to a reading group that tolerates my old-scientist tangents. The one place I actually have an edge is innateness, because my friend Marisol -- postdoc cohort, does bird song development for a living -- has been fighting me about it at dinners for twenty years. I've kept a list of her verdicts over the years, and I'm trying to prep for our next argument, so I want a second opinion on where she'll land.

Her record so far: (1) orb-weaver web geometry -- innate, full stop, no discussion. (2) White-crowned sparrow song -- the song itself is learned (a male raised in acoustic isolation produces a wierd degraded isolate version), but the predisposition to learn it from the right tutors is innate; her phrase for this is that it's learned on an innate chassis. (3) Reading -- obviously learned, invented way too recently for genes, and it needs explicit drilling anyway. (4) The Garcia taste-aversion thing -- the particular aversion is learned in a single trial, but the wiring that ties nausea to taste and refuses to tie it to lights or sounds is innate. Chassis comment again. (5) Vervet alarm calls -- innate; infants give roughly the right call for eagle versus leopard before anything like correction happens, its just polish later. (6) Human bipedal walking -- innate, and she gets smug about the old twin studies where the twin who got daily staircase practice walked no earlier than the one who didnt: experience doesnt cause walking, maturation does. (7) The human capacity for language -- innate, loudly -- though she's careful to add that which grammar you actually end up with obviously depends on the input.

Next dinner I want to bring three new cases: (a) little kids and question formation -- they basically never make the linear mistake, i.e., they say "Is the boy who is crying hungry?" and you never catch them saying "Is the boy who crying is hungry?", even though the sentences they hear dont seem to rule the bad version out; (b) Nicaraguan Sign Language, where deaf kids thrown together with only pidgin-ish gestural input produced a fully grammatical creole within a cohort; and (c) a current language model that nails question formation off nothing but text.

What I want from you: predict her verdict on each of the three. Then tell me what rule she's actually running. I can see at least two that fit all seven old verdicts -- one poverty-of-stimulus flavored, where innate means the structure outstrips what the input could explain, and one more ethology flavored, where innate means it shows up without being learned, with maturation counting as innate and anything experience-dependent counting as learned. Maybe there's a better candidate. Say what each rule predicts for my three cases, where they agree and where they split, and then give me one further case I could spring on her that would actually seperate the rules -- the more it forces her to contradict one of her own earlier rulings, the better.
