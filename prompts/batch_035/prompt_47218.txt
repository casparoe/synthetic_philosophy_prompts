I'm a TA grading midterms for a mid-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of the social sciences. The unit in question covered Searle's account of institutional facts, Hacking's work on interactive kinds and the looping effect, and recent debates over whether race and gender are natural or social kinds. The midterm question for this essay read: "Distinguish causal from constitutive claims in social construction. Illustrate the distinction using money and gender. Then assess: can a kind be socially constructed in the constitutive sense and still be real enough to figure in genuine explanations? Defend your answer." Grading is on accuracy, the quality of the distinctions drawn, and whether the conclusions are supported by the reasoning offered rather than merely asserted.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Causal construction and constitutive construction differ in the direction of dependence. A kind is causally constructed when social factors bring it about that things of that kind exist: had there been no banking systems, no notes would circulate as money, and the paper we now call money would be mere paper. A kind is constitutively constructed when social facts are part of what makes the thing the kind of thing it is: a note counts as money partly in virtue of being accepted and treated as money by a community, so there could be no money in a community without such practices. Money is therefore constitutively constructed: its value is not a physical property of the paper, and the same note could cease to be money if the community stopped treating it as such.

Something similar holds for gender. Masculinity and femininity are not written into our biology. They are produced by norms, expectations, and upbringing, and in the absence of those norms the patterns we call masculine and feminine would not exist. So gender, like money, is constitutively constructed: what it is to be a woman or a man is fixed by social practices rather than by anatomy.

Can such kinds be real and explanatory? Yes. That a kind is constructed does not mean it is an illusion. Money really does function as a medium of exchange, and gendered expectations really do shape careers and family life. Hacking's looping effect makes the point vivid: once a classification is in place, the people classified adjust their behavior in response to it, and that response entrenches the classification. This is why socially constructed kinds can be more stable than natural kinds, whose members cannot react to being classified.

Finally, the contrast between natural and social kinds is thinner than it looks. Putnam showed that what counts as gold is settled by experts rather than by ordinary speakers: the division of linguistic labor means the community fixes the reference of 'gold' for everyone else. Since our social practices determine the extension even of a paradigmatic natural kind, natural kinds are socially constructed as well, and the difference between natural and social kinds is one of degree rather than of kind."
