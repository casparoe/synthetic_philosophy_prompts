My study group for game theory got completely stuck on the centipede game yesterday and I said I'd find someone to referee. Quick context in case: it's the game where two players alternate between taking a slightly bigger share of a growing pot or passing, the game ends after finitely many moves, and backward induction says the very first player should grab immediately even though both would do way better if everyone kept passing. So the fight:

(1) Marco said backward induction is just obviously right: rational players with common knowledge of rationality exit at the first node, and anyone who passes is irrational, full stop, so the "paradox" is just people being irrational in the lab.

(2) Jenna said that's way too quick -- if I actually find myself at the second decision node, my opponent already failed to grab at the first, so the common knowledge of rationality that the induction relies on has been falsified by observation, and passing might now be the smart move.

(3) Marco answered that one deviation just proves your opponent is irrational or noisy, which is more reason to grab at your next chance, not less -- deviating yourself would be compounding their mistake.

(4) Jenna said that treats every deviation as noise rather than strategy: a rational opponent might pass deliberately precisely because they anticipate the induction argument, hoping to establish that they're the type who passes, so cooperation could be rational among players who each know the other is rational. Marco rolled his eyes and said that's smuggling reputation into a one-shot game -- there's no tomorrow to signal for, so the reasoning is incoherent.

(5) Then Jenna claimed the whole thing is structurally like the unexpected hanging paradox, where an airtight induction argument ends up undermining the very assumptions it runs on. Marco said that paradox is about knowledge and self-reference and has nothing to do with game theory.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And please tell me what would actually change your mind about the verdict -- like, which step of your reasoning is most likely to go wrong if you had to bet on one.
