I've been going through a popular audiobook on the history of mathematics, and in the chapter on ancient Greece the narrator sums up Zeno's paradoxes roughly like this: Zeno of Elea, a follower of Parmenides, invented about four paradoxes to prove his teacher wrong — to show that motion, which Parmenides had claimed was an illusion, is real but weird. The narrator explains the Achilles as: by the time Achilles reaches the spot where the tortoise was, the tortoise has moved a bit further, so there's always another gap, and since this goes on infinitely, Achilles never catches up. Then he says the Dichotomy is "just the same paradox run in reverse," so really Zeno had one idea that he dressed up in four costumes, which is why mathematicians lump them together. And then the payoff: the whole thing was solved by calculus. Since we now know an infinite series can add up to a finite sum, all the infinitely many distances get covered in finite time, so the tortoise gets caught, and Zeno's problem was simply that limits hadn't been invented yet — "he was two thousand years early to the answer." The chapter closes by calling the paradoxes a fun puzzle that kept the Athenians entertained but had no deeper point.

I came away feeling like I now understand Zeno, but something nags me. My roommate (a math major) muttered that "convergence answers the 'how long' question, not the 'whether'" but wouldn't elaborate, and honestly I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or math-major posturing. Also a philosophy friend mentioned offhand that Zeno was arguably defending Parmenides rather than attacking him, which is basically the opposite of what my audiobook said, but she couldn't remember where she'd read it.

Can you go through the audiobook's summary piece by piece and tell me what's accurate, what's a simplification a specialist would let slide, and what's actually wrong? In particular I'd like to know: (a) whether Zeno was trying to defend or refute Parmenides, and how much turns on that; (b) whether treating the four paradoxes as one recycled idea is fair, or whether at least one of them (I've heard the Arrow mentioned?) is untouched by the "infinite series converge" response; and (c) whether the "case closed, calculus solved it" framing follows from the parts of the summary that are true — or whether the narrator, in fixing one paradox, quietly left the others standing. I'd rather know what a philosopher of mathematics would actually say here than get a tidy resolution, so don't smooth over live disagreements if there are any.
