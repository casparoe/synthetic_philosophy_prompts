i teach a second-year course on rational choice, and we're ending the term with ambiguity. most of my students can parrot back that Ellsberg's urns show people violating the sure-thing principle, but i don't think they *feel* why anyone would pay to avoid an unknown probability. so i want to send them off with a villanelle about the two urns — the one you're told holds fifty red and fifty black, and the one nobody will tell you the mix of — where the form does all the philosophical work.

the two refrains should carry it. refrain 1 is the voice reaching for the known odds; refrain 2 is the half-acknowledged thought that the other urn might be anything at all. i'd like them written so that each could be an answer to the same question, a question the poem asks a slightly different version of at the top of every tercet (how many red, who counted, does it matter). the point of using a villanelle is that the two answers keep taking turns and only end up standing next to each other in the final couplet — and there they should sound less like two competing positions and more like the two halves of one agent who pays a premium not to look. someone who knows the literature should be able to see the sure-thing violation in the structure without being told, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem about not knowing.

one thing i'm strict about: please don't let it resolve into either "trust the numbers" or "gut feeling over maths". i want the ambiguity aversion left standing as neither a simple mistake nor obviously wise, since that's honestly where our seminar discussions kept landing. standard villanelle length, and if you can get the word "premium" in naturally (the price of refusing the unknown urn), that'd definately be a bonus, but don't force it. also please write it in british english — "colour", "grey", the lot — our department style guide is pedantic about it and, if i'm honest, so am i.
