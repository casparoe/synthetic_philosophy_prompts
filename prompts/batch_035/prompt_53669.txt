I'm auditing a seminar paper on whether self-deception is even coherent, and my thesis adviser and I have hit the usual wall: the paradox that deceiving yourself seems to require knowing the truth you're hiding from yourself, at which point you haven't hidden it. Rather than argue the paradox in the abstract, I've decided to collect my own gut verdicts on concrete cases and see whether they trace a coherent line at all. My adviser suspects they don't, and I'd like a second opinion before I build a paper on them.

Here are the verdicts. A wife whose husband shows every sign of an affair — the gym membership, the unexplained trips, the phone face-down — who tells her sister with perfect sincerity that he would never: I say she is self-deceived, full stop. A lifelong smoker who concedes the statistics on lung cancer but insists they apply to other smokers, not to her, because she's "always been a healthy type": self-deceived, I say, even though she could probably recite the statistics to her doctor. A patient with a terminal diagnosis who tells everyone she will beat it and makes plans for next summer: I say that is not self-deception, just hope, even though the epistemic situation looks identical to the wife's — and I can't fully defend the contrast. A man who simply never opens his biopsy results, and speaks and plans as a healthy man: I want to call this self-deception, even though he never forms any false belief — he just keeps the truth at arm's length. A woman who after every failed relationship announces that she always knew it wouldn't work, and seems to genuinely remember knowing it: self-deceived, I say, about her own past mind rather than about the world.

So my question is threefold. First, is there a single principle that captures all of these — for instance, a rule that self-deception requires actually believing the comfortable proposition (which the biopsy man strains), or that it requires doing something on purpose to keep the truth away (which the wife and smoker strain, since nothing they do looks strategic), or that it can only be about settled facts and never about open futures (which would explain my wife-versus-patient contrast but makes me nervous about the smoker, whose claim is really about her own future risk)? Second, tell me what the best candidate principle predicts about a case I haven't classified: a patient who sincerely says she will recover and books a cruise for the following year, while quietly updating her will and telling her sister where the deed to the house is. Is she self-deceived, merely hopeful, or somehow believing and not believing the same thing at once? Third, be honest about which of my verdicts the cleanest principle cannot save, because I suspect it's the biopsy man, and I'd rather know now than during my adviser's office hours.
