The exam question was:

"Explain Sartre's distinction between facticity and transcendence, and how bad faith exploits the ambiguity between them. Illustrate with the waiter and the woman on the date examples. Can bad faith be avoided, on Sartre's view?"

The student wrote:

"Sartre's whole system rests on existence preceding essence: there's no fixed human nature that tells us what we are before we act, so consciousness is always having to define itself. He splits being into the in-itself, which is full and thinglike, and the for-itself, which is consciousness. Facticity is the for-itself's given side: your body, your past, your job, where you were born. Transcendence is your freedom to surpass all that, to project yourself toward a future you choose. Bad faith is what happens when you try to escape the tension between these two, because living with total freedom is anguishing — you have no excuses and the outcome is entirely on you.

Sartre is careful that bad faith is not just lying to yourself the way you'd lie to a friend, because there's no hidden unconscious part of you holding the truth (he rejects the Freudian censor as just more consciousness dodging itself). But actually I think this lands him in trouble: if the unconscious doesn't exist, then to deceive yourself you'd have to know the truth you're hiding, and know the lie is a lie, all in full consciousness. That seems contradictory. So bad faith, for Sartre, must really collapse into ordinary self-deception or just lying projected inward, and can only occur in brief flashes before the contradiction catches up with you.

The waiter example: the waiter plays at being a waiter, exaggerates the gestures, becomes all-formula, because it's easier than facing that his job is just one project among many he could have chosen. Sartre's point is that he's in bad faith because he chose the wrong profession — he's pretending to enjoy it, which is a kind of dishonesty about his real preferences. In both the waiter and the woman on the date, bad faith works by denying transcendence: the waiter treats himself as a thing (a waiter-thing), and the woman on the date treats her body as a passive object so she doesn't have to confront the man's desire as a choice she must answer.

Can bad faith be avoided? Sartre is pretty clear in Being and Nothingness that the answer is sincerity: authenticity means trying to be fully what you are, so the sincere waiter should just accept that he is a waiter, the way you'd honestly fill out a job title on a form. Since we can never be fully sincere all the time, some level of bad faith is unavoidable for everyone, which is why Sartre mostly treats it as an ontological condition rather than something you can be blamed for."
