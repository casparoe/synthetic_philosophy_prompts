For the past three years I have chaired a political-philosophy discussion group whose two most senior members, call them C and N, have been arguing past each other about global justice. C holds a cosmopolitan position of a moderate, sufficientarian sort: every person has equal moral standing regardless of citizenship, states are best understood as instruments for discharging universal duties, and any extra weight a government may give its own people must be justified instrumentally (because we can help compatriots more effectively, or because attachment reliably produces good outcomes) rather than as a standing obligation of justice. N is not a cultural nationalist but an institutional one: he thinks the coercive, mutually supporting structure of a state generates associative obligations of justice among compatriots that simply do not exist between strangers abroad, so that giving compatriots priority at the margins is not a concession to effectiveness but what fairness itself requires. Both are tired of the other side caricaturing them, and they have finally agreed to a structured adversarial collaboration, with me as the neutral drafter. Write the joint statement they should be able to sign.

The statement needs four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, and I want these to be substantive rather than vacuous — things like: both reject the claim that the cosmopolitan must therefore demand full global equality of resources; both accept that states may permissibly give their own badly-off some priority over equally badly-off foreigners at some margin while disagreeing about the ground of that permission; both deny that state boundaries make moral difference by themselves, while disagreeing about whether the institutions bounded by them do. Find formulations that neither side would call slanted. Second, a disagreements table: one row per remaining disagreement, with two columns — the cosmopolitan's position phrased so that C would sign the wording, and the nationalist's phrased so that N would sign the wording. Third, the cruxes: the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of them to revise. I suspect one crux is whether obligations of justice, as opposed to duties of beneficence, require shared coercive institutions, and another is whether the analogy with familial partiality supports or undermines compatriot priority, but identify others of your own. Fourth, for each crux, at least one concrete case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

For the case-based section, use (among any further cases you devise) the following three scenarios, which the two of them already use in arguments and both accept as fair tests. The vaccine margin: a government during a pandemic has doses for 60 percent of its population, none of whom are at serious risk, while a much poorer country with far higher mortality has none; both agree the case turns on whether the surplus above some domestic threshold is owed as a matter of justice or only charity, and disagree about where the threshold sits and why. The nurse recruitment case: a rich country actively recruits nurses trained at the public expense of a poor country; here, oddly, I expect convergence, and the statement should note what the convergence shows and does not show. The welfare-or-aid case: a legislature choosing between expanding a modest domestic benefit above sufficiency and funding an aid program that would lift many more people from severe deprivation — C and N disagree about whether the comparison is even of the right kind, one treating it as a straightforward aggregation, the other treating the domestic claim as a claim of fairness within a cooperative scheme that the foreign claim is not part of. If, in drafting, you conclude that some portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for instance, that they use justice to track different things — say so explicitly, name the portion, and explain why the wording you chose for the table handles it.
