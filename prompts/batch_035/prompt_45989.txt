"Explain how Hempel's raven paradox is generated from Nicod's criterion together with the equivalence condition. Then assess the Bayesian response to the paradox: does it succeed in showing that a white shoe is not really evidence that all ravens are black? Defend your answer."

and here's the answer I have to grade:

---

Hempel's raven paradox comes from two principles that both seem obviously true. Nicod's criterion says a universal claim "All F are G" is confirmed by each of its instances (each thing that is both F and G) and disconfirmed by each F that is not G. The equivalence condition says that whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms anything logically equivalent to it. "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," and also to "everything is either black or not a raven." Now take a white shoe. It's a non-black non-raven, so by Nicod's criterion it's an instance of "all non-black things are non-ravens," and by the equivalence condition it therefore confirms "all ravens are black." The same goes for anything else that isn't a raven — green apples, my backpack, the moon. This seems absurd (checking my closet shouldn't count as ornithology) but every step looks valid, so we have a genuine paradox about the nature of confirmation.

The Bayesian response says the problem is ignoring background knowledge. Confirmation has to be relativized to what you already know, and once you do that, the white shoe is not evidence at all. We know there are vastly more non-ravens than ravens in the world, so observing one more non-black non-raven doesn't move the probability of the hypothesis at all — P(white shoe | all ravens are black) = P(white shoe | not all ravens are black), roughly, so there's no confirmation. Seeing a black raven, by contrast, does raise the hypothesis's probability. So Nicod's criterion survives fine in the ordinary case, and the "paradoxical" confirmation simply never occurs once your priors reflect what you know about the world.

This also shows which principle has to go. Hempel himself concluded that the equivalence condition fails — the contrapositive and the original sentence get confirmed by different evidence, so confirmation can't be closed under logical equivalence — and the Bayesian story explains why: "all ravens are black" and "all non-black things are non-ravens" are equivalent as sentences, but not as confirmation targets once background knowledge is in play. (This is basically the same lesson as Goodman's grue paradox, where the same evidence ends up confirming incompatible hypotheses.) So the answer to the exam question is no: the white shoe is not really evidence. The logical equivalence is real, but confirmation isn't preserved across it.
