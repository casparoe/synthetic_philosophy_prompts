I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of probability: fifteen students, one ninety-minute session, movable tables, a whiteboard, and no materials budget beyond paper, dice, and printed circles. They have had a semester of introductory probability but no measure theory, and they should get no lecture up front and should not hear the name of the paradox until the debrief.

Design an in-class activity that makes them feel why the principle of indifference fails as a standalone guide to probability: that "chosen at random" is not self-interpreting, and that being uniform relative to one description of the possibilities is being non-uniform relative to another. The shape I have in mind: the class is split into three "chord factories," each with its own written operating procedure for producing a chord "uniformly at random" in a circle. The procedures should be physically executable in the room (dice, spinners, blindfolded dart throws at a printed circle) and each should feel, from the inside, like the canonical way of being indifferent — but the groups should not be told in advance that the procedures differ. Before any data is generated, every student individually commits, on a collected card, to the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. The factories then produce their chords, compute their frequencies, and the three lead inspectors are brought to the front and required to agree on a single industry-standard number — a task they should prove unable to complete, since each factory's figure is correct given its own procedure and nothing visible distinguishes the procedures in advance.

Please specify, in enough detail that I could run it tomorrow: the three operating procedures, including how many chords each group must generate for the three frequencies to separate despite sampling noise, and what I should do if two groups' ranges overlap anyway; a minute-by-minute timeline; the recording and scoring scheme, which should not involve real money; a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the structure of Bertrand's paradox — where each procedure's implicit measure entered, why the students' committed card estimates scattered the way they did, and what the principle of indifference needed but lacked; the things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (aim bias in the dart throws, early discovery that the procedures differ, groups negotiating their answers toward each other); and an honest note on which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce — in particular that a physical randomizer arrives with its own natural measure, which is precisely the privilege that the abstract "random chord" lacks.
