i'm a grad student trying to decide whether to spend my next two years on the Meno problem -- why knowledge is better than mere true belief -- and i keep going back and forth on which version of the question actually deserves the time. theres the basic one (why is knowledge more valuable than true belief at all), the swamping version (if a true belief already gets you to the right action or the right assertion, what does the extra stuff that makes it knowledge add, value-wise), and then the ones people claim are downstream of those: whether its actually understanding rather than knowledge thats distinctively valuable, and whether knowledge's value comes from its role as the norm for assertion and practical reasoning rather than from anything internal to the state itself. if you were advising someone with two years, a logic background, and no particular loyalty to any school of epistemology, which of these would you tell them to work on, and in what order? i'm less interested in the list than in the dependency structure: which question, if answered, would settle or reshape the others, and which ones are overrated or maybe not even well-formed as questions. and heres the thing that worries me: every proposed answer i've seen (reliabilist ones, virtue ones, the anti-luck people) seems to just relocate the value somewhere else, which makes me wonder if the real open question is whether the value problem is a substantive question or a demand for unification that epistemology was never going to deliver on. so also tell me which of these is tractable enough that two years could actually move it, versus which ones are worth caring about mostly because so much other work quietly presupposes an answer. no headings, bullets, bold text or tables please, just continuous prose like you'd write in a letter
