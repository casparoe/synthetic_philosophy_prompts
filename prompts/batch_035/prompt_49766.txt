I should declare some background before putting my question, as it will explain the odd angle of approach. I spent thirty-five years as an entomologist, specialising in the social biology of honeybees, and since retiring I have been working steadily through the metaphysics literature. Recently I encountered Peter van Inwagen's special composition question — roughly, the question of when some things compose a further thing — and I have begun to suspect that my own former profession has been quietly taking a position on it for over a century without ever announcing the fact. In my working life my colleagues and I moved without embarrassment between two ways of counting. When explaining division of labour, colony-level thermoregulation, or the fact that it is the colony, not the worker, that reproduces (a swarm founds a new colony), we spoke of the superorganism as a single living thing; when applying inclusive fitness theory, we counted the individual worker and asked how her behaviour propagated her genes. The same ambivalence runs through the rest of biology: mycologists happily treat a slime mould such as Physarum, a single cell with thousands of nuclei, as one organism, and botanists describe the aspen grove in Utah known as Pando as a single tree while ecologists count its stems; taxonomists give a lichen a species name even though it is a composite of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, thus naming the partnership rather than either party. What I want to know is what these practices presuppose about composition, if anything.

Please take the role of a professional metaphysician with a sound working knowledge of biology, and begin by brainstorming the main candidate answers to the special composition question — eliminativism or nihilism, van Inwagen's own criterion (roughly that some things compose a further thing when their joint activity constitutes a life), and the deflationary view that there is no further fact of the matter and our counting is bookkeeping — before committing to an answer. Then address the following. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of composition in the practices I have described: do they presuppose that composition is fixed by functional integration, by shared ancestry, by the level at which selection acts, or nothing at all? Second, tell me whether the two counting practices within a single discipline are consistent, or whether the superorganism talk and the gene's-eye talk quietly presuppose incompatible answers. Third, a question that particularly exercises me: van Inwagen's life-criterion was presumably meant to tell us when composition occurs, but in a superorganism the parts are themselves living things, so it seems the criterion must either license nested lives (a life made of lives) or deny that a colony is an organism; does his criterion actually adjudicate these cases, or does it leave them indeterminate? Finally, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with — I suspect much of this is underdetermined — and say what would have to change in ordinary biological practice, in field guides or taxonomy say, if we adopted the deflationary view seriously. Please be precise and, where the literature leaves things open, say so rather than manufacturing certainty.
