**Why God kept the slaughter: Maimonides on sacrifice as divine weaning**

In the Guide for the Perplexed (Part III, chapter 32), Maimonides argues that animal sacrifice was never the point. The Israelites had grown up in a world where the only religious life anyone knew was temple service: offering animals, burning incense, bowing before images. God, on Maimonides' telling, could not simply cancel all of that overnight, because, as he puts it, "It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed." His analogy is telling: a prophet today who told us to give up prayer, fasting, and petitioning God in times of trouble, and to serve God "in thought, and not by any action," would leave us baffled. So instead of abolishing the sacrificial cult, God redirected it -- same forms, new object. The temple, the altar, the offerings, the priesthood: all of it was retained and transferred from the worship of created beings to the worship of God, so that idolatry would be erased "without deterring or confusing the minds of the people."

I want to push this a step further than Maimonides ever states explicitly. If the sacrificial system is a pedagogical device for weaning a particular population off a particular habit, then it is essentially training wheels. And training wheels, by definition, come off. On this reading, the natural endpoint of Maimonides' own logic is that sacrifice is destined for obsolescence: once the habit is broken and knowledge of God is established by other means (and what other means could that be but study and prayer?), the entire apparatus of korbanot becomes religiously inert, a shell kept around only out of historical inertia.

Maimonides seems to half agree. Elsewhere in the same part of the Guide he claims that the specific details of the offerings -- the species, the numbers, the flour and oil -- have no explanations at all, that they're pure Divine decree, like a doctor's exact dosages. But this sits very awkwardly next to the concession theory. If the details of sacrifice are inherited from the idolatrous forms of the surrounding nations (the Sabians, as he calls them), then the details do have explanations, namely historical ones. So which is it? I think the two claims can only be reconciled if we read the "no explanation" remarks as applying to the details taken individually, while the concession story explains the institution as a whole. The details are arbitrary because the idolatrous customs they copied were, from God's perspective, arbitrary.

The upshot, and the part I actually believe: Maimonides is the first major figure in the Jewish tradition to hold a view that most moderns would have to construct from scratch -- that our own liturgy and ritual life is a cage built to house an older, worse animal, and that the point of the cage is to let the animal die in peace. Prayer, on his account elsewhere, exists as a substitute for sacrifice, which makes you wonder what, if anything, is the substitute for prayer.
