I'm a philosophy of religion student putting together a position paper on the Euthyphro dilemma, and before I submit it I want a consistency audit of the package of views I've drifted into. My professor wants us to state our commitments plainly and defend them as a set, not piecemeal, so that's the standard I'm hoping you'll hold me to. Here's roughly where I've landed after a semester of reading around this: (1) I'm a theist who takes God to be essentially good, where God's goodness is not a matter of God satisfying some independent standard — nothing external to God constrains or measures God. (2) I reject moral Platonism outright: there are no moral facts or properties floating free of all minds, waiting to be discovered like abstract objects. (3) I hold a modified divine command view: moral obligations are constituted by God's commands, roughly the way the rules of chess are constituted by the decisions of the chess federation, though I'd say God's commands necessarily track God's loving nature, so God couldn't have commanded us to be cruel. (4) I think the wrongness of torturing children for fun is knowable by a reflective atheist with no theological premises whatsoever — a well-run conscience gets you there without any reference to God. (5) On the Euthyphro question itself, I give the two-part answer that has felt right to me: God commands what God commands because it is good (the goodness being God's own nature), and it is obligatory because God commands it — the dilemma is a false one because goodness and obligation come apart. (6) Finally, on the hard case: I think Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac was praiseworthy obedience, and praiseworthy precisely because the command was morally unthinkable by ordinary lights — that's what made it faith rather than calculation.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what would have to give in each case? For example, I can already anticipate someone pressing me on whether (4) is compatible with (1) through (3) — if moral facts depend on God, how can an atheist know them without knowing God? — but my instinct is that dependence and epistemic access come apart, the way the truth of my birthdate doesn't require me to know my parents. If that's right, tell me; if there's a deeper problem lurking there, tell me that. More generally, go through the pairs and triples, rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each genuine tension say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs elsewhere in the set. Then tell me, if you had to recommend a single revision, which one would clean up the most. Please don't try to guess which commitment I'd be most reluctant to give up — I genuinely don't know, and I'd rather have the severity ranking than a diplomatic one.
