You are a senior philosopher of decision theory, comfortable with both the mathematics of expected utility and the normative debates around it. Before answering, think through the argument below carefully — you may open with a short brainstorming pass that maps where the pressure falls, but your final answer must be an explicit diagnosis, not a survey.

A colleague in economics has put an argument to me that, if sound, seems to wreck expected utility as a normative theory, and I cannot find the joint where it turns. Here it is, reconstructed in numbered steps:

(1) In the St. Petersburg game — a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and if that happens on toss n the player is paid 2^n ducats — the expected monetary value is infinite, since the sum of (2^n)(1/2^n) over all n is a sum of ones that never ends.

(2) Bernoulli's familiar reply is that the utility of money is concave, so the game's expected utility is finite: it can justify a modest entry fee but forbids a ruinous one. Let us grant this.

(3) The concavity fix is not stable, however. Take any utility function u that is unbounded above. Because u is unbounded, we can find payoffs x_1, x_2, x_3, ... with u(x_n) at least 2^n, and build a custom St. Petersburg game that pays x_n ducats when the first heads arrives on toss n. Its expected utility is at least (1/2^1)(2^1) + (1/2^2)(2^2) + ..., which again diverges. So for any agent with unbounded utility there exists a gamble of infinite expected utility, and by the same dominance reasoning as before the agent should be willing to pay any finite price to play it — up to and including everything the agent owns or will ever earn.

(4) The only agents who escape the construction are those whose utility is bounded above. But bounded utility carries its own absurdity: past some wealth level the agent cares vanishingly little about further gains, so there are enormous sums W such that the agent is nearly indifferent between keeping W and taking a 50% shot at 2W. A rational agent who would not trade a vast fortune for half a chance at double a vast fortune is at least as irrational as one who would stake everything on the custom game.

(5) Therefore, for every agent, expected utility theory either demands an absurd gamble or is saved only by an absurd indifference at the top of the value scale — and a normative theory that imposes one absurdity or the other on everyone is not a viable normative theory.

I do not believe the conclusion, but each step looks defensible to me, and the argument appears valid as it stands. Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked fine going in, and then price the rejection: what does giving it up commit me to elsewhere, in cases outside this puzzle? For what it is worth, I would most like to keep expected utility theory itself; I have no antecedent attachment to unbounded utility, but my colleague insists that switching to a bounded function "builds the answer in," and I cannot tell whether that complaint is deep or merely rhetorical. Please address that question directly in your verdict.
