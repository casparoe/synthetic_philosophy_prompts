I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and I run a kinda-irregular "weird ideas" lunch group at the office — about 10-12 people, mostly engineers plus a couple PMs, one hour in a conference room with pizza. Last month I went down a rabbit hole about what happens when AIs or countries or companies have to bargain over things, and I came across this idea called a "safe Pareto improvement" that I think I actually understand: when two players are headed for some costly conflict — a price war, a lawsuit, an actual fight — you can sometimes have a mediator, or a piece of pre-committed machinery, randomize among the outcomes the conflict could have produced, e.g. flip a coin over who gets the disputed thing, or randomly pick who concedes, in a way where both parties are guaranteed to do at least as well as they would have in the conflict and strictly better in expectation. The key is nobody has to trust the other side's intentions or reveal how much they secretly value the thing; the trick only needs each side to prefer "whatever the coin says" over "burn resources fighting." When I explained this on the whiteboard at lunch, everyone sort of shrugged and said "yeah, coin flips instead of fights, obvously." But I think they missed the interesting part, which is the "safe" bit: the improvement has to hold for every possible valuation each side could secretly have, and it has to not mess with incentives in the cases where the players would've reached a good outcome on their own anyway, and apparently most natural-seeming interventions fail one of those conditions quietly.
