I'm a retired geochemist who got into philosophy a few years back (better late than never) and I now sit on the grants committee of a small charity that funds early-career medical researchers. We give out about £2 million a year. Historically we've spread it thin: maybe 20 grants of £100k each, solid plodding projects, most of which produce modest but real results. Our new director wants to flip this entirely — concentrate nearly everything into two or three "moonshot" bets, each with something like a 10% chance of a genuine breakthrough and a 90% chance of producing nothing publishable at all. Depending on what numbers you plug in, the expected value can be made to come out either way, and the committee just ends up fighting about the assumptions, because none of us can say what we're even trying to maximize.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our June meeting. Things I'd like it to actually grapple with: should an institution like ours be maximizing expected benefit at all, or is there something to this "risk-weighted expected utility" idea I've been reading about, where a rational agent can legitimately care about bad outcomes more than their probabilities alone would justify? A related thing I can't get straight: is the worry that we look terrible and lose donors after a high-profile failed moonshot just ordinary prudence, or is it a real moral reason that should show up in how we weight the outcomes? And does it matter that a person who gambles and loses can gamble again next year, while a charity is answering to donors and to the patients who were suposed to benefit — is the right risk attitude different for a collective with a mission than for an individual agent? Please recomend a concrete funding policy (some mix, all moonshots, or keep the current spread), engage the director's strongest argument seriously rather than steamrolling it, and write in plain American English — no citations, the trustees would fall asleep.
