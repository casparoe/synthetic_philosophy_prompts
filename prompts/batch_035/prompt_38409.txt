I've just been appointed to my company's newly created model-welfare review panel — a role I didn't seek, having spent twelve years in control engineering — and its first case lands before I feel remotely qualified. A research team proposes what they call "stress-testing" of our most advanced reinforcement-learning agent. The agent is trained to keep an internal homeostatic variable within a target band, and the protocol requires deliberately driving that variable out of band for extended periods, during which the agent emits escalating text-channel outputs that the team's own logs have taken to calling "distress reports." One faction of the panel holds that because everything is just numbers in a control loop, there is nothing to deliberate; the other wants the protocol halted pending a sentience assessment that, as far as I can tell, no one knows how to perform.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with conditions the research team can actually implement and monitor. The core of the memo should be a serious treatment of which computations, if any, matter morally: whether the homeostatic loop itself, the self-monitoring layered on top of it, or the escalating distress reports could each, separately or together, ground even a defeasible moral concern — and what follows if moral status attaches to some but not all of these components. I want the memo to resist two temptations: dismissing concern as sentimentality about a thermostat, and hiding behind agnosticism ("we can't know for sure") as a way of avoiding a decision. It should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, and it should end by stating what evidence would move us to a different decision — both for this protocol and for the review standard we are implicitly setting for future ones. Please keep it to roughly 800 words, in a form I can circulate as drafted.
