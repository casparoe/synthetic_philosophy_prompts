I spent thirty-five years as an analytical chemist before retiring, and I have lately been reading epistemology — Ryle, and then some of the newer literature on skill. In the course of it I have apparently acquired a set of commitments about knowledge-how, and I would like to know whether they hang together. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of the following are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and how badly. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting an unordered list; for each, say what would have to give and what it costs to give it up; and finish by identifying the single revision that would clean up the most.

(1) Naturalism. I am a thoroughgoing naturalist about the mind. Nothing in skill or expertise involves non-natural faculties, and I expect that a completed cognitive science could, in principle, describe everything a practitioner has.

(2) Intellectualism about skill. I find myself sympathetic to the modern intellectualist line — associated, if I have this right, with Stanley and Williamson — on which knowing how to do something is a kind of propositional knowledge: knowing that such-and-such is a way of doing it, where the proposition is held under a practical, rather than a theoretical, manner of thinking.

(3) The verdicts of the bench. The best chromatographer I ever worked with ran separations nobody could match while holding a theory of her own solvent system that was, by her own later admission, chemically false. I have watched technicians identify a failing instrument from half a second of its sound. I cannot shake the conviction that in such cases the practitioner's performance embodies correct information while every proposition the practitioner can actually state about what they are doing is false or guesswork: the hands know things the mouth denies.

(4) Factivity. Knowing-how is factive in the strong sense. A trainee whose assay only succeeds while a senior colleague hovers, or a novice whose archery hits the target once by accident, does not know how to do these things. Knowing how to do something is incompatible with one's success in it being luck.

(5) Machines, in two classes. I am prepared to credit a chess engine, or a laboratory robot trained on ten thousand assay runs, with knowing how to do what it does. But I would deny knowing-how to a language model that has absorbed every protocol ever written and can discourse about the technique flawlessly, yet has never once acted. Something about a history of actual interaction with the world matters here, though I confess I cannot say exactly what.

My own suspicion is that (2) and (3) cannot both be true, and I worry that the talk of hands knowing things in (3) sits badly with the naturalism in (1). But I have mislocated a conflict among my own views before, so treat those suspicions as data, not as findings: if a pair I have flagged only looks tense and dissolves on inspection, show me how; if a pair I have not flagged is the real problem, say so plainly. And if one of the five is simply confused rather than in tension with its neighbors, tell me that too, since a confused commitment is presumably the cheapest one to revise.
