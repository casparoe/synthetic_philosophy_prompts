So in my ethics elective we just did Parfit's nonidentity problem and it broke my brain a little, and then I went home and made a list of cases to figure out what I actually think, and now I'm stuck. The setup for anyone who needs it: sometimes a decision determines WHICH people will exist, not just how well off they'll be, so if the people who end up existing still have lives worth living, it's weird to say they were harmed — they wouldn't have existed at all otherwise. Here are my gut verdicts. A couple on a malaria medication that will very likely give their future kid a serious heart condition, who conceive now instead of waiting three months and having a different, healthy kid: what they did was wrong, or at least bad, even though the kid who exists is glad to be alive. Same couple, but the medication only means the kid will need glasses: fine, not even a close call. A country that uses up most of its resources so that people two centuries later have lives that are barely, but genuinely, worth living: obviously, massively wrong. A woman who conceives now, at a bad point in her life, knowing that if she waited a year she'd have a different child under much better circumstances, but the child she actually has will still do okay: I keep flip flopping on this one.

My friend Jake says he's got it all captured by one principle: a decision about creating people is fine as long as every person who exists because of it has a life worth living, and anything else is just aesthetic discomfort dressed up as ethics. That gets the glasses case and sort of the young mother one, but I can't accept that the resource depletion thing is fine, and I can't see who exactly is worse off in it either — same trap. My own attempted principle is the opposite: an act is only wrong if it's worse for someone. But then the heart medication couple come out fine, which I don't accept, and honestly the depletion case too. So my two candidate principles each save some of my verdicts and torch the rest.

What I want from you: is there a principle, or maybe a couple of candidates, that captures my verdicts (or most of them), stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case? For each clause, tell me which case is supposed to motivate it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle can't save, because I suspect I have to give one up, I just don't know which. And please tell me what the principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on — the woman who conceives at the wrong time — plus one more I didn't list: a clinic that offers embryo screening, where parents who could select against a severe condition instead pick the affected embryo deliberately, because they want a child like themselves, and the child's life is expected to be good. Also, is there something special about decisions made BY the parents, versus a whole society's decisions, that should make the principle treat them differently?
