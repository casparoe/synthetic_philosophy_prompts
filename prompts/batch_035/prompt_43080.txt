I've followed the arguments about AI and global coordination since the early LessWrong days — Bostrom on singletons, Hanson's multipolar pushback, the recurring forum threads on whether a single decisive power would be a blessing or a trap — and after years of commenting I've finally tried to write down where I actually stand. Before I turn this into a long post, I want a consistency check, because I suspect the package doesn't hang together, but I genuinely can't see where it bends. Here is the position set:

(1) Coordination failure is the root problem. The gravest long-term risks humanity faces — arms races, unendorsed great-power war, environmental externalities, and above all a race to build transformative AI — are collective action failures, and the only reliable permanent fix is a singleton: a single decision-making agency with overwhelming strategic advantage. I regard an aligned singleton as the endgame worth steering toward, not a dystopia to avoid.

(2) I take value lock-in extremely seriously. A singleton that stabilizes its values would bring moral progress to a permanent halt, and since essentially every past moral framework now looks at least partially monstrous, our own current values are almost certainly badly wrong somewhere too. Freezing them would be a catastrophe on the order of the worst historical moral errors, made permanent.

(3) My best account of how moral progress actually happens is variation and selection among semi-independent political societies: different norm regimes serve as experiments, and the better-performing ones get copied, adapted, and competed against. I think this decentralized mechanism, not the reasoning of any single reflective authority, is why the moral trajectory of history trends upward at all.

(4) I think the transition to transformative AI will likely be fast and winner-take-all: whoever achieves superintelligence first will plausibly be decisive, and we won't get to iterate through multiple failed attempts and learn from them.

(5) I oppose world government among present-day humans and have argued against it in more than one forum thread; I think it would be a disaster here and now.

(6) I'm nonetheless a strong critic of monoculture in AI development: I favor multiple labs, multiple jurisdictions, and a diversity of alignment approaches, and I've pushed back hard on the idea that one effort should absorb everything.

(7) I think the lock-in worry in (2) can be answered by design rather than by abandoning (1): a well-built singleton would hold its values under moral uncertainty, sustain something like a long open-ended reflection among its subjects, and build revisability into itself from the start.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the singleton literature. Go through these commitments pairwise, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which tensions only look real and dissolve on inspection, and for each genuine tension what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I don't know which of these I'd give up, so don't assume I have a favorite — and please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. Please write your answer in American English.
