I'm in a little philosophy reading group and we've just started working through Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, starting with the essay on history (the "uses and disadvantages of history for life" one). Before our meeting I listened to a pop-philosophy podcast episode on it, and the host summarized the essay roughly like this: in this late work, Nietzsche argues that modern people are drowning in historical knowledge, and he divides history into three kinds — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — which map onto three types of people: the man of action who needs great models from the past, the conservative type who venerates local origins and tradition, and the critical thinker who subjects historical sources to rigorous scrutiny. Nietzsche, the host said, clearly prefers critical history since the other two are just worship dressed up. And because all this history makes us sick, the essay is really a plea to forget: only the "unhistorical" — our animal ability to live in the present and not remember — can restore our health. The wisest people of all, according to the podcast, are the "suprahistorical" men Nietzsche holds up as his ideal, who see that nothing truly new ever happens under the sun and that history has nothing left to teach. So the takeaway was basically that Nietzsche wants us to study less history, maybe almost none at all.

I'm pretty sure the essay is early Nietzsche, not late, since I thought the Untimely Meditations came before everything famous — and my group's edition has an introduction claiming Nietzsche actually says we need history, which sits awkwardly with the podcast's "forget everything" reading. Also the framing of "critical history" as careful source criticism felt suspiciously like the host was smuggling in a modern meaning. But honestly I don't know the text well enough yet to say.
