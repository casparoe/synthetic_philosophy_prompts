I serve on the editorial standards committee of a national public broadcaster, and we must decide whether to greenlight a docudrama about the mid-century radio astronomer Ingrid Halvorsen (1919–1989). The dispute has split the committee roughly down the middle, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation ahead of next month's vote. Please draft that memo for me.

Two scenes drive the controversy. The first dramatizes as straightforward fact a 1961 meeting at which Halvorsen allegedly agreed to let her defense ministry quietly co-opt her antenna array for signals intelligence; the meeting is a real historiographical controversy, resting on one ambiguous diary entry by a third party, and the film invents dialogue and depicts her nodding along. The second scene is a deathbed confession, wholly invented by the screenwriter, in which Halvorsen admits that her celebrated 1967 discovery depended on data she lifted from a junior colleague who died soon after and never complained publicly. The screenwriter defends both scenes as psychologically true and argues that a film labeled docudrama buys exactly this kind of license, and that the dead have no interests left to harm. Complicating matters, Halvorsen's daughter, now 84, controls the family archive the production has been using and has threatened to withdraw cooperation and go to the press if the deathbed scene survives. Committee opinion ranges from "cut both scenes or kill the project" to "approve as written, with at most a card in the opening credits," with most members somewhere in between, favoring some version of disclaimer or on-screen separation of fact from invention.

Please write a decision memo of the kind I could circulate to fellow committee members. It should reach a clear bottom line — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — and, if you recommend conditions, they should be specific and enforceable rather than aspirational (e.g., what counts as an impermissible invention versus an acceptable gap-fill, and where and how any audience-facing separation of fact from fiction should occur, given that disclaimers at the start are skipped and credit cards at the end are ignored). The reasoning should engage the strongest argument on the losing side, not just the winning one; in particular, I would like the memo to take seriously the question of what, if anything, we owe the dead themselves — as distinct from what we owe their descendants, our audience, or the historians whose open questions the film pretends to settle with fabricated evidence. Please write the memo in American English.
