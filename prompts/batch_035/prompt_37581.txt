I'm on the epistemology comp committee this spring and its my turn to draft the closure question. Write one 90-minute question for PhD students on epistemic closure and Nozick's tracking theory, aimed at people who've read Nozick and the follow-up literature but shouldn't be able to pass by summarizing it. Anchor it in a case I made up: a customs officer's radiation scanner beeps because the container really does hold cobalt-60, but unbeknownst to everyone the scanner is also wired to beep whenever the port's surveillance system flags a container, and in the nearest worlds where the container holds no radioactive material the surveillance flag is still on. So candidates have to figure out whether her belief that the container holds radioactive material is sensitive at all, what that means for the beliefs she'd form about entailed propositions, and whether the case forces a choice between saving tracking's intuitive verdicts and saving closure.

The question must require candidates to (a) actually derive the closure failure from the structure of nearest-¬p worlds rather than just asserting that tracking theory denies closure, (b) say what Nozick's relativization to method or basis does and doesn't fix in a case like this, (c) confront the abominable conjunction head on instead of footnoting it, and (d) evaluate at least one closure-friendly repair — safety, restricting closure to conjunction-introduction, whatever they want to propose — and say whether it pays for itself elsewhere.

Then write a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each out of 20, each with a one-line note on how weaker answers typically go wrong there, plus a sentence on what separates an A answer from a B answer.
