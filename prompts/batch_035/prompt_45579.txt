I direct a small grant program in formal decision theory. Our next call will fund roughly six researchers for three years on one-shot cooperation — the cluster of issues Douglas Hofstadter brought to public attention in his 1983 Scientific American columns under the name "superrationality." To fix ideas: in his "Platonia" scenario, an eccentric billionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by the following noon, that person receives an enormous sum, whereas if no telegram arrives, or more than one does, nobody receives anything, and the participants are forbidden to confer; the superrational recommendation is to send a telegram with probability one in twenty. In the "Luring Lottery," which the magazine actually ran, readers could submit any number of contest entries on a single postcard, the prize being a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to the submitter of a randomly drawn entry; submitting many entries raises one's chance of winning while shrinking the prize, and the superrational recommendation was again a mixed strategy — enter once, but only with small probability, calibrated to the expected number of responders. In the event, readers defected en masse, several entering googolplex-sized numbers of entries, and the prize collapsed to a negligible fraction of a cent.

The question I need answered: which open problems in this area most deserve the funding, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each item you prioritize, tell me what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for theorists as opposed to, say, behavioral experimenters, and what developments over the next few years would move it up or down your ranking. The candidates I am aware of are these: (i) the formal status of superrationality — under exactly what assumptions about shared reasoning, shared values, and common knowledge of both does cooperative play follow, and is it a genuine rival to, or merely a fragment of, functional or updateless styles of decision theory; (ii) the randomization problem — in both of Hofstadter's games the prescribed act is probabilistic, which raises questions about whether a decision rule may prescribe lotteries at all, whether the participants' randomizations should be correlated with one another, and what an agent ought to do when it can only guess at how many others are even playing; (iii) the similarity problem — how much symmetry between agents the argument requires, and whether it degrades gracefully when agents are only partially alike, whether in values or in reasoning power; (iv) robustness — whether superrational cooperation survives contact with a minority of straightforward utility maximizers, and whether there is any selection-level or market-level argument that would wash one-shot cooperators out of a population; and (v) the external payoffs — whether the framework genuinely illuminates voter turnout, competitive bidding, or races between AI systems trained on similar data, as some people in our orbit claim, or whether those applications are being oversold. Please state explicitly which of these you would deprioritize and why, feel free to nominate an open problem I have not listed if you think it belongs ahead of mine, and make the criteria governing your ordering — importance, tractability, neglectedness, dependency — explicit rather than implicit. To be clear about what I am not asking for: a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want a defended agenda for three years of someone's working life.
