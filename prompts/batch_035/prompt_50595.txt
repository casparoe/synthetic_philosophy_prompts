I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of physics course on the psi-ontic vs psi-epistemic question, and I want a decision tree, but built in a slightly unusual way. Everywhere I look, interpretaitons get sorted by whether there's collapse, or by whether there are hidden variables, and I'm tired of that axis. I want the tree organized around one question only: what kind of thing is the wavefunction? That means Bohmian mechanics and GRW should end up sitting next to each other on the same branch even though they're usually mortal enemies, and Copenhagen and QBism should get split apart even though people lump them together as "the ones where the observer shows up."

The tree should start from a commitment any physicist in the room already has, something like "I accept that quantum mechanics gets the statistics right," and every internal node should be a question a student can honestly answer before they know which interpretation it leads to -- concrete cases, not labels. Questions along the lines of: could two agents with the same complete physical information still legitimately assign different pure states to the same system? does it even make sense to write down a wavefunction for the whole universe, and if so, whose beliefs does it encode? if the wavefunction is ontic, is it a field living on a 3N-dimensional configuration space, and if so, are we prepared to say that configuration space is the real space and the 3D world is somehow derived from it? If someone wants to lean on the PBR theorem as a branch point, fine, but they should also have to say what they're buying when they accept it (basically that independently prepared systems don't get correlated behind your back) and where someone could refuse.

I want roughly 8 leaves covering at least: Bohmian-style views with a real psi plus extra ontology, GRW/collapse-type views where psi is the whole story, Everett, QBism, the old Einstein-style statistical ensemble reading, and at least one leaf for people who think the whole ontic/epistemic question is badly posed (but an honest version of that leaf, not just a shrug -- say what they'd claim the question presupposes that they deny).

Two things I really care about: first, wherever the tree can't actually separate two positions, say so out loud instead of pretending the branches did the work. My own suspicion is that Copenhagen-in-any-of-its-versions is nearly impossible to pin down with these questions, but maybe you can find a question that splits pragmatist Copenhagen from the rest, I'm genuinely unsure. Second, mark the unoccupied branches -- combinations of answers that nobody seems to defend, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored. Like, psi-epistemic AND no extra ontology AND believing a single-outcome story has to come out somewhere -- is anyone in that room, and what's wrong with it if not?

Audience is advanced undergrads who know the math but not the literature, so keep every node answerable without having read anything specific. Eight-ish internal questions max, and phrased concretely enough that answering them is more like doing a thought experiment than taking a survey.
