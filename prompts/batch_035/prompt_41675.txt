I teach a first-year module on historical method, and my one shot at actually teaching source criticism is a single two-hour seminar. The students are bright but arrive with a basically broken picture: they think critiquing a source means finding out whether the author was biased, and if he was, the source goes in the trash. I want to get them from that to something like a real sense of how we know anything about the distant past -- Herodotus and Thucydides arguing about how to handle testimony, medieval scribes copying and miscopying manuscripts, the modern stuff with radiocarbon dating where physics elbows in. But don't write the seminar for me, plan it. What has to be established first because everything else leans on it, what comes second and third, and why that order rather than another. Which single case or chain of transmission should carry the whole two hours -- I want one source we keep returning to and stretching, not a tour of examples -- and tell me what that carrier has to have and what it mustn't have. I can already predict some of the misconceptions: the gullible-Herodotus-versus-scientific-Thucydides caricature, "a primary source is the truth," and the one where they decide any copied manuscript is worthless because it's not the original. But there'll be ones I can't see coming, so map out the misconceptions they'll bring or form and say exactly where in the sequence each one should get headed off rather than left to fester till the end. Also: I keep wanting to use the analogy between the textual critics' rule that the harder, more awkward reading is more likely original -- scribes smooth things out, they don't complicate them -- and the way historians treat a detail that's inconvenient for the author's own story as more trustworthy. Work out whether that analogy belongs in a first-year seminar at all, and if it does, where in the order it should land and what it would be doing there. There's also the problem I've never solved: what does criticism even do when a source is the only witness to something, with nothing to cross-check against? Decide whether that belongs in this seminar or is the thing I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so. Finally, give me one question or tiny task to close with that would show whether they actually absorbed the core idea versus just learning the vocabulary. Throughout, make committed calls and defend them -- I don't want three candidate orderings and a shrug about which is best, I want you to pick. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text, the way you'd write an email to a colleague. (Also, one thing I defintely don't want: readings lists, we already assigned those.)
