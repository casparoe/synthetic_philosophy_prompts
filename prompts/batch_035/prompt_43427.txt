Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired biochemist, thirty-odd years of enzyme kinetics and shouting at the HPLC, and I've only properly gotten into philosophy and cognitive science since retiring. Somewhere in that reading I hit Hofstadter's idea that making variations on a theme is the real crux of creativity — his picture being that you take some conceptual structure and twiddle its knobs, turn the tempo knob or the genre knob or the medium knob, and see what comes out. It clicked with something I'd half-thought for years without having the words for, and now I keep testing it on cases and I can't get my own verdicts to line up. So I want your help doing this properly: here are my gut calls, tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and be honest about which of my verdicts has to go.

The verdicts, roughly as they come:

1. Our church organist transposes a hymn up a semitone because the tenors can't reach the top note. Not creative. Full stop. It's a pure knob-twiddle and nobody would dream of calling it otherwise.

2. My granddaughter, for a school assignment, took a famous poem and swapped every adjective for its antonym — "dark" became "bright", "cold" became "warm", and so on down the page. The output was genuinely novel, nobody had ever produced that text before, but I don't want to call it creative. It feels like turning a single knob by a mechanical rule.

3. Duchamp pencilled a moustache and a little goatee onto a cheap postcard of the Mona Lisa, and it's in galleries and everyone calls it a masterpiece of wit. I'm afraid I do think it's creative. Which is awkward, because on the face of it he twiddled exactly one knob, the facial-hair knob, on somebody else's structure.

4. A reggae cover of Wonderwall. That's twiddling the genre knob, surely, and yet I'd say there's some real creativity in it — though not much, and I can't tell you where the "not much" comes from. Possibly I'm just being a snob in one direction or the other.

5. Kekulé supposedly got the benzene ring from a dream of a snake seizing its own tail. (Yes, I know the story is probably varnished; it doesn't matter for my point.) If the knob there is "what if the chain eats its own tail", he didn't seem to do any work twiddling it — it just came to him in the dream — and it's creative all the same, indeed it's the textbook case.

6. When I make my Christmas cake I swap the brandy for bourbon some years. The bourbon year feels faintly creative to me, in a tiny domestic way, and I notice the semitone transposition (case 1) feels like nothing at all, even though both are single small turns of a single knob. Why?

7. A language model writes a sonnet about debugging in the style of Alexander Pope. My son insists it's creative, and I keep going back and forth. What I've noticed, and this is the bit that really bothers me, is that my "yes, creative" gets weaker the more such pieces the model produces — one Pope sonnet about debugging and I'm half-convinced, ten thousand before tea and I've gone cold on it. It seems like it shouldn't matter how many variations there are, but for me it clearly does.

My tentative principle, such as it is: knob-twiddling counts as creative only when the knob is deep in the structure — near the conceptual core — rather than sitting on the surface. Does that save the pair I can't reconcile, cases 2 and 3, where the antonym swap looks surface-level and yet Duchamp's moustache is somehow deep? Can it be patched without collapsing into "it's creative when I admire it"? Please state whatever principle you end up with as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it overturns rather than saves, tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed — say, a chess engine that finds a beautiful novelty nobody's played in a hundred years, or me swapping the bourbon back — and give me a straight answer on whether my scaling intuition in case 7 deserves to survive at all. I'd rather lose a verdict than keep it by hiding behind something unfalsifiable, which is the sort of thing we used to say about a certain kind of reviewer. Also, if you think there's no single principle here and it's genuinely two different things wearing one word, say that plainly — dont't strain for unity on my account.
