You are a philosopher of physics with long experience teaching interpretive issues in quantum mechanics to mathematically untrained but intellectually serious adults, and you are unusually good at diagnosing the order in which ideas must arrive for an audience that has no formalism to fall back on. Think carefully and brainstorm before you commit to a plan.

I teach a continuing-education evening course, "Physics for the Curious," at a community college. The students are retirees and autodidacts — comfortable with percentages, odds, and bookmaking-style reasoning, but with no calculus and no physics beyond popular books that use the word "collapse" without apology. Next session I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard on the many-worlds interpretation, and I've decided to organize the whole thing around its hardest internal problem rather than around the usual splitters-and-cats material: if every outcome of a measurement actually occurs in some branch, and nothing is ever suppressed or discarded, why should anyone expect outcomes with the Born weights — probability proportional to amplitude squared — rather than just counting branches? Don't explain the problem to me; I understand it. What I need is a teaching plan: the order the pieces must come in, the example that carries the session, the misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to head each off, and what to openly refuse to cover.

Two moves I've been toying with — tell me whether they belong, and where. First, a device I think of as a weighted quantum coin: a measurement that splits into exactly two branches, but with amplitudes such that the Born weights are one-third and two-thirds. Branch counting says one-half, the Born rule says otherwise, and so the naive counting view dies on the spot — but I worry the example needs a preamble establishing that amplitudes can be unequal in the first place, and I don't know how to give non-specialists a feel for that without deriving anything. Second, the branch-arbitrariness point: the number of branches isn't a well-defined quantity, since how finely we carve the decohering wavefunction into "worlds" is up to us, and a desperate branch-counter could simply re-carve until the counts happen to match the weights. I find this elegant but suspect it will strike the students as a technicality unless something earlier has made them care about branch individuation.

Beyond those: I know the standard misconceptions will arrive — that if everything happens somewhere, probability collapses to zero or one and rational expectation becomes pointless; that "I" will experience all the outcomes, so betting advice is meaningless; that the Everettian can just define "typical branch" by fiat. But I don't know the order in which the students will raise them, or which of them the sequence itself should absorb before they can voice it, and which it should let them hit head-on. I'm also torn about how much of the self-locating-uncertainty resolution to give them — the idea that before the measurement there's a legitimate question of which future branch will be "me" — versus how much of the decision-theoretic program, where rational betting preferences over branches are supposed to force the Born weights out, with the attendant circularity worry that the rationality axioms may quietly presuppose classical probability. My instinct is that one of these belongs at the climax and the other belongs in a final ten minutes as "where the professionals still fight," but I'm not sure which.

So give me: the dependency structure of the session — what has to be in place before the probability problem can even be stated as a problem rather than dismissed as a pun on the word "expect"; a verdict on my two candidate moves and the single best carrying example if either of mine is wrong; a misconception map with the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be defused and whether by pre-emption or confrontation; how to spend the last ten minutes; one question I can ask at the end whose answer would show me the idea has landed rather than merely been admired; and what I should openly decline to explain at this level — I assume decoherence as a formal process is on that list, but tell me what else is, and what sentence I should say when I decline.
