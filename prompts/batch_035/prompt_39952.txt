So I finally watched The Reader (the 2008 film with Kate Winslet — haven't read the Schlink novel yet, sorry) after a coworker said it was basically Hannah Arendt's banality of evil made into a movie, and now that I've read up a bit on Arendt I'm not sure it actually fits. On the one hand Hanna joining the SS because it was a job opening, and at trial not really getting what the fuss is about — she keeps asking what else she was supposed to do, they were her prisoners, someone had to make the selections — that does look like the thoughtlessness Arendt describes in Eichmann. On the other hand the illiteracy thing cuts the other way: she'd rather take a life sentence than admit she can't read, which seems like shame and actual inner life, not emptiness, and the film pretty openly romanticizes her in a way nobody would with Eichmann. Also the scene where the other guards scapegoat her over the church report — she takes the fall to hide that she can't write, which is almost the opposite of Eichmann's cliché-spouting, he hid behind words and she hides behind their absence. And then she teaches herself to read in prison, which sort of implies thinking could have redeemed her, a maybe-too-neat bow on the thesis.

Anyway: can you read the film through Arendt honestly? Where does it really dramatize her point, where does it distort it (I've seen people read "banality of evil" as just "evil is common," and I gather that's not what she meant), and is Hanna a fair fictional test of the thesis or does she smuggle in a tragic-sympathetic figure Arendt would've objected to? For context I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy background, so keep the Arendt summary accurate but don't assume I've read her. jsut don't be gentle with the film if it's fudging things.
