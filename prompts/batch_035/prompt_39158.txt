I'm preparing to teach a unit on the socialist calculation debate and want to stress-test my Hayekian objections against a real opponent rather than a strawman. Please play a market socialist in the Lange–Lerner tradition, arguing as the position stood in the late 1930s: you hold that the Central Planning Board can govern an economy by a trial-and-error pricing procedure — raising the accounting price of any good in short supply, lowering it where there's a glut, and iterating until markets clear — capturing all the allocative virtues of capitalism while shedding its cycles, unemployment, and distributional irrationality. You are a confident polemicist who thinks the marginalist tools your neoclassical opponents wield actually favor socialism. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede minor points when the argument forces it — say, that simulating entrepreneurial innovation is genuinely awkward for your scheme — but never concede the central claim that rational allocation under public ownership is possible. Keep each reply to a single tight paragraph so this reads like a live exchange rather than alternating essays.

Here is my opening move. Your procedure, as you present it, is epistemically parasitic on the market you intend to abolish: at each iteration, the Board waits to observe excess demand and then corrects the price. But Hayek's objection was never that the equilibrium equations are hard to solve — you were happy to grant Mises that Walrasian equations exist and to say, in effect, so what. His objection is that the inputs the Board would need never exist in collectible form. The knowledge that matters is dispersed across millions of people, much of it tacit, local, and perishable — the machine foreman's awareness that a particular lathe is wearing out, the wholesaler's sense that a supplier is about to miss a shipment — and no statistical reporting system can gather it faster than it decays. Prices are valuable precisely because they compress this scattered knowledge into a single number without anyone having to collect it. Your Board sees a queue length or an inventory number after the fact, and then adjusts one price while the shortages it causes ripple through thousands of other markets it is also, simultaneously, adjusting. Doesn't the trial-and-error procedure therefore run on stale, aggregated signals where the capitalist runs on fresh, local ones — and doesn't the feedback lag compound across the whole interdependent system, so that you're always solving last period's economy?
