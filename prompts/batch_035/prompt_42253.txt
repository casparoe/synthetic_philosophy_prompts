I'm a philosopher who every other spring teaches our department's intermediate logic course, and by the last third of the semester we're into set-theoretic foundations — cardinals, ordinals, the axiom of choice and its cronies. Zorn's lemma is where it always goes sideways for me. The students can recite the statement cold, but the second I tweak a single clause on the exam they crumble, which tells me they memorized the words and never figured out which parts of the lemma are actually load-bearing. So this year I want a question that punishes recitation. Write me one exam question, 25 marks, sized for about 60 minutes of a three-hour final, in three parts: (i) I'll hand them four modified statements of Zorn's lemma — one that quietly drops the nonemptiness requirement, one that weakens "every chain" to "every finite subset," one that swaps "maximal element" for "greatest element," and one that demands an upper bound for the empty chain as well — and they must say for each whether it's still provable (in ZFC, or maybe even in ZF) or outright false, with an explicit counterexample whenever they claim its false; (ii) a standard application, say that every proper ideal in a commutative ring with identity is contained in a maximal one, where they have to actually specify the poset and verify the chain condition instead of waving at it; and (iii) a short reflective bit on why the lemma doesn't just fall out of naive transfinite recursion — where exactly does the axiom of choice have to show up in the argument?

Along with the question I want a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks allocated (totalling 25), a one-line note on each about how students typically botch it, and a clear statement of what separates an A answer from a B. My own suspicion is the A/B line runs through whether they see that checking the union of a chain of proper ideals can't contain 1 is the whole game in part (ii), and whether they treat the empty-poset counterexample in part (i) as a cheap gotcha or as a genuine insight about the convention on whether the empty set counts as a chain — but feel free to disagree with me if you can say why. Be opinionated throughout: commit to the canonical counterexample you'd accept for each false variant rather than offering me a menu of equally good ones, and commit to where the A/B line falls. Start your response with the question text itself — no preamble, no setup, no restating what you're about to do — then the model answer, then the marking scheme.
