i'm a phd student in phil of language and i've spent the last year and a half reading the two-dimensional semantics literature, and I've hit a wall I want help thinking through. Not the standard "does 2D rescue the necessary a posteriori" stuff — I mean the meta-debate about what the epistemic two-dimensionalists are even doing, which as far as I can tell has been parked in the same spot since roughly the early 2000s.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. On one side you have the epistemic two-dimensionalists (Chalmers is the obvious one, Jackson in his own idiom, with conceptual analysis of the folk doing similar work for him). The thought: the primary intension of an expression is a function from scenanios — described in neutral, epistemically available vocabulary — to referents, and it's supposed to be graspable a priori. "Water," considered epistemically, picks out the watery stuff in a scenario whether or not it's H2O there; that's what lets them reframe the necessary a posteriori, say smart things about conceivability and possibility, and so on. On the other side you have Stalnaker and Soames, who both basically say: fine, there's an interesting function there, but it's not meaning. Stalnaker's version is metasemantic — the two-dimensional apparatus is for describing how context determines what our words are about, a tool for describing speakers, not part of the content of what's said. Soames' version is that what Chalmers calls the primary intension is really a metalinguistic description of how reference gets fixed, so the "a priori" claims come out a priori only because they're covertly claims about descriptions rather than about the world.

And that's where it sits. The epistemic camp keeps building out the machinery — scrutability theses, canonical descriptions, handling for the empty-term cases where nothing in the scenario satisfies the term at all — and the metasemantic camp keeps saying "interesting, but that's not semantics," and I can't find a single person who's been moved across the line in either direction. So here's what I want from you: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a survey. My working hypothesis, which I want you to push back on, is that it's at bottom a verbal dispute about "meaning" — or, slightly less trivially, that the two camps are answering different questions under one label: one side is doing epistemology of thought (what can be grasped without empirical input) and the other is doing the theory of assertion content (what a sentence says in a context), and both keep calling their subject "semantics" out of habit. But I'm not sure it's that clean, because the fight has downstream consequences that don't feel verbal at all: whether "water is H2O" is contingent in any sense worth caring about, whether zombie scenarios are coherently describable, whether the epistemic/possible-world gap can do any anti-physicalist work. Those don't look like things you can dissolve by stipulating two senses of a word.

So: is it verbal, is it a substantive-but-stuck disagreement about whether the a priori hooks into content at all, is it two projects that should just split the territory and stop arguing? And crucially — tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Is there some test case where the two frameworks give genuinely different answers (the cases where the neutral description and the actual reference come apart in weird ways seem like the place to look), or a distinction somebody could draw that would move one side? Or is this one of those debates where nothing short of one camp losing interest ends it — and if so, say that plainly and tell me why.
