Our decision-theory reading group has got stuck on an argument one of the members brought along, which purports to show that updateless decision theory either does no work at all or forbids the ordinary use of evidence. I can see that something in it must be wrong, but I cannot say what, and I would like a careful diagnosis: name the step I should reject, explain why it looked fine, and tell me what rejecting it costs. Please answer in British English, and argue the matter out on the merits rather than by appeal to authorities, citations or reading lists.

First the setting, so that we agree on it. In the counterfactual mugging, a predictor flips a fair coin long before you exist. Had it come up heads, the predictor would give you $10,000 today — but only if, before flipping, it predicted that you would hand over $100 if asked after a tails. It comes up tails; the prediction was that you would pay; you are asked for the $100. Updateless decision theory says that you should act on the policy that looked best from the vantage of an earlier self who had not yet seen how the coin landed, the thought being that what the predictor modelled was your procedure, and a procedure must be appraised as a whole, before anyone knows which branch of it will be executed. From that vantage the policy of paying when asked is worth (0.5 × $10,000) − (0.5 × $100) = $4,950, so you hand over the $100 in full knowledge that the coin landed tails. Call this the updatelessness premise: a rational agent acts on the policy that was best from the pre-observation vantage, not on the act that looks best from inside the branch it actually occupies.

Now the argument.

(1) The updatelessness premise, as just stated.

(2) The verdict it delivers in the mugging is correct; I want to keep it, since it is what made updatelessness attractive to me in the first place.

(3) Consider a doctor who has ordered a biopsy for a patient with worrying symptoms. The result comes back positive. The observation she now holds is precisely the sort of thing the pre-observation vantage lacked: her earlier self knew the test's accuracy and the base rates, but not this outcome. So, by (1), she must act on the policy that was best from that earlier vantage rather than on the act that looks best from inside the branch she actually occupies.

(4) If she obeys (1), she must not let the positive result select her action, since that is just to let the branch she occupies choose; she must do whatever the vantage's best policy prescribes for the patient who might have had any result — that is, she must treat the average patient in the average way, the biopsy notwithstanding. A decision theory with this consequence is untenable.

(5) Perhaps you will say that the committed policy may itself be conditional — operate if positive, discharge if negative — so that nothing is lost. But then, by parity, the mugged agent's policy may be coin-conditional: collect if heads, refuse if tails. On that policy the agent simply refuses, exactly as any ordinary updating theory advises, and updatelessness loses its distinctive verdict. Either the pre-observation vantage is allowed to make its policy sensitive to observations, in which case updatelessness does no work anywhere and (2) was never delivered by it, or it is not, in which case the doctor in (4) is forbidden her evidence. Either way, (1) is false.

Our friend is content with that conclusion: he says you should pay in the mugging only if paying is best given what you know, and what you know is that the coin landed tails, so you should refuse and let the counterfactual $10,000 go. I am not content. I want to keep (2), and I want to keep the doctor's right to act on her biopsy, and my sense is that both can be kept and that the argument above smuggles something in between (4) and (5), though I cannot name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the two things I want to keep cannot both be kept, say so plainly, tell me which to surrender, and set out the price.
