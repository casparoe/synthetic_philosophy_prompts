My friend and I keep going in circles about insects. Her credence that flies have morally relevant experience is like 0.9, mine is 0.1. Her move is the scale argument: even if you think sentience is super unlikely, there are on the order of 10^17 insects alive at any moment, so a low probability times an astronomical large number still gives you more expected suffering at stake than in all factory farming combined — and she says that means we should at least fund research into reducing insect populations. My instinct is that this is somehow a misuse of expected value reasoning, that multiplying a number I genuinely don't believe in by a huge number doesn't conjure the belief into existence. But I can't really defend that instinct.

So two questions. First, give me your own credence that at least some insects (flies, bees, whatever) have states that feel like something, decomposed: the probability that valenced experience can exist without anything like our kind of centralised brain, the probability that the behavioral evidence (trade-offs between injury risk and foraging, anxiety-like states, self-medication type stuff) reflects feeling rather than hardwired reflex, and any other terms you think belong. Give a number and one line of justification for each, then combine them.

Second, and more importantly: is the scale argument valid as a decision procedure, or is there a principled place to resist it? If you think there is, say where — a probability floor below which smallness times hugeness stops compounding, a difference between acting on behalf of many barely-possible beings vs. few probable ones, or whatever else you'd actually defend. And then tell me: does the disagreement between me (0.1) and my friend (0.9) mostly live in the sub-questions, or in whether the aggregation step is allowed at all? That's the thing I most want untangled.
