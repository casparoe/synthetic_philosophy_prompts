Two historians of science in my department have been arguing for over a decade about whether the Scientific Revolution happened. One holds that the period c. 1500–1700 marks a genuine, coherent rupture in how inquiry into nature was conducted, legitimated, and organized; the other holds that "the Scientific Revolution" is a historiographical construction, and that the changes in that window, once examined closely, are gradual, regionally uneven, and deeply rooted in medieval natural philosophy. They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and — this is the part I need captured faithfully — they have accepted a framing proposed by a neutral colleague: every periodization claim in the document will be treated as a clustering claim. A period is "real" if, for a specified set of variables (for example: the degree to which natural inquiry is mathematized; the standing of experiment as a source of authority; the prevalence of mechanical or corpuscular conceptions of nature; the institutional location of inquiry — universities versus courts and academies; the role of print and instruments; who counts as a legitimate inquirer), the rate of change on those variables is markedly higher within the proposed window than in the intervals on either side. Both historians have signed on to this framing. What they now disagree about is which variables belong on the list, how to weigh variables that moved together against ones that moved independently or not at all, whether any single variable's change could be individually decisive, and whether "revolution" additionally requires something the clustering analysis cannot capture — for instance, a self-consciousness among participants that a new way of investigating nature had arrived, or a consolidation that forecloses reversion.

Draft the joint statement the collaboration should produce. It should contain five parts. First, the claims both historians can sign — about the medieval antecedents of the key ideas, about which things did and did not change within the window, and about the period label itself as a historiographical fact, namely that it was coined and popularized long after the events it names and partly for purposes its coiners were pursuing. Second, their precise disagreements, each stated in wording that the historian holding that view would accept as a fair statement of it, not a caricature. Third, the cruxes, which must include at least: the variable-selection question; the threshold question (how much clustering, over how short an interval, distinguishes a rupture from a mere acceleration); and the question whether any of the dispute is verbal — whether, for instance, "revolution" connotes suddenness and coordination that neither historian actually asserts, so that both could accept all the clustering facts and still differ only over a word. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete test case both agree bears on it. In particular, include their agreed methodological control: applying the same clustering analysis to the Renaissance, c. 1350–1550. One historian predicts the analysis will find rupture there too, which he takes to vindicate periodization; the other predicts the same result, which she takes to show that the method finds ruptures wherever it looks. Record both predictions and both interpretations of each outcome, written down in advance. Fifth, a closing section in which each states what finding or argument would move them to substantially revise their position, and what they concede the other has already established.

Both are senior scholars with a shared loathing of fudge. The statement must not paper over the disagreement with "both perspectives have merit" language, and it should be possible to tell, from the finished document alone, exactly what each historian would predict — and bet on — if the clustering questions could actually be settled empirically. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table with one row per disagreement, then the cruxes with their test cases and recorded predictions.
