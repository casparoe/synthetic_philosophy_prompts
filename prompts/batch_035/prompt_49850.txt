I'm TA-ing an intro metaphysics course, and next week is the problem of universals. My professor keeps insisting the dispute is purely metaphysical with no observational consequences, and I want to test that by constructing a case where it actually matters — something I can bring to section to make the students care. My own idea of a worked answer would help too, since I need to know what a good one looks like before they try.

Here's the assignment: build a realistic scenario — workplace, insurance, warranty claims, licensing, inventory, nothing science-fictional — where a realist about universals (Armstrong-style: a property is literally one thing, wholly present in each instance) and a resemblance nominalist (there are only particulars, and "sharing a property" just means resembling in a respect) are forced to different practical verdicts, not merely different descriptions of the same verdict. The classic trap is the two red apples: everyone agrees the apples match, and only the metaphysical story differs. I want a case where the metaphysical difference changes a count, a payout, or a yes/no classification — think paying a bonus per "distinct defect" found, or a license that covers "the same design" however many times it's stamped out, but invent your own if you've got something better. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories themselves; you can't just stipulate that a contract uses the phrase "the same property," because a nominalist will happily reinterpret any clause like that. The case has to make one reading genuinely forced.

After the scenario: walk through each theory's verdict and why, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it. Also give the strongest counter from whichever theory comes out looking bad. If you decide the case can't honestly be built, say so and explain where the trick fails — that's a legit answer, my professor would probably love it more than the case itself. Format: a numbered list, exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion — (1) the scenario, (2) the realist's verdict and why, (3) the nominalist's verdict and why, (4) the feature doing the work and what removing it does, (5) the best comeback from the losing side. Keep the scenario something my students could actually picture happening, no philosophers names inside it.
