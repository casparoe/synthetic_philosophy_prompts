I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, writing a dissertation on fictionalism about arithmetic. I have been asked to serve as rapporteur for a semester-long adversarial collaboration between two members of my department: a committed Quinean platonist, who holds that mathematics earns its ontological commitments through the indispensability of mathematics to our best scientific theories, and a committed nominalist in the tradition of Hartry Field's programme of nominalising science, who holds that the appearance of such commitment reflects an expressive convenience that sufficiently hard philosophical work can eliminate. They have met every two weeks since September, and I must now draft the joint statement they will be invited to sign. Although my dissertation sides with the nominalist, the draft must be scrupulously neutral, and I would ask you to write it for me to the following specifications.

The two parties adopted a steelman-first protocol: before recording a disagreement, each party states the opponent's position in wording the opponent has accepted, and the draft must preserve this throughout — every disagreement must appear twice, once in the vocabulary each side prefers.

The statement should contain four parts.

Part 1: a numbered section of agreed claims, eight to twelve in all. Each claim must be non-vacuous in a specific sense: it must be something both parties accept but that a sympathiser of a third view — a formalist, an intuitionist, or a Mill-style empiricist about mathematics — would reject. Anything only a platonist or only a nominalist could sign belongs in Part 2, not here. The agreed section must cover at least: the legitimacy and undisturbed character of classical mathematical practice, whatever the metaphysics turns out to be; the determinacy of the truth values of arithmetical statements; the point of Benacerraf's identification problem (no particular set-theoretic reduction of arithmetic is privileged, so numbers are not simply sets); the fact that the semantic and the epistemological challenges facing platonism come apart and can be answered separately or together; and that mathematics is, as science is actually practised, indispensable — with the agreed claim stopping strictly short of any statement about what follows from this.

Part 2: a table of residual disagreements, one row per disagreement, each row stated twice — once in wording the platonist would accept as fair to his view, once in wording the nominalist would accept as fair to his view — with no row phrased so as to smuggle in a verdict.

Part 3: the cruxes, at least four, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The likely candidates are: (a) indispensability, including the fork between the hard road of Field-style nominalisation, which requires showing that mathematics is conservative over nominalised science, and the easy road of treating mathematical discourse as a representational aid rather than literal assertion; here the test both parties accept as bearing on the crux is what happened, and what would happen, when the nominalisation programme is extended beyond Newtonian gravitation to theories with less friendly ontologies, and each side must write down its prediction before the test is described; (b) epistemic access, in the neighborhood of Benacerraf's 1973 challenge, where the platonist's deflationary response to the demand for a causal story must be stated as he would state it, and the nominalist must record what he would count as that response succeeding; (c) the semantics of mathematical vocabulary — how the numeral "3" gets at the thing it is about, if there is such a thing; and (d) whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal, in the manner of an internal/external question about whether there really are numbers — each side should say which part it regards as verbal and why it thinks the other side should agree.

Part 4: the claims that could not be jointly signed. For each, diagnose the failure: whether one or both parties found the claim question-begging, whether it presupposed the resolution of a crux listed in Part 3, or whether it was simply a claim neither had reason to accept as stated.

Do not let either party capitulate: no claim may appear as agreed unless both would sign it on the stated terms. Where the logical space contains a combination that neither party occupies — for instance, easy-road nominalism conjoined with a robust commitment to literal mathematical reference, or platonism conjoined with the claim that mathematical existence statements lack determinate content — add a closing paragraph noting these, and say for each whether you judge it incoherent or merely unexplored.

Please write the statement in British English throughout (e.g., "nominalise", "programme", "recognise", "sceptical"). Keep it to roughly 1,500 words. Do not append a bibliography, footnoted citations, or a reading list; where a work is invoked, name it inline, as I have done above with Benacerraf's papers of 1965 and 1973, and paraphrase its content rather than quoting from it.
