The setup I've been using: a man is driving home at night on a route with exactly two highway exits, one after the other. He is absent-minded in one specific way — when he comes to an exit he cannot remember whether he has already passed one, and the exits look identical, so he genuinely cannot tell whether the exit in front of him is the first or the second. Leaving at the first exit means a dreadful road home (call that a payoff of 0); leaving at the second means a wonderful road home (payoff 4); missing both means a long loop (payoff 1). Before setting out, with his head clear, he works out his best rule: at each exit, continue with probability 2/3 and exit with probability 1/3. That gives an expected payoff of 4/3, which beats always exiting (0) and always continuing (1).

Now put him at an exit and ask what he should do right then. He might reason: under my own plan, I always reach the first exit but only reach the second two times out of three, so the exit in front of me is more likely the first — 3/5 versus 2/5. Working the numbers through with those weights, exiting now is worth 4 times 2/5, which is 8/5, and continuing now (and following the plan afterwards) is worth 3/5 times 2 plus 2/5 times 1, which also comes to 8/5. Exactly tied. So the best plan turns out to be one he is only just barely willing to keep following at the moment of choice. And if instead he split his uncertainty evenly between the two exits, exiting now would be worth 2 against 1.5 for continuing, and he'd want to tear the plan up on the spot.

My own view is that the plan made before the drive is what settles the matter, and that the knife-edge indifference at the exit somehow confirms this rather than undermining it; I'd put my credence around 0.7. My brother thinks the only real decision is the one made with the exit visible out the windshield, and that the plan being self-enforcing by a hair is a coincidence rather than a resolution; he's around 0.3.

Give me your number for the claim that the driver should adopt the continue-two-times-out-of-three plan before setting out and should keep following it at each exit, and decompose it. I would expect terms at least for: whether the puzzle as I've set it up has a single right answer at all (can he really be unable to tell the exits apart, and does it matter that he knows the route in advance?); whether what he should do is fixed by the perspective before the drive or by his situation at the exit; whether his 3/5-versus-2/5 way of locating himself is the right way to divide the uncertainty, as against splitting it evenly or something else; and whether the fact that the plan enforces itself only by a hair strengthens the case for it or exposes it as fragile. Assign a rough probability to each term, show how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term your number is most sensitive to and what would have to change about the setup — or about the way the uncertainty should be divided — to move it.
