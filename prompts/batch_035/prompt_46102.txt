I'm a software developer with no academic background, and for the past year I've been reading popular books on Gödel's incompleteness theorems and their supposed implications for minds and machines, including Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. The phrase that keeps coming up everywhere — from Hofstadter himself (who calls it "jootsing," for Jumping Out Of The System) to blog posts to people I argue with online — is some version of the slogan you can't do it from inside the system; you have to jump out of the system. I first met it in the MU-puzzle, where you start with the string MI, apply Hofstadter's typographical rules, and try to produce MU; you never can, but you can prove you never can by stepping outside and noticing that the rules never let the number of I's become a multiple of three, while MU contains no I's at all. That case seems clear enough, but the slogan plainly isn't one single claim, because I've seen it used to mean very different things. Last week at a meetup about artificial intelligence, another attendee ended an argument with it: he claimed that a sufficiently intelligent AI could never step back and question its own goals, because no system can jump out of itself — and he said Gödel proved it. I suspect he got that for free from a slogan that may not bear that weight, but I can't pin down why. What I would like is a menu of the distinct precise claims hiding in that one sentence — from the mathematical fact that within any given consistent formal system of sufficient power there are true statements it cannot prove, through something about how every jump lands you inside some further system so that there is no final outside, to something nearly mystical about no mind ever escaping its own concepts — and, for each, whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false. Then tell me which of those readings, if any, actually supports what my fellow attendee wanted the slogan for, and whether anything in Gödel's theorems transfers from formal systems to an AI examining its own goals. Please answer in American English.
