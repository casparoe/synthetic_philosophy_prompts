I'm a graduate student in cognitive science, and in our reading group on machine behavior the phrase "stochastic parrots" — a one-liner that entered circulation with a widely cited 2021 paper on the risks of large language models — has started to do a lot of load-bearing work. Last meeting it was used twice in one evening, for two quite different jobs. First, to dismiss a study in which an LLM produced apparently insightful feedback on student essays: since a parrot reproduces form without meaning, the argument ran, nothing the model does could count as evaluating an essay, so the study's apparent success must be an artifact of clever pattern-matching over surface features. Second, to support a prediction that no amount of scaling or fine-tuning will close the gap between current models and genuine understanding, because probabilistically recombining fragments of training text can never constitute grasping anything.

I suspect the slogan is compressing several distinct claims, only some of which support these uses, and I want to stop my group from sliding between them. Please disentangle them for me. Lay out, as a list I can bring to the next meeting, the different precise theses "language models are just stochastic parrots" could be expressing — beginning with near-trivialities about next-token prediction and sampling from distributions estimated over training data, moving through intermediate claims about reference, grounding, communicative intent, and the difference between producing sentences and asserting them, and ending with the strongest defensible version, whatever you take that to be. For each reading, tell me: whether it is trivially true of any statistical text generator, substantive and defensible, or outright false; whether it is an empirical claim, a conceptual claim, or a disguised stipulation about what deserves the word "understanding"; and, crucially, whether it supports (a) dismissing the feedback study as measuring nothing, and (b) the prediction that scale will never yield understanding. Along the way, flag which readings survive the observation that human language production is itself highly predictable from context and prior usage, and which survive a hypothetical model that never reproduces a training string verbatim yet answers novel questions fluently. Conclude by identifying which single reading the two uses in my meeting actually depend on, and whether that reading is one a reasonable opponent could accept while still maintaining that the model's outputs are reliable and useful in practice.
