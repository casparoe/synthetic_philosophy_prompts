Quick background: I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and through a series of editorial decisions I had no say in, I've become our unofficial happiness rankings guy. Every March I write the "Finland again" story, every March I lose a bit more of my soul. This year I want to smuggle something actually funny past my editor disguised as commentary, so help me out.

Write me an Onion-style news article — headline plus 450-550 words — about a fictional mid-sized country (invent it, vaguely European is fine) whose government has just opened a Ministry of Ranking Position, a whole department tasked with taking first place in the World Happiness Report. Sources inside the ministry leak its actual playbook: a national ad campaign to lower citizens' mental image of the best possible life (so the Cantril ladder question works out better for everyone, no lifestyle changes required), guidance nudging respondents away from answering 10 because a 10 "reads as unserious" to the coders, and a lone internal statistician who keeps pointing out the gap to Finland is well within the noise and keeps getting reassigned.

The jokes have to come from getting the methodology right — a happiness researcher should laugh, not wince. So, the load-bearing facts to build on: the ladder question is self-anchored against the respondent's own imagined best life, which is a genuinely exploitable loophole; the top of the table is seperated by hundredths of a point on a three-year average of a 0-10 life evaluation, not a measure of moment-to-moment joy; and there's a real open question of whether the survey captures how lives are going or how willing a population is to complain to a pollster about it. Don't have anyone say "Goodhart's law" out loud — let the reader do that part themselves. Include at least one quote from a furious survey methodologist and a spokesperson who talks about "momentum in the rankings" like it's a football table.

Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader. And one constraint I feel strongly about: nobody in the piece gets to say "happiness can't be measured." The whole engine of the satire is that it sort of can, which is precisely what makes gaming it both possible and a little sad. If you want a closer, something about the ministry celebrating when they climb one place while the actual average score goes down would be nice, but you do you.
