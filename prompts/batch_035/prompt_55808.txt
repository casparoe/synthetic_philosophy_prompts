I am a retired physical chemist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy of mind, and I have become mildly obsessed with the frame problem: the difficulty of saying what stays unchanged when an agent acts, and the broader worry, pressed by Pylyshyn and taken up by Fodor, that it reveals something deep about the absence of any general account of which facts are relevant to which inferences. My reading group — mostly retired scientists — keeps talking past one another about whether the problem was ever genuinely deep, whether machine learning has simply solved it, or whether it was a confusion all along. To impose some order, I should like a decision tree we can work through as a quiz. The starting commitment is: an agent carrying out an everyday task cannot, even in principle, update every consequence of everything it believes. Each branch node should be a question answerable by someone who has never heard the labels — for instance, whether a system that learns statistical associations between observations dissolves the problem or merely relocates it, or whether an agent with no internal model of the world escapes it, or whether the real depth of the problem lies in relevance rather than in inference at all. The leaves should be the recognisable positions: the logicist who expects a formal solution in the spirit of circumscription, the deflationist for whom modern machine learning has settled the matter, Fodor's pessimism about a general solution short of heavy modularity, Pylyshyn's reading of the problem as an old epistemological one in computational dress, and the antirepresentationalist or engineering-naturalist stance of the sort associated with Brooks and Dennett. Please give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate (I suspect the last two), and note any unoccupied combinations of branch answers, with a brief diagnosis of whether nobody sits there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
