I work in policy engineering for a big platform's trust & safety org — not the moderator side, the side that writes and implements the rules moderators enforce. So my world is: policy docs with tiered definitions, classifiers that output labels like "harassment," enforcement ladders (warning → takedown → account restriction), appeals, and a permanent fight about whether a label means the rulebook definition or what the pipeline actually does with it.

Here's my temptation and I want it stress-tested. There's a view in metaethics, I think called expressivism or noncognitivism, that says a normative claim like "lying is wrong" isn't describing a fact — it expresses an attitude, boo lying, or commits you to a stance. When I squint, that looks like exactly what we do: "this constitutes harassment" on our platform isn't really a description of the message, it's an enforcement move — us taking a stance and triggering the ladder. The label means, roughly, whatever we're prepared to do about it. I gather there's a famous objection to this view, the Frege–Geach problem, something about embedded contexts — conditionals, negation — where the claim isn't asserted outright, and I want it translated fully into my vocabulary, since I keep hearing about it and bouncing off the examples.

So, the actual ask: restate expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem in terms I actually use — policy conditionals ("if a message constitutes harassment, a second violation escalates to account-level action"), label negation, mixed inferences where one premise is a norm and one is a fact, and users and moderators who sincerely disagree about whether something was harassment (which in my world is the entire appeals system). Show me where the "label = stance" reading breaks. If the label's meaning just is our stance, what is it contributing when it sits in the antecedent of a conditional we're not asserting? What is the classifier outputting when it's functioning as a premise rather than a verdict? Why can't we say the antecedent gets some separate "as-if" meaning without admitting the label has a stable content across asserted and unasserted contexts — which is, I suspect, the whole fight.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What in the philosophers' problem translates cleanly onto my conditionals-and-ladders world, what had to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all — e.g. do we even have their problem of the attitude going missing in unasserted contexts, or does a rules architecture just sidestep it in a way natural language doesn't? And when the expressivists patched their view — quasi-realism, or whatever the moves are called, I don't need the names — what would the equivalent patch look like as an architecture on my side? Something like a two-layer system where the stance layer generates the rules but the logic layer enforces stable meanings? Would that be vindicating my operationalist instinct or quietly smuggling realist meaning back in? Last thing: as we move from keyword rules toward models that actually apply judgement, is that transition going to expose something the rules-based system hid, philosophically speaking?

Background on me so you can pitch it right: strong on logic at the level of someone who writes rules engines for a living, one intro philosophy course about a decade ago, so translate the jargon as you go. Be honest about where my operationalism is subtly wrong, not just where it's right — I can take it.
