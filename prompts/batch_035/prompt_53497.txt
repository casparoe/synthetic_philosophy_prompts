Analyze the Rwandan genocide of 1994 as a test case for the disagreement between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen about what explains ordinary people's participation in mass killing. Browning's Ordinary Men (1992), reconstructing the shootings and deportations carried out by Reserve Police Battalion 101 from the testimony of the perpetrators themselves, attributed the killing to situational pressures — obedience to authority, conformity to the immediate group, the gradual escalation of what was asked, the bureaucratic insulation of pulling triggers far from home, and the simple option, rarely exercised, of stepping aside. Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996), studying much the same battalion, answered that this whitewashes the perpetrators: what moved them was an eliminationist antisemitism so deeply embedded in German political culture that the killing was something most of them were prepared to do willingly, and Goldhagen laid particular stress on the fact that no German was ever seriously punished for refusing. Neither author wrote about Rwanda, so the mapping is yours to construct.

Take as the salient facts of the case the following: between April and July 1994, roughly 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed in about a hundred days, most by machete and club rather than by industrial means; the killing was set in motion within hours of President Habyarimana's plane being shot down, with roadblocks and target lists appearing with startling speed; the direct killing was done by the Interahamwe and other militias, by the presidential guard and army units, and in many communes by ordinary Hutu civilians recruited at village meetings and roadblocks; propaganda organs such as Radio RTLM had for years been calling Tutsi inyenzi — cockroaches — an enemy within, drawing on Hutu and Tutsi identities that colonial rule had rigidified into racial categories; victims were frequently killed by neighbors and acquaintances who knew them by name; and participation was unevenly coerced — in some places a Hutu who refused to kill suffered little worse than mockery, while in others refusal was itself punished, and looting of houses, livestock, and land was an openly acknowledged incentive.

Then run the analysis in both directions. First, what in Rwanda occupies each of Browning's explanatory slots: the peer group at the roadblock, the burgomaster or army officer giving orders, the habituation that set in as the weeks went by, the moments at which an individual could quietly decline? And what occupies Goldhagen's slot of eliminationist ideology: does Hutu Power as broadcast by RTLM count as an eliminationist creed in his sense, given that it was deliberately cultivated by a political elite over years rather than a sedimented national attitude centuries deep? Second, which features of the episode fit neither account well — the face-to-face intimacy of killing one's neighbors, the fact that many killers were unpaid and some volunteered, the mixture of ideological fervor, opportunism, and fear of being taken for a sympathizer in a single perpetrator, the speed with which a country that had been at war fell into slaughter? Third, what does the case do to the theories: does it vindicate Browning's situationalism, Goldhagen's ideological explanation, some mixture of the two, or does it show that the dichotomy itself is an artifact of the Holocaust — the only case either author had before them? Where a framework is genuinely silent, say so rather than stretching it to speak. Conclude with what you think Rwanda most clearly establishes about explaining perpetrators, and what questions it leaves precisely where Browning and Goldhagen left them.
