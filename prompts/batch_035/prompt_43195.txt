I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics working on issues at the interface of cosmology and epistemology, and I've become preoccupied with why the Boltzmann-brain debate has gone nowhere for the better part of two decades. The empirical situation, as everyone on all sides concedes, is fixed: several well-motivated cosmological models (roughly, any with an eternal or very long-lived de Sitter-like phase) predict vastly more observers who randomly fluctuate into existence out of a thermal bath, complete with pseudo-memories, than observers who evolve from a low-entropy beginning. Yet no one thinks they are probably a Boltzmann brain, and no one thinks any observation could reveal it. So the fight is not about evidence in the ordinary sense; it is about what follows for theory choice. One camp, most prominently Sean Carroll (in his 2017 paper "Why Boltzmann Brains Are Bad"), holds that a theory predicting BB-dominance is unacceptable because it is cognitively unstable: a theory cannot be simultaneously true and justifiably believed if it predicts that most of the believers in it hold their beliefs on the basis of random fluctuation. A second camp treats the whole problem as an artifact of the measure problem in cosmology: on the correct (as yet unknown) probability measure over observers, ordinary observers dominate, and the appearance of catastrophe comes from illegitimate ways of counting. A third camp — in the spirit of Hartle and Srednicki's work on typicality — presses the skeptical point that the anti-BB arguments silently presuppose that we are typical observers, which is itself a substantive assumption that science cannot simply help itself to; on this view, even Carroll's cognitive-instability argument smuggles in a prior it claims to derive. Relatedly, Don Page has argued that if you take the anti-BB reasoning fully seriously, it yields the further conclusion that theories of this kind predict the universe should not have lasted as long as it visibly has — which some find a reductio of the reasoning and others a further constraint on theories. These positions have been re-articulated many times since roughly the mid-2000s without anyone budging, and I have a hypothesis about why: the disagreement is neither empirical nor really about cosmology at all — it is a disagreement about what may serve as a legitimate criterion of theory choice, namely whether epistemic self-undermining counts as a defect of a theory or merely as a fact about theorists. If that is right, the impasse is partly an artifact of everyone formulating the issue as a physics question when it is a question about the epistemic status of self-locating probability. But I want a genuine diagnosis rather than confirmation of my own story, so: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, empirical in disguise, a difference about which cases count as data, or a clash of bedrock priors that no argument can reach? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a principled derivation of a preferred measure, a decision-theoretic treatment of self-locating uncertainty, a principled way of distinguishing Boltzmann brains from ordinary observers whose memories are anyway unreliable? Or is there a reason to expect no progress, and if so, what is it? Please support the diagnosis with what the parties actually claim, and be honest about whether my hypothesis survives.
