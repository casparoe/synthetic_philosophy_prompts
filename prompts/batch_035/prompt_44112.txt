I sit on the curriculum and instruction committee of a mid-sized public school district, and we must decide within the month whether to adopt a new K–8 mathematics program for district-wide use. The program under consideration front-loads procedural fluency and content coverage: it introduces formal algorithms and standard notation from the earliest grades, sequences topics strictly by prerequisite, and measures progress through timed mastery checks that gate advancement. Its supporters — two school board members, a well-organized parents' group, and a consultant who cites international benchmarking data — argue that our current program has drifted into open-ended exploration that leaves too many students without reliable skills, and that disciplined, cumulative content coverage is the proven corrective. The opposition — a faction of our own teachers and a math-education professor at the local university — does not dispute the fluency data; they argue that the proposed program institutionalizes a picture of learning in which the material never becomes the student's own, and that what looks like rigor in October looks like apathy by April.

Before the vote, several of us want the committee to weigh the proposal against Alfred North Whitehead's essays on education, which our late superintendent used to press on the district. Whitehead's framework holds that education moves through a repeating rhythm of stages — romance, in which the student's interest is kindled by the sheer novelty and possibility of a subject; precision, in which technique and exact knowledge are drilled; and generalization, in which the student throws what has been learned into fresh combinations and uses it beyond the context in which it was acquired. He attacked what he called inert ideas: propositions received into the mind without being used, tested, or thrown into new combinations, and he held that this stage-misplaced instruction is worse than no instruction — that it actively kills the capacity for later learning, and that forcing precision before romance has run its course merely teaches the student that the subject is dead.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee ahead of the vote. It should:

1. Give a concrete recommendation — adopt the program outright, reject it, or adopt with specified modifications or a pilot structure and timeline — that the committee could act on as written.
2. Apply Whitehead's rhythm honestly to the specifics of this proposal: say which elements of the program sit at which stage, whether the program can be read as compressing the romance stage or as skipping it entirely, and what the timed mastery gates do to the generalization stage. Where Whitehead's framework cuts against the fluency data, say so plainly; where the fluency data cuts against Whitehead, say that too.
3. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation rather than restating it as a caricature — and note, if it is true, that Whitehead himself prized exactness and was contemptuous of aimless discovery, so that neither faction gets to claim him for free.
4. Address the practical objection that a philosopher's rhythm is not an implementation plan: tell us what the recommendation implies for scheduling, teacher training, or assessment that the superintendent's office could actually cost.
