So at our rationality meetup this week someone brought up the absent-minded driver problem and two members ended up arguing about it for forty minutes while the rest of us just watched. Setup for anyone who needs it: a driver passes two identical intersections and can't remember which one he's currently at — that's the absent-mindedness — and at each one he can turn or go straight. Turning at the first intersection pays 0, straight at the first then turn at the second pays 4, straight through both pays 1. The intersections look identical, he has no notes, no navigation, no way to leave himself a signal; the amnesia is total and there's no commitment device anywhere in the problem.

Dev opened with "this is trivially a planning problem: pick a fixed policy, go straight with probability p at each intersection, maximize p²·1 + p(1−p)·4, that gives p = 2/3 and expected payoff 4/3, done. Whatever the driver does 'at the intersection' is just the plan executing itself — the planner already knew the driver would wake up at an intersection with no extra info, so in-the-moment deliberation can only add noise." Maya pushed back: "the whole point of the setup is that the driver WILL find himself standing at an intersection, deciding, with the policy question live again. And from the inside, 'I am at an intersection' is real evidence — the more the policy goes straight, the more likely I am to be standing at the second one when I find myself at an intersection, and turning at the second pays 4. That's self-locating uncertainty, same shape as Sleeping Beauty, and your ex ante number just quietly assumes he can bind his future self, which the problem forbids." Dev actually ran the numbers back at her: "under the 2/3 policy, conditional on being at an intersection you're at the first with probability 3/5 and the second with 2/5. Turn now: worth 4·(2/5) = 1.6. Go straight now and continue on policy: also 1.6. The plan is a fixed point — the driver at the intersection has literally zero reason to deviate, so it's self-enforcing, no binding required." Maya's reply was that indifference isn't compliance: "an exactly-indifferent driver flips on the slightest bias — say turning feels safer at an intersection, or his probability estimate is off by a hair — and your plan survives only on that knife's edge. A solution that needs the driver to be indifferent rather than preferring to follow it is a hope, not a plan. I want the policy the driver at the intersection would actually endorse, and I don't believe ex ante optimization gives you that in general — maybe it does here by accident." Dev's last word: every policy faces the robustness issue, and if you demand the driver positively prefer his continuation you're smuggling a commitment device back in after stipulating it away; 2/3 is where his in-the-moment best response includes the plan itself, and that's the strongest self-enforcement on offer.
