I am a philosopher of religion who has defended a Molinist account of divine providence for most of my career: God possesses, logically prior to the creative decree, knowledge of the truth values of subjunctive conditionals of freedom — what any possible free creature would do in any appropriately specified set of circumstances — and this so-called middle knowledge enables God to select, from among the worlds that are feasible for Him, which one to actualize, all while the creatures remain libertarianly free. I regard it as the only account on the market that secures both exhaustive providence and genuine libertarian freedom, and I have staked a good deal of my work on it. Tell me what would count against it.

I do not want the standard recitation of the grounding objection, though I do want an assessment of how bad it really is. It has been repeated for so long that I have lost track of whether its force has been blunted by the responses or merely rephrased. What I want instead is a map of the view's exposure, and I have a particular hunch about where its deepest vulnerability lies, which I will state at the end.

Consider, at least, the following candidate defeaters, and feel free to add others. First, the semantic entanglement: Molinism's fate appears to be tied to a question in the philosophy of language that is normally debated without any theology in view — namely, whether indeterministic "would" conditionals have truth values at all. If a coin toss is undetermined, many philosophers deny there is any fact of the matter about how the coin would come up in this very toss; and if that is right for coins, it is obscure why it should be wrong for free choices. If subjunctives of freedom lack truth values in the indeterministic case, middle knowledge has no object, and Molinism collapses into simple foreknowledge or open theism, views it was supposed to improve upon. Second, the feasibility problem: Molinism explains providence by appeal to which worlds are feasible, but it offers no account of which worlds those are, and some of my colleagues argue this leaves God's choice among feasible worlds either arbitrary or inexplicable — and in either case threatens to make God responsible for evil in a way the free will defense was meant to preclude. Third, the collapse worry raised from a different direction: if God's selecting a world on the basis of the counterfactuals is what makes certain actions actual, one might argue the creature's freedom is compromised after all, so that Molinism fails by its own lights and buys neither of the goods it advertises.

For each candidate defeater, tell me: what exactly would have to be true for it to succeed, which of these are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones, what committed Molinists have themselves conceded as counting against the view (as opposed to what critics allege), and where each should be ranked on a scale from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I am also interested in the meta-question: can anything count against Molinism at all, or has it been insulated by qualifications — retreating, for instance, from claims about which counterfactuals are true to the bare claim that they are true? If it has been insulated, say so plainly, and then say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind, or whether it is on a par with, say, a fundamental physical theory whose auxiliaries are inevitably underdetermined.

I will tell you what I would personally find most persuasive, since I want you to press on it. I have come to suspect that the whole structure stands or falls with the philosophy of language: that middle knowledge is only as secure as the thesis that indeterministic "would" conditionals are truth-apt, and that if the semantics of such conditionals goes the way many think the semantics of future contingents should go, Molinism does not merely lose an auxiliary assumption but loses its subject matter. If you think that is wrong — if the view can survive the loss of truth-apt "would" conditionals, or if the analogy with coins and future contingents is a poor one — I want to hear exactly where it breaks.
