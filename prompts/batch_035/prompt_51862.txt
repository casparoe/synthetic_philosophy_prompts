I teach a graduate seminar on the foundations of set theory, and every year it ends the same way: I present Russell's paradox, walk through the main escape routes, and then the class discovers that the professionals have been arguing about which route is correct for well over a century without visible movement. The iterative conception tells us to build the universe in well-ordered stages, so that no set can ever be a member of itself; the limitation-of-size tradition says a collection forms a set only when it is not "too big," which recasts the paradox as a boundary phenomenon at the edge of Cantor's absolute infinite; the predicativists blame the impredicative definition itself and rebuild mathematics under a much more austere comprehension principle; and a small but stubborn minority, following the dialetheists, propose to keep naive comprehension and simply tolerate the contradiction inside a paraconsistent logic. All four camps agree on the theorems, all agree ZFC is a safe place to do mathematics, and none has budged the others on the philosophical question of which reaction, if any, captures what a set really is.

I have a working hypothesis I would like tested. My suspicion is that the debate is largely, though perhaps not entirely, verbal: that "set" has become a term of art whose meaning is fixed differently by each formal system, and that the residual substantive question is not about sets but about whether the iterative hierarchy is an interpretation that captures a pre-formal concept or an explication that quietly replaces it — in something like Carnap's sense — with a more useful one. On that reading, the parties are answering different questions under one heading: one group is doing conceptual analysis, another is doing engineering for the working mathematician, and a third is really asking about the epistemology of consistency claims.

Is that diagnosis right, in whole or in part? Identify precisely where the disagreement is verbal, where it is substantive, and whether any hidden formal or empirical question is doing real work. Then tell me honestly what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here — a determinate verdict on the continuum hypothesis, a sharper explication of "explication," a philosophical argument that one camp's question is simply misconceived — or whether this is an impasse we should expect to be permanent, and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the question.
