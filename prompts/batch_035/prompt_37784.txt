I help run the cause-selection reading group at my university's effective altruism chapter, and this term we've been working through how the movement actually picks causes — the importance, tractability, and neglectedness framework that shows up in the career and charity advice literature. The group has quietly split into two camps, and I've realized the split isn't really about any empirical question: it's about which analogy each camp carries around in their head.

The first camp treats cause prioritization as fundamentally a matter of investment research. On their picture, the philanthropic landscape is a market: other funders and researchers are market participants, their attention is capital, a cause being crowded is like a stock being efficiently priced, and neglectedness is mispricing — the reason a small donor or career-seeker can get outsized returns by looking where nobody else is. Tractability is your information edge. They use the analogy to argue that the whole point of cause selection is finding what everyone else has missed, and that as the movement grows and the mispricings get corrected, our advantage should erode — so we should expect to keep moving. The second camp is disgusted by this and says the right picture is an emergency room. A triage nurse doesn't look for undervalued patients; she orders by severity and urgency, because the point is that someone is bleeding out. On their picture, importance is severity, the constraint is fixed capacity (beds, staff, our limited budget and careers), and crowdedness matters only in the boring sense that you can't double-treat the same patient. They use the analogy to argue that speculative, low-probability causes are a failure of triage discipline, and that the movement's flirtation with them reflects the investor mindset's tolerance for long-shot bets.

I want to adjudicate this properly, or at least understand it. Please audit both analogies. For each, give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the market, the price, the investor, the dividend; and what plays the patient, the severity, the triage protocol, the stock of beds — and then say where the mapping breaks down. I'm especially interested in what each analogy smuggles in: does the market picture assume that doing good generates returns the doer can capture, that attention equilibrates the way capital does, that other actors' causes are priced by anything like rationality? Does the triage picture assume a single decision-maker with a fixed capacity that can't be expanded — and does it silently treat "how likely is the treatment to work" as less important than triage actually treats it? Then give me at least one test case where the two analogies yield opposite verdicts (I'd guess something like climate change — enormous and severe but very crowded — versus some small, totally neglected, fairly intractable cause), and say whether the framework itself adjudicates the disagreement or just averages the two intuitions together. Finally: which analogy, if either, deserves to be the official picture, and if neither, what would a better one look like — something like portfolio allocation, or something that isn't economic at all?
