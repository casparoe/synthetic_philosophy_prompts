I'm the student representative on my school district's wellness advisory committee, and next week we vote on whether to recommend universal mental-health screening for all ninth graders using a standard social anxiety questionnaire. The superintendent argues that early identification means early support. But our school psychologist has reservations: she worries that we would be telling a large number of ordinary, shy fourteen-year-olds that they have a treatable condition, that the questionnaire results would go into their files, and that teachers who see those files may start interacting with the students differently. I've been asked to draft a short written opinion for the committee, and I'd like it to be more than a privacy discussion. Specifically, I want it to take seriously the possibility that a label like socially anxious is not just a passive description of a student but can itself change how the student sees and acts — that sorting people into a category can help produce the very traits the category names. Please draft the memo: a concrete recommendation (universal screening, screening only for students who opt in or are flagged by teachers, or no screening), a proposed timeline, explicit reasoning the committee can respond to point by point, and a fair treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. My fellow committee members will notice if I dodge anything, so keep the reasoning specific enough that a teacher could push back on a particular sentence.
