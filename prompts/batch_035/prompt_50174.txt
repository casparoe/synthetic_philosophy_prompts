My ethics professor swears Kant was basically right about the murderer at the door, and my study group built what we thought was a reductio of the standard Kantian defense of that position. Except now we cant tell whether it's a reductio or whether it's just... sound. Here's the chain.

Background move first. The universalizability test says a maxim is impermissible if you can't even conceive of it holding as universal law -- the classic case being false promising, since if everyone made promises they meant to break, promising would degrade into noise and the false promiser's own end (being believed) couldn't be achieved. The standard extension to the inquiring murderer runs the same way: if everyone lied whenever the truth would endanger a friend, then answers to "where is he hiding" would carry no weight, so the lie would stop working, so the maxim self-destructs. Hence lying to the murderer is out, and Kant's verdict in the Constant exchange is vindicated.

Now our addition:

(1) Whatever else the test requires, a maxim fails it if its universalization makes it impossible for agents acting on it to achieve the maxim's own end.
(2) Consider the maxim of staying silent when answering truthfully would hand someone over to be killed. Universalize it: everyone with someone hidden refuses to answer inquiring killers.
(3) Under that universalization, silence from a householder is a perfectly reliable signal that somebody's hidden there, so the inquirer searches wherever people go quiet and finds them anyway. The protector's end -- keeping the friend hidden -- fails.
(4) So by (1), the silence maxim is impermissible in exactly the way the lying maxim is. The test doesn't just forbid lying; it forbids stonewalling, evasion, and every refusal to cooperate.
(5) And it generalizes: any strategy for shielding people from wrongdoers only works while it's rare, so universalizing it kills it, so the test forbids all of them.
(6) So on this reading the Kantian has to say you must truthfully reveal the location. Kant sort of bit that bullet himself, but most of his defenders won't.
(7) Therefore something between (1) and (3) is a misapplication, or the contradiction-in-conception test just proves too much and needs replacing.

We keep circling between two diagnoses and I genuinely can't pick. One half of the group says the flaw is in (2) and (3): the silence maxim gets universalized under the wrong description. Its real content is something like "refuse to be an accomplice," and that universalizes fine -- in the world where nobody ever assists a killer in locating a victim, killers just fail, and nothing self-destructs. But then the liar seems entitled to the same move -- describe his maxim as "protect the innocent by whatever means" -- and if maxim descriptions are that elastic, the test permits or condemns whatever you already wanted it to, which makes it useless as a test. The other half says the real difference is that the liar exploits a standing practice, assertion, whose usefulness depends on his maxim being rare, while silence doesn't exploit anything; it declines to enter the game. In the universalized world the game just shrinks rather than self-destructing. But neither half can say why their story doesn't also license the liar to re-describe, or the silent one to be condemned, respectively.

So: which step do I reject, and what do I pay for it? I'd rather keep the universalizability test in some form than accept that Kantian ethics makes you inform on your friends, but I also want to know what the elastic-descriptions escape really costs, because if it's free then it's too free. If it helps, tell me which of the two diagnoses you'd develop and where the other one breaks.
