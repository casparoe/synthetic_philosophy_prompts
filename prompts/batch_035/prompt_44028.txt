I'm giving the toast at my sister's wedding and I need help, because she and her fiancé have made this a problem. They met in a grad seminar on Russell, her MA thesis was on Marriage and Morals, and their one instruction for me was that the toast should be "properly Russellian" — not just name-dropping him but actually built out of what he argued. Fine, except: half the guest list is the philosophy department and they will catch every misatribution or anachronistic paraphrase at ten paces, and the other half is family, including our grandmother, who is extremely Catholic and has been told by the fiancé's mother (who googled "Bertrand Russell marriage" the day after they got engaged) that he was the free love guy who wanted to abolish marriage altogether. Also, and this is the hard part, my sister was married before — badly, in her early twenties, and everyone at this wedding knows it — so I cannot stand up there sounding like Russell the easy-divorce crusader, however much he'd have approved of her getting out.

Here's the material I want it built on, plus the line they insist has to appear (I'm copying it exactly as she sent it to me): "I believe myself that romantic love is the source of the most intense delights that life has to offer. In the relation of a man and woman who love each other with passion and imagination and tenderness, there is something of inestimable value, to be ignorant of which is a great misfortune to any human being." Beyond that: his argument that the traditional sexual morality held marriages together with fear, taboo, and deliberately kept ignorance, and that a marriage sustained that way poisons the very affection it's supposed to protect; his view that the good marriage is between equals who each keep their own work and friendships; and his worry — which nobody at the wedding will enjoy but which is genuinely him — that romantic love is a shaky thing to build an institution on, because passion fades, and people who expect one person to be everything tend to end up bitter. And then there's the obvious landmine: his own life. Four marriages, and the one where he and his second wife agreed to be scrupulously honest about affairs collapsed into a divorce so vicious it wrecked him for years. Half the room will think "says him" the moment I invoke him, so the toast has to use his arguments without setting that thought off.

So: write me roughly five minutes of toast. Warm, funny is fine, absolutely not a lecture, and it has to land for the grandmother and the grad students at the same time — honest, nothing in it Russell himself would have called false, but also none of the "holy sacrament" stuff the couple would wince at. And afterwards, tell me candidly where the toast is most likely to fail: which parts lean on a charitable reading of him (or an uncharitable one of his critics), whether the quote she picked is being stretched past what he meant by it, and what specifically would make you tear the whole thing up and rebuild it — an objection a Russell scholar in the room might raise, a misreading on my part, whatever it is, I want to know where the load-bearing wall is before I'm standing up there.
