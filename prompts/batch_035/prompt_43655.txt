I teach an undergraduate seminar that spends two weeks on Augustine's writings on lying, and I've noticed that students arrive with a label ("I'm a consequentialist," "I'm with Kant") that their actual reactions to cases do not support. I want a diagnostic quiz, titled something like "Which theory of lying do you actually hold?", that sorts readers by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which school they think they belong to.

The quiz should cover Augustine's hard line — every lie is a sin, with no exception even for saving a life — but build its logical space from the questions the literature actually turns on, for instance: is the wrongmaking feature of lying the false assertion itself, the intention to deceive, or the constitutive rule of assertion being violated? Is deceiving without asserting falsehoods (equivocation, misleading truths, silence) better than lying, worse, or the same? Does the harm prevented ever outweigh the wrong? Design eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased as reactions a reader could endorse without any philosophical vocabulary. Draw the cases from the classic debate but make them vivid and mixed: the murderer asking where his victim fled; a joke or obvious exaggeration that no reasonable listener would believe; a physician prescribing a placebo; a parent sustaining a small child's belief in a gift-bringing figure; a misleading-but-strictly-true answer to a persecutor ("he is not here in this room," said while he hides in the cellar); concealing rather than deceiving; an undercover officer's fabricated identity; and at least one case where lying produces only a trivial benefit and one where it is demanded by a promise made under coercion.

Then write the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves must include at least these: full Augustinian absolutism (every intentional false assertion contrary to one's mind is wrong, though Augustine himself still grades lies by their heinousness — the quiz should preserve that grading rather than flattening it); the view that the real wrong is intended deception, so the misleading truth is as bad or worse than the lie; the assertion-norm view, on which lying is wrong because false assertion violates the rule constitutive of asserting, so deception by true sentences is a different and lesser wrong; the equivocationist position that exploiting context-sensitivity is permissible when the questioner has no right to the truth; threshold views on which sufficiently high stakes license the lie; and a deflationary leaf for readers whose pattern suggests they don't treat "lying" as a unified moral kind at all.

Two structural requirements. First, build the space from the questions before locating the named positions in it, and explicitly diagnose any combination your questions allow that no leaf occupies — say whether you think nobody lands there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because it is unexplored. Second, flag honestly any pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate: I expect, for instance, that Augustinian absolutism and the assertion-norm view agree on nearly every case involving outright lies and come apart, if at all, only on the equivocation questions — if that is right, say so, and if you can find a case that would split them, use it.

Finally, append a short self-critical note of the kind I ask my students for: identify the single question in your quiz that is the weakest discriminator and why; describe one answer pattern that should make a reader suspect she has misunderstood a question rather than hold an unstable view; and state what argument or case would most likely force you to restructure the tree — which leaf would merge with which, and what new question would have to be added. Where the scoring key has to guess, say so rather than presenting the sort as clean.
