I've recently taken up a post as a legal analyst with a small documentation NGO that gathers testimony and open-source evidence on an ongoing campaign of forced displacement: villages emptied, children transferred to state-run boarding schools and foster placements far from their families, the minority language banned in schooling and local administration, shrines and archives deliberately destroyed. The killing has been comparatively limited, and that fact is now the center of an internal fight. I have been asked to present to our case-review board the position that this campaign should be documented and advocated for as genocide, not merely as ethnic cleansing or crimes against humanity, on the ground that the perpetrators' evident aim is the destruction of the group as such — as a social and cultural entity — through means that are individually non-lethal. My own grandparents were displaced in a comparable campaign two generations ago, which is partly why I took this job, and partly why my supervisor has warned me that I will be arguing this with more heat than the room will tolerate.

The board is small but sharply divided. Three members are career international prosecutors who spent decades at the ad hoc tribunals and the ICC; they are committed to the strict reading of the special-intent requirement and are viscerally wary of stretching the category, having watched prosecutions collapse over intent. Two members are survivor-community representatives who support the label in principle but fear both the political fallout of the accusation and the dilution of a word that, for their own communities, means the murder of parents; one of them has said in a meeting that calling displacement genocide insults the dead. The chair is a historian of the 1948 Convention who knows Lemkin's drafts and the drafting record in detail, including what was cut and why, and tends to reward arguments that engage that history rather than gestures toward it. I intend to anchor my position in Lemkin's own conception — destruction of a nation or ethnic group as such, through a coordinated attack on its political institutions, culture, language, religious feeling, and economic existence, with physical destruction as only one technique among several — and to argue that a body-count-centric reading betrays that conception, while conceding what I must about the narrow list of prohibited acts and the intent standard as the courts have applied it.

Please prepare me a Q&A sheet for this presentation. Give me the objections these specific people will actually raise, in roughly the order they will arrive and ranked by how much damage each could do to my position, each phrased in the vocabulary that faction would use — the prosecutors' doctrinal objections about evidencing intent from non-lethal conduct, the survivors' objections about dilution and about whose suffering the word belongs to, the historian's objections from the drafting record. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I should concede part of the ground rather than defend it. Tell me what to concede up front so that I am not seen as naive about the law, and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks, along with the least bad answer to it.
