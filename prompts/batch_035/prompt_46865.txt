I'm a PhD student in econ, working on decision theory, and an argument between two friends of mine from grad school has been living rent-free in my head because I could not referee it and they eventually made me promise to ask someone smarter. The context: my friends Maya and Tomás are trying to decide whether to have their first kid. Over dinner, Dan (another friend, ex-philosophy master's, the type who keeps a copy of L.A. Paul's Transformative Experience basically on his nightstand) told Maya that the framework I work in goes completely silent on this choice. His claim was that becoming a parent is a transformative experience in Paul's sense — an experience you can't even represent from the inside before having it, because the epistemically relevant part is what it's *like*, and you only get what-it's-like by living it. So, he said, expected utility maximization can't get started: Maya would have to attach probabilities and utilities to outcomes she literally cannot imagine, and any calculation she does perform is theater dressed up as analysis.

Maya, who is very much not a philosopher and doesn't care what Dan reads at night, gave what I thought was a surprisingly strong reply. We make rational decisions under unrepresentable novelty all the time — she moved alone to a new country at 25, chose her PhD program sight unseen — and nobody says those choices are arational. And for the kid question specifically she has testimony (basically every parent she knows reports it as the best thing in their life), population-level wellbeing data, regret studies if you want them. Dan wasn't moved: he said testimony from parents is a selected sample, since nobody un-becomes a parent, sunk-cost-ish pressure and cognitive dissonance push the reports positive, and more fundamentally the proxies miss the point because the value at issue is indexical — knowing how *others* fare doesn't tell her how her own experiential future would feel, which is the whole thing she's choosing. He added a kicker that unsettled me honestly: even the evaluation problem is broken, because if the kid changes her preferences wholesale, then there's no fixed subject for the outcome to go well *for* — the woman assessing the decision isn't the woman who made it. Maya's last word was that Dan's argument proves too much and is asymmetric: he treats pre-kid Maya's inability to forecast as a reason against, never as a reason for, but if the change is genuinely so radical, then her current hesitations have no more authority over the future self than her current hopes do — he's smuggled in a status-quo bias and called it epistemology.

They've both asked me to settle it and I keep going back and forth, so please adjudicate move by move: for each step in the exchange, say who's right about what, where each argument overreaches, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. I'd also like your view on whether Dan's actual conclusion is as strong as he stated it — "EU theory goes silent" — or whether there's a weaker and much more defensible version he should retreat to, and if so what follows from the weaker version for someone actually making Maya's choice. I have a vested interest in the answer, since if Dan is right about the strong version I may need to find a new dissertation topic lol.
