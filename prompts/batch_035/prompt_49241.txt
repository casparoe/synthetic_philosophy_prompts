Below are my considered verdicts on cases in which a rescuer must choose whom to save. I want the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. A rescuer can save either one stranger or five strangers, at equal cost and with no further risk to anyone. Verdict: the five must be saved — here the numbers count.
2. The same case, except that the one is the rescuer's close friend. Verdict: saving the friend is permissible, and I would not criticize anyone who did.
3. Flipping a coin weighted so that each of the six people has an equal one-sixth chance of rescue. Verdict: permissible, even admirable, yet somehow not required — though I cannot explain why it isn't.
4. Save one person for certain, or instead give each of five people a treatment that saves each with independent probability 0.3 (1.5 expected lives). Verdict: give the treatment. Yet if the choice were one for certain against a single person at probability 0.9, I would save the one for sure. Apparently I let numbers count inside probabilities but not straightforwardly against certainty.
5. When the five are saved and the one dies, I do not think the one was wronged or treated unjustly by the rescuer: taken one at a time, each of the five had a claim no stronger than the one's. This feels consistent with verdict 1 to me, but I suspect it cannot be.
6. However many people suffer severe but non-fatal injuries, I never think relieving them outweighs preventing a single death; yet five deaths straightforwardly outweigh one. So numbers count within lives but not across different harms, and any threshold drawn between the two looks arbitrary to me.

My tentative principle is something like: the numbers count in what a rescuer has most reason to do, but not in what any individual can demand or be wronged about.
