You are a senior university examiner with joint training in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, setting the final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar titled Concepts and Induction. Write one exam question, worth 25 marks and answerable in about 75 minutes, on Bongard problems and what they reveal about how concepts are induced from a small set of examples.

The question must do the following. First, it must present, described entirely in words, a miniature Bongard-style puzzle of your own invention: six boxes, three labeled Class A and three labeled Class B, each box containing exactly two simple geometric shapes (e.g., circle, square, triangle) standing in some spatial relation (e.g., one inside the other, one to the left of the other, overlapping). The descriptions must be unambiguous as stated. Second, the question must require the student to (i) state at least two distinct rules that each classify all six boxes correctly yet disagree on how to classify at least one further box, which the student must themselves construct and describe; (ii) assess what, if anything, beyond the six sample boxes could privilege one rule over the other — simplicity, expected behavior on unseen cases, the puzzle designer's intent, or perhaps nothing at all — and say what follows if the answer is nothing; and (iii) connect the issue to the contrast between mere discrimination on a fixed sample and genuine concept acquisition, drawing on at least one broader theme from the course such as Wittgenstein on family resemblance, simplicity preferences in induction, or the way contemporary few-shot machine classifiers can separate training examples while inducing a concept the designer never intended.

After the question, supply a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme allocating the 25 marks across five or so must-hit points, with a one-line note for each on how students typically lose the marks. The scheme must separate an A answer — one that constructs a test box on which the two candidate rules genuinely diverge and that keeps distinct the questions of separating a sample versus grasping a concept — from a B answer that merely recites the standard associations (Hofstadter, pattern recognition, ambiguity of figures) without doing the constructive work.

Before writing anything, brainstorm and double-check your own puzzle: verify that both of your candidate rules actually fit all six boxes as you have described them and that they truly disagree on your test box. An examiner's most common failure here is a sloppy puzzle in which one of the two "rules" secretly misclassifies a box, or the two rules turn out to be mere notational variants of each other. Take your time getting the details right, and do not require any citations in the model answer beyond the names of figures a well-prepared student would know.
