so here's my situation. I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been reporting a piece about thresholds — the EPA's parts-per-billion limit on some contaminant, the BMI cutoff where "overweight" starts, the income line where a household counts as "in poverty." The through-line of the piece is that everybody treats these numbers like they're written into nature, when really at 4.9 parts per billion the water is "safe" and at 5.1 it "isn't," and nothing in the chemistry changed at that line except our language. A philosopher I interviewed for color offhandedly brought up the sorites paradox — the heap, the bald guy, one grain at a time — and now it's basically the spine of the whole article. My editor, who is annoyingly game for this kind of thing, wants me to close the piece with a poem instead of my usual kicker graf, and frankly I think it could work better than anything I'd write straight.

so what I want is a villanelle about the sorites paradox itself (you can nod toward my threshold examples but the heap should be front and center). and here's the trick I've been daydreaming about — tell me if its too cute to pull off. the first refrain starts out as the induction step, something like "take one grain away, a heap remains" (wordsmith as you see fit), but every time the line comes back it loses a word, the way the heap loses a grain: "take one away, a heap remains," then "a heap remains," then "remains." since the rhyme word never changes, the scheme survives even as the line gets thinner, which is sort of the whole point. the second refrain should be the epistemicist's counterpoint — the voice insisting there was a last grain all along, one specific grain, even though no one could ever point to it — and it should stay perfectly intact every time it returns, refusing to shrink. that contrast IS the argument: one view dissolves, the other digs in.

I'd like someone who knows the philosophy to be able to read off which refrain is which — the no-sharp-cutoff reading versus the sharp-but-invisible-cutoff one — just from how the two lines behave, and someone who knows nothing should still feel it when the closing couplet puts "remains" next to the stubborn intact line and something has clearly gone missing between them. keep the rhyme scheme honest (the -ains sound gives you grains, chains, plains, drains, whatever you need), keep it darkly funny if you can manage, and don't turn it into a lecture. this is for a magazine, not a seminar.
