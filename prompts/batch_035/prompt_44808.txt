I cover science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and I've been assigned to review a pop book about the deep human past that runs a version of the story you see everywhere now: foragers had varied diets, worked maybe fifteen hours a week, shared everything in egalitarian bands with no chiefs, and were taller and healthier than the farmers who replaced them, so the switch to agriculture was basically a trap that gave us famine, epidemic disease, drudgery, and hierarchy. The author leans on Marshall Sahlins's original affluent society idea and ends by basically declaring agriculture the worst mistake in the history of the human race, in the spirit of that famous Jared Diamond essay. My problem is my editor wants a real verdict in the review, not a "some scholars disagree" cop-out, and I genuinely don't know which parts of this are consensus and which are the author overreaching. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful anthropologist or archaeologist would sign off on, which are overstated, and where the book quietly slides between different things — like life expectancy at birth versus how long adults who survived childhood actually lived, or "affluent" in Sahlins's technical sense (people who want little and get it) versus just "they had plenty of stuff", or inequality appearing with farming versus only showing up much later with hereditary chiefs and states? Also the "fifteen hours of work" thing — where does that even come from and is it as solid as pop writers make it sound? I need you to actually take a position on whether the book's overall arc is fair or slanted, and give me the one or two sentences I can hang the review on, since the arc matters more to me than any single stat. And be honest about where the evidence is just thin and any verdict is a judgement call — but still give me the verdict.
