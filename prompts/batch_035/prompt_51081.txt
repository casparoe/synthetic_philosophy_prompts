My two roommates got into it at dinner on friday and the argument is now somehow my problem, because they both want me to referee and I genuinely can't. The topic was whether "better than" has to be transitive -- you know, if A is better than B, and B is better than C, then A had better be better than C.

Jake went first and his position was basically that it's not up for debate. "Better than" is a comparative like "taller than," and comparatives are transitive by their nature; a non-transitive "better than" isn't a coherent relation, it's just noise. He also had a practical point: if your preferences cycle, you can be money-pumped. Someone can offer you a sequence of trades, each of which looks like a strict improvement to you, and you end up exactly where you started minus a fee. So cycles in preference are irrational, and he said non-transitive better-than either produces cycles or it's not doing the job better-than is for.

Maya said she's not claiming cycles, and then brought up sugar in coffee. Add it one grain at a time. A cup with 500 grains and a cup with 501 grains -- there is no such thing as preferring one to the other, you cannot taste the difference. But a cup with zero grains versus a cup with ten thousand grains? Obviously different, and for either of them it wouldn't be close. So adjacent cups are tied, endpoints aren't, and if ties chain up you get black coffee being exactly as good as syrup soup, which is insane.

Jake said that's just the sorites paradox wearing a costume, and that the lesson people take from heaps isn't that logic breaks, it's that perception is coarse. There IS a fact about which cup is better; you just can't detect it. The failure is in our access, not the relation.

Maya said fine, forget taste, because she had a version that isn't about perception at all. Preventing a year of daily headaches versus preventing a year-minus-one-hour of daily headaches: no life plan hinges on that hour, no one would pay anything meaningful for it, it's a tie, and that judgment is not blurry. But chain down from a year to a stubbed toe and each step is a genuine wash while the endpoints are night and day. She said the pairwise judgments are all CORRECT, and what breaks is the assumption that ties behave.

Jake's last move was that this can't be right, because a tie means interchangeability -- if X and Y are tied, you'd take either, so they're interchangeable in every choice, and if Y and Z are too, then via Y, X and Z are interchangeable, contradiction. So Maya's individual tie-judgments can't all be right, and since those judgments were her whole evidence, at least some of her premises are false even if she can't say which.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, who comes out ahead overall, and what (if anything) they're both missing? I have a feeling they're each talking past the other on what a "tie" even is but I can't put my finger on it.
