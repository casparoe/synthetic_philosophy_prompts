Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have spent years arguing past each other about how much rule-following a society should demand of its members. The first camp — call them the tighteners — holds that social order is fragile: a broad package of norms, enforced strictly and predictably with little tolerance for deviation, is what keeps corruption, free-riding, and interpersonal distrust in check, and that looseness is a luxury affordable only by societies that have temporarily forgotten the threats their norms evolved to handle. The second camp — the looseners — holds that strict norm enforcement ossifies culture, criminalizes harmless difference, entrenches majority prejudice as law, and starves society of the tolerance for experimentation on which adaptation and creativity depend; they note that norm enforcement machinery has historically been turned against dissenters, minorities, and the merely eccentric at least as often as against genuine free-riders.

I want the document such a collaboration should produce:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not vacuous — e.g., both sides should be able to accept something about the observed correlation between histories of ecological threat (famine, disaster, invasion, epidemic) and norm tightness, while disagreeing about what it shows; and about the fact that at least some norms genuinely solve collective-action problems that individuals cannot solve alone. Separate claims enough that each can be signed independently.

2. A disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should be stated twice if necessary — once in wording the tighteners would accept as a fair statement of their view, once in wording the looseners would accept of theirs — rather than in a supposedly neutral wording that in fact tilts toward one side. Include the practical disagreement about thresholds: how much deviation from how many norms a society can absorb, and whether there is a stable middle or an unstable equilibrium that drifts toward one pole.

3. A cruxes section. I suspect (but leave you to judge) that at least some of these are genuine cruxes, and I'd like you to consider each and keep, revise, or reject them:
   - Whether norms can be usefully sorted into (a) norms that solve collective-action problems, (b) norms that are arbitrary conventions whose entire value is coordination on something, and (c) norms whose function is to mark insiders and exclude outsiders — and, crucially, whether a society's enforcement apparatus can be trained on (a) and (b) without inevitably enforcing (c) as well. The tighteners presumably think selective enforcement is achievable; the looseners presumably think enforcement technology does not discriminate and that (c) rides in on the back of (a). Whose prediction is this a test of?
   - Whether tightness is an adaptive response to threat or whether the causal story is partly reversed — threat-prone societies being tight because tightness amplifies the perception of threat (a ratchet or feedback loop) — and if the feedback version is true, which camp it favors.
   - Whether increased internal diversity argues for looseness (so that no group's norms dominate) or for tightness (so that a shared minimal code prevents fragmentation). Both sides have arguments here; the statement should record what each would count as evidence.
   For each crux you keep, specify a concrete case, natural experiment, comparative observation, or argument that both sides would agree in advance bears on it, with each side's prediction written down before the case is examined.

4. A verbal-disputes section, if warranted: name any parts of the disagreement that dissolve once terms are clarified — for instance whether the two camps mean the same thing by "tolerance" (the looseners may mean non-enforcement while the tighteners hear indifference), or whether "strict enforcement" as each side uses it includes or excludes informal social sanction as opposed to formal punishment.

After the joint statement itself, append a short drafting note in which you say how confident you are that each side would actually sign the agreed claims as worded and single out the single formulation in the document you are least sure both camps would accept, explaining what makes it tendentious or unstable. The finished statement will be used in a seminar on social norms, so it should be rigorous enough to reward careful reading, but it must not presuppose any specialist literature — everything should be intelligible from the text itself.
