This has been bugging me since last weekend's meetup and I've been turning it over all week. My friend Dev (very EA, has the whole Singer worldview internalized) walked me into an argument and I still can't find the clean place where it breaks. We were arguing about whether the drowning child thing commits you to anything weird, and it turns out he thinks it commits you to mandatory procreation. Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) If you can prevent something very bad from happening to a person, and doing so costs you nothing of comparable moral importance, you must do it — and it makes no moral difference whether you're the only one who could help or one of millions, or whether the person is right in front of you or on another continent. This is just the pond argument, the part everyone in EA accepts. (2) Now take a couple who could conceive a child that would live a long, happy life, but who use contraception because they simply don't want kids. There is something very bad here that they could have prevented: an entire happy life's worth of good is missing from the world, and indeed missing from that person's life. (3) If you accept the pond argument, you already accepted that "I don't feel like it" is not a moral reason — that's just the muddy-shoes excuse wearing a different outfit. (4) The cost of raising a child well is real, but it's clearly not comparable in importance to a whole happy human life. (5) So every healthy couple in a stable situation is obligated to keep having children until the point where the next child's life would go badly, and using contraception for convenience is on par with walking past the pond. He says he's fine with this conclusion?? I am definitly not.

My instinct is that (2) is the bogus step — the never-conceived child isn't a person that something bad happens to, there's no one there to be deprived. Seperate question, but Dev has a comeback I haven't been able to shake: the people who'll suffer from climate change in 2150 don't exist yet either, and (this is some Parfit point, he cited Reasons and Persons) their very identities depend on what we do now, since different policies mean different people eventually get born. So if "no subject, no harm" is what lets me reject (2), it looks like it also dissolves any duty to future generations, and I really don't want to give that up. It feels like I have to pick between biting the mandatory-procreation bullet, accepting that we owe future people nothing, or giving up the pond argument — and all three cost me something I care about.

So, where does this thing actually go wrong? Which step is the load-bearing mistake, and is it really (2)? If I reject (2) the way I want to, what exactly am I paying for it, and is there some coherent middle position that keeps a duty to future people without sliding into "everyone must be gestating at all times"? And if the honest answer is that the pond argument really does commit you to (5), I want to hear that too — I'd just like to know what I'm signing up for before I sign.
