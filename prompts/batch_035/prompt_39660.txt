I run a monthly evening seminar for educated non-specialists — mostly retired engineers, a lawyer, a nurse, and a few computer science undergraduates — on the misuse of technical results in popular argument. The next session, forty-five minutes with a whiteboard, is on Gödel's incompleteness theorems. For weeks beforehand the participants have been submitting what they call misuse bingo cards: paraphrased claims they've encountered, including that Gödel proved science is built on faith, that he showed human minds outrun any machine, that his theorem implies the legal system or even interpersonal trust is ultimately incomplete because all systems are incomplete, and — the submission I most want to address — that Gödel demonstrated there are truths no conceivable method could ever establish. One participant is also confused in the opposite direction and insists she read that Gödel proved logic is complete, so something must be wrong with one of these claims.

Do not write me the explanation itself. Plan how I should deliver it. Specifically:

1. The order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what. I want the theorem statement to land as a precise, almost mundane result about a particular kind of system, not as a mystical pronouncement — so tell me which prerequisites must be secured first (for instance, where the distinction between truth and provability-in-a-system has to be nailed down, and how early the qualifier that we are talking about systems capable of formalizing arithmetic has to appear and in what form).

2. The one carrying example. My instinct is the liar paradox, but I worry it invites the response that Gödel just repackaged a cheap trick — so tell me whether to use it, and if not, what concrete construction should carry the whole explanation, and what that example must contain and what it must avoid.

3. The misconception map: the objections and confusions I should expect, in the order the participants will actually raise them given this audience and these bingo cards, and the exact point in the sequence at which each one should be absorbed rather than merely rebutted at the end.

4. What to do about my participant who read that Gödel proved completeness — whether this is a liability in the room or an opportunity, and where in the arc it fits.

5. One question I can pose at the end that would distinguish someone who has genuinely grasped what the theorems do and do not show from someone who has merely been impressed.

6. What I should openly decline to explain at this level and say so — I'm thinking the arithmetization of syntax and whatever the Rosser improvement amounts to, but tell me if I'm wrong and what the deferral should sound like.
