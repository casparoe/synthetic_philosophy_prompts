A friend in my history seminar has a settled line on war guilt, and next week we turn to who, if anyone, is responsible for the Cold War becoming a decades-long confrontation rather than a brief standoff. I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. Her rulings so far: the Second World War — Germany bears the chief blame, undiluted by the Versailles grievances; the Falklands War — Argentina, since the junta chose invasion, though the sovereignty talks were not yet dead; the Korean War — North Korea, plainly, whatever one thinks of the South's regime; and the First World War, where she sits between Fischer and his critics: Germany gets the largest single share for handing Austria-Hungary the blank check in the knowledge that a general war might follow, Austria-Hungary nearly as much for an ultimatum meant to be refused, Russia somewhat for mobilizing first, and Britain and France hardly at all; she rejects the stronger Fischer claim that Germany had wanted the war years in advance, and she holds the German people as a whole not guilty, only the decision-makers. Given that record, what will she say about the Cold War? Which rule is she implicitly running — blame the power that chooses confrontation while a settlement is still available, blame the power that could most cheaply have prevented the conflict and did not, blame the power challenging the established order rather than defending it, or something else? Those rules all seem to fit her earlier verdicts; do they diverge on the Cold War? If so, give your best prediction with reasons, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules; if not, say what it is about the Cold War that flattens them.
