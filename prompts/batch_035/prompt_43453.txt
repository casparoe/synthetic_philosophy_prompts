I'm a novelist — literary fiction mostly, family sagas, the kind where a trait echoes down three generations. I'm starting a new novel about a family where the same cold streak keeps resurfacing: grandmother, mother, daughter, each bent differently by it, and I'm trying to figure out how much of it to treat as inherited and how much as made. My writing group keeps telling me to "respect the science." One friend in particular — she reads alot of pop genetics — says twin studies show personality is about 50% heritable, intelligence something similar, so "half of who a person is, is written at birth," and that if I have my characters genuinely change over their lives I'm being unscientific, or at least sentimental. When I pushed back, she explained heritability to me like this: think of a person as a recipe and heritability as the ingredient proportions — if a trait is 50% heritable, the genetic ingredients are half the recipe and environment is the other half. So a character is, at bottom, half-fixed, and my job as the novelist is to narrate the remaing half.

Something about this smells off to me but I can't put my finger on what. I dimly recall that heritability is about variation across a population, not the composition of one person — or at least I think I recall that — but I don't know what actually follows from it for my real question, which is: can a novelist depict a temperament as both substantially inherited and substantially reshaped by a life, without fudging either side?

If the proportion reading of heritability is wrong in a way that matters for her conclusion, say so in exactly those terms.
