I write novels for a living, which mostly means I know a great deal about nothing, but my current book has a philosopher of biology as a narrator, and to get her voice right I've spent a year reading the argument over whether Darwinian explanation can do all the work its fans claim -- you know, the skyhooks versus cranes business. The way I understand it (correct me if I've mangled it): Dennett says every bit of apparent design in the world, minds included, has to be built up from mindless parts by cranes -- gradual, mechanical, historically situated processes -- and that reaching for a skyhook, some designing mind that comes down from above, is cheating. He's also strict that this isn't what he calls greedy reductionism, jumping straight to physics and skipping the levels that actually do the explaining; the cranes have to be built at the right level. On the other side you've got people like the late Jerry Fodor, who argued in his last years that natural selection can't even say what a trait was selected FOR -- hearts pump blood, but they also make noise, and selection theory itself has no way to pick out the pumping as the point of it all without some mind quietly doing the sorting, which rather gives the game away. And Nagel waded in from another angle with his book claiming consciousness and reason can't in principle be accommodated by biology as we understand it.

Here's what struck me, as an outsider: the fight appears to have been running in exactly this shape since roughly the mid-90s, everyone knows all the moves, and nobody moves. The Darwinians say Fodor confused a theory of the mechanism with a theory of how we describe it, Fodor said that was precisely his point, and round it goes. Nagel gets told he doesn't understand the science, and responds, in effect, that no amount of the science he understands addresses the thing he's asking about. Reading it is like watching two people play chess where one of them thinks they're playing draughts -- or at least that's my novelist's guess, and I want to know whether it's a fair one. I can't tell whether this is a substantive empirical disagreement, a verbal one about the word "explanation", two groups answering different questions (how did this design get here? versus can this design be got at all?) under a single heading, or just bedrock intuitions about whether meaning and consciousness are the sort of thing that can be assembled from parts, which neither side can argue for without begging the question.

So that's what I'd like from you: a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Use what each side actually claims to work out which of those kinds of disagreement this really is, and be honest about what, if anything, would count as progress -- a distinction somebody hasn't drawn yet, some future result in cognitive science or developmental biology, or a change in the question. And if your diagnosis is that the whole thing is ill-posed, or that no progress should be expected because the sides are separated by something arguments can't touch, say so plainly, because I need to know whether my narrator should be written as a person in a genuine argument or a person performing one. I'd rather not embarass her, and frankly I'd rather not embarass myself either.
