I'm a grad student TAing an intro bioethics course mostly taken by non-majors, and next week I've got one fifty-minute section on whether brain size is morally relevant. Not the explanation itself, to be clear -- I want a teaching plan. The order things have to go in and why, since some of this has to be in place before the question even looks like a question: the students will arrive having vaguely absorbed that humans have big brains and that this is why we matter, so I suspect something has to get disentangled first, maybe the difference between brain size mattering in itself versus being a proxy that tracks something else that matters, but I'm not sure that distinction should come before or after the empirical stuff about how badly size actually predicts anything (whales, elephants, the weak within-human correlation).

Then the carrying example. I've been toying with a scenario of my own: suppose selective breeding or some future intervention produced humans over many generations with substantially smaller brains who are cognitively indistinguishable from everyone else -- my hunch is that's better than the obvious whale/elephant route, because the whale example lets students conclude "oh, elephants are just smart, size was a stand-in the whole time" and never face the real question, which is whether the thing size was standing in for is what matters morally. But I'd like a second opinion on which example should carry the whole session, and what it needs to have and to lack.

Misconceptions: I can predict two and want to know where in the sequence each should be headed off. One is "bigger brain = smarter = counts for more, morally" and the overcorrection where someone announces that brain size is obviously irrelevant and intelligence too because all humans have equal dignity, which just assumes the conclusion. The one that worries me is the ableism trap: once you teach that capacities matter for moral status, some student applies it to people with cognitive disabilities right there in section. Do I head that off early, or does heading it off early collapse the whole question before it's been asked? Where exactly does it go?

Also tell me what to refuse to explain at this level -- I assume the neuroscience of encephalization quotients is out, maybe also any real clinical cases, and I should say openly that I'm skipping them rather than pretend they don't exist. One check question at the end that would show whether the distinction between a proxy and the thing itself actually landed, not just whether they got the right vibe.

Two constraints on your answer. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, like a long email from a colleague -- I'll be reading it on my phone between sections. And keep settled stuff separate from contested stuff: what's genuinely not in dispute among people who know this area versus what's a live fight, e.g. whether cognitive capacities matter to moral status at all versus whether many animals are sentient, and don't let a contested view masquerade as the consensus in my lesson plan. If some pedagogical choice of mine rests on a contested assumption, say so.
