I am a junior at a public high school of about 1,400 students, and I serve as one of two juniors on our twelve-member Student Council, which meets biweekly with our assistant principal as a non-voting advisor. At our next meeting we will consider a constitutional amendment proposed by a group of students in our civics club: abolish the annual all-school election for the five executive officers (president, vice president, secretary, treasurer, and communications chair) and replace it with a lottery, in which the officers would be drawn at random each spring from the pool of students who volunteer for the drawing, subject only to a minimum GPA of 2.5 and a signed commitment to attend all meetings. The club's argument is essentially that our elections have become popularity contests won by whoever runs the most polished social media campaign, that the officers represent only a narrow slice of the school (for the past six years, all of them have come from the same two friend groups and the honors program), and that a lottery drawn from a self-selected volunteer pool would be at least as fair, would better mirror the actual composition of the student body, and would remove the incentive to promise things the council cannot deliver just to get votes. They also point out that we already accept lotteries for parking spots and for the lottery-based admission to our oversubscribed elective in forensic science, so the principle is not foreign to us.

My role on the council is to prepare a written recommendation memo that will be distributed to all members before the vote, and the assistant principal has asked that it be substantive rather than a rubber stamp for either side. I would like your help drafting it. Please write the memo in American English, in a formal register suitable for circulation to the full council, with a clear bottom-line recommendation (adopt the amendment, reject it, or adopt it with specific modifications, such as term limits on lottery service, a smaller vetting committee, or a hybrid system in which, say, the president is still elected while the rest are drawn by lot). The reasoning should engage the strongest arguments on both sides seriously. Some issues I would especially like the memo to address: whether a lottery over a self-selected pool of volunteers actually delivers the representative cross-section the club claims, or whether it just selects for the personality type that likes being randomly chosen; whether removing elections really removes accountability, given that lottery officers cannot be "voted out" for doing a bad job; whether an elected officer who has to persuade classmates learns something democratically valuable that a lottery officer never has to; and whether it matters that our elections, whatever their flaws, at least give all 1,400 students a participatory experience of choosing their representatives twice a year. Please make sure the memo does not simply assert that democracy means elections; treat the pro-lottery side as making a genuine case, and give the losing side of your recommendation its full due before the recommendation is stated.
