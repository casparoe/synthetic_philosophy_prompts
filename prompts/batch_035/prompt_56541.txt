I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am asking for help drafting a research proposal. Here is the situation. I work on the control software for closed-loop spinal-cord stimulation implants: devices that sense markers of a patient's pain state and adjust stimulation in real time, in something like the way an autopilot continuously corrects an aircraft. My company has a standing arrangement with a nearby university under which we can fund one six-month, part-time collaboration with a small group of researchers, and this year I get to pitch the project. The pitch needs to be a proper proposal document, not just an email, and the review committee includes clinicians and engineers but no philosophers, so it has to be written for technically literate non-specialists.

My half-formed idea grew out of something I came across called pain asymbolia. These are patients, typically after damage to certain midline brain structures, who report that they do feel pain — they can locate it, identify it as pain, and rate its intensity — while insisting that it does not bother them at all; some smile or laugh while being pinched hard. There are also, apparently, patients on morphine who say something similar: the sensation is still there, but the sting is gone. My suspicion is that when people, including those of us building these devices, say that pain is intrinsically bad, they are conflating two separable things: something like an alarm signal (detection, localization, intensity) and a downstream evaluative or motivational response that makes the alarm matter to the person. In my own world, we would never confuse a monitoring system with its escalation policy, and the policy is what you rewrite when the alerts are producing panic disproportionate to the incidents. If nociception is analogous — a signal whose badness is conferred by what it is wired to, rather than built into it — then there is an immediate engineering question my team is not equipped to answer: our closed-loop controller targets the signal itself, but perhaps the legitimate therapeutic target is the minding, or some defined boundary between the two. Worse, our clinical endpoints are largely built around pain-intensity ratings, so if a patient's real complaint is the aversiveness rather than the signal, we may be optimizing the wrong variable and calling it success.

What I need is a proposal that does several things, and I will let you organize them as you see fit. First, state the underlying question precisely enough that it could in principle get a determinate answer — something in the vicinity of whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the phenomenal character of the sensation or is instead constituted by a separable affective-evaluative response, and, conditioned on each answer, what follows about the correct target for neuromodulation. Second, explain why the existing philosophical positions, whatever they are, leave this open: my impression is that there is a familiar dispute between those who hold that the awfulness is part of the very feel of pain and those who locate it in attitudes toward the sensation, but neither camp, as far as I can tell, has been asked to say where a causal intervention ought to land, or how to treat the awkward cases — the patient who would happily keep the sensation if it lost its sting, or the one who wants the signal silenced even when it no longer distresses them. Third, lay out the method concretely: what the researchers and I would actually do month by month over the six months, which I imagine involves analyzing the dissociation evidence conceptually, working through case comparisons such as asymbolia, morphine-induced indifference, and chronic pain with distress out of proportion to measurable signal, deriving candidate intervention targets from the analysis, and then stress-testing those targets against realistic clinical scenarios that I would supply from our side. Fourth, say what a successful deliverable looks like for the people who will use it — in my mind, a white paper that partitions the pain system into components and, for each, states whether and under what conditions it is a legitimate engineering target — and be honest about what would count as failure, for instance if the distinction turns out to be impossible to state in terms any clinician, patient, or regulator could act on. Finally, scope it honestly: the full metaphysics of affect and valence is obviously too big for six months, so the proposal should fence off what gets treated as a fixed assumption and explain that choice.

Please write it in the register of an internal research partnership pitch — precise, but without assuming the reader knows any philosophy — and do not include a bibliography or literature review; positions and arguments can be referred to by description, and I will track down and add the references myself before submission.
