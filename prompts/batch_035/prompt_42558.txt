I sit on the editorial board of a human-rights documentation organisation — we publish field reports and advocacy briefs; we do not litigate and we are not a court. We are finalising our report on the situation in the northern provinces of Veldania, and we are deadlocked over a single word. I would like you to draft the board's decision memo. Treat the facts below as stipulated; you need not know anything about Veldania, which I am calling it for this exercise.

The facts, briefly: Veldania's Sorvi minority is a linguistic-ethnic group concentrated in the north. Over the past eighteen months, security forces and allied militias have destroyed or emptied some two hundred Sorvi villages; roughly six thousand Sorvi are confirmed killed and several hundred thousand displaced across the border. Killings cluster overwhelmingly around villages that resist eviction, and military documents obtained by our field team include a provincial directive ordering the "removal of the Sorvi presence from the province by year's end." Alongside the violence, the authorities have closed Sorvi-language schools, banned Sorvi publications, and bulldozed a number of ancestral shrines. There is no evidence of extermination camps or of orders to kill Sorvi qua Sorvi regardless of flight.

Our board has split three ways. The legal team argues that under the 1948 Genocide Convention the destruction must be of the group as such, with intent to destroy the group in whole or in part, and that the evidentiary pattern here fits forced displacement and persecution — crimes against humanity, and grave ones — but that a court applying the special-intent requirement could reasonably find that Veldania's forces would have been content had the Sorvi simply left. Our field researchers reply that this courtroom standard betrays the category itself: Lemkin, who coined the word, understood genocide as the destruction of a group's pattern of life — its language, institutions, and culture — and not merely its members' bodies, and a report that reserves the word for shooting squads into pits fails to name what is happening to the Sorvi as a group. Our director worries about the consequences either way: if we use the word, we trigger expectations of response that we cannot satisfy and hand the government a pretext to expel our researchers; if we withhold it, we join the long history of actors who found the G-word available for enemies and unavailable for the awkward cases — and "ethnic cleansing," our compromise candidate, is a phrase that entered circulation precisely as a way of describing mass atrocity while avoiding the legal obligations the word genocide carries.

Please draft the memo the board will vote on. It should: (1) recommend a concrete editorial decision — which term or terms the report uses for the campaign, and whether the report may use different vocabulary in its descriptive, legal, and advocacy sections; (2) give reasons that engage both the special-intent objection and the Lemkian objection rather than dismissing either — including, where relevant, what follows from the Convention's exclusion of political groups and its narrowing of Lemkin's cultural conception at the drafting stage; (3) address the standard-of-proof question: whether a documentation organisation is entitled to use a word whose legal meaning is settled only by tribunals applying proof beyond reasonable doubt, or whether it should say what it believes and say why; (4) confront the politics of naming honestly — the strongest argument for the recommendation you reject, stated as its best defender would state it, and what the organisation is choosing to pay for its choice; and (5) propose criteria, not just for this case, that our future report teams can apply when the same question arises again. The memo should be one our board could adopt verbatim, with a clear bottom line.
