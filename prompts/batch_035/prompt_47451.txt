I'm a novelist, and this year I'm serving on the jury for a mid-sized prize in experimental fiction. One submission has split the five of us down the middle, and as the member least committed to either camp, I've been asked to write the framing memo for our final session.

The manuscript is some three hundred pages, written in English interleaved with a language its author has spent a decade constructing entirely alone. She says it exists to record what she calls "the inner weather of composition" — recurring sensations, she insists, that English has no words for and that only she can identify. She has refused to supply a translation or glossary on the ground that any rendering would falsify the text: correctness of use, for her, consists in her own recognition that the sensation has returned. She has, however, appended a set of rules she wrote for herself — pairing each sign with a felt quality, fixing the grammar — and maintains that a future page is correct when it conforms to those rules as she intends them.

Two jurors want to shortlist it as the most serious attempt in years to give inner life a form not hostage to shared convention, noting that no reader has direct access to any author's experience anyway. Two want it rejected outright: a novel is an act of communication, they say, and an untranslatable text removes the public standard that both reading and judging presuppose — we would be awarding a prize to a claim about meaning rather than a work that means. I am the fifth vote, and undecided.

Draft the memo, roughly 800 words, with a concrete recommendation — shortlist, reject, or shortlist with stated conditions — and reasoning the other jurors could actually engage with. Whatever you conclude, take seriously the crux beneath the practical question: whether rules whose application is fixed only by one person's private recognitions could settle anything about her future use — or whether, if nothing would distinguish her applying the language correctly from her merely feeling that she is, the notion of correctness, and with it the idea that the invented passages mean anything even to her, has quietly gone missing. Address too whether our obligation runs to books or to readers, and be honest about the strongest point on the side you argue against.
