I'm taking a course on the philosophy of cognitive science, and we've just finished a unit on the frame problem. We worked through the technical version first — the proliferation of frame axioms in situation calculus, and how the Yale shooting problem suggested that nonmonotonic "solutions" carry their own puzzles about default persistence — and then the philosophical inflation of the problem, via Dennett's robot that dutifully pulls its own wagon out of the room with the ticking bomb still aboard. In seminar this week, a classmate kept repeating that "the frame problem is just the problem of relevance," and deployed the slogan to argue two things at once: (a) that the formal work on frame axioms was a red herring from the beginning, since the real difficulty was never about representation size but about relevance; and (b) that contemporary systems have essentially solved the problem, because learned attention mechanisms are precisely machinery for determining what is relevant. My suspicion is that the slogan is doing different work in each claim, and possibly no work at all in one of them, but I can't articulate why without a firmer grip on what the slogan actually asserts.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing? I imagine the candidates differ along several axes: whether "relevance" means relevance to an agent's goals, relevance to the truth of a specific prediction, or something like cognitive salience; whether the claim is about the intractability of enumerating what is irrelevant, about the impossibility of specifying relevance criteria in general, or merely about the volume of facts that must be left unchanged; and whether the problem is being located in logic, in architecture, or in the world. For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; and then say whether it supports (a), supports (b), both, or neither — including whether any reading is strong enough to license dismissing the Yale-style literature as a red herring, or generous enough to count attention mechanisms as a solution. I'd like a menu I can bring to seminar, with verdicts, rather than a single interpretation argued for.
