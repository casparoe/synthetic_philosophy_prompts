Here's the problem set question:

An urn contains 30 red balls and 60 more balls that are black and yellow in unknown proportion (you know only that black + yellow = 60). One ball is drawn at random. A subject first chooses between gamble 1A (win $100 if the ball is red) and gamble 1B (win $100 if the ball is black), and then between gamble 2A (win $100 if the ball is red or yellow) and gamble 2B (win $100 if the ball is black or yellow). Most subjects choose 1A over 1B and 2B over 2A. (a) Show that these two choices are inconsistent with any single assignment of subjective probabilities over the ball colors. (b) Which principle of decision theory does this pattern conflict with, and why? (c) Briefly: does this show the subjects are irrational?

And here's what the student wrote: (a) Since exactly 30 of the 90 balls are red, betting on red is a known 1/3 chance of winning. Preferring 1A to 1B therefore means the subject's subjective probability of drawing black is below 1/3. For the second pair, yellow pays out $100 in both 2A and 2B, so the yellow balls cancel out, and preferring 2B to 2A means the subject's subjective probability of black is above 1/3. The same subjective probability can't be both below and above 1/3, so no single probability assignment over the colors yields both choices. (b) This is a violation of the independence axiom of von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility theory, the same axiom that the Allais paradox targets. (c) The best explanation of the choices is ordinary risk aversion: people shy away from gambles where the winning chance might be very low (black could be zero out of 60), and since risk aversion is just curvature of the utility function for money, that's the whole story. Ellsberg was essentially doing what Kahneman and Tversky later did — cataloguing systematic biases as deviations from the rational benchmark — so the subjects are irrational in the technical sense, though excusably so.
