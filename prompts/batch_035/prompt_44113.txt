My logic reading group has been working through the semantic paradoxes, and I've become sympathetic to the dialetheist's line: the Liar sentence L, which says of itself that it is not true, cannot be consistently assigned only truth or only falsity, so the honest verdict is that L is both true and not true. What I like about the position, as I understand it, is that we get to keep all the ordinary reasoning that generates the paradox — the unrestricted truth schema, modus ponens, conditional proof — and simply give up the claim that every contradiction is fatal. A classical logician friend of mine says this position trivializes itself, and he showed me an argument I cannot get out of my head. Here it is, spelled out as he gave it.

(1) Let p be any proposition whatsoever — say, "2 + 2 = 5." Let C be a sentence that says of itself: "If C is true, then p." Sentences like C can be constructed by the same self-referential means as the Liar (this is usually called Curry's paradox).

(2) The truth schema applies to C as it applies to any sentence: C is true if and only if (if C is true, then p).

(3) Suppose C is true. Then, by the left-to-right direction of (2), we have: if C is true, then p. And we are supposing C is true. So p. Discharging the supposition, we have proved: if C is true, then p.

(4) By the right-to-left direction of (2), from "if C is true, then p" it follows that C is true. We proved exactly that conditional in (3). So C is true.

(5) From "C is true" and "if C is true, then p," modus ponens yields p.

(6) p was arbitrary. So every proposition whatsoever is provable — including contradictions and the sentence "every sentence is true." Dialetheism collapses into trivialism.

What worries me most is that the argument does not proceed the way the Liar does. It never derives a contradiction and then detonates it; there is no negation anywhere in the derivation from (2) to (5), only the truth schema, a conditional proof, and modus ponens. So the dialetheist's signature move — let the contradiction stand, since contradictions are tolerable — has no purchase: there is no contradiction to stand. If the argument is sound, the position that accused classical solutions of panic-driven surgery must itself perform surgery, or else prove everything.

So I want a diagnosis, and I want it priced. Which premise or inference in (1) through (6) should the dialetheist reject? Rejecting the truth schema for C in particular looks ad hoc in exactly the way dialetheists accuse Tarskian hierarchies of being. Modus ponens is off the table as far as I'm concerned. My vague suspicion is that the conditional in (2) is somehow doing work twice — the very conditional established in (3) seems to get used once to extract C's truth in (4) and then again in (5) — but I cannot turn that suspicion into a diagnosis. Is my friend right that something must go? If so, name the precise step, explain why it looked innocent when the argument was first laid out, and then compare the costs: is the dialetheist's repair here cheaper or dearer than what the classical logician pays to handle the Liar, or does the friend's argument show that everyone is in the same boat? If you think the argument fails outright, say where the equivocation or scope error is and why it is not visible on the face of the derivation.

Lead with your verdict in the first sentence — the step you reject or the claim that the argument fails — and give the reasoning after, with no preamble and no restating of the setup. Please answer in American English.
