I edit a small philosophy newsletter and am preparing an issue on peer disagreement. I'd like a diagnostic decision tree, not another survey of the literature. The tree should start from the concrete situation "you discover that someone you consider your epistemic peer — equally intelligent, equally well-informed about P — disagrees with you about P" and end at the epistemic policy the reader actually holds. Each branch node should be a case or question answerable without knowing the labels, for example: whether your confidence should move when you can recheck your own reasoning but find no flaw in your peer's (the mental-math case); whether learning that your peer's evidence is a strict subset of yours licenses holding firm; whether learning that your peer has a higher IQ should matter, and if so in which direction; whether the policy must apply to disagreements about peer disagreement itself; and whether the answer is the same for mathematical, empirical, moral, and religious propositions. The leaves should be named positions — equal-weight conciliationism, more moderate conciliation, the total-evidence view, the right-reasons view, and any hybrids the tree generates — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, such as how it handles the self-undermining worry for conciliationism or the dogmatism worry for steadfastness. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate (moderate conciliationism versus the total-evidence view is one candidate) and say what further question would be needed to prise them apart. Aim for ten to twelve nodes: the point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they'd choose for themselves.
