I've been rereading the old LessWrong-adjacent decision theory material — the threads on updatelessness and precommitment — and I've gotten thoroughly stuck on the absent-minded driver problem, which goes back to Piccione and Rubinstein's 1997 paper in Games and Economic Behavior. Setup, in case anyone's rusty: a driver heads home along a highway with two identical exits. He is absent-minded in a precise technical sense — he cannot remember how many intersections he has already passed, and nothing he observes distinguishes the first from the second, so from behind his eyes the two intersections are subjectively identical. Exiting at the first gets him home very late (payoff 0), exiting at the second gets him home on time (payoff 4), and continuing past both means a motel (payoff 1). With a working memory, the plan "continue past the first, exit at the second" is available and obviously optimal. Without one, it isn't, since he can't tell where he is — that's the whole tragedy, or so I thought.

Now the argument that's eating me: (1) Since the intersections are indistinguishable, the driver's entire strategy is captured by a single number: the probability q with which he continues at whichever intersection he finds himself. (2) Straightforward arithmetic then gives an expected payoff of q²·1 + q(1−q)·4, maximized at q = 2/3 for an expected payoff of 4/3. (3) His pure strategies are only "always exit" (payoff 0) and "always continue" (payoff 1) — "exit only at the second" is unimplementable. (4) So the best plan is a mixed one, and it strictly beats every pure plan: 4/3 versus at most 1. (5) But randomization can only help you against an adversary or another agent whose behavior responds to yours; mixing over your own options yields a weighted average of their payoffs and can never exceed the better option. In a solo decision problem, against a fixed environment with payoffs posted on the wall, a coin flip has nothing to buy you. (6) This problem has no adversary — no other agent, no moves by nature after the driver chooses; the road is not plotting against him. (7) So either randomization creates value from nothing in a one-player game, or the driver does have an opponent, and the only candidate is the driver himself, his choice at one intersection strategically entangled with his choice at the other. (8) Therefore this is not really a decision problem but a game among the driver's temporally extended selves — and presumably the same holds for any agent whose decision points are linked by what he can and cannot remember.

Conclusion (8) seems to prove far too much: it suggests that whenever one agent's choices are correlated with each other — which is always, since they issue from the same agent — solo decision-making collapses into self-versus-self game theory, and the boundary between a decision problem and a game dissolves entirely. I'm unwilling to give up (2) or (4), which feel like mere arithmetic, and I have real affection for (5), which feels like it should be a theorem. So: which step do I reject, what exactly goes wrong there, and what does rejecting it cost me? I suspect there's an equivocation lurking somewhere between (1) and (5) about what "a strategy" even is, but I can't pin it down, and I'd like a diagnosis precise enough that I could apply it to other cases where an agent can't distinguish his own decision points.
