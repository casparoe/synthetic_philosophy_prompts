i'm teaching a "great paradoxes" elective at a community college, next week's session is on supertasks, and I want Thomson's lamp to be the centerpiece. My students have had at most one semester of calculus, so they know what the limit of a sequence of *numbers* is, but they've never thought about whether a sequence of *states* has a limit. Plan the session for me -- don't just explain the paradox to me, I want the teaching plan.

Some things I want you to work out:

- The order things have to come in. My instinct is to start with the Zeno-style runner crossing the room so they first see a case where infinitely many sub-tasks add up to a perfectly ordinary, well-defined outcome, and only then introduce the lamp as the case where that breaks. But tell me if that sequencing is right or if something else needs to be in place first.

- The carrying example and what it must have and lack. Should the whole thing be built on the runner, or on the lamp itself, or on something more concrete (a friend toggling a real switch at shrinking intervals)? And is there an example I should NOT use even though it seems natural -- like the "1/2 = the answer because the series averages out" framing -- because it plants exactly the wrong idea?

- The misconceptions to expect, in the order the students will hit them. Off the top of my head I expect: (a) "the lamp must be either on or off, law of excluded middle, so one of the two is true and we just can't know which" -- this one is the hardest because it sounds like wisdom; (b) "the switch or the bulb would physically break, so the scenario is impossible and there's no puzzle" -- I don't want to just concede this and let it swallow the whole class, but I also don't want to pretend physics is irrelevant; (c) "it's just Zeno again" -- they'll pattern-match to last week and stop listening. For each, tell me where in the sequence to defuse it, not just how.

- Where to put a variant I made up: same lamp, except every time it toggles it also slides one inch along a table, so by the two-minute mark it's arrived at a perfectly definite resting place even though its on/off history never settles down. My hunch is that this contrast -- some questions about the supertask have answers, others don't -- is the actual lesson of the whole session, and that the variant should come after they've wrestled with the plain lamp for a while. Confirm or correct that, and tell me exactly what question I should ask about the variant when it lands.

- A check for understanding at the end -- one question whose answer would show me they've grasped the distinction between a limit of states and a state at a limit, rather than just enjoyed the ride.

- And what I should openly refuse to get into at this level (infinity machines actually existing, measure theory, whatever else lurks) and how to say so without sounding like I'm dodging.

Fifty minutes, whiteboard, no readings. Go.
