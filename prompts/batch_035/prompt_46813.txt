The Lucas/Penrose anti-mechanist thing has been going since the early sixties, and as far as I can tell both sides' positions calcified decades ago. Nobody has moved and every so often someone revives it with the same moves. Let me lay out where it stands as I understand it, then tell you my hypothesis about what the disagreement actually is.

The anti-mechanist says: take any proposed machine M whose outputs are supposed to be exactly the arithmetic a human mathematician can see. Given M's description, Gödel's construction hands us a sentence G_M that M can't prove, but which we can see is true (granting that M is consistent, or sound — people slide between these, and I think the soundness version is the one that actually works). So we out-see M, so no machine matches the human, so mechanism about mathematical minds is false. The mechanist replies, roughly: (1) the human's "seeing" that G_M is true is only as good as the assumption that M is consistent, and we can't establish that non-circularly — Penrose himself grants it's conceivable that we're inconsistent; (2) the argument only defeats the particular machine we're handed, and since we don't know which machine we are, we can't run it on ourselves; and (3) going from "no machine we can specify beats us" to "no machine at all" quietly assumes we can survey a whole infinite collection, which is exactly what's in dispute.

My hypothesis: the disagreement isn't about Gödel at all. Both sides agree on the theorem, nobody disputes the math. The real fight is over the evidential status of that moment of "seeing" that the Gödel sentence is true — the anti-mechanists count it as a data point, a kind of perception, and the mechanists refuse it that status and say until it's cashed out as a proof it can't do any argumentative work. If that's right, the Gödelian packaging has been disguising a fight about what counts as evidence in the philosophy of mind for sixty years.

So diagnose it for me. Is my hypothesis right? Is there instead a substantive formal question hiding in there — for instance whether "the sentences human mathematicians can see" even forms a determinate set, or which mechanist thesis is actually the target (the narrow one about human mathematical outputs being recursive, versus Penrose's later, much stronger claim that no physical process relevant to physics is non-computable — those look like they get refuted or defended by completely different things)? Is some of it merely verbal, two senses of "understand" or "see" being run under one word? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a sharpened account of surveyability, an agreed formulation of the mechanist thesis, some test case both sides would have to bite a bullet on — or is this one where we should expect no progress and why? I want the diagnosis to actually engage the specific replies (1)-(3), not a generic survey of the debate.
