My philosophy of science reading group spent its last session on verisimilitude, and two members left mid-argument, each convinced the other had talked past the point. I'll try to relay the exchange faithfully; I was mostly a bystander and couldn't referee it myself.

The context: Popper introduced verisimilitude (closeness to truth) to handle an embarrassment for his own picture of science. If every theory we've ever held is strictly false — even Newton's — in what sense is the history of science progress rather than one falsified guess replacing another? Popper's answer was that later theories, though false, are closer to the truth, where a theory's closeness depends on how much truth it gets right versus how much falsehood it contains. With that on the table, the argument ran as follows.

(1) Marcus said the notion is indispensable and basically sound. Kepler's ellipses beat Ptolemy's circles on verisimilitude even though no planetary orbit is an exact ellipse — perturbations from other planets see to that. Both theories are strictly false, but the elliptical model gets vastly more of the actual positions right, and "false but closer" is exactly the relation we need to make sense of saying Kepler improved on Ptolemy rather than merely differing from him.

(2) Elena replied that the formal program collapsed almost as soon as it was stated. In the 1970s, Pavel Tichý and David Miller independently showed that Popper's own definition has a disastrous consequence: no false theory can ever come out closer to the truth than any other false theory — every comparison among strictly false theories collapses into a tie. Since ranking false theories against each other was the whole job description, the definition that was supposed to vindicate Marcus's Kepler-over-Ptolemy intuition can't even express it.

(3) Marcus conceded the definition failed but denied that the concept fell with it. The natural repair, he said, is to think in terms of consequences: a theory's true consequences count for it, its false ones against it, and while nobody has produced a measure everyone accepts, the core constraints are firm — conjoin a theory with a truth and it gets closer; conjoin it with a falsehood and it gets farther. That much, he claimed, already fixes many of the rankings we want.

(4) Elena raised what she called the deeper objection: language dependence. Miller later showed that if you redescribe the weather with predicates defined as mixtures of the ordinary ones, two false forecasts that ranked one way under the old vocabulary rank the opposite way under the new, even though nothing about the world or the forecasts has changed. If closeness to truth can be flipped by relabeling, it isn't a fact about theories at all — it's an artifact of how we happen to write them down, which is not what scientific progress could consist in.

(5) Marcus answered that the flips are driven by artificial predicates no forecaster would use, and that in any case the redefinition is not a mere change of notation: the new mixed predicates make claims of their own, so the "same" forecasts under the new vocabulary aren't the same forecasts. Restrict comparisons to the vocabulary of the inquiry at hand, as any actual judgment of better-or-worse already does, and the instability never arises. "Closer to the truth" being framework-relative, he added, is no more damning than that simplicity and explanatory power also need a vocabulary.

(6) Elena's last word was that even granting an objective ordering, it would rank descriptions against the facts described, not theories against the deep truth about the world. A theory's unobservable machinery — Newton's absolute space, say — can be entirely wrong while its observable consequences are nearly right, and the consequence-counting picture of verisimilitude only sees the consequences. So "closer to the truth" either quietly reduces to "more of its checkable claims came out right," which we could say without the metaphysical dressing, or it claims something the evidence can never support.

Neither would budge, and the rest of us had long since stopped trying to follow every step. Could you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it — who is right about which specific points, where each missteps, and whether either of them is missing something that would dissolve or reframe the disagreement? I'd especially like to know whether Elena's step (2) really does to Marcus's step (1) what she thinks it does, and whether Marcus's step (5) is a genuine repair or a relabeling of the problem.
