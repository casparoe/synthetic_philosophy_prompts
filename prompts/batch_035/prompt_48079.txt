Over the past few months I've been trying to write up, for a political-theory seminar, a working account of what "left" and "right" actually denote — prompted by a friend who insists the terms are empty because the two sides have swapped positions on free trade within living memory. I disagree with him, but when I tried to state my own view precisely, I noticed I was holding a number of things at once, and I can't tell whether they fit together. Here is the package. First, the left-right distinction is real rather than merely nominal: it carves political life at a genuine joint, and the fact that we inherited the labels from the seating arrangement in the French National Assembly is an accident of naming, not evidence that the axis itself is a convention. Second — and this is what supposedly makes the axis deep — the distinction is at bottom about hierarchy: the right is the standpoint from which existing inequalities and hierarchies are seen as natural or at least defensible, while the left is the standpoint from which they are seen as artificial and to be flattened. Third, the axis is fundamentally economic; cultural issues acquire their left-right alignment only contingently, through the coalitions built over the twentieth century, so in principle that alignment can come apart, and one should not infer someone's position on the axis from their stances on immigration, religion, or crime. Fourth, I hold two verdicts on concrete cases that I feel confident about: a present-day voter who wants a generous welfare state and steeply progressive taxation, but also strict immigration control and active state promotion of the national culture, is in her bones right-wing; and a libertarian who wants open borders, free markets, and a minimal state is right-wing, even though the open-borders component looks like it points the other way. Fifth, the distinction is a single axis: any two political positions are comparable as more-left or more-right, so talk of "left-libertarians" versus "right-libertarians" is really just a way of saying that one of them is further left.

What I'd like from you is an audit of this package rather than a lecture on the history of the terms. Go through the five commitments, tell me which ones are genuinely in tension and which tensions only look real, and rank the genuine ones by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere. If the case verdicts in the fourth commitment are what does the damage, I want that said plainly; but don't protect them by fiat either — if my theory can't deliver them, it may be the theory that has to go, and I'd like to know which single revision would clean up the most.
