I'm a third-year PhD student in continental philosophy, and my department lets grad students run a little film-and-theory series every semester where you pair a movie with a reading and then just let people argue about it afterwards. I'm doing Foucault this spring, and my session is One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest paired with the panopticism chapter of Discipline and Punish. On the surface it's a slam dunk: Nurse Ratched runs the ward through constant surveillance and self-monitoring, the daily group-therapy meetings are basically the examination crossed with confession where the patients are made to interpret themselves, McMurphy is the delinquent body to be corrected, and the lobotomy at the end is the institution quietly disposing of what it couldn't normalize. And in the novel the Chief's whole hallucination of the Combine — a vast machine for processing and adjusting people — reads like something Foucault could have written himself.

But the deeper I get into prepping the session, the more I think the film actually resists the reading I'm supposed to be giving it. The entire emotional logic of the movie is that there is a real McMurphy in there — a natural, vital, pre-institutional self that the ward crushes, and that the Chief literally recovers when he throws the control panel through the window and escapes. The fishing trip is the same move: get the patients out of the institution and their spontaneous, healthy selves re-emerge. Isn't that exactly the repressed/liberated subject that Foucault spends his career saying is a fiction generated by the very power it claims to escape? The film wants a villain (a nurse, a person, a will) and a hostage, and that's arguably the one thing Discipline and Punish won't give you.

So I want a reading that takes both sides seriously rather than using the film as an illustration with footnotes. Where does the mapping actually hold — what plays the role of the tower, the examination, the norm, docile bodies, the therapeutic apparatus — and where does it break? Is the film's humanism a fatal confusion, or does it dramatize something the book misses, maybe that power is actually lived from the inside as a somebody doing this to me, so that a theory with no center can't explain why resistance gets aimed at centers? And since the room will be full of grad students who've all read the book, what's the move I should make (or avoid) so the discussion doesn't just collapse into "the film is naive, Foucault is deep"? I've also been told I have to say something about why the Chief, the narrator, is the one who gets out — people will ask.
