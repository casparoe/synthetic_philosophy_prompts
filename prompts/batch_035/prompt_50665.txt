In a seminar on utilitarianism, a classmate took the separateness of persons objection — the thought, associated with Rawls and Nagel, that utilitarianism wrongly aggregates across lives, treating many as one, and so licenses imposing burdens on some for the greater benefit of others — and used it in an argument that appears to prove far too much:

(1) The objection's force lies here: when a cost falls on one subject and the compensating benefit on a different subject, there is no one for whom the exchange is a net gain — the one who pays never receives what the payment was for.
(2) What produces this feature is not the metaphysics of persons but the structure of experience: what matters is that cost and benefit attach to distinct centers of experience, such that the bearer of the cost never enjoys the benefit that redeems it.
(3) The subject who undergoes a sacrifice now and the subject who enjoys its fruits later are, in exactly that sense, distinct centers of experience: the one who bears the cost never experiences the payoff, since by the time it arrives that experiencer is past, and nothing is ever felt by both.
(4) So the same reasoning condemns prudential sacrifice: no one can now be justified in bearing a cost — the dentist's drill, the years of training, the deferred consumption — for the sake of a benefit that will be enjoyed only later.
(5) But tradeoffs of exactly this form within a life are not merely permissible; making them is a paradigm of rationality, and refusing them a paradigm of irrationality.
(6) Therefore the objection cannot rest on (1): what it tracks cannot be distinct centers of experience, and it must either retreat to the bare numerical distinctness of persons or be abandoned.

She does not want this conclusion — she wants the objection to utilitarianism to survive, and she says she would rather give up (2) or weaken (3) than accept (6). Identify the premise or step that should be rejected, explain why it looked so plausible, and say what rejecting it costs. In particular, if the objection retreats to the numerical distinctness of persons, what work can that bare fact do — is it a principled stopping point, or has the argument exposed the objection as resting on an arbitrary boundary?
