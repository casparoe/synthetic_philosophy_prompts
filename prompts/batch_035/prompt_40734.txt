I edit a small satirical economics newsletter whose readership is mostly graduate students and one tenured macroeconomist who pretends he doesn't find it funny. For our next issue, please write an Onion-style news article — a headline in that deadpan wire-service house style, followed by roughly 450 to 550 words of body copy — reporting that a mid-sized quantitative hedge fund has voluntarily wound itself down after its research team spent two quarters proving internally that no speculative trade among its portfolio managers could ever be mutually beneficial. Give the fund a name of your own invention, and invent its personnel (the chief investment officer, the portfolio managers, the head of research) rather than naming anyone real.

The jokes must come from getting the economics right rather than from garbling it, so that a reader who knows Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade setup laughs instead of winces. Concretely, the piece should be built on the actual mechanics of these results: the fund maintains a single scrupulously shared "house prior"; all research flows through one data room that every trader can see; and everyone knows that everyone is rational, and knows that everyone knows, and so on down the tower. Let individual gags turn on these gears — two portfolio managers who agree to disagree about an upcoming rate decision are summoned before the CIO, since if their posteriors genuinely differ and everything else in the setup holds, something has gone badly wrong somewhere; a trader who proposes a bet on his "private information" is patiently shown that placing the order would itself render that information common knowledge, annihilating its speculative value; meanwhile the fund quietly bleeds money on hedging and liquidity trades — the one category of trading the theorem never forbids, and therefore the one nobody at the fund has any interest in being clever about. If a character does trade speculatively anyway, the article should make legible exactly which assumption of the theorem they are violating: a genuinely different prior, a non-informational motive like risk-sharing or a margin call, or garden-variety irrationality.

Include invented quotes from at least three sources: one portfolio manager, one outside observer (a behavioral economist who believes the whole episode vindicates her view that markets run on bias), and one more of your choosing — perhaps the prime broker, who can note that the theorems say nothing about fees. Somewhere in the piece, let it surface that the behavioral economist and the fund's founders have disagreed for a decade about whether markets are efficient, and that this disagreement itself is quietly awkward for anyone who takes the common-prior framework seriously. End with the fund's final act — its last trade or its final, principled refusal to trade — and make the closing beat do real epistemological work: what it would even mean for prices to aggregate information if every trade disclosed what the traders knew. Keep the voice flat and deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader, and no joke that depends on the article getting the theorem wrong.
