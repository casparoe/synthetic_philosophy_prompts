I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a purely pragmatic explanation of Moore's paradox: she holds that the absurdity of asserting "It's raining, but I don't believe it" lies entirely in the norms governing assertion — asserting the first conjunct represents the speaker as believing it, while the second conjunct says she does not — and not in any logical inconsistency, since the conjunction is perfectly possible and could well be true. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the load-bearing parts of her position, with a follow-up for each keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question should target the contrast between the commissive form ("p, but I don't believe p") and the omissive form ("p, but I believe not-p"), since norm-based accounts often end up treating the two asymmetrically. At least one should target the datum that embedding the sentence in a conditional ("If it's raining but I don't believe it, the picnic is off") seems to drain away the absurdity — a point that looks like evidence for her view, but which threatens to leave her unable to explain why even the unasserted, merely contemplated sentence strikes us as strange. And at least one should target the past-tense versions, which strike nearly everyone as perfectly assertible, and what that temporal asymmetry suggests about whether the operative norm governs belief itself or only its expression.
