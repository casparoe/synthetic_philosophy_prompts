I teach an intro ethics section and we just did the Taurek "should the numbers count" debate — the coin flip, the claim that five deaths isn't worse than one, all that. My students come out thinking the anti-aggregationist just obviously loses, mostly because the standard rescue cases make it too easy to think that. I want a case that cuts the other way, at least part of the way.

Build me a two-stage thought experiment in a realistic municipal setting (no trolley, no magic machines, no stipulated probabilities). Stage one is a rescue or effort-allocation choice where the anti-aggregationist's coin flip actually comes out looking more respectful of the individuals involved than the utilitarian's answer — make the utilitarian do something the students will wince at. Stage two is the pressure point: a later budget or prevention decision where the same anti-aggregationist, from their own denial that five deaths is worse than one plus some other commitment they really do hold, gets driven to an answer about safety spending that the class will find clearly wrong. So the case pressures them from both directions instead of one.

Constraints: the awkwardness in stage two can't come from a duty you invent and pin on them — it has to fall out of their own view; keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so; and the two stages should be connected so it's the same people or the same institution, not two unrelated dilemmas stapled together.

After the scenario, walk through what the straightforward utilitarian aggregator, the Taurek-style anti-aggregationist, and a Broome-ish weighted-lottery view each say at each stage, tell me exactly which feature of the case is doing the work, then give the best Taurekian escape move (like asking "worse for whom?") and be honest about what that escape costs them elsewhere. If the whole thing cant be built to spec, tell me which constraint to drop and why rather than fudging it.
