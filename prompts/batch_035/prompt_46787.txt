I write about science and health for a national daily, and an editorial dispute from last month has left me unable to settle a question I've come to think is more philosophical than journalistic.

We ran a story on a large observational study — several hundred thousand patients followed over a decade — which found that people who regularly took a widely available mineral supplement had roughly a third lower incidence of a form of early-onset dementia. The paper itself was scrupulous: its authors stated that the design could not distinguish causation from confounding, observed that supplement users in the cohort were also thinner, wealthier, more exercised, and less likely to smoke, and noted that a randomized trial was underway. My article reported all of this faithfully, three paragraphs in.

The headline, written by the desk over my objection, read: "Daily mineral supplement cuts dementia risk by a third, huge study finds." Our push notification said: "The pill millions already take may protect against dementia, study suggests." The desk's position is that both are covered: every word is true, the finding is real, the study is accurately described in the body, and our corrections policy fires only on factual error. My position was that "cuts," in a headline, does causal work, and that the large majority of our readers — our analytics show most never get past the notification and the headline — will come away believing something the study's own authors explicitly disavowed.

For comparison: last year an outlet we compete with covered a similar study with a headline asserting the causal benefit outright, and we treated that, rightly I think, as a straightforward falsehood. When I try to explain why our piece differs in kind rather than merely in craft, I sound like a press officer.

So my questions. Is there a principled moral line between the two pieces, or only a difference in how the falsehood is delivered? Does it matter that the misleading content travels through headline conventions — compression, verb choice, the "study finds" tag that hands responsibility to the researchers — rather than through any sentence I myself wrote? Does it matter that the headline wasn't written by me, and that my objection was lodged and overruled? Does it change the analysis that our readers have spent decades learning how headlines work, such that a practiced reader may discount the causal verb while a naive one is taken in? And my editor adds a final point I can't dismiss: if we run the careful, clunky headline, the aggregators will simply run someone else's punchy one, and the mistaken belief will exist whether or not we deliver it.

I would like to know whether the honest-but-misleading headline is genuinely different, morally, from the false one — and if it is, what exactly the difference consists in. If it isn't, I need to know that too, because it means our entire standard of accuracy is built on the wrong axis.
