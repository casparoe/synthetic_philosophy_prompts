I am at work on my second novel. The first was, in the end, a book about grief; this one is, I suppose, about inheritance. Its conceit is that a physicist, dying, agrees to have his brain's responses transcribed, page by page, into an enormous printed book — and that his daughter, decades later, "converses" with him by finding, on each turn, the page that answers what she has just said. My editor is enthusiastic, but I am uneasy: I suspect that whether this conceit is moving or merely sentimental depends on a philosophical question I have not yet made myself answer, namely whether the book is, in any defensible sense, him — or whether I have smuggled a mind into a stack of paper. Before I commit several years of writing to it, I want to work through the piece that clearly gave me the idea, whether or not I admit it in interviews.

The piece is "A Conversation with Einstein's Brain," which closes the Hofstadter and Dennett anthology The Mind's I (1981), and, as I recall it, Hofstadter wrote it while giving it out as a hoax byline to Einstein himself. It is staged, in his usual manner, as a dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and the Tortoise's tale runs roughly as follows: surgeons keep the dying Einstein's brain alive long enough for a team to transcribe its every possible response, so that after his death the professor survives as a book with some hundred billion pages; a young visitor is invited to interrogate it, and the conversation goes surprisingly well, each answer located and read from the proper page. The dialogue opens with a disagreement about records: the Tortoise cannot see why one should not simply hang a playing-record on the wall and take in the whole symphony at a glance, and Achilles has to explain, laboriously, that music is essentially something drawn out in time. That analogy, I take it, is the hinge of the whole piece, and I want it taken seriously rather than waved at as a decoration.

What I am asking for, then, is a reading of the piece through the philosophy of mind, with my own novel's problem as the stake. Specifically: what view of consciousness would have to be true for the book to be conscious — functionalism, multiple realizability, and the rest — and what view would make the whole thing an illusion, whether Searle-style biological naturalism or the older worry that consciousness needs a running process rather than a static object, all of its states there at once like the unplayed record? Where does the conceit quietly cheat — does the answer to the daughter's question come from the book at all, or from the transcribers' enormous prior labor and the reader's page-turning, and is that a fatal confound or precisely the point, as with the Chinese Room, where the understanding is somewhere in the system without being anywhere in particular? I'd like you to say which moves in the dialogue carry the real argument, whether the piece is right to stage the question "is the book conscious?" and then leave it standing, and — the part I most need — what my own conceit commits me to: if I let the daughter believe she is talking to her father, am I writing a functionalist novel, and would a philosopher say that is a coherent thing for the fiction to assert? Please be candid about where the piece resists the reading, and do not treat it as a mere illustration of positions worked out elsewhere; tell me what it dramatizes that the standard arguments, on paper, do not. I have my copy on the desk, so there is no need to quote; I only need to know what to look for when I open it again.
