I'm the research director for a state sentencing commission, and for the past two years I've watched two of our commissioners argue past each other about literally every revision to the guidelines. One is a die-hard retributivist — she thinks the point of sentencing is that offenders get the punishment they deserve, full stop, and anything that happens to reduce crime is at best a side effect that can't be the justification. The other is an equally committed consequentialist — he came to sentencing policy from an economics background and thinks punishment is just a costly intervention whose only justification is what it prevents (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation), and that if it prevents nothing we shouldn't be doing it. They're both smart and argue in good faith, but their debates go nowhere because they never manage to write down exactly where the disagreement is, and each one's statement of the other's view is a bit of a strawman.

We have a guidelines revision vote coming up in the spring, and the chair wants their dispute converted into something the rest of the commission can actually use. My idea — modeled on those adversarial collaborations they do in other fields — is to get the two of them to co-sign a joint statement instead of each producing another dueling memo. Please draft that statement as I imagine it coming out of their collaboration. It should have a numbered section of claims both could sign their names to (about what we actually know regarding deterrence, about proportionality being at least a defeasible constraint, whatever else they genuinely share). Then the precise disagreements, each phrased so each side would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own position — this is the part they've never gotten right. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them, rather than just re-stating their starting points.

Here are the live issues to run it through if you can. First, a proposed repeat-offender enhancement — he wants it because people with priors predictibly reoffend, she says it punishes people for who they are rather than for what they did this time. Second, an across-the-board sentence reduction for a burglary offense class — he supports it because the evidence on the marginal deterrence of long sentences is weak and prison is expensive and arguably criminogenic, while she's torn because she thinks current sentences happen to be roughly what's deserved. Third, a restorative-justice diversion program that leaves victims more satisfied but lets some offenders escape formal punishment entirely. For each crux, write down a test, a piece of evidence, or an argument both would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance. And if any part of their fight turns out on inspection to be verbal — say, about what the word "justification" even means in this context — please flag which part and why, since I'd genuinely like to know whether the spring vote will be moved by any of this or whether me and the chair are just managing personalities.
