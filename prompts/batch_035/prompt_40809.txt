I write novels, and I've written myself into a corner: I gave one of my characters an argument I now can't beat, which is awkward because his granddaughter is supposed to win their big scene. He's a retired actuary, she's a climate activist, and they're arguing on his porch about whether the further future counts for less. His case:

(1) It's irrational to discount a benefit merely because it occurs later in time — when something happens is no more relevant to its value than where it happens.
(2) So his granddaughter is right that we can't count the welfare of future generations for less just because it's future.
(3) But the same reasoning applies inside a single life: my prudent concern for my future self can't rest on bare identity, since (as the fission-type thought experiments are meant to show) identity isn't what matters in survival — psychological connectedness is what does the work.
(4) By 2060, the person drawing on my pension will share fewer memories, plans and tastes with present-day me than my best friend does today.
(5) So rational concern should fall off with connectedness: looking after my near-term self is fine, but saving for my eighty-year-old self is only marginally more rational than saving for a stranger, and putting the money toward my sister instead is, rationally speaking, a wash.
(6) Therefore either prudent saving is just a bias, one more form of partiality no better than nepotism, or else prudence is special after all — in which case bare temporal position does matter, and the granddaughter's whole climate case collapses along with his own.

He concludes that she either admits her retirement fund is a prejudice, or concedes his discount rate. I want her to win the scene honestly, not by authorial cheating. Which premise or step should she attack, and what does rejecting it cost her? For what it's worth I'd rather she keep (1) — the whole book leans on the future mattering. Answer in British English please.
