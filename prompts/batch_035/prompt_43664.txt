I've just left primary-school teaching to join a research lab that is trying, quite deliberately, to take Turing's learning-machine route seriously: rather than programming in what we know, we're to build something child-like and then educate it. Before I commit to a pedagogy, I've been asked to prepare the questions for the lab's podcast, which stages counterfactual interviews with dead scientists — the next episode is with Alan Turing, set in 1952, shortly after the Mind paper and the BBC discussions. Draft eight to ten questions, none of which just asks him to restate the child-machine idea. I need at least one on whether the shift from programming an adult mind to educating a child one is a principled method or a quiet admission that the direct route failed — with a follow-up keyed to his likely reply that we don't understand the original child either. At least one on his reliance on reward and punishment: does it saddle him with a behaviourism about learning that the rest of his argument can ill afford, and what could reinforce a machine that feels no hunger? At least one on the split between what the child machine must be born with and what its education supplies — how much innate structure, and how we could ever tell the innate from the taught. At least one on his confidence that human teachers, largely ignorant of what goes on inside their pupils, could carry such a machine far — press him on what ordinary pedagogy silently presupposes about its pupil, since that is now literally my job. And at least one on the objection that a machine raised like a child makes the imitation game a test of its upbringing rather than of the machine, with a follow-up anticipating whatever appeal to the symmetry between child and machine he is most likely to make. Weight the questions so that his answers would change what we actually do, and afterwards tell me which single answer you'd most expect to unsettle my current plan of treating the machine exactly as I treated my Year 2 class.
