I've been teaching Putnam's argument against the mind-brain identity theory for years, and I've hit upon what seems to me a decisive objection to functionalism that I'd like someone to address. Putnam pointed out that psychological processes like pain cannot be identified with any single brain process, since a state of that kind would have to be realizable in a mammalian brain, a reptilian brain, a mollusk's brain (octopuses being his favorite example), and presumably in silicon-based systems too. But here is my worry: if my pain and an octopus's pain are realized by utterly different physical mechanisms, then they have nothing whatsoever in common — no shared physical property, and presumably no shared qualitative character either. In that case, on what basis does the functionalist continue to call both of them "pain" rather than treating the word as a mere label for a grab bag of unrelated states, like the word "junk"? It seems to me that multiple realizability doesn't explain the mind so much as dissolve it: the more realizations we allow, the emptier the mental predicate becomes. Please tell me where I've gone wrong, as I suspect I must have.
