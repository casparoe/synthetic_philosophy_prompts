My borough council's integration panel keeps ending arguments with 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do' — most recently to insist that newly arrived families adopt 'local values' wholesale, and before that to wave off a British-born Sikh family's objection to a school's Easter assembly tradition. I'm fairly sure the proverb is doing several different jobs across those uses, and I'd like to see them seperated out before I concede anything. Set out exactly five distinct precise claims the proverb could be making — e.g. about whose norms govern, the majority's customs or the actual law; about who counts as 'the Romans' once newcomers are themselves a large share of the population; about whether it reaches deep moral and religious commitments or only matters of etiquette; about whether it binds the host society too — and for each, one or two sentences saying whether it's a platitude, defensible but contestable, or flatly false, and which of the two uses above it would actually support. Numbered list, exactly five items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion.
