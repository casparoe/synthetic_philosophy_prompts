Retired chemist here (35 years mostly in kinetics and spectroscopy), got into philosophy a few years back and I've gotten myself into a weird position: I've come around to something like al-Ghazālī's take on causation. No necessity binding fire to cotton burning, no event necessitating its effect -- what we call laws of nature are just the regular way things happen, sustained by something outside the chain, and in principle the habit could be broken (his miracle point). Honestly this got *easier* for me to believe after a career in the lab, not harder: I observed one thing following another ten thousand times and never once observed the necessitation part. The connection was always something we drew in, not something the data contained.

So -- what would count against this view? Not the reflexive "it's unscientific" stuff, I want real defeaters. A few things I'd especially like you to address. First: my old physicist instinct says the sheer *precision* of the regularities (constants holding to umpteen decimal places, across the universe) feels more like necessity than habit -- is that a genuine argument that habit-talk underdescribes what we see, or just an aesthetic preference? Second: is there a version of the Averroes-style reply -- that watching fire interact with cotton over and over teaches you something about fire's own nature, not just about the habits of whatever is behind the show -- that a working scientist could take seriously, or does it collapse back into vague powers-talk? Third: does my view predict anything at all different from plain Humean regularity plus "God does it," and if it doesn't, is that fatal or just makes it not a scientific hypothesis? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest if the position has quietly been insulated into unfalsifiability -- and whether that's a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind.
