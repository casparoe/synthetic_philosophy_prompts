For the past several months I have been trying to work out, on my own, whether the size of a brain has anything at all to do with how much a being's suffering and flourishing matter morally. I have no training in philosophy or neuroscience, and I keep going in circles, so I want to set down the verdicts my gut actually delivers on concrete cases and ask whether some single principle could capture them — and if none can, which of my verdicts I ought to give up.

Here are my verdicts. A blue whale has a brain several times larger than mine, and I do not think for a moment that a whale's interests count for several times what mine do. Our extinct relatives, who had brains somewhat larger than ours on average, would not have counted for more than we do. My neighbor, who is a brilliant mathematician and presumably has an unusually capable brain, does not count for more than I do. A person born with a much smaller brain than typical counts exactly as much as anyone else, and so does a small child, whose brain is not yet fully grown — if anything, children seem to deserve extra protection, not less. A chicken counts for less than a human: I would save a human before a chicken from a burning building without hesitation. A honeybee, with its tiny brain, counts for very little. And yet if a future person's brain were engineered to be far smaller but computationally equivalent to ours — same capacities, same inner life — I hold that they would count fully.

I did have a tentative principle of my own, namely that what matters is the capacities of a mind, not the organ that produces it, so that brain size is irrelevant but intelligence and sensitivity matter. But I can see this is in trouble: it seems to explain the chicken and the honeybee, and the small-brained future person, but I have no idea how it treats the person with microcephaly or a severe intellectual disability, whose capacities really are reduced, and yet whom I refuse to count for less. And it says nothing about why the brilliant mathematician doesn't outrank me, since his capacities presumably are greater.

What I would like from you, then, is this. State, as precisely as you can, one or a small number of candidate principles that could govern these verdicts — for instance, some kind of threshold view on which anything at or above a certain level of mental complexity receives full and equal status, with nothing above the threshold counting for more, and things below it counting for less in proportion to something. Say which case motivates each clause. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and why I should let that verdict go rather than complicate the principle. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed, in particular these three: a nonhuman animal whose brain is larger than ours but whose inner life we have no reason to think is richer than a dog's; a being whose brain is gradually made more and more efficient and smaller over decades while its capacities and personality stay exactly constant; and an artificial mind whose physical substrate is minute but whose capacities exceed ours in every respect. I care most about whether the principle draws the line where I actually feel it, or whether I have been holding two beliefs that cannot both survive.
