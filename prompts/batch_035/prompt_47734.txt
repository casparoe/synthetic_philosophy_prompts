I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about what makes consent valid, and I've collected my gut verdicts on cases over the past few months, mostly from conversations with my sister, who is a hospital social worker, and my roommate, who is a labor organizer. Deception cases first: a surgeon tells a patient a procedure is routine and low-risk when she knows the patient has an elevated chance of a serious complication, and operates — I think the patient's consent was invalid. A patient conceals his heavy drinking from the transplant committee to get on the list — I think his consent to the committee's rules was still valid, though he behaved badly. A man removes a condom during sex after agreeing to use one — invalid consent, obviously. The same man lies about being divorced rather than separated before the sex — I think the consent was valid, even though the lie is worse in some ways than the condom one is in others. A friend agrees to be the victim of a hidden-camera prank where the deception is the entire point, like a fake arrest staged for a video — valid. Then coercion: a surgeon says "you can refuse the amputation, but you'll be dead in a week," and the patient consents — valid. A landlord says "sleep with me or I won't renew your lease," and the tenant agrees — invalid. A boss says "work the weekend or I'll give the promotion to Dana" — invalid, but somehow less clear to me than the landlord case, and I can't say why, since a job offer withdrawn feels like it might not be a threat at all if it's "work the weekend and I'll consider you for the promotion." Competence: a mildly drunk friend consenting to a large tattoo at 11 p.m. — invalid, and I'd hold the tattoo artist responsible. The same mildly drunk friend ordering another round — valid. A woman with moderate dementia, lucid and consistent for an afternoon, refusing a pacemaker her family wants — valid, and binding.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting you. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case forces it. I particularly want you to confront the two tensions I can't resolve myself: the deception cases, where consent survives some lies (the divorced one, the transplant concealment, the prank) and not others (the surgical one, the condom one) — my instinct is that it has something to do with which decision the lie corrupts, but I can't make that work for the prank case, where the deception corrupts the very decision being made and the consent still seems fine; and the coercion cases, where threatening to withhold a benefit and threatening to impose a harm seem to come apart, but I can't draw the line without making ordinary market transactions coercive, which I don't believe they are. Please state which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and be honest if the cost of saving every verdict is a principle so baroque it explains nothing. Also give me your principle's verdict on two cases I haven't listed: someone consenting to a psychology experiment after reading a consent form that truthfully lists all the risks but buries the most serious one in deliberately impenetrable jargon, and a refugee agreeing to an exploitative factory wage because the alternative in her circumstances is destitution — I suspect my intuitions on the second one are doing something different from what they do on the landlord case, and I'd like to know if that's just inconsistency on my part or something the principle can register.

Finally, I'd like you to be explicit about the weak points: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration — say, a hypothetical case of a certain shape, or a general theoretical claim about what consent is for — would move you to a different principle or to overturning one of the verdicts you currently preserve. I'd rather have an answer that flags its own soft spots than one that projects confidence throughout.
