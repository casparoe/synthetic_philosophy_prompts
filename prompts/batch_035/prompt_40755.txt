Each year I must decide how to divide the portion of my income I give away between a fund that buys mosquito nets and a fund that works on safeguarding humanity's very long-run future — say, by reducing the chance that civilization permanently derails. The case for the second has always rested on scale: even a small probability of improving the prospects of a future containing an astronomically large number of lives can outweigh anything achievable in the present century. A friend has lately been pressing an objection I find hard to dismiss. He observes that we cannot rule out living inside a computer simulation run by some later or higher civilization; and if we do, he says, the long run I think I'm protecting is not what I take it to be. Simulations, he argues, are run for research or entertainment and then switched off, so from our vantage point the future is probably short, and the astronomical numbers that make the long-run fund look so important shrink accordingly. He adds a second line: even if our simulated descendants persist, civilizations like ours are presumably simulated many times over, so my donation is duplicated across a vast number of near-identical copies and its net marginal effect should wash out. And a third, blunter one: if all of this is a simulation, perhaps there simply are no future people for my money to help. My own reactions pull in opposite directions. It seems right that any serious probability of a short-lived simulation should reduce how many descendants I should expect, and that this should feed into the comparison with mosquito nets. Yet I suspect the same fact cuts the other way too: if my choice is mirrored across many copies, a single donation may be more consequential, not less, and the argument may amplify the very reasoning it was meant to undercut. Before I revise my giving plan, I want the reasoning examined rather than my intuitions soothed: where, precisely, does my friend's argument succeed or break, is there a coherent way to weigh it against the ordinary case for the long-run fund, and should it move the allocation of someone with a modest income and no claim to expertise in any of this?
