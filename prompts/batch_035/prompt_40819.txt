Philosophers of mind have a favorite example: the digger wasp who drags her paralyzed prey to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside alone to check the burrow, and only then hauls the prey in. If an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she is inside, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold — and goes in to check again, apparently without registering that she just checked; she can be made to loop through this indefinitely. Dennett called behavior of this shape sphexish, and the wasp has become the standing picture of intelligent-looking rigidity. A friend of mine now leans on the wasp in arguments about free will: human cognition, she says, is the wasp with a bigger subroutine library — whatever looks like deliberation, spontaneity, or responsiveness to reasons is just sphexishness at greater scale with more variables, so since the wasp is plainly not free, we are not either; the difference is one of degree, and freedom dies at the first degree. A mutual friend disagrees about what the example was ever for: on his reading the wasp merely fixes the contrast class — behavior insensitive even to gross features of the situation — so that any creature whose behavior varies with those details, however mechanically the variation is produced, already has the kind of flexibility worth wanting. Audit the analogy as my friend uses it: set out what is supposed to map onto what (the inspection routine, the looping, the experimenter, the rigidity itself), say where the mapping breaks — is there anything in our case that corresponds to the experimenter resetting the situation, and how should the cases where we are demonstrably wasp-like, from anchoring to autopilot, be classified? — and identify which features of the wasp story the eliminativist reading quietly imports, such as a clean behavioral test for the absence of flexibility or the thought that a mechanistic explanation of flexibility would disqualify it. Then tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying none of the weight and the real argument lies elsewhere; and if there is a better analogy than the wasp for the flexibility at issue, say what it is and what it gets right that the wasp gets wrong. As you go, indicate how confident you are in each step of the audit, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
