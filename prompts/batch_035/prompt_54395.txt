In early September I am due to deliver the opening remarks at a three-day residential workshop whose working title is "Backsliding: Concept, Measurement, Case". The organisers have gathered three groups who rarely share a room: constitutional lawyers who advise courts and assemblies, quantitative political scientists who build democracy indices for a living, and advocacy staff from organisations that brief journalists and donors. I have been asked to open the proceedings by framing the workshop's central question, and I want that question to be genuinely open rather than rhetorical — the following panels debate particular countries, and my job is to make the concept itself the day's problem, not to settle the panels in advance.

The speech should run about eight minutes and should be built around a real tension I want the workshop to chew on. On one account, backsliding is defined by mechanism: a now-familiar repertoire of moves — court-packing, capture of media and electoral regulators, changes to the rules of the game adopted through formally legal means, sometimes even by supermajority — that hollow out the competitive arena while leaving every outward form intact. On another account, it is defined by outcome: whether elections remain genuinely competitive and power can actually change hands. These two accounts come apart in instructive ways, and the speech should set up at least one case where they diverge — for instance, a government that has rigged the arena in every procedural respect and then loses an election anyway. That looks like backsliding on the mechanism account but not the outcome account, and how one reacts to the case is likely to reveal which account one is really holding.

The speech should also pose, without resolving, the question of who effectively decides these matters. Part of what makes the concept difficult is that its application is not epistemology alone: index producers, supranational bodies with treaty procedures at their disposal, and advocacy organisations each hold part of the pen, and their verdicts do real work in the world. I want to say something honest about this to the advocates in the room — they need words that survive contact with a press release — without implying that concept-choice is merely political, and something honest to the scholars without implying that the advocates' pragmatism is a corruption of the concept rather than a fact about it.

Two constraints on what the speech can say. First, since the later panels will debate Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States, my remarks must not pre-judge any of them; those countries may appear only as cases whose status is precisely what is in dispute, never as settled illustrations. Second, the speech should acknowledge the baseline problem: the word itself, with its suggestion of sliding back from something, quietly presupposes a starting point, and for many states the relevant baseline — a founding constitutional settlement, say, or an accession standard — was itself contested. Whether backsliding can be a neutral analytical concept at all, or is essentially contested in a way the workshop should study rather than regret, is the question I want hanging over the three days.

Please write the speech. It should be spoken English rather than an abstract, and the conceptual framing should do real structural work — determining what can and cannot be said to this particular mixed audience — rather than serving as decoration. Please write it in American English.
