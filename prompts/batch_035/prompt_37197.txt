I am preparing a position statement on weakness of will for a graduate seminar that pairs philosophy of action with behavioral economics, and I would like a consistency audit of my draft before I circulate it. The set of views I have committed myself to is as follows:

1. Akrasia is genuine: an agent can, while acting freely and intentionally, act against her own all-things-considered better judgment, a judgment she still holds at the moment of action. I follow Davidson in rejecting the Socratic escape routes — the akratic agent is not ignorant of what is better, and she has not simply changed her mind; the judgment remains, and the action goes the other way.
2. The correct psychological model of most ordinary akrasia is hyperbolic discounting: the dieter who judges the diet better but eats the cake exhibits the same preference-reversal structure observed when a smaller-sooner reward is preferred at close range and a larger-later one from a distance.
3. Preference reversal across time is the formal signature of intertemporal irrationality: a rational agent's discount function must be exponential, and an agent whose preferences reverse in that way is, to that extent, rationally defective.
4. I am nevertheless sympathetic to the hazard-rate argument: where there is genuine uncertainty about whether delayed rewards will arrive at all, something like hyperbolic discounting may be the appropriate response, so that hyperbolic discounting is not universally irrational but can be the right policy in environments of the sort agents actually inhabit.
5. Akratic action is fully culpable: weakness of will neither excuses nor mitigates responsibility, and the akratic agent is an apt target of blame in the ordinary way.
6. Precommitment — Ulysses ordering himself bound to the mast, buying only small cakes — is often rationally required, not merely permissible, and it is not an illegitimate act of domination over one's future self.
7. The preferences of my future selves, at the times they hold them, have full normative standing in determining what is best for me considered over a whole life.

I take it for granted that some of these commitments are in tension, but I do not have a clear view of which tensions are real, which are merely apparent, or how serious the real ones are. Please go through the set and tell me which pairs or clusters are genuinely inconsistent and which only appear to be, being explicit about the reasoning that reveals each verdict. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity rather than treating all as equally bad, say what would have to give in order to resolve it, and what that resolution costs elsewhere in the set. I am also unsure whether the problem is localized in one commitment or distributed across several: if you think the cleanest repair requires giving up more than one item, say so and defend the choice. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most tension at the least cost, and whether the resulting position would still be recognizably mine.
