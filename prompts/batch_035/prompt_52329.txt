I've noticed something odd about how people react to convention violations, and I want to know what it says about whether these conventions are actual moral obligations. When a tourist cuts a queue in London, or jaywalks in a place where that's taboo, people mostly shrug or quietly help them fix it. The same act by an obvious local gets the glare, the tutting, sometimes a confrontation. And it's not just about legal consequences — jaywalking is illegal where I live but a local doing it barely registers, while in some countries the queue etiquette feels enforced more fiercely than actual laws. So here's my question: what does this asymmetry presuppose about how everyday conventions bind us?

My working hypothesis is that the tourist/local distinction tracks something like common knowledge: a local is presumed to know the convention, so their violation signals disregard rather than ignorance, and maybe the obligation only exists once you're inside the circle of people who know the practice. But that can't be the whole story, because tourists get the benefit of local conventions (their tip gets accepted, their spot in the line is honored) without apparently being bound by them. Is the practice presupposing that conventions create obligations through knowledge rather than through consent or through receiving benefits? That seems weird — it's almost the opposite of how we think about, say, accepting a ride from someone: you can't just take the benefits and disclaim the norms.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory here. Does the tourist-forgiveness pattern strictly require one view of how conventions obligate (knowledge/uptake-based, consent-based, benefit-based, or just coordination-value-based), or is it compatible with several? Where would each view actually predict that locals should treat a tourist's violation the same as a local's, and does the practice then count as evidence against that view? Also tell me whether the same implicit theory can handle the visitor's side: if I spend a month in a country, is there a principled point at which "I didn't know" stops being available to me, and does that suggest the obligation is gradually acquired rather than switched on?

One more thing I'm genuinely unsure about: whether the pattern is even about obligation at all. Maybe the glare at locals isn't moral blame but something more like enforcement of a coordination equilibrium, and the shrug at tourists is just a cost-benefit judgment about the pointlessness of correcting someone who leaves tomorrow. If that's right, the practice might presuppose nothing about morality at all — conventions as pure Lewis-style coordination solutions, with the "moral" flavor just an enforcement mechanism. Tell me if that deflationary reading can really explain the data, or whether something about the reaction (the personal offense, the sense of disrespect) escapes it.
