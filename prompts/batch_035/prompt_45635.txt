A few weeks ago I watched a biopic about a musician I happen to know a fair amount about, and afterwards my friend and I got into a long argument about one scene: a confrontation between the musician and his brother that, as far as I can tell, never happened — the filmmakers seem to have invented it wholesale, and also merged two different real bandmates into a single character. What struck me was that neither of us objected to most of the film's liberties. The dialogue was obviously invented, several events were shuffled out of order, and one real-life decade was compressed into what felt like a year, and all of that felt fine to both of us, somehow. But the invented confrontation bothered her, and the composite character bothered me, in a way the other stuff didn't. Neither of us could say why we drew the line where we did, and the argument has been nagging at me since.

So here is what I want to figure out. The whole industry of historical drama and biopics seems to run on a shared but unspoken understanding of what audiences will accept: inventing words that a real dead person never said is fine; inventing a whole scene in which that person does something that flatters or shames them, not so much. Contracts and customs reflect this too — estates are consulted, some portrayals get lawyers' letters, and reviewers will praise a film for being "true to" someone while acknowledging it plays havoc with the facts. It's as if everyone involved believes a dramatisation can be unfaithful to someone without containing a single false claim about what that person did, or faithful to them while getting the events badly wrong. What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the underlying view of truth that this practice presupposes. Does it assume that a real person has some essence — a character, a spirit, a "what they were really like" — that survives changes to the record of events, such that a film can be measured against that essence rather than against the facts? Is that even coherent, or is "true to the person" just a polite fiction that lets audiences enjoy invention while feeling they haven't been deceived? And where exactly is the boundary — is it about kinds of departures (compressing time versus inventing motives), or about departures that change how we judge the person, or about what viewers are likely to actually believe afterwards?

I'd also like to know whether the practice really needs this theory or whether it's compatible with several rival ones. Could a strict "just the facts" view of truth licence the same customs, if we thought audiences implicitly discount dramatisations and so nothing false is really being asserted? Could a view on which a person just is the sum of their life events also explain the line my friend and I drew? And finally: if the implicit view were replaced by a straightforward demand for factual accuracy — everything shown either happened or is flagged as invented — what would actually change in how these films get made, and would we lose something worth having? I'm not looking for a defence of artistic licence or an attack on it; I want the hidden theory reconstructed and tested.
