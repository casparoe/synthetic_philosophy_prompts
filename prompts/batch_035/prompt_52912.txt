I am a PhD student in economic history, and I keep running into the same methodological fight in my field without anyone ever stating what would actually settle it. When someone asks why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain and not in China — the Needham question, the Great Divergence debate — I can never tell whether the answers on offer are competing answers to one question or answers to several questions that have been sloppily merged. Before I read another monograph, I want the specification the monograph is supposed to meet.

Act as a senior philosopher of history with a specialty in the methodology of causal explanation in historiography and comparative social science. Before you draft anything, spend a moment mapping the logical space: what an answer to a contrastive "why here and not there?" question must do, which of the things economic historians instinctively demand of such answers are genuine requirements and which are prejudices of particular schools (the demand for a single prime mover, the demand that the answer not be "contingent," the demand for China-side evidence and not just Europe-side), and whether the requirements can even be jointly satisfied. Then produce the deliverable.

Write the desiderata an adequate answer to a "why Britain and not China?" style question must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — a concrete situation in which an answer violating that requirement would clearly be defective — and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a camp preference, and of which camp. At minimum, the list should confront the following, plus anything else you judge essential:

1. Contrast-class discipline. An answer must pin down which comparison is being made: Britain vs. China as wholes, Britain vs. the Yangzi delta as regions of comparable development, Northwest Europe vs. East Asia as systems, or England vs. what China would have become absent some specific contingency. Different contrast classes license different answers; answers that silently swap between them are answering different questions while appearing to disagree.

2. Symmetry. My advisor insists on what he calls a contrast-stability condition: whatever explanatory structure you propose for the Britain–China comparison should not fall apart when you run the same structure on adjacent contrasts — why Britain and not France or the Dutch Republic, why the Yangzi delta and not, say, the poorer interior provinces of China, why the nineteenth century and not the Song dynasty in the twelfth, when China was arguably the world's technological leader. If an explanation works only for the one contrast class that inspired it, is it an explanation or a retrofitted narrative? Decide whether this condition is a genuine desideratum, an unreasonable one, or something that must be weakened, and say why.

3. Counterfactual load. The question is contrastive, so any answer commits to a counterfactual claim — that with factor X absent or present, the divergence goes away or does not happen. Specify how much counterfactual discipline the answer owes: must it identify a difference that would have flipped the outcome, or is it enough to identify differences that were load-bearing without claiming reversal? Note what this implies for answers built on deep, non-modifiable features like geography or long-run cultural inheritances, which appear to resist the counterfactual test entirely.

4. The well-posedness challenge itself. Some historians hold that the question is malformed — that picking out Britain and China as the pair to explain smuggles in the answer, since salience was conferred by the outcome. Decide where this challenge belongs: is it a fatal objection that belongs on the desiderata list as a requirement no answer can meet, or a constraint that an adequate answer must discharge (say, by showing the comparison would have struck a well-informed observer in 1700 as natural rather than post hoc), or a confusion the list should dissolve?

Finally, do three things with the completed list. First, identify any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, and explain the tension — this is the part I most want to see. Second, say which single requirement does the most work excluding the familiar styles of answer (the single-cause explanations, the "fortuitous conjuncture" explanations, the deep-roots explanations), without you needing to adjudicate between those styles. Third, note honestly whether any adequate answer would have to be a book rather than a sentence, and what that fact does to the idea that there is a "Needham question" waiting for an answer at all. Do not name specific authors, works, or bibliographic items; I am after the methodological structure, not a literature review. A table for the main list is fine, with the surrounding argument in prose.
