In my historiography seminar last term we spent two weeks on the outbreak of the First World War, and the discussion kept collapsing into a ritual. Whenever anyone raised a counterfactual — what if the driver hadn't taken a wrong turn in Sarajevo, what if the Serbian government had succeeded in stopping the assassins on their first attempt — one classmate would recite the old nursery rhyme: for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of the shoe the horse was lost, and so on up through the rider, the message, the battle, and the kingdom. His point was that history turns on tiny contingencies. Our professor would then push back with one word: overdetermined. The alliance commitments, the mobilization timetables, the German General Staff's fear of Russian rearmament — enough independent causes were converging on a general European war that removing any one assassination changes at most the date and the pretext. Both of them, I think, believed they had a principle and not just a temperamental preference (the classmate for narrative drama, the professor for deep structures). I want to know what the proverb actually claims, and whether it's the kind of thing that can lose an argument like this.

So: lay out the distinct precise theses hiding inside "for want of a nail." I can dimly see several, and I want them separated and made precise. One might be the mere platitude that every event has some proximate trigger. Another is that large outcomes counterfactually depend on small ones — the nail is necessary for the kingdom in the closest possible worlds. A third distinguishes triggering causes from merely background or enabling conditions, and says only the trigger is the "real" cause — but that would make the proverb a claim about which cause deserves the credit, not about how history works. A fourth might be a kind of chaotic-sensitivity thesis: historical systems are like weather, so at some fine grain everything depends on everything. And there may be a reading about causal chains being fragile — single points of failure strung in series — versus redundant systems, where the proverb is then a description of how kingdoms differ from well-built ones. I'd also like you to be careful about necessity versus sufficiency throughout: which readings say the nail was necessary for the kingdom, which say the rest was nearly sufficient without it, and how the two interact. Where it helps, please bring in the standard apparatus from the philosophy of causation — Mackie-style INUS conditions, overdetermination and preemption, maybe the distinction between causes and background conditions — but explain it rather than name-dropping it, since my seminar never covered it.

Then I need the professor's side taken seriously rather than caricatured. Explain how overdetermination is supposed to work as an objection: if there are multiple independent sufficient causes converging on an outcome, what exactly follows about the small trigger's causal or explanatory status? Note that textbook overdetermination is two simultaneous riflemen, which doesn't fit the July Crisis at all — the "alternative" causes there are slow-moving structural conditions that would only have produced a war years later. So say what the professor's argument really needs: perhaps not overdetermination strictly, but something like preemption, or a claim about how far the actual trigger's effect was from the counterfactual replacement. If the professor's reading is right that the war would have come by 1917 anyway, does that make the assassination explanatorily irrelevant, or just one cause among several of a slightly different war?

Finally, the part I actually want to bring back to class: a verdict. Say which of the readings the classmate probably had in mind, whether that reading is defensible, and whether the classmate and the professor were genuinely disagreeing about history or merely using the word "cause" to pick out different links in one chain they agreed on.
