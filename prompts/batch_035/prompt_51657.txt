I teach political theory at a middling state university, and last week a student flat out refused to engage with our unit on long-run demographic forecasting. His line was that Popper proved in The Poverty of Historicism that laws of history are impossible, so anything like projecting fertility rates a century out is pseudo-science on par with Marxist prophecy, and he wasn't going to waste his time. And honestly, my first instinct was that he's basically right and I just assign the stuff out of habit? But then I realised I can't actually reconstruct the argument to my own satisfaction, which is embarrassing since I've been telling students for years that Popper "showed" this.

Here's where I get stuck. My half-remembered version of the argument is: the course of history is strongly influenced by the growth of knowledge, we can't predict future knowledge (by whatever we'd have predicted it with would already be present knowledge), so we can't predict the future course of history. Fine. But that seems to show only that *predictions* are impossible, not that *laws* are — demographers don't claim to know what contraception technology looks like in 2125, they just extrapolate conditional regularities, which I thought Popper himself was fine with given his enthusiasm for piecemeal tinkering. And worse, the argument looks self-undermining: "no one can predict future knowledge" is itself a pretty bold prediction about the epistemic future, and I've heard people say Popper conceded something like this in a footnote somewhere, though I can't remember if that's real or something a colleague invented.

So here's what I'd like from you. Is my student's reading of Popper actually right — did he show that there can't be laws of history in a sense that would genuinely sink demographic long-range forecasting — or is he running together Popper's target (grand developmental prophecy about the whole of history) with something much more modest that Popper happily allowed? What exactly would have to be true for a regularity to count as a "law of history" in the sense Popper denied? And is the self-reference worry a real problem for the argument or a cheap trick people use to dismiss it? Please be straight with me about what's actually settled among people who know this literature versus what's genuinely contested — I gather the status of the unpredictability-of-knowledge premise is itself argued about, and I don't want to present some contested reading as the consensus when I go back to this kid. Also, write it in British English — recognise, not recognize etc. — he's a Commonwealth exchange student and it's become a whole thing with him.
