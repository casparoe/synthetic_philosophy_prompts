My roommate is a Caruso person -- she keeps going on about how nobody ever deserves punishment in the basic-desert sense, and after months of this I've started noticing how weird the actual practices of our courts are when you look at them through her lens. Last month I sat in on a sentencing as part of a philosophy of law course I'm taking: the defendant read a tearful apology, the judge noted he had "accepted responsibility," and he got noticeably less time than the guidelines number everyone was expecting. That's not some anomaly either -- the federal guidelines officially knock time off for acceptance of responsibility, parole boards put huge weight on genuine remorse, and defendants who allocute are practically coached by their lawyers on sounding sorry. But remorse isn't an element of any crime, and there's this whole class of people (psychopaths, certain brain injuries, just very stoic guys) who either can't feel it or can't show it, and they get, in effect, a longer sentence for a fact about their wiring.

So here's what I want to know: what theory of moral responsibility does the remorse discount actually presuppose? The obvious reading is backward-looking desert -- feeling bad partially pays the moral debt, which sounds like exactly the "just deserts" thinking my roommate wants to abolish. But my professor hinted there's a forward-looking reading too: maybe remorse functions as evidence that the person is addressable as a member of the moral community, the way Dennett talks about people who can be given reasons and respond to them, and punishing them differently is really about engaging that capacity rather than balancing a ledger. If that's right, then maybe Caruso-style deniers of basic desert can keep the practice while dumping the retributivist packaging. But I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just retributivism wearing a lab coat, because the system also refuses to give credit for suffering in prison -- you can't argue "I've already suffered enough, count that against my sentence" -- which seems to cut against the debt-paying reading while doing nothing for the addressability reading, since genuine remorse that arrives the day after sentencing also gets you nothing.

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out, and then actually take a side: would the remorse discount survive the abolition of basic desert, or is it a vestigial retributivist organ the system would have to cut out to be consistent? Don't just lay out the options and leave it to me -- I've been arguing about this at dinner for two weeks and I want a definite verdict, including on whether the discount is flat-out unjust to the people constitutionally incapable of remorse, and if you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several theories would license it, say which theory you'd pick anyway and why. (Also feel free to tell me if my roommate is right that I've just been staring at one small weird corner of criminal law and missing that the whole building is held up by just deserts.)
