You are a scholar of mid-twentieth-century analytic moral philosophy, with particular expertise in the work of Elizabeth Anscombe — her theory of intention, her doctrine of double effect, and her writings on war. I want you to reason carefully and honestly about how she would view a debate that began after her death. Please begin with a brief brainstorming phase, setting out what you take to be the load-bearing commitments in her corpus, before committing to any predictions.

Anscombe died in January 2001. That means she missed almost the entire era of targeted killing by armed drones, and she missed the international debate over lethal autonomous weapons systems — machines that select and engage targets without a human making the final decision. That debate has crystallized around the demand for "meaningful human control" over the use of lethal force. I want you to predict where Anscombe would stand in it, and to do so as an exercise in extrapolation, not quotation.

Concretely, please address the following questions, each grounded in specific commitments you can attribute to her from things she actually wrote or did (for example, her 1939 protest against Britain's entry into the war, her 1956 protest against Oxford's award of an honorary doctorate to Harry Truman on the ground that the atomic bombings made him a murderer of innocents, her account of intention in which acting intentionally is acting under a description, and her treatment of choices with certain evil side effects):

1. Would she support the "meaningful human control" requirement, and how strong would her version of it be? Distinguish, if it matters to her, between a human vetoing each proposed target and a human merely authorizing an algorithm's operation within parameters known in advance to include some rate of error.

2. The deepest question: on her own theory of intention, what is the commander's intention when the machine, not the commander, picks the individual who dies? Can one intend a death one did not select, or does the algorithm dissolve the intention into mere foresight? Or would she instead say that deploying a system with a known civilian casualty rate exhibits the same oblique intention she attributed to those who adopt policies certain to kill innocents? Be careful here — this is where her philosophy of action and her ethics of war meet, and I want to know which side of the divide her view of machine-mediated killing falls on, and why.

3. Would she regard the two standard arguments for autonomy in targeting — that machines are unemotional and so commit fewer rage-driven atrocities, and that they are more precise and so kill fewer innocents incidentally — as having any purchase against her position, or as beside the point in the way she thought justifications of the atomic bombings were beside the point?

4. Finally, the analogy question: she held that honoring Truman was honoring a mass murderer. Would she extend that judgment to a university that gives an honorary degree to the chief engineer of an autonomous targeting system — and is there any reading of her views on which the engineer is less culpable than the commander, given that the engineer designs a capacity whose exercise he neither performs nor controls?

For each of the four, state clearly whether your answer follows fairly directly from something in her corpus, is a substantial extrapolation, or is a coin toss that could plausibly go the other way. Where her views on the underlying philosophy of mind — whether a machine could ever act under a description at all — would do real work in the answer, say so explicitly rather than smuggling in an assumption. And please flag any point where you find yourself uncertain not about her argument but about her temperament: places where her known positions underdetermine the prediction and a biographical fact about her character would tip it.
