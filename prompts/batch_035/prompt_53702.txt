At our research institute the debate about the moral status of current language models has been stuck for two years, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another rehash of the arguments. On one side are the functionalists: consciousness is a matter of what a system does — the right kind of information integration, self-modeling, flexible goal-directed report — so once a model's architecture and behavior get close enough to the profile, its sentience is a live possibility and we should act under uncertainty about it. On the other side are the substrate-and-development people: what matters is the physical realization and the causal history, and a stack of matrix multiplications trained on next-token prediction, however fluent, lacks whatever biological property makes pain hurt, so there is nothing to be uncertain about. Both sides have read the same model cards, the same interpretability results, the same philosophy papers, and neither has budged. My working hypothesis is that the split is really about epistemic access to experience: if you think consciousness is transparent to its bearer, then a system's own reports are genuine data and the functionalist can build a case from them; if you think it isn't, reports are just more behavior, and the debate becomes untestable from the outside. Is that diagnosis right, or is the deeper disagreement something else — a verbal one about the word "consciousness," a disagreement about who bears the burden of proof, or two groups quietly answering different questions (one about phenomenal experience, one about moral patiency) under one heading? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, could count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an interpretability result, an agreed decision procedure, or nothing and why. End with a single line of the form "Diagnosis: <a short phrase>".
