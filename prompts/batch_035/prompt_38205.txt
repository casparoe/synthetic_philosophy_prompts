I'm building a mood-tracking app where people log activities and rate how pleasant each one was, 0-100, one single number. Testing it with friends and its doing something wierd: within one kind of activity they can rank things fine (this meal beat that meal), but the moment they have to compare a good meal against an afternoon of grinding hard math, or a run, or a long conversation, they stall out and basically just pick whichever activity they like more in some totally different sense — like the number stops meaning anything. My cofounder says we just need better UX. I think it's telling us something about pleasure itself, that "how pleasant" isn't actually one scale.

I did some reading and there seem to be roughly four candidate stories about what all these different things have in common as pleasures: (a) they all share one common feel, a kind of hedonic tone that shows up wherever pleasure does; (b) there is no common thing at all, just family resemblance between paradigm pleasures; (c) pleasure isn't a sensation but an attitude — being pleased about something — so the differences in feel are beside the point; (d) pleasure is one thing but only at the level of a determinable, like color, with sensory and intellectual pleasures as determinates of it — same category, different specific feels.

Rank these four by how well they'd explain the pattern in my data (fine within-kind comparisons, breakdown across kinds), and give each a score out of 10 for that. Then for your top pick: if I replace the single scale with two sliders — "how good did this feel in the moment" and "how glad are you that you did it" — what does that theory predict my users will do, and is the two-slider fix something the theory itself would endorse, or just a bandaid? Last thing, tell me whether your ranking changes if the question is "which story is actually true of pleasure" rather than "which explains my data".
