For about two years I've been running a small evening coding club at my local library — a dozen adults who taught themselves some Python and now like reading about the ideas behind computing rather than just the syntax. Next month I want to do a session on the Church-Turing thesis, and when I sat down to plan it I got tangled, so I'd like you to plan the session for me rather than explain the thesis to me. The audience has no maths beyond school and no formal logic at all; I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard.

Here is the tangle, from thinking about this on my own. The thesis is easy to mishear as a claim about machines — roughly, that Turing machines can do anything any computer could — and my group will arrive primed to hear it that way, since they know real hardware is Turing-complete in a loose sense and have all seen headlines about quantum computers supposedly going beyond it. But as best I can tell, the thesis is really a bridge claim: it says that an informal notion (a task that could be completed by someone following fixed instructions with unlimited time and paper) picks out exactly the same tasks as a precise formal definition. If that's right, the thesis is not a theorem and not a prediction about technology — it's closer to a definition of a concept, which makes it neither provable nor straightforwardly testable. That contrast between a limitation on machines and a definition of an idea seems to be the hinge of the whole session, and I suspect it's also where every misconception will live: that the thesis was proven, that the halting problem refutes it, that it rules out quantum computers, that physics could falsify it. I also want to be ready for the question someone is bound to ask — whether some physical process might compute something no Turing machine could — without letting it swallow the evening.

So this is what I'd like from you. Plan the order the pieces have to come in and tell me what depends on what, so the thesis lands as the closing move rather than the opening one. Pick the single carrying example that makes blindly following instructions concrete — I've been toying with having them act as a clerk who shuffles paper cards according to rules they're forbidden to interpret, but tell me if something else carries better and why. Map the misconceptions I listed onto the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and add any you think I've missed. Give me one question to end on whose answer would show they've grasped the thesis as a definition-like claim rather than a result about hardware. And tell me plainly what to refuse to cover in ninety minutes — I assume Gödel and the lambda calculus are out, and probably the physical side of the last question too, but I don't trust my own sense of where the line is, and I'd rather say out loud what I'm leaving for another evening.
