I write novels for a living, which means I've spent twenty years thinking about the gap between what a sentence says and what the person uttering it believes -- only I think about it in craft terms, not philosophy terms. Help me out: there's this thing philosophers call the Frege--Geach problem for expressivism, and I want it restated in my vocabulary, then I want to know what doesn't survive the translation.

My vocabulary, so you know what I'm fluent in: free indirect discourse (reporting a character's thought in the narrator's third-person voice, where register does the work of attribution), unreliable narration, the distinction between authorial voice and character voice, irony (saying something in a voice you don't endorse, where the meaning stays put so the irony can land), and the workshop rule about showing vs telling. That's it. No philosophy background beyond skimming stuff in airport bookstores.

As I understand it, the expressivist says a sentence like "Tormenting the cat is wrong" doesn't state a fact but expresses an attitude -- it's closer to hissing at the cat than to reporting the cat is on the mat. And the problem is that the very same sentence shows up inside "If tormenting the cat is wrong, getting your little brother to torment it is wrong too," and in denials and questions, where nobody is expressing anything, and yet it apparently has to mean what it meant standing alone or the whole argument falls apart.

Now translate the debate, both sides of it, into my terms. My instinct is that this is just the most basic problem of narrative voice. When I write, in free indirect discourse, "Murder was, after all, the only solution he had left," the sentence lives in my voice while the attitude belongs to the character, and the reader holds both at once without confusion. An ironic narrator says "war is glorious" in a register that endorses nothing, yet the words still mean war-is-glorious-enough for the irony to work. Sentences seem to keep their meaning no matter whose attitude, if anyone's, they carry -- that's practically the foundation of my trade. So is the Frege--Geach problem just my daily problem wearing a suit, or not?

Give me the residue report, honestly: which parts of the philosophers' problem map cleanly onto irony and free indirect discourse, which parts you had to stretch or fudge to make them fit, and whether anything in their debate has no counterpart in narrative craft at all. And then the question I actually care about: does my craft vocabulary contain an answer they're missing -- readers resolve irony effortlessly, after all, so maybe whatever mechanism lets that happen could handle embedded moral sentences too -- or does it just lack the resources to even see what they're worried about? I have a suspicion the philosophers could learn something from how audiences track voice, but I might be missing the point entirely. Dont hold back on that. Skip the name-dropping and citations too, I want the actual ideas, not a reading list.
