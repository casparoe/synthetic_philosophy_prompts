I'm a novelist three books into a career of straight literary fiction, and my current manuscript has drifted somewhere I never planned to go: a character who, once a season, steps into a device that records and destroys her while an identical woman steps out the other side. I've discovered I can't tell whether the scene I'm drafting is a horror scene, a grief scene, or — if Parfit is right — not a scene of loss at all. I know Parfit only secondhand, from a philosopher friend's summary over dinner, so I want you to do the philosophical heavy lifting while I supply the film.

Read Christopher Nolan's 2006 film The Prestige through the machinery of Parfit's discussion of the teletransporter in Reasons and Persons. The relevant pieces, as I half-understand them: the teleporter that records and destroys; the case where the machine breaks and the original survives alongside the copy; the claim that psychological continuity is what matters and that personal identity is not what matters; the denial that there is any further fact about whether the woman stepping out of my character's device is her; and Parfit's reported reaction to his own conclusion that his life had seemed like a glass tunnel he was trapped in. Work with the film's plot as common knowledge, don't quote either the film or Parfit, and ignore the Christopher Priest novel it's adapted from — I can't expect you to know it.

Three questions I especially want engaged, though don't feel confined to them. First, does the film's horror at Angier drowning in the tank every night presuppose exactly the further-fact view Parfit rejects — that the man in the water is the one who matters and the man taking the bow is an impostor — or does it show what Parfit's calm laboratory prose leaves out about how it feels from inside? Second, the film rigs Tesla's machine so that the original is always the one in the tank and the copy always survives, which builds in an original/copy asymmetry I gather Parfit thinks is superficial; is the film cheating his case, or is the rigged asymmetry doing dramatic work that a genuinely branching version couldn't? Third, Borden's secret — two men living one life — seems like the inverse of Parfit's fission cases, and I want to know what the film is doing with the fact that everyone, including the woman who loves him and the man who hangs him, deals with the twins as one person.

Please structure your response in five sections, in this order: "The translation" (about 250 words — the film's devices mapped onto Parfit's cases, flagging where the mapping bends); "The tank and the further fact" (about 400 words); "Borden's inversion" (about 250 words); "Where the film resists" (about 300 words — where a Parfitian reading strains or fails, and whether the film has earned its horror); and "What I take to my desk" (about 200 words — what the film dramatizes that the philosophical argument cannot, and what I should steal for my manuscript). I'd rather have an honest account of where the reading breaks than a tidy one where everything fits.
