My cofounder Mara and I are stuck on the wording of an insurance filing for our warehouse robotics product, and her philosophical track record is now the bottleneck. If the system is classified as merely matching worker instructions against trained phrasings, we have to constrain inputs to an enumerated command list, which guts the product; if it is classified as understanding instructions, we can let workers speak freely to it, but we take on a different liability story. Mara gets the deciding vote, and she is the most consistent person I know about which things understand and which do not, so I want to predict her verdict on our own system before the meeting.

Her verdicts so far, accumulated over years of arguments at whiteboards: a pocket calculator computing long division does not understand arithmetic. The 1997 chess machine that beat Kasparov does not understand chess, despite playing it better than any human who ever lived. Phrase-based statistical machine translation, with all its reordering tables and phrase pair probabilities, does not understand anything, because it is sophisticated mimicry of the output of people who do. A border collie in an exclusion trial — one it has never heard before, several known toys present, one novel object, and it correctly infers that the new word names the new thing and still uses the word correctly weeks later — does understand, at least that words refer to things. A toddler who calls a cow doggy and then, after correction, calls a horse doggy too, also understands: overextension is a live hypothesis under active test, which is understanding under construction. The man in the Chinese room does not understand Chinese, and here is the part that matters: she is unmoved by the systems reply. The room as a whole does not understand either, and no amount of additional rulebooks gets you there. And the current generation of text-only LLMs does not understand, however fluent and flexible they are. She endorses the stochastic parrots line: they are lossy mirrors of the understanding embodied in human-produced text, and a mirror of understanding is not understanding, however good the reflection.

Now my two cases. The first is our lab configuration: the same model class, but multimodal from the start of training, with cameras pointed at real scenes. It describes photographs it has never seen, flags when a caption does not fit its image, and when shown a Penrose-triangle-style impossible object and asked whether anything is wrong with the picture, it says so. But it never acts. The second is the deployed product: that model coupled end-to-end to an arm, receiving visual feedback after failed grasps, with its control policy updating on those failures. It also follows instructions no user has ever issued, like putting the red thing that is not a box next to the tallest cylinder.

What will she say about each configuration? I am less interested in the answers than in the rule she is running, because I suspect her seven verdicts fit more than one. One candidate rule says understanding requires commerce with the world through perception and action together; another says it requires training signal that reaches the world unmediated by human-produced artifacts like text or notation; a third says it requires revising one's own hypotheses from one's own failed actions. If you can reconstruct her implicit principle, tell me which rule fits her verdicts best and what each rule predicts about my two cases — in particular, whether the camera-only configuration counts as perceiving the world or only as consuming more human-curated data, and whether her rejection of the systems reply commits her to saying that in the deployed product only the coupled robot as a whole understands, not the model inside it. Give me your best guess with your reasons. And since our filing will invite follow-up questions, tell me which further case would best separate the candidate rules — for instance, a version trained only on synthetic images rendered in a simulator with no camera at all, or a giant precomputed lookup table that happened to produce the same behavior as our robot — and what each possible verdict on that case would reveal about the principle she has been applying all along.
