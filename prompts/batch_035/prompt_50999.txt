For the past two years I've co-moderated a seminar that brings together decision theorists and philosophers of economics, and every term it ends the same way: a standoff between our two factions over Aumann's agreement theorem. One faction — the strict Bayesians — holds that once two agents have common priors and common knowledge of each other's posteriors, agreement is forced; since reasonable people visibly do disagree about politics, markets, and science, they conclude that someone, somewhere, is being irrational or dishonest, and that persistent disagreement is a kind of scandal the theorem exposes. The other faction — call them the reasonable-disagreement camp — replies that the theorem's assumptions are doing all the work: real agents can't literally exchange their entire posteriors, priors can legitimately differ when agents start from different bodies of evidence, and bounded agents who must communicate with finite words and finite time can rationally fail to converge. The two factions have debated this for years, and it has become clear that a lot of the argument has been about words — about whether "rational disagreement" means disagreement among ideal agents or among realistic ones — rather than about substance.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement these two factions could actually sign after a properly run adversarial collaboration. Structure it in two parts. First, exactly five numbered claims that both camps could accept, each stated in one or two sentences, with no introduction before the list and no conclusion after the whole document — just the claims, then the table. Second, a disagreements table in which each row states one genuine disagreement, worded so that each side would endorse the description of its own position; identifies the crux beneath it (I suspect one crux is the common-prior assumption and what to say about agents whose priors differ because their initial evidence differed, and another is whether the idealized theorem's spirit survives once you acknowledge that exchanging posteriors is computationally intractable and agreement can only be expected at a coarse-grained level); and, where a concrete test exists — forecasting tournaments where superforecasters see one another's forecasts and still disagree, prediction markets where trade volume persists despite no-trade-style arguments, calibration studies of experts who read each other's work — records in advance what each side would predict the test would show. Where you find that part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Do not adjudicate who is right; the document belongs to both parties, not to you.

One final request: as the last row of the table, flag the single agreed claim from your numbered list that you are least confident one of the two factions would genuinely sign off on, and state how confident you are in that diagnosis — a short, honest note about where your drafting is on thin ice is worth more to me than a polished document that papers over it.
