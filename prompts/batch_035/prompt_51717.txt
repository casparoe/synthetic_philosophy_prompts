I hold a qualified version of the Lucas–Penrose position: that Gödel's incompleteness theorems reveal a principled gap between human mathematical cognition and mechanism. As I hold it, the core claim is that for any formal system whose axioms a competent mathematician can recognize as sound, the mathematician can in principle see the truth of that system's Gödel sentence, which no machine implementing that system can derive; and that iterating this observation shows our mathematical understanding is not fully capturable by any single formal system. I want to stress-test the position honestly rather than have it defended.

Tell me what would count against it. Consider challenges along the following lines, but treat the list as provisional rather than exhaustive: the epistemic demand that we know our own consistency or soundness, which we have no independent way of establishing, so that the argument may prove only that if we are sound then we are not a machine — a weaker conclusion than the one advertised; the indeterminacy of which machine we are, which blocks the diagonal step until the machine is fixed, at which point the human advantage may merely shift to the next system up without ever yielding a stable gap; and the fact that machines already produce proofs — computer-assisted proofs, formal verification, and increasingly machine-generated arguments that survive expert review — which the position needs to classify as either awkward, irrelevant by its own lights, or genuinely threatening. For each defeater you identify, rank it from fatal through serious to merely awkward; say which of them a committed defender would concede as counting against the position rather than explain away; and be explicit about where you think the position has been insulated by successive qualifications — the retreat from machines cannot do mathematics, to machines cannot do mathematics in the manner Gödel describes, to something about qualitative understanding that no output could disconfirm — and whether insulation of that kind is disqualifying for a view that presents itself as an a priori argument rather than an empirical thesis.

One last thing: state what the position predicts that its rivals do not. Concretely, which observable developments — a system deriving, unprompted, the Gödel sentence of the very architecture it runs on; a machine-generated proof of a major open conjecture that no human can survey but that formal verification certifies; or a persistent plateau in machine mathematical creativity at some fixed expressive level — would genuinely move the position, in either direction, and which of these, in your judgment, are actually live possibilities rather than comfortable hypotheticals for one side or the other.
