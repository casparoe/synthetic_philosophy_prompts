I've never studied philosophy, but for the past year I've been working on an essay about whether art made by bad people — or art with bad morals baked into it — can still be good art. It grew out of arguments with friends about particular films and musicians, and out of my own discomfort at how those arguments always ended with someone repeating the slogan about separating the art from the artist, as though that settled something. Since I don't know the academic literature, I'd be grateful for comments from someone who does. I'm pasting the essay below in slightly condensed form.

The essay's core, as it stands:

People run together at least three different moral complaints about a work: complaints about what the work depicts or celebrates, complaints about how it was made, and complaints about the maker's unrelated conduct. A film that glamorizes cruelty, a photograph whose production involved cruelty, and a symphony by someone who was privately cruel are three morally different situations, and the "separate the art from the artist" slogan does different, sometimes illegitimate, work in each — in the first case it can hide the fact that the work is an argument for something bad; in the third it can be perfectly reasonable.

But that's the setup, not my real claim. My real claim is that the question "can immoral art be good art" is ill-posed because "good art" means two things that can come apart. A work can be good the way a well-made weapon is good: superb craft, achieving exactly what it sets out to achieve. And it can be good the way a friendship is good: something worth having in your life, good *for* you. The first is a fact about the artifact; the second is at least partly a fact about the experience the artifact affords, and experiences can be bad for you even when they are perfectly engineered — excellent junk food. So immoral art can be good in the artifact sense while failing in the nourishment sense, and most of the shouting matches happen between people holding the word "good" in different hands.

The final section pushes a step further: the experience-relevant goodness can't be read off the work's content either. A work with noble content can afford an ignoble experience — self-congratulation, sentimentality, the pleasure of feeling superior to the villains it gives you. So the familiar separation between work and maker conceals a second one, between what a work says and what it does to the person taking it in, and our moral assessment of a work tracks the second more than we admit.

I'd like comments on three things specifically. First, does the distinction between artifact-goodness and experience-goodness actually do any work, or does it collapse into the ordinary difference between aesthetic and moral value that everyone already accepts? If philosophers already draw my line under other names, tell me what the standard positions in this debate look like — I mean the shape of the views and the standard objections to them, not references, which I'll track down myself later. Second, what is the strongest objection to the weapon analogy, and does the essay as sketched have an answer to it? Third, is the content-versus-experience separation a genuine distinction, or does it just relocate the problem — since the only evidence we have for what an experience affords is, in the end, the work's content plus testimony about the audience? Finally, be blunt about whether the whole thing is worth writing: if this is just the standard position restated in home-made vocabulary, I'd rather know now.
