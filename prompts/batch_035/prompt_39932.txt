I hold a position about metaphysical grounding that I want stress-tested: not the standard deflationary line that grounding talk can be paraphrased away, but a more specific view with three parts. First, the grounding relation itself is fine and indispensable: when we say that the fact that a chess game has been won is grounded in facts about the positions of the pieces and the rules of the game, we are tracking a genuine explanatory relation, not a metaphor or a stylistic variant of causation or entailment. Second — and this is the heterodox part — the notion of an absolutely fundamental fact is a confusion. There is no level of facts that is ungrounded simpliciter; fundamentality is always fundamentality relative to a body of explanatory interests, in something like the way many philosophers now accept that confirmation, or even chance, is interest-relative. What physics calls fundamental — fields, say — is fundamental relative to the interests of physical explanation; a chess-theoretic fact can be fundamental relative to the interests of chess explanation; and neither fundamentality is more fundamental in an absolute sense, because there is no such sense to have. Third, chains of ground therefore need not terminate: a world with no absolutely fundamental level is not thereby an incomplete or defective world, any more than a world with no first moment is temporally incomplete.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them to include the ones that would worry a sympathizer rather than only the ones a partisan of absolute fundamentality would raise — for instance, whatever force there is in the thought that grounding was supposed to help explain why anything exists at all, where my view seems to leave that question either unanswered or answered in a circle. For each objection, tell me which of my three commitments it really targets, and which commitment, if I dropped it, would cost the view the least overall. Is the interest-relativity clause doing real work, or have I qualified the view into something that no possible discovery or argument could count against — and if so, is that disqualifying for a metaphysical thesis, or exactly what we should expect of one? Concretely, what should I say to a defender of absolute fundamentality who argues that unless some facts are fundamental absolutely, nothing explains why the different interest-relative fundamentality structures dovetail as neatly as they do — that the smooth meshing of chemistry-relative structure with physics-relative structure would be a coincidence unless there were an absolute level underneath both? Play it straight: if part of my view is simply wrong, say which part and why.
