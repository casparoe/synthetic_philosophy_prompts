I serve on the board of my neighborhood association in a mid-size city that has grown fast over the past decade, and I've been asked to draft our positions document for an upcoming candidates' forum. My friends tell me my views on housing are all over the map — one week I'm signing a regional letter demanding that the city permit far more housing, the next I'm siding with the association against a mid-rise proposal two blocks from my house — and before I put anything on paper I want an honest consistency check. So here is the full set of positions I'd like to defend, as I actually hold them: (1) everyone has a right to live in the region where their life is anchored — where their work, family, language, and caring obligations are — and not merely wherever housing happens to be cheap; (2) land-use decisions should be made at the most local level that is feasible, and when the city council or the state legislature overrides a neighborhood plan, something has gone wrong democratically even when the override produces housing I want; (3) rent control is a mistake, because it suppresses new supply and misallocates the housing that exists; (4) new developments above a certain size should be required to include a substantial share of below-market units; (5) a family's equity in its home is a morally serious stake — for most households it is the entire retirement — and it is unfair to ask one cohort of owners to absorb, through falling home values, the cost of a regional problem they did not create; (6) when a neighborhood becomes desirable, its long-time residents have a real claim to remain there, justifying right-to-remain programs, legacy preferences, and where necessary limits on redeveloping occupied buildings; and (7) owning land carries a strong right to develop it as the owner sees fit, constrained only by direct harms to neighbors such as noise, shadows, or toxins — not by neighbors' aesthetic preferences or their worries about parking.

What I want from you is not a lecture on housing policy and not a survey of what planners or philosophers think — I want an audit of this specific set. Go through the commitments pair by pair (and in combinations, where it takes three to make trouble) and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how badly. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest consistent fix would be and what following through on that fix would cost me — in policy terms, in fairness to particular people, or in whatever currency the conflict is actually denominated in. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as one undifferentiated blob, and be specific about which commitment has to give in each case. I honestly don't know where the worst problem is, so don't soft-pedal anything to spare me; but equally, don't manufacture contradictions — if two of my positions can be held together once they're stated carefully, tell me that too and show me the careful statement.

Once you've worked through the analysis in prose, close with a summary table so I can see the whole audit at a glance. Use exactly these columns: "Commitments involved" (the numbers from my list), "Real or merely apparent" (your verdict on whether it's a genuine tension), "Severity" (high / moderate / low), "Cheapest fix" (which commitment gives, and how), and "Cost of the fix" (what I lose or owe by making it). One row per tension you identify, ordered from most to least severe, and a final row for anything in my set that you judge fully consistent with the rest. After the table, add two or three sentences telling me which single revision would clean up the most, in case I decide I'd rather reorganize the whole package than patch it.
