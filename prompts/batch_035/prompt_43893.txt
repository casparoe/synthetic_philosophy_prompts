I teach a graduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I am writing the take-home final. I want a single essay question on Bernard Williams's integrity objection, but I have a specific problem with how the topic is usually examined, and the question should be built around that problem.

Students arrive having memorized the standard summary: utilitarianism, Williams says, alienates agents from their ground projects and treats people as channels through which the utility of the world merely flows; and there is a famous remark about a man who rescues his drowning wife when he could equally have rescued a stranger, and who, if he reaches that decision only by way of an impartial calculation that her rescue is the marginally better outcome, has had one thought too many. Students recite the two complaints together and treat them as a single objection wearing two hats. What I want to test is whether they can see that the complaints are distinct, that they come apart under pressure, and that the more famous of the two is arguably the weaker.

The question you write must therefore force the following. First, it must make the candidate say precisely what work the notion of integrity is doing, and then confront the fact that the familiar version of the objection — the one running through the thought that agents need their projects to have a tolerable life at all — is exactly the version that an indirect or two-level consequentialism can absorb, by building project-loyalty and partial concern into the dispositions that in fact maximize. Second, it must make the candidate say whether the one-thought-too-many complaint is the same objection in different clothes or a distinct complaint about the order and standing of deliberation rather than the content of outcomes; and it must push them to ask whether the complaint is literally about a number of thoughts, or instead about which kinds of reasons need no impartial backing to justify acting on them. Third, it must require the candidate to construct an original case — not the drowning wife, not Jim and the Indians — in which one of the two objections applies cleanly and the other does not, and to use that case to adjudicate whether whatever is deepest in Williams survives once the indirect-consequentialist rescue is granted in full.

Some constraints. The question should be answerable in about ninety minutes by a strong student. It must not be answerable by summarizing the published dialectic: if a candidate could pass by reciting the exchange with J. J. C. Smart and appending a paragraph of throat-clearing about agent-relativity, the question has failed. And nothing in the question or the expected answer should trade on close reading of particular passages or on recalling who wrote what and when — it should test grasp of the structure of the objections, not textual history.

Please deliver three things. (1) The essay question itself, in the voice I would hand to the students. (2) A model answer of about 600 words that reads as an argument taking a stand — specifically on whether the two complaints are ultimately one — rather than as a survey of positions. (3) A marking scheme out of 20 marks, organized around four must-hit moves, with the marks allocated to each, and under each a single sentence naming the mistake that most often costs candidates those marks — for instance, the mistake of answering the alienation complaint by invoking the distinction between decision procedures and criteria of rightness without showing why Williams might be entitled to doubt that the distinction does any real work for the utilitarian here.
