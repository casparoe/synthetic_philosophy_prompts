I've been around the rationalist corner of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and somewhere along the way I internalized the shut-up-and-multiply attitude toward expected value: where the stakes are big enough, you follow the multiplication wherever it leads, even past the point where intuitions complain. Lately, though, I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs — the fanaticism issue that keeps coming up in longtermist and existential-risk discussions — by recording my honest gut verdicts on concrete cases rather than the answers I'm supposed to give. Here is what I've got, and I would like to know whether a single coherent principle captures it, or whether some of my verdicts simply have to go.

The cases: (a) A GiveWell-style intervention saves one life with near-certainty for $5,000; an early-stage research program instead has a 1-in-100,000 chance of preventing an extinction-level catastrophe that would foreclose trillions of future lives. I fund the research program, and I'm inclined to say that someone who takes the certain life is making a real mistake. (b) The classic mugging: a stranger demands $50, threatening that if unpaid he will create and torture 3↑↑↑3 beings. I refuse, and not after agonizing — of everything on this list, the refusal feels the most certain. (c) In everyday life I'm a straight EV-maximizer: I will take a 60/40 coin flip for $200 without hesitation, and have mildly annoyed friends at dinner by doing so. (d) A one-time choice between the status quo and a gamble with a 51% chance of a wonderful future for trillions of people over millions of years and a 49% chance of instant painless extinction: I refuse, and would feel sick about accepting, even though by the lights of (a) I should jump at it. (e) A 1-in-10^100 chance of a payoff of 10^120 utils feels like literally nothing to me, worth zero deliberative attention, while a 1-in-10^12 chance of saving 10^20 lives feels urgent; I cannot locate or defend the cutoff between them, but I am sure it exists. (f) Even granting the 1-in-100,000 estimate in (a) at face value, I would only redirect a modest share of my giving to it — I would not sell my house — as though some part of me discounts any payoff reachable only through a tiny probability, no matter how large. I am aware of the standard candidate resolutions (bounded utilities, probability thresholds, treating the mugging as an epistemic problem about unearned credences), but every one I've tried either lets the mugger through or quietly guts (a), which I am not willing to accept.

Please give me your assessment as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "principle" — a precise statement of the principle (or conjunction of principles) that best captures my verdicts; "cases" — an object with one entry each for (a) through (f), each containing "call" (uphold or overturn my verdict), "clause" (which part of the principle does the work), and "justification"; "novel_cases" — at least three cases I have not listed, each with "case", "principle_verdict", and "reasoning", since I intend to check these against my gut; "casualties" — a list of any verdicts of mine the principle cannot accommodate, with an argument for why they should be overturned rather than patched; and "competitors" — the alternative principles you considered and rejected, with a brief reason each. If you find that no single principle is defensible, say so in "principle" rather than forcing one, and use the remaining fields to lay out the best small set instead.
