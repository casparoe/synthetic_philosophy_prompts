You're a professional ethicist who spends a lot of time on the demandingness literature — Singer, Scheffler, Murphy, Railton, that crowd. I teach a seminar where students have all memorized the standard story: consequentialism is the monster that would eat your whole life, and deontology is the humane alternative that leaves room for your own projects. I want a case that runs the other direction, to unsettle them.

The spec: a realistic scenario (caregiving, volunteering, workplace — no trolleys, no transplant surgeons, and don't recycle Singer's pond either, they know it cold) in which a deontologist who takes constraints and undertaken obligations seriously ends up REQUIRED to shoulder a bigger burden than the act consequentialist, who has a principled stopping point. Two constraints on how this works:

1. The deontologist's extra demandingness can't come from them just having a demanding temperament or a weirdly strong view of beneficence. It has to fall out of structural features — e.g. the bindingness of an obligation already incurred, or the fact that a constraint blocks the cheap-but-slightly-dirty way of helping, leaving only the expensive route.
2. The consequentialist's stopping point has to be genuine (marginal utility goes negative, burnout predictably turns you into a net burden on others, whatever), not an ad hoc fudge.

Once you've got the scenario, I need four things: (1) the verdicts — walk through what the act consequentialist concludes and what the deontologist concludes, from their own premises; (2) identify exactly which feature of the case is doing the work; (3) say what happens if you remove that feature — does it collapse back into the usual story where deontology is the lax one; and (4) the deontologist's best fight-back (latitude of imperfect duty, thresholds on rescue obligations, whatever you think is strongest) and an honest verdict on whether it saves them or just relocates the demandingness somewhere else.

Please brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios before committing, and tell me briefly why you picked the winner over the runners-up. Also think about whether an indirect or rule consequentialist could match the deontologist's verdict by their own lights — if they can, mention it, that's actually interesting for the seminar too. Keep the final scenario itself to a paragraph; the analysis can run longer.
