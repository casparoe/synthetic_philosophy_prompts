I am a final-year student in my school's International Baccalaureate diploma programme, where I take Theory of Knowledge, and our teacher has asked me to design one practice essay question for the revision session I run for the first-year students before their mock exam. The topic she assigned me is the regress problem in epistemology: when you give a reason for a belief, that reason itself seems to need a reason, and the three standard responses — stopping at basic beliefs (foundationalism), allowing the reasons to loop in a circle (coherentism), or going on forever (infinitism) — are precisely the labels our students can recite without understanding anything behind them. So the question must be unanswerable by reciting those labels. It should instead force them to argue about what makes a regress vicious in the first place, and I have a suspicion I would like the question to build on: that the real issue is not the length of the chain but whether each extra link merely transmits justification that was already present or somehow generates new justification, since a chain that only transmits never explains why anything is justified at all, while a chain that generates seems to get something from nothing.

Concretely, I would like you to write one essay question that students answer in 60 minutes, then a model answer of about 600 words, and then a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically miss each one. The question should require students to do at least the following: distinguish a regress of giving reasons from a regress of having justification; explain what the infinitist gains by insisting the chain never loops back on itself, and what that buys over the coherentist's circle, whose members can all be perfectly consistent with one another while none of them is made true by anything outside the circle; and confront foundationalism's difficulty that its basic beliefs must be either too thin to support anything built on them or too strong to be undisputed. The model answer should cite no texts and name no authors — our students cannot check references, and our teacher wants positions described generically, as "the infinitist" or "the coherentist" — and it should mark the difference between an A answer and a B answer by naming the specific move only the A answer makes, so that a student who writes a polished but purely descriptive essay cannot reach the top band.

Finally, since this material will actually be used to grade younger students, please tell me at the end how confident you are in the marking scheme's calibration, and single out the one point you are least sure of — whether that is whether 60 minutes is enough for what the question demands, whether the transmission-versus-generation framing is fair to all three positions, or something else entirely — so that I can raise it with my teacher before the session rather than discover it mid-exam.
