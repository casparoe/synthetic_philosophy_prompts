I am in my final year of sixth form, working on an extended project on the question of whether people ever really get what they deserve. My starting point was an argument with my brother: he maintains that things have a way of balancing out in the long run, while I suspect that what we call desert is something we project onto luck after the fact. My supervisor suggested that before I read anyone's theory of desert, I should first work out what I would be asking of one, and I have come round to the view that she is right. So what I would like from you is the specification itself: a list of the requirements any adequate theory of moral desert must meet, with the case or consideration behind each. For instance, what does such a theory owe me on cases like the driver who is over the limit but is stopped before anything happens, and his exact double who, through no difference in choice or character, kills a child; or the student whose grade turns on which topics happened to come up; or the person whose admirable qualities plainly trace to an upbringing they did nothing to earn? Which distinctions must the theory respect — between deserving an outcome and deserving treatment because of it, between desert and mere entitlement, between what one deserves and what it would nonetheless be good to give one — and what is lost when a theory collapses any of these?

I would also like you to mark which of your requirements are non-negotiable and which are really the preferences of one camp rather than shared ground. Most importantly, tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: my suspicion is that requiring desert to track only what is under a person's control, while also requiring it to make sense of our ordinary practices of praise, blame and punishment, cannot both be done, and if the list contains a hidden contradiction I want that stated plainly rather than smoothed over — that may end up being the most interesting thing in my project. Finally, and only briefly, hold three candidates up against your list: karma as traditionally understood, on which desert operates through a cosmic mechanism and possibly across lives; ordinary retributivism about punishment; and the view associated with Rawls that nobody deserves their starting place, so that much talk of desert is respectable cover for luck. I care far more about the criteria than about the scorecard, since my supervisor will make me defend the criteria line by line. Please write in British English, and pitch it so that a strong sixth-former can follow — technical terms are welcome, provided each is defined the first time it appears.
