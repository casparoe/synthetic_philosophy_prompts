Our local U3A has a little philosophy circle that meets in the back room of a pub, and somehow I've let them talk me into running a session on care ethics, because I kept going on about it after we did a fortnight on Kant. The audience is maybe a dozen retirees and one suspicious engineer: bright, articulate, no formal philosophy at all, and each of them arriving with a settled opinion that ethics means either the Ten Commandments or trolley problems. I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard, and I'd rather not lecture.

Don't write me the explanation itself — I need a plan for it. What has to be established first before care ethics can even register as an ethical theory rather than a mood (I suspect the dependency runs something like: partiality is not a bug but a fact about moral life, THEN care as a practice with standards you can fail at, and only then the theory as a rival to impartialist frameworks — but correct me if I've got the order wrong). Which single example should carry the whole thing and why — I'm wary of mother-and-child, it's too easily heard as instinct rather than ethics, and I half want something like a stretched friendship or a care worker with an impossible caseload instead, but you tell me what the example must contain and what it must lack. And the misconceptions: I'm guessing the big three are "care ethics is just being nice," "it's really an argument that women are naturally more moral," and "it forbids you from ever treating people impartially, so it can't handle the lifeboat" — but I want to know where exactly in the sequence each one should be headed off, not just listed at the end. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level (I'm guessing the debates about whether care ethics is a full moral theory or just a corrective, but maybe something else bites harder), and give me one question I can put to the room that would show me they've actually got it rather than just nodded along. If it helps to know: I think in scenes and characters, so I'm quite capable of making the whole session warm and evocative and intellectually empty, and that's exactly what I'm trying to avoid.

One boring request: whatever you write, please do it in British English — organise, recognise, the lot. My copyeditor has opinions and I've agreed to honour them even outside of manuscripts.
