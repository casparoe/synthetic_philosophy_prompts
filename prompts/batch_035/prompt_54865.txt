I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I run a monthly lunch-and-learn series at my company where I present ideas from philosophy and decision theory to colleagues. The audience is roughly twenty-five engineers: comfortable with probability, linear algebra, and the no-arbitrage arguments they've seen in finance contexts, but with no formal logic or epistemology, and quick to dismiss anything that smells of overclaiming. Next session I'm covering Dutch book arguments, and I want the treatment to concentrate on a layer that popular summaries skip: the difference between the synchronic argument (the axioms of probability for degrees of belief at one time) and the diachronic argument (for updating by conditioning), together with the fact that the conclusion is about vulnerability to a bookie rather than about actually losing money.

I'm asking for a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Specifically:

1. The order of the pieces, with your reasoning about what depends on what. My instinct is to open with the betting setup — an agent who posts quotients and stands ready to take either side of a bet on any proposition at any stake — and I suspect the sequencing inside that setup matters: whether to build the first two- or three-proposition book before saying anything general, whether the diachronic case should come before or after the audience can already construct a synchronic book themselves, and whether the "vulnerability, not loss" distinction has to be in place before any book is shown at all.

2. One carrying example that the whole session runs on. It needs small numbers the audience can verify in their heads, a bookie whose profit is fixed in advance regardless of how the propositions resolve, and a scenario that is not casino gambling, which this audience will file under "interesting, doesn't apply to me." I'd like your judgment on what the example must contain and what it must avoid.

3. The misconceptions to expect, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate: that the argument shows you will lose money for sure; that it only applies to people who gamble; that the bookie needs to know which propositions are true; that the whole thing is just no-arbitrage pricing carried over from finance, so it constrains betting behavior but says nothing about belief; that you can defeat the argument simply by refusing to bet; and, on the diachronic side, that any departure from conditioning, however small or evidence-driven, leaves you bookable. If there are others engineers specifically are prone to, name them.

4. A closing question I can ask whose correct answer would demonstrate that the audience has grasped the vulnerability-versus-loss distinction and not merely enjoyed the arithmetic.

5. What I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the deeper decision-theoretic programs that try to justify the betting setup itself, and the literature on whether the argument is normative or merely pragmatic, but tell me if something else must be cut, and how I should flag the cut so the audience knows it's a boundary rather than the edge of the subject.
