I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a long-form feature on the return of Epicurus in unexpected places — hospice movements borrowing his consolation that death is nothing to us, longevity scientists who dismiss that consolation as adaptive self-deception, therapists teaching "death acceptance" as a clinical intervention. As a companion piece, my magazine commissions a short story for each feature, and this one is yours.

Write me a short story, roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, that dramatizes the Epicurean argument about death rather than merely gesturing at it. The core claim to take seriously: death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because when death is present the person is not, and a harm requires a subject to be harmed — no one is ever around to experience being dead. I want the story to give this argument its strongest form, not a strawman. In particular, two companion ideas deserve real dramatic weight. First, the symmetry argument going back to Lucretius: the eternity of nonexistence before our birth troubles no one, so why should the identical nonexistence after death? Second, the standard rejoinder, associated with Thomas Nagel: death can be bad for a person without being experienced by her, the way a betrayal behind your back is bad for you — it deprives the person who exists of goods she would otherwise have had. A story that never lets the deprivation objection make its case, or that never lets the Epicurean make hers, will not serve.

Here is the premise I'd like you to work from, though you're free to modify its surface details. A woman in her sixties survived a cardiac arrest years ago — several minutes of clinical death, no experiences, no light, nothing — and the argument that used to comfort her as abstraction has since become something more like a settled disposition. She genuinely does not fear death, and this frightens her adult daughter, who reads the serenity as denial, or as a kind of pre-bereavement abandonment. The two of them spend a week together, and the story's tension should live in the gap between the philosophical soundness of the mother's position and the emotional asymmetry of the people around her: if death is not bad for the dead, why is the prospect of it so bad for the living, and does the mother owe her daughter a fear she cannot honestly perform? Somewhere in the story, I'd like a scene where the symmetry argument is put to actual use — perhaps the daughter, researching, comes across the prenatal-nonexistence point and tries to wield it, or to break it.

Two constraints. The ending must not cheat: no last-minute revelation that death was bad all along, and no serene dodge in which everyone simply learns to accept. Let the reader be left where the philosophy actually leaves us — the disagreement between "no subject, no harm" and "harm can be unexperienced" remaining live, but now felt from the inside of a particular relationship. And the mother's Epicureanism should cost her something: a position that convenient should have an edge. When you're done, append a brief author's note — a page at most — explaining where you let the philosophy bend the characters and where you let the characters bend the philosophy, and which of the two arguments you privately think the story favored, since I'd like to quote from it in the feature's introduction.
