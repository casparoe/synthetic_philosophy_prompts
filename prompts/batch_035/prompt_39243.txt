My ethics prof keeps explaining supererogation with an exam analogy: duties are the required questions, and going above and beyond the call of duty is extra credit. you either answer the required questions or you fail (that's blame), the extra credit is optional — skipping it does nothing to your grade — but doing it earns praise, and there's a cap on how much it can count toward the total. A friend of mine who's a died-in-the-wool consequentialist uses the analogy to argue her own view can't allow supererogation at all: consequentialism grades on the raw total, not pass/fail, so "optional but praiseworthy" looks incoherent on it — whatever maximizes the total just IS what the exam demands, there's no pass mark to exceed.

Can you audit the whole thing? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the exam's total points, the grader, the pass/fail line, partial credit — and then where the mapping breaks. The obvious suspects: exams have a ceiling (you can't be asked for more than 100%, so "extra" has a natural start), but does morality have anything like that, or is the scale open-ended, in which case what could "extra" even mean? Is blame really like failing a required question, or can you be blameworthy while passing? Extra credit problems are usually cheap bonus trivia, whereas heroic acts are costly and risky — does the analogy quietly import effortlessness? And then the part I actually care about: does my consequentialist friend's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are out in the open, or was she only getting it because the analogy fixes a pass mark that her view might not need?

Also, the analogy gets strained on easy rescues: if wading in to save a kid from a shallow pond is a required question rather than extra credit (which is what one reading of the pond case says), the extra credit section shrinks down to just the risky, costly stuff — running into the burning building. Is that actually what we mean by a hero, or did the analogy just quietly define heroism for us? And if the required section really does expand that far, is there any room left for the optional-but-praiseworthy at all, or does supererogation collapse into either duty or mere preference? If you think there's a better analogy that does the work this one was doing without importing a fixed ceiling and an external grader, I'd want to hear it.
