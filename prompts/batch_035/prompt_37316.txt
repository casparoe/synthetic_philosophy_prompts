In a political philosophy seminar we've been running into the same analogy from both directions, and I'd like a proper audit of it. The nationalists among us say the nation is like an extended family: you don't choose your compatriots any more than you choose your siblings, shared history and mutual dependence generate associative obligations in the same way shared upbringing does, and just as it isn't arbitrary or suspect to spend your resources on your own children rather than strangers' children, it isn't arbitrary for a state to privilege its own citizens — so compatriots really do have special claims on one another that foreigners lack. The cosmopolitans reply with the same analogy at a larger scale: humanity is one family, the "family of man"; if familial ties generate obligations, and all humans are related by descent from common ancestors and by the interdependence of a global economy, then the special claims of compatriots are just nepotism writ large, morally on a par with a parent refusing to feed a neighbour's starving child.

I'd like you to assess how far each analogy holds. Please give a correspondence table for each — what is supposed to play the roles of the parent, the sibling, the shared household, the unchosen relation, the ongoing reciprocal care — and then identify where each mapping breaks. I'm particularly interested in the points that cut differently depending on the direction: for instance, family membership is essentially unacquirable and unlosable while citizenship can be naturalised into and revoked; family obligations seem to survive the total collapse of mutual benefit while the nationalist case leans heavily on ongoing cooperation; and the cosmopolitan version has to explain why descent from common ancestors, which is common to all humans, generates anything like the intensity of sibling obligations. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion (that compatriots do, or do not, have special claims) from the ones that don't. Then tell me whether either side's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether each analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion all along. Finally, if there is a better analogy for the relation between compatriots — teammates on a cooperative scheme, fellow members of a club, co-signatories of a social contract, co-beneficiaries of a shared coercive institution — say what it is and what conclusion, if any, it supports.

Please answer in British English.
