So, I'm a novelist — three books out, midlist, mostly literary fiction with the occasional speculative detour — and for the last year I've been writing a novel where the narrator is gradually revealed to be something like the god of the world she's describing: she can't intervene without wrecking the story, or so she keeps insisting. The research rabbit hole led me into philosophy of religion, specifically the problem of evil, and it turns out philosophers have been having a version of the argument I've been having with my editor: why does the plot need all this suffering in it? I know the broad moves well enough to follow the encyclopedia version — the free will defense, the soul-making theodicy, Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds thing, the distinction between the logical problem and the evidential one — but not much past that.

Here's what I want. Restate the main positions on the problem of evil in the vocabulary I actually work in: character arcs, stakes, what a story needs versus what it merely contains, the difference between an obstacle that develops a character and one that just happens to them, why readers accept some suffering as earning the ending and reject other suffering as gratuitous, what an author is entitled to do to the people she made. Translate them one by one, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report, because I suspect that's where my book lives: what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit into narrative terms, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Two things I especially want flagged. First, my characters aren't conscious — every novelist knows the suffering is staged, whereas the theodicist has to claim it isn't — and I can't tell whether that's a distortion of the philosophy or a hole in it that my vocabulary can't even state. Second, the free will defense's line between free creatures and puppets: from where I sit, a character feels free because I wrote her to feel free, and I genuinely don't know if that's a bad translation of the defense or a counterexample to it.

Then tell me straight: does the residue point at something narrative vocabulary can't say, or at something in the philosophical debate that's less substantive than it looks? Concretely, I want to know whether my narrator-god's excuse — the story needs it, suffering is what makes the arc mean anything, take out the pain and you take out the person — is a real philosophical position dressed up in workshop language, or a sophisticated-sounding dodge that the other characters should be able to call her on by the third act. My editor thinks the second. She might be right, she usually is, but I'd like the argument laid out properly before I decide who gets the last line.
