I am a retired physicist — condensed matter theory, mostly, though I kept a careful amateur's eye on gravitation — and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of time. I have constructed an argument about the grandfather paradox that I cannot get out of my head, because if it works, it proves vastly more than I want it to. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. Please write your answer in British English.

The background I accept: general relativity admits spacetimes containing closed timelike curves, and a common physicist's response is the self-consistency principle associated with Novikov — only globally self-consistent histories are realised, so a traveller who arrives in, say, 1920 and attempts to prevent his grandfather's birth will fail, for some ordinary-seeming reason or other. My argument starts there and runs as follows.

(1) If past-directed time travel is possible, a traveller who arrives in 1920 can genuinely act there — shoot at his grandfather, bribe the midwife, tamper with the parish records.
(2) Any such attempt fails: the fact of the grandfather's birth is fixed, and in a self-consistent history the attempt fails however determined, well-equipped and repeatedly re-supplied the traveller is.
(3) If failure were left to ordinary chance, a sufficiently persistent traveller would eventually succeed; so something must guarantee failure — a constraint that reaches back from the requirement of global consistency and shapes events (a jammed trigger, a missed train, an opportune change of heart) precisely at the points where the history would otherwise become inconsistent.
(4) Such a constraint settles outcomes independently of the agent's deliberation and effort: the traveller cannot bring about what he most wants to bring about, however sound his plan and however hard he tries.
(5) Now consider a native of 1920. Her past — the state of the world in 1850 — is exactly as fixed as the traveller's target fact, and by parity of reasoning something must ensure she does not alter it. Whatever applies to the traveller applies to her.
(6) So either the consistency constraint singles out time travellers, stripping them of an agency that ordinary agents retain — in which case stepping into the machine is stepping into a world where one's projects are hostage to global bookkeeping — or the constraint falls on everyone alike, and no agent ever acts freely.
(7) Both horns are unacceptable: the second is fatalism about everything, and the first makes a machine built from physics deliver a kind of self-administered enchantment.
(8) Therefore past-directed time travel is impossible, whatever the spacetime geometry permits.

I dislike the conclusion, and I also dislike the route: an argument of this shape would apparently prove fatalism outright if time travel were never invented, which suggests something has gone wrong. The steps I would most like to keep are (1) and (2); I would even keep (4) if I could. But every time I try to deny (3), I picture the persistent traveller with a crate of rifles and a loyal assistant, and I find myself insisting that eventually he would succeed; and every time I try to deny (5), I find myself saying that the native's past is somehow less fixed than the traveller's, which sounds absurd on its face.

Tell me which premise or step to give up, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If the argument equivocates between two readings of some step, identify precisely where the meaning shifts. And please address the "proves too much" character directly: on the answer you favour, is the persistent traveller's agency genuinely intact, and if so, what exactly stops him succeeding on the ninth attempt — and why does that same thing not stop me from acting freely tomorrow?
