I teach an upper-level graduate seminar that straddles philosophy of mind and machine-learning interpretability, and I'm setting the take-home final. The unit the students found hardest was our week on mechanistic anomaly detection in neural networks, and specifically the lurking question of mechanism identity. To set this up for you: mechanistic anomaly detection is a proposed safety technique in which, rather than only flagging inputs as out-of-distribution, one trains a monitor on the network's internal activations during normal operation and flags deployment-time inputs on which the network appears to produce its answer via a different mechanism than it used on benign data — for instance, a model that ordinarily performs arithmetic through something like a genuine adding circuit suddenly producing correct answers via memorised lookup, or a backdoored model switching to a trigger-activated circuit. The technique plainly presupposes an account of when two forward passes use the same mechanism, but the interpretability literature mostly leaves that notion implicit. In seminar we laid out three candidate individuation criteria: (a) an overlap criterion, on which two passes use the same mechanism to the extent that they engage the same internal components (but two genuinely different algorithms can share components, and one algorithm can be spread differently across them); (b) a causal-interventionist criterion, on which mechanisms are the same iff interventions upstream propagate to correlated downstream effects; and (c) a functional-role criterion, on which mechanisms are the same iff they play the same input–output role in the larger computation, which allows multiple realizability. We then connected this to the old debate between type-identity theory and functionalism about mental states: the overlap criterion is a kind of type-identity theory of mechanisms, the functional criterion is functionalism about them, and the question of what counts as "the same mechanism" is a scaled-down version of asking whether pain in an octopus and pain in a mammal are the same thing.

Please write me one essay question for the final, together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes by a student with the background above, and it must not be answerable by reciting a summary of the interpretability literature: the crux should be that the choice of individuation criterion silently determines what the monitor counts as an anomaly, and hence what the safety technique can and cannot deliver — a coarse criterion will let genuinely new, dangerous mechanisms pass as "the same" merely because they reuse familiar components, while an overly fine criterion will flag ordinary variation as anomalous and destroy the monitor's usefulness. A strong question will force the student to take a stance on whether the functional criterion can be made to do the work without collapsing into mere behavioural similarity (which would make the internal monitoring pointless), and to say something about whether the analogy with multiple realizability in philosophy of mind is load-bearing or merely suggestive — for instance, whether the fact that we design and can intervene on these networks, unlike nervous systems, changes which criterion is appropriate.

For the marking scheme, please build it around five must-hit points, with marks allocated totalling 20, and for each point add a one-line note on the specific way students typically fail to earn it — for example, gesturing at the analogy with functionalism without ever saying which way the analogy is supposed to cut, or diagnosing the tension between coarse and fine criteria without noticing that behavioural collapse and component chauvinism are not the only options. Please make the model answer one you would genuinely expect to earn an A, and indicate in the marking scheme which moves separate an A answer from a B answer that merely rehearses the seminar material correctly. Please write everything in British English.
