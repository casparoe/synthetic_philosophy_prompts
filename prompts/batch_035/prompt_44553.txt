I'm a novelist, fluent in the technical vocabulary of my craft—focalization, free indirect discourse, protagonist versus minor character, plot versus backstory, worldbuilding, the omniscient narrator—and I've been reading historians argue about scale. Fernand Braudel writes the history of the Mediterranean around empires, trade routes, and slow structural time; E. P. Thompson insists on recovering the political agency of artisans whom later generations condescended to; Carlo Ginzburg reconstructs the cosmology of a sixteenth-century Friulian miller from Inquisition records. Restate this debate—the grand structural and elite-state history on one side, microhistory and history from below on the other—in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. Tell me whether Braudel is essentially writing a setting-driven novel in which human beings are furniture and the real protagonist is a structure; whether Thompson's project is a point-of-view correction, the equivalent of Jean Rhys taking the madwoman in the attic and making her the focalizing consciousness; whether the shift from state archive to parish archive is a change of narrator or a change of story. Then give me the residue report. My suspicion is that at least part of the debate is just our old fight about whose story deserves to be told, which novelists settled long ago by simply moving the camera—but part of it is a causal claim, that the miller and the stockinger were agents of history rather than backdrop, and I don't have a clean way to express that in craft terms, since in fiction everyone is an agent by default. And there's a second residue: microhistorians lean on exceptional archives, what they call the exceptional normal, arguing that an unusually well-documented life can reveal the normal one—in fiction, the closest thing is a minor character so vivid they threaten to take over the book, which is an aesthetic fault, not a method. So tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. I want to know whether the residue points to something real the historians are arguing about that my vocabulary cannot say, or whether the debate, once translated, is less substantive than it looks.
