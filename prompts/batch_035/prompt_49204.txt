My closest friend was a concert pianist until a progressive injury ended her performing career three years ago. She still teaches, reluctantly, and has been increasingly open about how much she misses the stage. Last month she told me she has been accepted into a two-year residential program that, as far as I can tell, sells accomplished lives. For the duration, she would live at a conservatory-style estate, rehearsing daily, performing, touring, reading reviews of her recitals — and all of it would be staged. The audiences would be hired, the reviews written for her, the standing ovations professional. She is being told all of this now, in complete detail, but the program begins with a monitored period in which the knowledge is allowed to fade, and from then on her life would be, from the inside, exactly the life she lost. She would emerge in two years with a settled memory of a triumphant late career.

We argued about it for four hours last night. Her position is that if she cannot tell the difference from the inside, there is no difference that matters, and that anyone who insists otherwise is asking her to trade her actual happiness for the applause of strangers. Mine is that she has spent her whole life saying she plays in order to reach people — not to feel as though she is reaching people — and that this looks like a fact about where her music lands, not a fact about her head. A life in which no one is reached is not the life she wanted, however indistinguishable it feels from the inside. But she pressed me hard on what exactly is lost, and to whom, given that she will never suffer the loss, and I did not have a clean answer.

She also made two moves I have not been able to shake. First, she says that everyone who performs takes the audience on faith anyway; the program merely makes the faith misplaced, and the felt difference between her life and a real performer's is zero. Second, she says her choice is fully informed — she is deciding now, with complete knowledge of the staging — so the life will be deceived but the decision is not, and she wants to know what more I could reasonably demand of a person than that.

I would like to know whether her principle — that a life indistinguishable from the inside is as good as the real thing — can be defended, and whether my objection from what she wants her music to do actually refutes it or merely reports another of her desires, one she is now entitled to set aside. And if her principle survives, I would like to know whether I should still be trying to talk her out of this, or whether that would just be me preferring my picture of her life to her happiness in it.
