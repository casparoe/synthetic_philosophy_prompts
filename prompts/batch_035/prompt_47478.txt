I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and I've mostly managed to stay out of the arguments. Last week I failed to stay out of one — or rather, I got drafted as referee and failed at that. I'd rather settle it here. I'll report the exchange as it happened and take no side myself.

The subject was bet sizing for positive-expectation wagers. The two participants, Vikram and Elena, are both well-read in the decision-theory material this community discusses; neither was arguing in bad faith; and I genuinely could not tell who was right.

(1) Vikram opened with the standard result: for a wager with a known edge, the Kelly fraction of your bankroll is the one that maximizes the long-run exponential growth rate, and this is equivalent to maximizing expected log wealth. So a rational agent — whose utility in money plausibly shows diminishing returns anyway — should bet full Kelly when the edge is known, and any deviation from that needs a special excuse.

(2) Elena attacked the "when the edge is known" clause. In practice, she said, the edge is an estimate, and the estimate is biased upward in precisely the environments where betting looks most attractive. And the consequences of error aren't symmetric: half Kelly still earns roughly three quarters of the maximum growth rate, while double Kelly takes the growth rate down to approximately zero, and overbetting further turns it negative. Since our errors run in one direction, she concluded, the rational response is fractional Kelly — betting, say, half of what the formula says — as insurance against our own overconfidence.

(3) Vikram answered that the real case for Kelly sizing isn't any particular utility curve; it's the structure of compounding. Across a sequence of wagers, outcomes multiply rather than add, so what a single bettor actually experiences is the time average along one path, not the ensemble average over counterfactual bettors, and for multiplicative dynamics those two come apart — the ensemble average is dominated by a vanishingly few lucky paths. Someone with no view at all about logarithmic utility should therefore be pulled toward Kelly sizing by the shape of the problem itself. And since a life of ongoing investing is a long sequence of multiplicative exposures, the argument reaches well beyond the card table.

(4) Elena granted the arithmetic but denied that it picks out the quantity she should be growing. The time-average argument holds for whatever it is that compounds; but her bankroll is not her life's total capital. Career, reputation, health, and relationships are not fungible with the bankroll, cannot necessarily be topped up once depleted, and do not compound multiplicatively in any clean way. Vikram's argument quietly decides that the bankroll is the boundary of the compounding system, and that decision — which is, in her view, the whole question — is exactly what the argument fails to justify.

They closed with one line apiece. Vikram said that "half Kelly, roughly, as insurance" is not a decision procedure but a mood, and that any fraction whatsoever could be defended with those same words. Elena said that "the bankroll is the thing that compounds" is not an argument but a bookkeeping choice, and that a theory whose content turns on an unargued bookkeeping choice has no content.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four numbered steps and the two closing lines, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify anything of substance that both of them missed.
