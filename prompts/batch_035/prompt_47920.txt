I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar — fourteen students, one 75-minute session, and they've already done two weeks on testimonial knowledge in general. Next session is on aesthetic testimony: whether you can acquire aesthetic knowledge, or even justified aesthetic belief, from someone else's report about a work you've never experienced yourself, and whether this is an interestingly harder case than, say, getting a weather report second-hand. Design an in-class experiment that makes them feel the difficulty rather than just read about it.

My sketch, which you should improve rather than merely obey: three groups judge the same set of unfamiliar visual works (obscure enough prints or abstract pieces that nobody recognizes them) under different conditions — one group gets only written judgments from a trusted fictional critic, one gets only the works themselves, and one gets aggregate "critics' scores" from many fictional reviewers before seeing anything. Somewhere in there I'd like two wrinkles: at least one work carrying a false label about its origin, to test whether the testimony and the rankings track features of the object or of its story; and a dissenting-expert card that contradicts the aggregate, to force the aggregation wrinkle — whether a hundred matching second-hand judgments feel epistemically different from one, and whether they should. After the first pass, everyone sees all the works and can revise, and we compare revision patterns across groups.

Deliverables: rules, group assignments, what to put on the cards and labels, a scoring or reporting scheme that creates stakes without real money, a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the live philosophical questions — the acquaintance requirement, whether testimony here transmits or at best generates justification, and whether the aggregation and mislabeling results support or undercut the standard pessimism about aesthetic testimony. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the problem no classroom version can reproduce and I will have to assert in the debrief.

Finally, include a short self-assessment of the design: say which parts of the debrief's intended verdict rest on contested assumptions a critic of the lesson could reject, where you are least confident the activity measures what it's meant to measure, and what observable outcome when I run it would tell you the activity is teaching the wrong lesson — what would have to happen for you to withdraw or revise the debrief's framing.
