so, background: I'm a retired chemist, 35ish years, mostly process chemistry and catalysis in industry, and after I retired I finally had time to read properly and went down the philosophy of science rabbit hole — popper, then kuhn, then a bunch of stuff on philosophy of technology I only half understand. and somewhere in there I got obsessed with the "should frontier AI be open-sourced" debate, because it reminds me of fights we had in chemistry about publishing synthesis routes for genuinely nasty compounds. I've ended up with a bundle of positions on it and I genuinely don't know if they hang together, so audit them for me. here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) the whole machinery of open science — open data, open methods, everyone can check everyone — is what makes science self-correct instead of self-deceiving, and model weights are just data plus code, so they fall under the same norms. secrecy is how you get another cold fusion debacle, except with AI nobody can replicate anything at all.

(2) at the same time I think the unilateralist's curse is a real and serious problem: when lots of actors can each independently release something, what gets released is determined by whoever has the lowest bar, so you can't just do the openness calculus per-actor. this seems like it applies to releasing weights as much as to publishing a dangerous synthesis route.

(3) the genuinely dangerous part of AI capability is the methods and techniques, which are all in arxiv papers anyway. finished weights add basically no marginal capability beyond what the literature gives you.

(4) the dangerous-capability evals people talk about are close to useless — easy to game, measuring the wrong thing, and anyone who's actually worked in a synthesis lab knows the "uplift" questions are framed by people who've never had to make anything at scale.

(5) but I'd still say a model that fails a serious dangerous-capability eval should not be released, open or closed. the eval is the gate, even a bad one.

(6) safety fine-tuning can be stripped off open weights with a few thousand dollars of compute, so "we released safe open weights" is mostly marketing. which I don't really mind, since I think open weights aren't where the risk is anyway.

(7) the systems actually worth worrying about are the closed frontier models being run at massive scale by a handful of labs, and the fact that they're closed means far fewer outside eyes on them, which is itself a hazard — and one of the best arguments for open weights is that researchers outside those labs can't do real interpretability work on systems they can't touch.

(8) and honestly even if a given release would be a mistake, someone will leak or open-source something comparable within a couple of years anyway, so per-release gating mostly just delays the inevitable.

go through these and tell me which ones are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest fix is in each case. rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad. for what it's worth I arrived at all of these separately over maybe two years of reading and I have no instinct at all about which one I'd give up, so don't hold back — I'd rather find out now than in an argument with my grandson, who thinks im naive about the whole thing.
