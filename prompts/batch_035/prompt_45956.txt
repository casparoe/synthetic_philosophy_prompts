I'm a second-year graduate student in a cognitive neuroscience lab that studies consciousness with EEG and fMRI, mostly in the global workspace tradition: we look at the ignition-style signatures of stimuli that cross the threshold for report versus those that don't. My advisor, like many working in this tradition, treats global workspace theory as having essentially settled the operational question — the neural correlates of consciousness just are the correlates of global availability, and subjects' reports are how we measure availability. Lately, though, I've been reading the other side of a debate I only knew as a footnote: Block's insistence that phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness come apart, and that workspace theory at best nails down the neural basis of the second while staying silent on the first. What strikes me is not that the two sides disagree but that the disagreement hasn't moved in roughly fifteen years. The same experiments keep getting cited by both parties — the partial-report paradigm going back to Sperling, the more recent change-detection studies that seem to show rich but fragile representations just before they're cued, and the no-report variants designed to strip away the neural signature of the act of reporting itself — and each side walks away convinced of the opposite conclusion: one that the data show phenomenology overflowing access, the other that they show only fragile, unconscious representations plus a post-cue guess.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and would like tested rather than endorsed, is that the impasse isn't empirical at all. Nobody in this dispute disagrees about what the neurons do. The disagreement seems to live one level up, in what a report is supposed to be evidence of: the workspace camp treats report as constitutive or at least criterial — if it can't in principle be reported, there's nothing to the claim that it was experienced — while the overflow camp treats report as a lossy channel that can only sample a small fraction of what's present, so that the gap between what's represented and what's reported is exactly where phenomenology lives. If that's right, then the two camps aren't answering different questions so much as operating with different evidential standards for the same question, which would explain why new data keeps failing to move anyone. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: that the dispute is verbal, hinging on whether "conscious" means reportable or means having a subjective character, with each camp entitled to its own word. And a third possibility someone raised with me: that it's a genuine substantive disagreement about theoretical virtues — whether positing inaccessible phenomenology is a legitimate posit or an untestable epicycle.

So here is what I want from you: a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who's right. Which of the three readings I sketched — evidential standards, verbal dispute, or dispute about theoretical virtues — best fits what the participants actually say, or is there a better diagnosis I haven't considered? And, crucially for someone who has to pick a dissertation topic in this field: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Is there a concrete experiment, a conceptual distinction yet to be drawn, or a change in the question itself that could actually dissolve or resolve the impasse — or should I conclude that the phenomenal-versus-access issue is not something empirical work like mine can address, and choose my questions accordingly? I'd rather have an honest structural analysis, including one that tells me the dispute is ill-posed, than a survey of positions I can find in any handbook chapter.
