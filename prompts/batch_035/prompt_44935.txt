I'm assembling closing material for a graduate seminar on the evolution of cooperation, and I'd like a sonnet whose form carries a specific piece of the theory we've spent the last month on. We've worked through the iterated prisoner's dilemma, the tournament literature, tit-for-tat and its more forgiving cousins, and the way cooperation gets sustained by the shadow of the future — I cooperate today because you'll be around tomorrow, able to reciprocate or retaliate, and so the long-run incentives do the work that one-shot self-interest never could.

Here is the structural assignment. The octave should inhabit that world: the long middle of a repeated game, cooperation holding because the future is open-ended, each move answered by a remembered past and a feared future — the experience of being a strategy that is nice, provoked to retaliation, and then willing to move on. The volta should be the announcement of the endpoint: the moment the players learn the game ends at a fixed, common-knowledge final move. The closing couplet should be the backward induction doing its work — with no tomorrow to fear, the last move is a one-shot game, so defect; but then the second-to-last move becomes the effective last move, so defect; and the unraveling should run all the way back through the octave the reader has just read, so that by the end, the cooperation in those first twelve lines turns out to have been unsustainable from the very first move. The couplet, by being the poem's end, should retroactively poison the octave the way the known endpoint poisons the whole finite game.

A few constraints. Don't use the technical vocabulary — no "prisoner's dilemma," "backward induction," "tit-for-tat" — inside the poem itself; the verse should run on the lived, first-person experience of the strategies (grudges, forgiveness, dread of the future ending) rather than on named concepts. A reader from our seminar should feel the couplet make the octave's trust look naive in retrospect, exactly as the theory says it was; a reader who knows nothing of the formal result should still get a good poem about a trust that couldn't survive learning when it would end. One optional complication: if you can do it without blunting the couplet, put a faint tremor of doubt into the octave — a hint that the players aren't perfectly certain of the endpoint or of each other's rationality, which is why real cooperation doesn't fully unravel — but if it softens the ending, cut it. I want the poem to hurt the way the theorem does.
