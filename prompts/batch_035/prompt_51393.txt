Write a satirical policy memo — roughly 650 words, formatted as an executive summary followed by numbered recommendations — from the newly created Office of Ladder Positioning in a mid-ranked country whose cabinet has resolved to top the World Happiness Report within ten years. The voice should be deadpan bureaucratic throughout, and the humor must come from the actual mechanics of the rankings rather than invented absurdities. Background the satire should exploit: the ranking rests on the Cantril ladder question, in which survey respondents rate their current life from 0 (the worst possible life they could imagine for themselves) to 10 (the best possible life they could imagine for themselves), averaged over roughly a thousand respondents per country; it is a life-evaluation measure, not a measure of moment-to-moment mood; and a country's score therefore depends not only on how good its citizens' lives are but on what they imagine the best possible life to be. The directorate's proposals should accordingly spend its budget on managing reference points rather than improving conditions — for example, curriculum reform to lower students' imagined "best possible life," a state modesty campaign, and careful thought about who ends up holding the survey. Where the recommendations brush against real objections — Goodhart's law, the gap between life evaluation and experienced well-being, whether governments should compete on this at all — the memo should absorb them as operational risks to be mitigated, never acknowledging them as objections. Keep the country, its officials, and its rivals unnamed and invented. A reader who knows the psychometrics should judge every recommendation plausibly score-raising; a reader who doesn't should still find the memo funny and come away uneasy about what the index actually measures.
