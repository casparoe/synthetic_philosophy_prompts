I've been reading about methods in the science of consciousness and hit a characterization of Dennett's heterophenomenology that I can't quite evaluate. A review essay I came across presents it roughly like this: heterophenomenology is Dennett's proposal for studying consciousness scientifically, set out in Consciousness Explained. The idea, the essay says, is that since conscious experience is private and inaccessible to observers, the scientist must give up on studying it directly and instead treat subjects' reports as the data, recording what people say about their experiences under controlled conditions and building a theory around that verbal behavior. On this reading, the method is essentially a sophisticated behaviorism: whatever cannot be intersubjectively observed is excluded from the start, so the experiences themselves — the very thing a science of consciousness should explain — are defined out of the evidence base. The essay adds that Dennett calls the subject's reported world a kind of fiction: the subject tells a story about an inner realm, and the heterophenomenologist takes this story as seriously as a novelist takes his characters, but at the end of the day knows it is made up. From this the essay concludes that heterophenomenology begs the question against the realist about consciousness: by confining itself to reports, it presupposes that there is nothing more to explain than what subjects can say, and therefore can never discover anything about experience that subjects cannot already tell us.

My suspicion is that this is not entirely fair. I recall Dennett protesting that he is not a behaviorist and that his critics misread his "fiction" talk, but the essay sounded knowledgeable, and I can't tell which parts are accurate, which are the critic's tendentious framing, and which are simply wrong. Could you go through the characterization piece by piece and tell me: which claims would Dennett accept as a correct statement of the method; where is the essay conflating two things he distinguishes (for instance, does he distinguish the subject's reports from the experiences the reports are about, and what exactly is his "fiction" supposed to be a fiction of); which claims are outright misattributions that he explicitly denies; and finally, does the essay's conclusion — that the method prejudges the question in favor of denying consciousness its explanatory role — follow from the parts of the summary that are actually true? If some of the accurate parts, taken alone, still put real pressure on the method, I'd like to hear that too; I'm not looking for a defense of Dennett, just an audit I can trust.
