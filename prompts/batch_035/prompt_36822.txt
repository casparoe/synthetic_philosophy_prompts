My grandmother passed away in the spring and, long story short, I inherited her seat on the board of the small family foundation she started. There are only three voting members: me, my cousin Marta, and my uncle Jonah. I've got the swing vote at Friday's meeting and honestly i'm stuck, because Marta and Jonah had it out at the planning dinner last week and I can't tell who's right.

The agenda has two items. Item 1: we can fund either (a) a deworming-and-vitamin program overseas with really solid measured outcomes, which is Marta's whole thing, or (b) a local program of arts workshops for adults with intellectual disabilities, which is Jonah's — he talks about dignity and participation, and the outomes are basically unmeasurable. Item 2: a big betting company has offered $100k to sponsor our community health fund. Jonah thinks taking gambling money is wrong, full stop — it's extracted from the very people we're supposed to be helping. Marta thinks refusing it is the actual moral mistake: that money buys vaccines, and clean hands don't vaccinate anyone.

At dinner, Marta proposed a package deal: she'll vote for the arts workshops if Jonah votes to accept the sponsorship. Her words, roughly: you get your top priority, I get mine. If instead we split the difference on each item, the workshops end up half-funded and maybe not viable at all, and we throw away the sponsorship money, which by my lights costs lives. The trade leaves us both better off by our own values; the split leaves us both worse off. What is a process for, she asked, that makes everyone worse off by their own lights?

Jonah wouldn't have it. He said that's not compromise, that's a swap meet. Real compromise, he said, means both of us bear some of the cost of what the foundation does that we each object to — that's what makes it compromise instead of just winning on a different issue. If we trade, I've helped you do what I think is wrong, you've helped me do what you think is second-best, and the two of us walk around with clean hands about our own particular votes while jointly authorizing everything either of us wanted. The burden gets allocated so nobody feels it, instead of shared.

Marta answered that the burden-sharing thing romanticizes loss — that sharing the stain is aesthetic, and what he's calling the moral cost of compromise is just both of them ending up with less of what they value.

Jonah came back harder. He said the deeper problem is that she treats values like tradeable endowments, a bag of intensities. Some of his commitments aren't preferences over outcomes at all — they're about what he can put his name to. And he said the trade has a structural bias: it hands everything to whoever happens to rank their issues highest. Compromise is supposed to protect the person with the minority values, not reward whoever's preferences burn brightest. With split-the-difference, at least each of us gets part of what we want on every single item.

Marta's last word was that any objection can be made untradeable just by redescribing it as a principle — call your preference a conviction and suddenly it's off the market, and then all the gains from trade evaporate by decree. Jonah's last word was that the difference between a preference and a principle is exactly that a principle isn't for sale, and if she can't see the difference, that says more about her accounting than about his ethics. Then they both looked at me, and I said I'd think about it. So now I'm thinking about it.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? To be clear, I don't need you to tell me how to vote on the two items themselves — I need to know which of their arguments about compromise actually holds up: where Marta's gains-from-trade story works, where Jonah's burden-sharing story works, and whether his preference/principle line can survive her "just call it a principle" reply. Please end with a single line in the form: Verdict: [one sentence saying who's right about what, and who's more right overall].
