so my advisor wants a one-page research proposal before she signs off on my term paper topic, and the topic is value of information. The thing everyone learns is Good's old result: a free, rational agent with coherent probabilities can never expect to be worse off after observing new evidence for free — always look before you leap. Then there are the exceptions everyone shrugs at, supposedly a grab bag: the agent has to be free to act on what she learns, the information can't cost anything, it can't be about the agent herself, other agents can't be watching. My suspicion is its not a grab bag at all. The exceptions look like they split into two kinds: ones where the information changes what *other* agents do (you leak your test result to the insurer, you signal just by asking a question, blackmail), and ones where the information is about *your own future choices* (you learn what you're going to do anyway, you precommit to not conditioning on it, updateless-style agents who would pay good money to stay ignorant). And behind both, maybe, one question: is the nonnegativity of information a theorem about your prior, or about your decision rule? It could be that causal and evidential decision theorists actually disagree about whether some free observation helps you, and "always take the free information" is only sound relative to one of them.
