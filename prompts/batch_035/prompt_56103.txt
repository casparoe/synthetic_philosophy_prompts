I teach a mid-level philosophy of language seminar, and for our unit on speaker meaning I want a handout that does something a lecture cannot: sort students by their actual reactions to cases rather than by which ism they find sympathetic. Please design the handout as a decision tree titled "What is it for a speaker to mean something?" -- starting from an ordinary act (an utterance, a gesture, an object deliberately left somewhere) that a hearer interprets, and ending in one of the positions that live in the logical space opened up by the Gricean project of analyzing non-natural meaning in terms of intentions, together with the repairs proposed once the counterexamples started coming in.

Every branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case, answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the labels -- the tree should sort people by what they are willing to say about the cases, not by what they call themselves. The battery of cases must include, at minimum: a photograph of a man in dire straits shown as evidence of his condition versus a drawing produced to the same effect, where only the drawing seems to mean anything non-naturally; a soldier facing imminent capture who leaves behind a diary containing, in a language the enemy reads, reports calculated to make them believe the area is unsafe; a rival merchant who arranges for a competitor's wares to appear smeared and spoiled, hoping shoppers draw the obvious conclusion while never suspecting a human hand; a passenger in a sleeper car who displays his ticket in a way that makes clear he does not wish to be disturbed, wanting only to be recognized as not wanting disturbance, not to induce any belief; a hearer who fully recognizes a speaker's intention to produce a belief in him and precisely because of that recognition refuses to form it; spontaneous exclamations and conventional greetings, which seem to mean without aiming at any belief in the hearer; and at least one case of natural meaning, like smoke or spots, to anchor the tree's first fork.

The leaves must include the strict reflexive-intention analysis on which the speaker intends to induce an effect through the hearer's recognition of that very intention; the weakened emendations on which recognition is intended to play some role in the hearer's reasoning without being necessary for the effect; accounts that ground utterer meaning in conventions of a population rather than an individual's mental states; views on which the covert persuader does count as meaning something despite wanting no recognition at all; and a deflationary leaf on which the search for an analysis was misconceived from the start. Roughly eight to twelve cases and five to seven leaves is the right scale for a two-page handout.

Three further requirements. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: state the fork questions first, then locate the positions in the resulting space, and flag any combination of answers that no leaf occupies, with a sentence on whether that gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Second, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate -- cases on which two named positions predict the same verdicts -- rather than pretending the instrument is sharper than it is. Third, because several of my students work on dialogue systems, end the handout with one clearly marked speculative question: where do each leaf's principles place artifacts with no intentions at all -- a recorded departure announcement, a terminal that prints "I am broken" precisely when it is broken, or a language model's output -- and does any leaf's account of speaker meaning commit its holders to a verdict about whether such systems can mean anything non-naturally? The handout should be usable in class without external references, so everything a student needs to answer a fork must be contained in the case description itself.
