For thirty years I worked on life-history evolution in insects, and I have followed the dispute over the levels at which selection acts — kin selection versus group selection — since I was a graduate student in the late 1970s, when group selection was still a byword for error in the aftermath of Wynne-Edwards and the Williams critique. What puzzles me now, rereading the literature with a philosopher's eye rather than a working biologist's, is that the dispute appears to have been declared formally resolved several times over — most notably through the Price equation, whose covariance decomposition lets one partition the very same change in allele frequency into within-group and between-group components more or less at will — and yet it flared up again spectacularly around 2010, when Nowak, Tarnita, and Wilson published a paper in Nature arguing that inclusive fitness theory has contributed little to our understanding of eusociality, and were answered in the same journal by a reply signed by what seemed like half the community of evolutionary biologists. When a mathematical equivalence result fails to settle an argument among mathematically literate people, something other than mathematics is doing the work, and I would like to know what.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading. One side takes the object of explanation to be the causal statistics of allele-frequency change — where, and partitioned how, the covariance of genotype with fitness actually resides in a given population — while the other takes it to be the pragmatics of research: which quantities are estimable in the field, and which framework has historically generated the testable models. On that reading the disagreement is partly verbal, turning on whether selection for altruism at the group level denotes a causal fact about the world or merely a permissible re-description of the same fact, but there may also be a genuine empirical residue that the equivalence results cannot touch, concerning such things as the historical transition from solitary to eusocial life that prompted the 2010 exchange in the first place.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, substantive, or a case of two research programs answering different questions under one name. In particular, does the Price-equation equivalence show that the disputants agree once the terms are fixed, or only that their frameworks are intertranslatable, which I take to be a considerably weaker achievement? Where, if anywhere, is a real empirical or conceptual claim being contested — for instance about what actually happened in the origin of eusociality, or about whether inclusive-fitness methods genuinely break down under any but exotic assumptions such as strong synergistic interactions? I am especially interested in whether the philosophers who have taken up the question — I have been working through Sober and Wilson's 1998 book and Samir Okasha's more recent treatment of the levels of selection — have identified a distinction that would dissolve the dispute, or have merely furnished it with a more refined vocabulary in which to continue. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a theorem, a field study, a terminological reform, or nothing at all — because the dispute is a rivalry between research programs that no shared result, however elegant, could end.
