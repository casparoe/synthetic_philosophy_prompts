I direct the bedside music program at a hospice with about forty beds. The musicians on my team — trained performers, none of them philosophers — take requests from patients and families and also propose pieces on their own, and at the moment we choose by instinct and by whatever the patient says they like. I have been reading Schopenhauer, who held that music is unlike the other arts: painting and poetry copy the world at one remove, through images and ideas, while music bypasses representation altogether and gives a direct analogue of willing itself — striving, tension, delay, satisfaction, resumption — so that music works on the listener in a way no representational art can. Whether or not that is true in full, it strikes me as the beginnings of an actual selection procedure: something that distinguishes pieces that reach the listener through what they represent (a text, a story, an association) from pieces that work directly through the shape of tension and resolution in the sound itself, and that tells my musicians which kind of situation calls for which.

Please turn this into a procedure my team can actually run in the ten or fifteen minutes they have between rooms. I want an ordered set of questions about a candidate piece — whether it carries a text, whether its harmonic motion goes somewhere and resolves, whether it is likely to agitate or settle the listener, whether its sadness is of the kind that satisfies rather than distresses — with a clear account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must stop and hand the choice to the musician because the question turns on the patient's life and associations rather than on anything in the music. Then run the procedure on three cases: a dying woman who asks for the hymns she sang at church, sung aloud with the words; a man with no religious background, near the end, for whom someone has suggested the Adagietto from Mahler's Fifth Symphony; and an anxious patient in the middle of the night for whom a musician proposes the first prelude from Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier.

Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the person applying it will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether this way of thinking can ever justify choosing music that is theoretically better suited over music the patient simply loves. Please write the procedure and the worked cases in American English, and keep the philosophy in the reasoning rather than on the floor: my musicians need a checklist they can follow, not a lecture they cannot.
