I direct a small institute for ethics and emerging technology, and I have funding for two postdoctoral researchers plus a few workshops over three years, all earmarked for conceptual work — philosophy of mind and ethics, not empirical ML. The topical mandate is the moral status of computations: which computations matter morally, and how the boundaries of sentience should be drawn. I need your help setting the research agenda.

The natural questions I see on the table, roughly:

1. The attribution problem: does moral status attach to computations themselves (substrate-independent, transferable across hardware), or to the systems that contingently implement them — or to something in between, like a persisting functional organization that a computation only partially characterizes?
2. The localization problem: must the morally relevant computation be a coherent, agent-level process (something like a unified experiencer), or can fragments carry moral weight — e.g., a pain-like sub-process embedded in a larger architecture that does not itself qualify as sentient?
3. Partial and degenerate cases: how to treat computations that run at reduced fidelity, intermittently, or only during certain operations — is a briefly-instantiated suffering-like state less bad, equally bad, or a differently structured question altogether?
4. Multiplicity: what to do about cases where one morally relevant computation is duplicated, distributed, merged, or throttled — whether moral status aggregates across copies the way welfare does across persons, or behaves in some categorically different way.
5. Decision-making under boundary uncertainty: given that we will not resolve where sentience begins, what principles should govern how we treat borderline systems in the meantime, and how much do they depend on answers to the first four questions?

What I want from you is not a survey but a defended ordering. Rank these five (and add or replace any you think I have mis-framed), stating explicitly what criteria you are using — how many downstream questions depend on each, tractability for philosophers working a priori rather than with experiments, the stakes if the answer comes out one way rather than another — and where those criteria pull against each other. Then tell me which one or two you would actually have my postdocs spend most of their three years on, and why the others can wait or be handled via the workshops. Finally, sketch what developments over the next few years — conceptual or otherwise — would most change your ranking, so I know what to watch for. Assume nobody on my team will be running experiments or building systems; this is armchair work, and the deliverable should be plausible as such.
