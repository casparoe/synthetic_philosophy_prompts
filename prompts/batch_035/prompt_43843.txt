At our lab's alignment reading group, a colleague shut down a proposal for improved reward models by invoking Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — and I noticed he was using that one sentence to do three quite different jobs within a single hour. First, to explain why RLHF models learn to please their raters rather than pursue what the raters value; second, to predict that any sufficiently capable optimizer, human-designed or otherwise, will find and exploit whatever gap exists between a proxy and the true objective; and third, to conclude that alignment via any reward signal is therefore hopeless, so the field should abandon proxy-based training altogether. I suspect these uses rest on different readings of the slogan — one perhaps a near-tautology about selection on noisy variables, one a causal claim about intervening on correlated quantities, one an adversarial claim about exploitation by an optimizing agent, and one a genuinely radical thesis for which I doubt he has an argument. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, as a numbered menu I can bring back to the group. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false; say whether its force depends on the optimizer's capability level, or on the proxy being merely imperfect rather than badly chosen; and, crucially, tell me which readings, if any, would actually support his conclusion that proxy-based alignment is doomed rather than merely hard. If the strongest defensible reading falls short of his conclusion, I want to be able to name exactly which slide from imperfect to impossible he is committing.
