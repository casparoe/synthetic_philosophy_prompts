My background is facilitation, not math, but I've picked up that there's a classic result in voting theory (Gibbard–Satterthwaite, I think?) that says roughly: with 3 or more options, where everyone is allowed to rank the options in any order, any voting rule that's non-dictatorial and doesn't rule an option out in advance is manipulable — meaning there's always some situation where somebody gets a better outcome by reporting preferences that aren't their real ones. So "just find the voting rule that can't be gamed" is off the table, which is very much not what the orgs expect to hear when they call me.

What I want from you: a procedure I can actually run during a ~20 minute phone call, that tells me which voting rule to recommend for a given vote — and that's honest about the moments where the answer is "no rule does what you want, here are three bad options, the members have to pick one as a group." The procedure should ask about things like (adjust as you see fit): how many options there are; whether the options genuinely fall on a single spectrum, since I gather single-peaked preferences are the main escape hatch from the theorem; whether the org can stomach randomness (I've half-remembered something about picking randomly between top options, or random choice among individuals, being harder to game — correct me if I've garbled it); whether they can afford a second round / runoff; how much strategic voting actually costs them given they'll vote together for years; how obvious the gaming would need to be before it poisons trust. It needs explicit stopping points: one where it says "every rule here is gameable, so aim for making gaming rare and hard to pull off quietly rather than impossible," and one where it says "this is a values question, like simplicity vs. fairness, don't decide it for them — put it to a vote or a discussion."

Then run it on three real-ish cases: (1) a 40-person co-op electing one of three board candidates, high stakes, they have a tradition of ranked ballots; (2) an 8-person team choosing between four retreat dates, where I'd guess preferences run "middle of the range is fine, extremes are annoying" — my hunch is this is the single-peaked case and there's a clean rule for it, is that right?; (3) 60 members choosing between five logo designs, where any "left-right spectrum" story is obviously nonsense. Make sure at least one case produces an answer the caller won't like (I suspect case 3, where the honest output might be "accept a designated chooser, draw straws, or live with gaming"), and end by telling me what the procedure flat-out cannot do. And if I've misdescribed the theorem anywhere above, say so — I'd much rather learn it from you than mid-call with a chairperson on the line.
