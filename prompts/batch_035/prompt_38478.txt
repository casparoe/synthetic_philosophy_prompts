I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and we just spent two seminars on Dennett's picture of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the idea that a self is a kind of abstractum we attribute to an organism, the way a physicist attributes a center of gravity to an object, because positing it makes the organism's behavior vastly easier to organize, explain, and predict. One member of my study group claims he can show that Dennett's picture, taken seriously, dissolves all of ethics, and the rest of us have not been able to agree on where the argument goes wrong. He presents it like this:

(1) On Dennett's picture, a self is a center of narrative gravity: an abstraction we attribute to a body to streamline the explanation and prediction of its behavior, just as we attribute a center of mass to a bridge. (2) A center of mass is a mathematical fiction: no force acts on it and it acts on nothing; when the bridge collapses, concrete masses and forces do all the causal work, and saying "the center of mass fell" is mere shorthand. (3) Whatever is a posited abstraction rather than a concrete entity has no causal powers of its own. (4) What has no causal powers of its own can neither undergo nor do anything: nothing can be done to it — it cannot be harmed, wronged, punished, or addressed — and it cannot choose, promise, or break a promise. (5) So no self can be harmed, wronged, punished, or addressed, and no self can choose, promise, or break a promise. (6) If no one can be wronged, there are no victims; if no one can choose or promise, there are no agents. (7) Therefore there have never been victims or agents: no one has ever wronged anyone, broken a promise, deserved punishment, or chosen anything at all.

He insists each premise is just Dennett plus common sense about abstract objects, and he says the conclusion is simply the bullet a consistent Dennettian must bite. He also adds, gleefully, that the argument equally shows that no one has ever believed anything or argued for anything, so strictly speaking nobody endorses it — he takes that self-undermining flavor as evidence it's onto something deep. Our group has splintered: one person wants to reject (3), one wants to reject (4), and one says the real problem is an equivocation somewhere between (1) and (5) that none of us can quite name.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who has taught Dennett for years and think this through carefully before answering. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey: pick the single step you would reject (or argue that a step is invalid as stated), explain exactly what the flaw is — where the equivocation or hidden assumption enters and why the step looked fine to smart people — and then price the alternatives: what each rival rejection of (3), (4), or the (1)-to-(5) transition costs, and whether a Dennettian can consistently accept the deflationary picture of selves while still keeping victims and agents in the full-blooded sense ethics seems to need. Take a definite position and defend it; don't leave me a menu of options to choose among.
