I've spent enough years in the decision-theory corners of the internet — the money-pump arguments, the Dutch-book canon, the whole coherence-based case that rationality just is expected utility maximization — that I thought I was comfortable with the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, including the one nobody likes defending in public: continuity. A friend has now walked me into an argument that the coherence position makes rationality monstrous, and I can't find the exact step where it breaks. The argument runs:

(1) The coherence arguments show that a rational agent's preferences over lotteries must satisfy the VNM axioms, including continuity: if you prefer getting a dollar to the status quo, and the status quo to dying today, then there is some probability p such that you are indifferent between the status quo and a lottery that yields the dollar with probability p and death with probability 1−p. (2) Call the indifference point p*; then, given independence, every bet that puts the probability of death below 1−p* is one you strictly prefer to refusing — so the axioms don't merely permit you to accept a nonzero chance of dying for a dollar, they require it. (3) Now imagine an adversary who knows your threshold and offers you a long sequence of such bets, each individually just barely on the acceptable side of it; at each offer, refusing is the irrational move. (4) The individual death probabilities compound, so after enough offers you will have accepted a package with, say, a fifty percent chance of dying today in exchange for a few hundred dollars. (5) Therefore coherence-based expected utility maximization requires a rational agent to play Russian roulette for pocket change. My friend takes this as a reductio: if the coherence arguments really force this, they prove far too much, and something in the standard story is rotten.

The problem is that every step looks locally defensible, and the standard escape routes each look bad to me. If I reject continuity and make survival lexicographically prior to money, I seem to walk straight back into money-pumpable territory — the very thing the coherence arguments were supposed to preempt — though I admit I have never seen the pump actually spelled out for the non-Archimedean case, so I'm partly bluffing there. If I bite the bullet on (5) and say the threshold is just so astronomically small that no realistic adversary could exploit it, that doesn't obviously block the compounding in (3)–(4); it only shrinks the per-bet numbers. And if I appeal to bounded and diminishing marginal utility of money, so that the threshold drifts as you win earlier bets, I genuinely cannot tell whether that halts the compounding or merely slows it. So tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs — including the cost I'd be quietly incurring if I rejected (2)'s move from there being a threshold to being required to accept anything below it. For what it's worth, I would rather surrender my intuition that no rational agent takes a fifty percent death chance for a few hundred dollars than give up the coherence arguments themselves; but I want to be forced into that surrender honestly, not stampeded by a sloppy slide I can't name.
