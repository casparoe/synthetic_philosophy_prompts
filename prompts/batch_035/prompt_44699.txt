I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading about Buddhist no-self (anattā) on my own, mostly by way of Derek Parfit's reductionist take on personal identity, which a colleague told me was basically the Buddha's position in analytic dress. I also write occasional verse — badly, but sincerely — and I want to commission something ambitious. Please take on the role of a formally trained poet who also knows early Buddhist philosophy well enough that a scholar would not catch you in an error.

Write me a sestina about the doctrine of no-self, specifically about the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) as the exhaustive inventory of what a "person" turns out to be. I want the form to carry the argument, so here are the constraints, which I'd like you to follow exactly:

1. The six rotating end-words shall be: form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, and self. The five aggregate-words recur unchanged through all six stanzas, exactly as sestina rules require — and that's the point. The same words keep coming back in new combinations, and the poem is supposed to make the reader feel that nothing persists except the labels and the relations: the recurrence of the end-words enacts continuity-of-pattern without continuity-of-substance, so that the formal constraint itself becomes the argument that identity is a matter of recombination, not an underlying owner.

2. The end-word self is the exception and the crux. Each time it lands at the end of a line, it should refer to something different — the chariot, the flame passed from candle to candle, the self that the questioner assumes must be there, and so on — so that the word stays fixed while everything it points to shifts. By the final stanza, the reader should notice that self is the only end-word that never names anything twice.

3. The envoi (the three-line closing) must use self once, but only in a construction where it is functioning as a grammatical placeholder that the other five words are holding up — something the syntax needs and nothing instantiates.

A couple of notes on tone and accuracy. Please don't have the poem assert that the Buddha simply denied personal identity in the crude sense; the interesting and historically defensible claim is the reductive one, that what we call the self is exhaustively composed of the aggregates and their causal continuity, and that a further fact of ownership is never found. Someone who knows the Milindapañha chariot passage or the flame-and-milk similes should recognize the poem as getting this right, not as a caricature of "nothing exists." And please keep it a genuinely good poem — concrete images, no clunky exposition — such that someone with no exposure to Buddhist philosophy would still read it as a strong sestina about recurrence and loss.

Before you write the poem, please think through the following briefly and show that reasoning: (a) how the fixed end-words versus the shifting referent of self will dramatize the reductionist point; (b) which stanza should carry the turn, i.e., the moment the reader stops expecting the word self to cash out into a referent; and (c) any places where sestina convention might fight the doctrine, and how you'll handle them. Then give me the finished poem, followed by three or four sentences explaining, in plain language suitable for a software engineer, how the structure delivers the argument.
