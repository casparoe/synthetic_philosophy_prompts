Design a thought experiment in which cognitive penetration of perception makes a perceiver *more* accurate rather than less — the opposite of the usual direction in which the phenomenon is used to threaten the epistemic standing of perceptual experience.

The scenario must be realistic: an ordinary setting such as a hospital ward, a fishing boat, or a farm, with no science-fictional technology and no artificial stimulation of the brain. The penetrating state must be a standing belief or a current expectation, not expertise acquired over years of training: a defender of the encapsulation of early vision should not be able to dismiss the case as long-term perceptual recalibration of the sort found in expert chicken-sexers or chess players, since that phenomenon is arguably not cognitive penetration at all. The improvement must also be synchronic — the belief and the improved perception must co-occur within a single episode — and it must not be reducible to a shift of attention or to a post-perceptual judgment dressed up as seeing: the perceiver should be able to report a change in how things look, not merely in what they conclude.

After presenting the case, do the following:

1. Identify precisely which cognitive state penetrates, which aspect of perceptual phenomenology is altered, and why the case resists the two standard deflationary redescriptions (attention-shifting and judgment-after-the-fact).
2. Show what verdict the case earns from each of three positions: (a) the encapsulationist, who holds that early vision is cognitively impenetrable and will therefore predict that the apparent improvement in accuracy should be located somewhere outside perception; (b) the theorist who accepts cognitive penetration but argues that any etiological dependence of experience on belief undermines the justificatory role of that experience; and (c) a hybrid position on which penetration is epistemically evaluable case by case, depending on the reliability of the penetrating state in that context.
3. Explain which single feature of the case is doing the work — the feature that makes the accuracy gain genuinely perceptual rather than inferential — and describe what happens to the case if you remove that feature while leaving everything else fixed.
4. Give the encapsulationist's best attack on the case, and say honestly whether it succeeds or merely raises the cost of holding the view.

Note what the case is for: it is designed as a pressure test of the claim that belief-driven influence on experience is epistemically corrupting *as such*. A case that merely stipulates the perceiver became more accurate fails; the accuracy gain must fall out of the structure of the scenario, so that the theorist in camp (b) either has to deny that the penetration occurred or accept that penetration can enhance rather than degrade justification. State explicitly which of those two escapes your case leaves open.
