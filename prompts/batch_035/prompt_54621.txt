My closest friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about whether our growing reliance on AI systems — for writing, coding, analysis, even structuring our own thoughts — is eroding our understanding rather than augmenting it, and we've reached the stage where I suspect we are partly talking past each other. Rather than another round, I want to commission the document we should have produced instead.

Let me state the two positions as fairly as I can. Mine first: offloading intellectual work to systems that interpose fluent, finished output between us and the doing of the work doesn't merely free up cognitive capacity; it starves the formation of the internal competence that makes it possible to check the output, catch its errors, and go beyond it. Skills decay without practice; a generation that never struggles through the process gets the product without the process-derived judgment; and people routinely mistake smooth retrieval for genuine understanding — the illusion-of-explanatory-depth phenomenon, where people report understanding mechanisms they demonstrably cannot reconstruct. My friend's position: this is the same panic that greeted writing, printing, the calculator, and GPS, and in every prior case the panic mistook a redistribution of cognitive labor for a loss. Cognition has always been scaffolded by artifacts; the proper unit of evaluation is the coupled system of person-plus-tool rather than the unaided individual; and the live questions are about the reliability and transparency of the artifact, not about whether I can still perform the feat with my bare brain, which was never the standard for anything else we offloaded.

Several distinctions surfaced during our argument that seemed to carry real weight, and I'd like the document to engage them rather than flatten them: between performing a task and the understanding that used to be a by-product of performing it; between understanding-that and understanding-how, and whether heavy tool use can supply the first while quietly blocking the second; between what holds for an individual and what holds for a profession or a whole epistemic community; and a distinction I coined mid-argument that my friend found suspicious — between understanding-on-demand and understanding-in-reserve — the thought being that understanding you can retrieve at need may not be understanding you can reason from when retrieval fails or the system is confidently wrong, and that the interesting question is what fraction of a person's or a profession's understanding can safely be kept on-demand.

Here is the task. Draft the joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the claims both would endorse, including about what the calculator, GPS, and writing analogies do and do not establish; the precise formulations of their residual disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one camp; and, for each crux, a concrete case, intervention, or measurement both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

Two further requests. First, an honest verdict on whether part of our dispute is merely verbal — for instance, whether "understanding" is doing two different jobs in our two positions, or whether the erosion camp is really making a claim about learning while the scaffolding camp is really making a claim about performance, so that much of the collision dissolves once the questions are separated. Second, I have certainly planted my own thumb on the scale somewhere in the framing above, most likely in the coined distinction and in how I have set the terms of the comparison. If you find that the two-camp structure is confused, that a formulation I have offered is slanted, or that the task as I have posed it cannot be done well, say so plainly and adjust rather than producing a polished document that papers over the problem. I would much rather be told my framing is broken than be handed a neat table built on it.
