My case: Sweden's 1967 switch from left- to right-hand traffic, Dagen H, 3 September 1967. The salient facts as I understand them: Sweden had driven on the left for centuries while all its neighbours drove right, and most Swedish cars had the steering wheel on the left anyway, which made overtaking genuinely dicey. A 1955 advisory referendum had gone heavily for keeping left — something like four in five, if I remember right — but parliament went ahead anyway. The switch itself was preceded by years of preparation and a saturation public-information campaign, and on the day traffic was banned for hours while signage and signals were physically swapped.

Run the episode through Lewis's account of convention as a coordination equilibrium sustained by reciprocal expectations and common knowledge. I want a careful mapping: what the coordination problem actually was (mismatched neighbours plus mismatched steering), where the arbitrariness lies, how the announcement campaign does the work of manufacturing common knowledge of the new equilibrium, and whether the announced date becomes salient in roughly Schelling's sense. First, the 1955 referendum: conventions have a normative character on Lewis's picture — each party wants the others to conform — but apparently they're not majoritarian, so what exactly was overridden? One thought I'd like you to take seriously and then either defend or dismantle: the referendum arguably measured the wrong quantity, since a vote about what Sweden should do reads as an unconditional preference, whereas Lewis's account only cares about conditional ones — would you rather drive on the right *if everyone else did*? Does that rescue the theory from the referendum result, or is it an ad hoc dodge?
