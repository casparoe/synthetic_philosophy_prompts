At thanksgiving my cousin and her boyfriend got into it over whether it's okay to say stuff like "the Germans are rule-followers" or "Japan is a shame culture." The boyfriend, who reads a lot of history, kept leaning on one analogy: a nation is like a person who's been through things. France is proud, he said — look at how it reacted to Napoleon's defeat and then to 1940; a person humiliated twice like that would carry it too, and you can watch the personality play out in its decisions for two centuries. He said it's exactly how we infer any personality — repeated behavior over time plus formative events early on — and that anyone who accepts "my grandma is stubborn" but balks at "Russia is paranoid" is just being squeamish about scale. My cousin thinks the whole move is lazy and a little gross, but when pressed all she could say was "countries aren't people," which didn't land with him because he'd already told her what the mapping was supposed to be.

I'll be at their place again over the holidays and I want to walk in with something better than she had. Can you audit the analogy for me? I want what actually maps onto what — person, temperament, formative childhood, growing out of it, being in therapy, maybe an author's biography — and then where the mapping breaks in ways that matter rather than pedantic ones. It seems to smuggle in at least unity (one voice per country when most historical moments involve factions that hate each other), consistency across situations, and being the kind of thing that can be blamed, and I want to know which of those the boyfriend actually needs for his generalizations to work and which he's just getting for free from the picture. Like, does "France is proud" do any predictive work that "French elites in 1940 had a specific set of institutions and recent memories" doesn't, or is it just a compression?

And then the bottom line: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If some scaled-down version of national character talk is legitimate — historians clearly can't write without SOME collective subject language — I want to know what the legitimate version commits you to and what it forbids, in terms the boyfriend would accept. If there's a better analogy than "nation as person" for what he's gesturing at, tell me what it is: I keep wanting to say something like a family firm that periodically fires all its employees, or a sports team with constant turnover that somehow keeps a style of play, but I can't tell if those fix the problems or just relocate them.
