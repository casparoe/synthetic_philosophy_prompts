I teach an upper-level seminar on early Chinese philosophy, and I'm setting the final. I want your help with one essay question on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature (xing).

Here's the background, and the reason I'm asking. A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about this. She has read both texts, though only in translation, and her position was that the whole dispute is verbal. On her telling, Mencius counts the flicker of alarm we feel at the sight of a child about to fall into a well as part of our nature, while Xunzi counts as nature only what is present prior to any deliberate activity — so of course they disagree: each has fixed the word differently, each argument is valid relative to his own definition, and nothing substantive is at stake. She compared it to two botanists who agree about everything regarding the tomato and quarrel only over whether to call it a fruit. I pushed back that this makes the dispute too cheap: if it were merely verbal, Xunzi's polemical hostility to Mencius would be puzzling, and there do seem to be genuine disagreements underneath — about whether ethical response is ever spontaneous, or is always an artifact of training. But I'll admit I got little further than insisting it doesn't feel verbal.

So here is the ask. Write me one essay question, suitable for a 90-minute in-class final, whose whole point is that a student cannot pass it by reciting the standard contrast — that Mencius holds we are born with incipient moral tendencies while Xunzi holds that whatever goodness we display is the product of deliberate cultivation. The question should force students to adjudicate between my friend's verbal-dispute reading and my substantive-disagreement reading, and it should leave room for them to find both of us confused — for instance, by arguing that a definitional difference over xing can itself carry substantive consequences for what education and ritual are supposed to do, or that Mencius's thought experiments are designed precisely to embarrass Xunzi's definition rather than to bypass it.

Then give me a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — for example, what an A answer must do with the child-at-the-well case, and what a B answer typically substitutes for it. Total marks 20, distributed across the must-hit points, with a one-line note on how students most often lose each one.

One more thing. If you think my friend's framing, or mine, rests on a confusion — say, that the verbal/substantive dichotomy is simply the wrong tool for a dispute conducted partly through definitions — tell me so plainly in a short note at the end, rather than quietly building the question around whichever framing you find less wrong. I would much rather hear it now than discover it during the marking meeting.
