I was recently appointed executor of my grandmother's estate, and her will has dragged me into questions of material constitution that I cannot stop thinking about. She leaves "the sculpture" to the art school where she trained, "the wedding ring" to my sister, and "the farm" to me, and provides for her cat's care; the family has begun arguing about what exactly each bequest picks out, and I have discovered that I hold a set of verdicts I cannot square with one another.

The sculpture is an unfired clay figure. I accept that it and the clay constituting it are two objects occupying the very same place: they have different persistence conditions, since firing the figure would destroy the wet-clay lump while the sculpture, as I see it, survives to become a ceramic piece, and squishing the figure into a ball would destroy the sculpture while the clay persists. This even seems to matter legally, on my view: had she left the sculpture to the school and the clay to my cousin, that would be two gifts of two things, not one gift described twice. And if a mover squishes the figure and my cousin, meaning well, reworks every gram of the clay into a new figure, I would say the bequest fails — the thing willed is gone, though all its matter remains.

Her cat had a tail amputated last year and plainly survived. But before the amputation, I do not believe there was a second cat — the cat-minus-its-tail — sitting there alongside her, a thing that could not have survived an operation the whole cat did survive. One cat before, one cat after. Yet the whole cat and that merely would-be shorter cat differ in persistence conditions in just the way the sculpture and the clay do, and there I happily count two things. This is the pair I cannot reconcile.

The wedding ring was resized twice, its stones reset, and in 1998 its band replaced entirely; I still hold that my sister inherits the wedding ring, that it never ceased to exist, though none of its 1952 parts remain. Had it instead been destroyed by a jeweler's error in 2010, and had my grandmother, intending to keep the line unbroken, bought an indistinguishable replacement, I would call that a new ring and say the bequest failed — even though the two rings can end up materially indistinguishable, which suggests the difference between them lies somewhere other than what they are made of at a time.

The farm's original farmhouse burned in 1985 and was rebuilt that same year on the same foundation using its salvaged timbers, and no one in the family thinks the farm ceased to exist in the fire; I would say the same of a barn rebuilt plank by plank over a decade. But I confess I have no confidence about the neighboring farm, whose buildings were all demolished in 2005 and rebuilt from the same brick starting in 2015 — whether anything survived that gap, or whether what stands there now is a new farm under an old name.

I want the principle behind these verdicts, if there is one, or an honest statement of which verdict has to go. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with nothing before the first or after the last: point one, the principle stated precisely; points two through five, its verdict on each of the four clusters above (sculpture and clay, the cat and its tail, the ring, the farm and its buildings), flagging wherever it overturns one of my verdicts and defending that in a single clause.
