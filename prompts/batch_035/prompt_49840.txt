I do interp work on deployed models, and my teammate and I have been going in circles for weeks about what "same mechanism" should even mean in our anomaly detector. She's basically a functionalist about it: a mechanism just is a causal role in the computation graph — whatever set of ablation and activation-patching experiments gives you the same counterfactual behavior, that's the mechanism, and it doesn't matter which weights implement it. I'm more of a circuit realist: the mechanism is the actual learned structure in the weights, and two models (or two finetunes of the same model) can pass every functional test we know how to run while running on genuinely different machinery — which is exactly what the anomaly detector is supposed to be catching.

Write the two of us into a dialogue. The setting is a case we actually have: a model that was safety-finetuned, then jailbroken with a prompt style the finetune didn't cover, and we can't agree whether the jailbroken behavior counts as "the same mechanism" as the pre-finetune behavior. Her: yes, same causal role, so by our detector's own lights it's not an anomaly and flagging it would be a false positive. Me: no, the finetune rewired things, the SAE features we were tracking shifted, and a detector that calls this "same" is missing the one thing we built it for.

Keep it technically real — patching, ablations, SAE feature directions, cosine similarity, held-out behavioral probes, that kind of thing — no hand-waving, and nothing so niche we'd both have to go look it up. Neither of us gets to be a strawman. She should push back hard on me that functional individuation is the only thing we can actually define operationally and test, and that my circuit-talk slides toward metaphysics nobody can measure. I should push back hard on her that ablation-based equivalence classes are way too coarse: mechanisms that are behaviorally equivalent can still differ in robustness, in how they generalize, in how they fail under distibution shift — and those differences are the whole point of anomaly detection. By the end we should each have conceded something real, but not everything.

Format: exactly five exchanges, where an exchange means one turn from each of us. Keep each turn short — a couple sentences max, this is how we actually argue, in bursts. No narrator, no scene-setting, no intro or conclusion, just the dialogue itself. And don't resolve it for us — end with us still disagreeing, but with the actual crux located (my guess is it's something like: whether causal-role equivalence becomes fine-grained enough once you condition on the right battery of counterfactuals, or whether no functional test, however clever, can capture implementation-level facts that matter for safety). If you think one of us is secretly right, you're allowed to let it show only in who makes the sharper concession.
