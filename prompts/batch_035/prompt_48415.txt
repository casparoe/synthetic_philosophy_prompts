A popular history of Chinese philosophy I've been reading devotes a chapter to the classical disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi and summarizes it roughly as follows: Mencius argued that human nature is good, by which he meant that people are born already possessing a moral sense — every child, on his view, knows right from wrong the way it knows fire is hot, and needs only to be reminded of what it already knows. Xunzi replied that human nature is bad: we are born selfish and greedy, and left to ourselves we grab and fight, so goodness must be imposed on us from outside by teachers, ritual, and punishment, much as Hobbes would later argue. On this reading, Xunzi is the realist whose darker view was vindicated by how the world actually works, and Mencius the idealist whose optimism could not survive contact with reality. The chapter closes by claiming that modern developmental psychology — experiments showing that infants prefer helpful characters to obstructive ones — has finally settled the ancient dispute in Mencius's favor.

I suspect this summary is partly fair and partly distorting, but I don't know the primary texts well enough to say where the seams are. Please go through it piece by piece. Which claims would a scholar of early Confucianism accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, I want to know whether Mencius's position is fairly rendered as being born with a fully formed moral knowledge rather than something more like incipient tendencies that require cultivation; whether Xunzi really thinks goodness is simply imposed from outside, or whether his account of how the sages arrived at ritual and how anyone can, with effort, become good complicates that picture; and whether the disagreement between the two is best described as an empirical dispute about what infants are like, or as something else — perhaps a dispute about what counts as part of human nature in the first place, or about the relation between what we bring with us and what we make of ourselves. Finally, assess the chapter's closing inference: even granting the infant-study findings, does vindicating Mencius follow from what he actually claimed, or does the conclusion rest on a reading of his position that a careful Mencian would reject? And if the popular framing gets the structure of the debate wrong, say what a more accurate framing would be and which parts of the chapter survive under it.
