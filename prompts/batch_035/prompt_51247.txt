For the past two years, since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading seriously in the philosophy of causation, and I have become stuck on causation by omission. In the laboratory an omission was never a puzzle: the sample that was never exposed to the field simply belonged to a different experiment, and we explained the difference between the samples by citing the field's presence, never its absence. My reading group divides on the issue, and I would like a careful comparison of the two positions. On one side stands the counterfactual account, in the tradition of David Lewis: the orchid died because I didn't water it in the same sense in which the bridge collapsed because the bolt sheared — had the watering occurred, the death would not have. On the other side are those who insist that absences are nothing, and that nothing cannot stand in causal relations, so every putative case must be re-described as ordinary causation by some positive event — the trip I took instead, the tap's remaining shut — together with a good deal of context-dependent selectivity about which of the infinitely many absences we single out. Please compare the two accounts on the following points: what each takes the relata of causation to be; how each handles the selectivity problem (why my not watering the orchid, rather than my neighbor's not watering the rainforest, counts as the cause); how each accommodates the laboratory intuition that an absence explains only against a background expectation; and whether either can make sense of the controlled experiment, where the control group's not receiving the treatment is treated as a genuine causal difference-maker. Which account is ultimately more defensible, and why?
