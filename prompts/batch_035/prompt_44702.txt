been reading rationalist blogs and forums since like 2012, and somewhere along the way (a post I can't find anymore, maybe on LessWrong, maybe Slate Star Codex comments) I picked up the standard take that sad art is just the emotional rollercoaster. You know the picture: it's basically Rozin's benign masochism, same as eating a hot chilli or paying to be dropped down a steep track in a harness — the body fires off a danger/distress signal, the mind knows it's perfectly safe, and the gap between the two is itself the pleasure. I've been using this for years to argue that art-emotions are quarantined: simulated grief that never touches your actual values, which is why crying at a film tells you nothing about how you'd handle real loss, and why "it moved me deeply" is basically worthless as evidence about anything.

but last week someone in a comment thread pushed back and now I'm genuinely unsure. Their point: a rollercoaster is a burst of physiological arousal your brain can relabel in seconds, whereas the classic paradox of tragedy is that we seem to feel real, sustained sadness about people we know never existed, and then somehow want more of it. if the emotion were quarantined you'd expect it to be shallow and easily interrupted, but everyone has had a novel wreck their whole week, and some people's entire personality got rearranged by a book at age 16. so either the quarantine story is wrong, or "quarantined" was doing a lot less work than I thought.

can you audit the analogy properly for me? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the coaster track, the harness, the fear, the safe knowledge that it's a ride, the souvenir photo afterwards — and then where the mapping breaks down. please sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for my "quarantined emotions, hence no evidential value" conclusion versus the ones I shouldn't care about, since I suspect I've been lumping them together. and then give it to me straight: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? if there's a better comparison lying around (sad music as a controlled dose of the real thing? fiction as a flight simulator for grief?), sketch that instead and say what it would predict differently. one more thing — please write the whole answer in British English (realise, colour, theatre, the lot), it's for a mixup with some friends over here and I want it consistent.
