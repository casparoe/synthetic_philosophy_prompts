I'm a graduate student writing a paper that applies no-trade theorems to a somewhat unusual case, and I'd appreciate critical comments on the core argument, which I'll sketch below.

The classic no-trade results say, roughly, that if traders are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers with common priors, and they trade solely on the basis of private information, then the mere willingness of another such trader to take the other side of a bet should kill the trade: my knowing you want to buy is itself information that makes me unwilling to sell. My essay tries to extend this style of reasoning to a new setting: markets increasingly populated by algorithmic trading systems, including large language model-based agents, that are trained on overlapping public corpora. The standard defense of trading in the face of no-trade theorems is that real agents have heterogeneous priors, different risk profiles, and non-informational motives (liquidity needs, hedging). But my thought is that algorithmic agents with shared training data are unusually close to the theorem's idealizations: they may have something approaching literally common priors, since their "beliefs" are distilled from the same corpus, and they can be given identical risk parameters by their designers. Yet these systems demonstrably trade with each other at enormous volume, and a naive reading of the no-trade literature suggests such trades should be pure noise, or even that mutual awareness of shared training data should collapse their willingness to trade toward zero. I want to argue that this creates a new puzzle for the epistemology of markets: either the no-trade framework misses something important (e.g., that "shared priors" over token prediction is not the same as shared priors over the economic variables that matter for the trade), or algorithmic markets should be expected to converge toward a strange equilibrium in which most volume is essentially self-canceling noise that nonetheless performs a social epistemic function, like a distributed computation that rehearses consensus rather than discovering it.

Please comment on three things specifically. First, accuracy: am I representing the no-trade theorems and their assumptions correctly, and are there known weakenings or extensions of the results that already address my puzzle, rendering it less novel than I think? Second, the conceptual pivot: is the move from "trained on the same corpus" to "approximately common priors" defensible, or does it conflate different notions of a prior (predictive distributions over text versus credences over trading-relevant propositions)? Third, the payoff: is the concluding claim about self-canceling volume as distributed consensus-rehearsal a genuinely interesting contribution to the epistemology of markets, or a fancy restatement of the observation that agents with common priors have nothing to bet about? Please be candid about the weakest link in the argument.
