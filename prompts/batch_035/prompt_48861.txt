Regulators now prohibit online platforms from designing their interfaces to manipulate users: Article 25 of the EU's Digital Services Act forbids online interfaces that "deceive, manipulate or distort" users' decisions, and its enforcement guidance targets a familiar taxonomy of dark patterns -- forced continuity that makes cancellation harder than sign-up, intermediate steps inserted to fatigue users out of choosing the cheaper option, "confirmshaming" that makes declining an offer carry a sting, nagging dialogs that reappear until the user relents, hidden costs disclosed at the last possible screen. Meanwhile, the same platforms are entirely free to run emotionally charged advertising, craft brand narratives that associate products with status or belonging, deploy scarcity claims and social proof ("500 people are looking at this hotel right now"), and otherwise persuade users in ways that plainly exploit the same psychological machinery the banned designs exploit. So the practice, viewed as a whole, draws a line and punishes crossing it: some influence through design is prohibited, and other influence through design is not merely allowed but is the business model.

What account of the difference between manipulation and legitimate persuasion does that regulatory line presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Is it that manipulation works by bypassing deliberation rather than engaging it -- and if so, what does the line presuppose about how normal deliberation proceeds, given that an appeal to fear or status also does much of its work outside the deliberative channel? Is it that manipulation exploits identifiable cognitive weaknesses while persuasion addresses the person as they are -- and could a defender of the line hold that in the absence of any substantive account of which motivations are the user's own? Or does the line presuppose something more modest, perhaps only that there is a morally significant difference between exploiting the structure of a decision task (defaults, friction, timing) and exploiting its content (what is said about the product)?

Say whether the presuppositions are internally consistent -- a regulator banning last-screen cost disclosure while permitting the whole apparatus of emotional marketing has some explaining to do -- and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me what the line would look like if the underlying theory were replaced: if manipulation were instead understood on an autonomy-undermining model, where the wrong is not in how the influence reaches its target but in whether the resulting choice is genuinely attributable to the user, which currently legal practices would have to go, and which banned dark patterns would arguably survive? I'd also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which substantive theories of manipulation would license more or less this same line, so that the regulator needn't be committed to any particular one?
