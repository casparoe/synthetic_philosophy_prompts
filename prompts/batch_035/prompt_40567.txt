Science and ideas journalist here, deep in a feature about free will, and more specifically the old sphex wasp chestnut. Quick refresher if you need it: the female digger wasp drags her paralysed cricket to the burrow, pops it on the threshold, nips inside for a quick inspection, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. Nudge the cricket an inch to the side while she's inside and she comes out, repositions it, and — crucially — does the inspection all over again. Nudge it every time and she'll re-check forever, apparently. Dennett's term for this kind of mechanical rigidity was "sphexishness", and it's become the go-to image for behaviour that looks smart but isn't really.

My problem: everyone uses the wasp as a punchline — ha, dumb robot — but nobody explains what the wasp has *less of* than a rat, or a corvid, or me, or a modern LLM, for that matter. And I want an example, not vibes. So here's the ask: build me one concrete, worked example of a genuinely borderline case on the rigid-to-flexible spectrum, one where the verdict could honestly go either way. It can be a real-ish system (a chess engine, a delivery robot, one of those RL agents that learns to exploit its own physics simulator, a scrub jay) or a cleaned-up hypothetical — your call — but walk me through exactly where the difficulty lives. Not "it's complicated", but *this specific feature* is what makes it hard to call.

I keep circling an idea I've half-invented for the piece, so engage with it in some way — use it, sharpen it, or tell me its bin-able. Call it "reprise sensitivity": does the system treat its second encounter with a literally identical situation differently from the first? A sphex-ish system doesn't (it can't), and I suspect a flexible one always sort of does. Is that a real marker of agency or just another behavioural tell dressed up?

Two more things I'd really like in the answer. First, don't let the example lean only on behavioural clues — if the whole case for flexibility is "it did something different this time", say why that might be cheap (a glitchy thermostat also does something different). Second, and this is the bit I'll actually be held to: tell me explicitly what would change your verdict. What new fact about the system, or what argument, would flip you from "basically sphexish" to "genuinely flexible"? And where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail? I don't want to publish a confident line I can't defend down the pub.

Keep the audience in mind, too: educated general readers, definately not philosophers. If you need jargon, earn it.
