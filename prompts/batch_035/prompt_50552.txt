I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of time, and we've just spent three weeks on tomorrow's sea battle — the ancient puzzle about whether statements about the future already have truth values. My own verdicts on cases are now on the table, and my tutorial partner, who is a devoted champion of Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic, tells me they don't add up to any coherent story. I'd like to know whether she's right, or whether there's a principle underneath them that I haven't managed to state.

Here are my verdicts, all concerning statements made today about tomorrow. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow": neither true nor false — the admirals haven't yet decided. "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be a sea battle tomorrow": true, and I refuse to give this one up. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow and there will not be a sea battle tomorrow": false. "The sun will rise tomorrow": true — and I don't feel any tension with the first verdict, since the sun's rising seems somehow already settled in a way the admirals' decision is not. "The battle will be won by the Greeks," said on the assumption that the fleets will in fact meet: neither true nor false. Then the two I keep going back and forth on. First, "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or the fleet will remain in harbour": this feels as though it must be true, the way the excluded-middle disjunction is, but the disjuncts aren't strict contradictories (a storm could wreck the fleet tonight), and if I'm honest it also feels like one large future contingent rather than a logical truth. Second, "It was true yesterday, while the admirals were still arguing, that there would be a sea battle tomorrow": part of me says that of course it was, and part of me says that if it was true yesterday, then it was settled yesterday, which is precisely what I denied in my first verdict.

My tentative principle is that a future-tensed statement is true exactly when what it says is already settled — fixed by how things presently are, together with the laws of nature — and otherwise lacks a truth value altogether. But I can see the principle needs repair: it isn't obvious what makes the excluded-middle disjunction true when neither disjunct is, and I genuinely cannot decide whether the "sea battle or harbour" disjunction counts as settled once the storm is in the picture. So here is what I want: is there a single account — my settledness idea, suitably repaired, my partner's three-valued truth tables, or something else entirely — that delivers all of my verdicts, or nearly all? Where it cannot, which verdicts should be surrendered, and why those rather than the others? What does the best account say about the two cases I waver on? And what would it predict about a case I haven't yet considered — say, "The coin I will toss tomorrow will land heads or will land tails," where my instinct says true until I remember that coins occasionally stand on edge?
