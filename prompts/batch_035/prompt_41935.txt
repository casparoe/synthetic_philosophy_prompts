i teach an upper-level ethics seminar, 15 students, 90 minutes, and next week we hit the rescue cases — you know, the famous 1977 paper about whether the numbers should count when you can save either the one or the many, the one with the coin flip. Design me an activity where they run into the problem before I name any of it.

Rough shape I have in mind: they play a mountain rescue commitee during a storm, I play dispatch, there's one helicopter with limited fuel, and each round the committee picks between two crash sites before the weather closes in. A real coin is available at every decision point. Escalate across rounds from a straight 1v1 tie, through 1v2 and 1v5, up to 1 vs an entire campsite (call it 30-40 people). I want the 1v2 round to carry the mathematical punch: someone in the room should notice that flipping the coin between the two sites gives each of the two people at the second site the same chance of rescue as the lone person at the first — the coin treats the pair as a single claimant, while a lottery over persons gives all three a third each — and that neither procedure makes the numbers do any work even though the expected-deaths count does. If nobody notices on their own, tell me how to seed the room so someone does.

Give me materials (casualty cards with one humanizing line each, nothing gory), the round structure, a payoff scheme with no real money but some score they'll actually care about, and a debrief that maps each round onto a piece of the debate: the tie round onto the thought that equal claims just cancel out; the 1v2 round onto the question of whether groups can be claimants or only persons can; then the threshold hunt — each group writes down the exact n at which they'd finally drop the coin, and then has to give the reason, so they get to watch themself fail to produce anything that distinguishes n from n+1. that margin problem is the part i most want to survive into the debrief.

Also: I expect the committee to start voting on its own decisions somewhere around round three or four — flag that in the debrief, since the vote is numbers counting through the back door, and tell me whether to let it ride or shut it down. Include the usual honest notes: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this, and what the activity flatly can't reproduce — nobody is actually dying, the rescuers pay no cost of their own, so the whole facing-each-person aspect has to come from me in the debrief rather than from the rules, and I need to know which parts are irreducibly lecture.

One last thing: at the very end, tell me how confident you are that this runs as described, and single out the piece you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the soft spot is than get a confident pitch.
