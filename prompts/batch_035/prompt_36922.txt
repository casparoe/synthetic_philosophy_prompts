In my AP Biology class we have been studying levels of selection, and my teacher has been ruling on examples one by one. I want to work out the rule she is applying before she presents next week's case, but I have gotten stuck, and I would appreciate your help thinking it through.

Her verdicts so far: the peacock's tail is individual selection, full stop. Ground squirrels that give alarm calls and thereby draw hawks toward themselves are kin selection, and she insists this is not group selection. Sterile worker honeybees are also kin selection, not group selection, even though she concedes the colony as a whole functions rather like an organism. When we discussed a colony of genetically identical bacteria that secretes an enzyme into its shared surroundings — so that no single cell's contribution is distinguishable and the enzyme level is a property of the colony as a whole — she called that genuine group selection, with the colony as the unit of selection. Finally, when a classmate asked about slime mold cells from genetically different spores that aggregate and sacrifice themselves to build a stalk, she said that is not group selection either, because by that point the aggregate behaves as an organism and so it is just selection at the organism level.

Next week's case involves a colony of aphids founded by a single asexual female, in which some individuals develop into sterile soldiers that defend the colony against predators. Every aphid in the colony is a genetic clone of every other, yet the sacrifice is made by particular, individually identifiable soldiers.

What will she say about the aphids? My problem is that I can see at least two rules that fit everything she has said so far. One rule keys on genetic identity: group selection only when the group's members are clones, so the group is effectively a single genotype, with kin selection reserved for related-but-distinct individuals. Another keys on where the trait lives: group selection only when the trait under selection is a property of the group as a whole, like the colony's enzyme level, while a cost paid by a particular individual counts as kin or individual selection. These rules agree on all her previous verdicts, as far as I can tell, but they seem to come apart on the aphids, which are clonal yet defended by individual sacrifices.

Please predict her verdict on the aphid case, lay out the candidate rules consistent with her track record and what each one predicts, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and suggest the further example — beyond the aphids — that would most cleanly distinguish between the rules. If you think there are other rules that also fit her verdicts, such as one about whether the group functions as an integrated organism, I would like to hear about those too, including whether any of her verdicts quietly rules them out.
