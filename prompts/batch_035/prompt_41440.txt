I'm a senior in high school, and in two weeks I'm presenting my extended essay to our school's philosophy society. My essay defends a higher-order theory of consciousness — specifically, the idea that a mental state becomes conscious only when there is a suitable higher-order thought about that state, and I argue for the dispositionalist version, where the higher-order thought does not have to be an occurrent episode but can be a standing readiness to have the thought. I use the example of suddenly noticing, mid-argument with my sister, that I've been annoyed for ten minutes: the annoyance was there all along, but only the retroactive higher-order awareness makes it part of my conscious life, and I claim the dispositionalist reading explains both the delay and the fact that the notice feels like discovering rather than creating. The audience will be about twenty students, plus three teachers: my psychology teacher, who thinks metacognition experiments already explain everything the theory is after and that the extra machinery is redundant; a classmate who spent last summer reading Dennett and thinks the whole question of what makes a state conscious is a confusion we should dissolve rather than answer; and the philosophy teacher who supervises the society and takes a broadly Kantian line that self-knowledge is constitutive rather than observational.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I'd like the objections I'm actually going to get from this room, ordered roughly by how much damage each would do to my essay, with each phrased the way that particular person would put it — the psychology teacher in terms of experiments and parsimony, the classmate in terms of intuitions about zombies or "mere" description, the philosophy teacher in terms of whether I've smuggled a little inner observer back in. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where the reply is soft, and one thing I should concede up front so I don't look naive. I especially want to be ready for the misrepresentation problem — the worry that a higher-order thought could make me conscious of an angry state when there was no anger there, so that consciousness would be consciousness of something that doesn't exist — and for the regress worry about what makes the higher-order thought itself conscious, since I'm counting on the dispositionalist move to stop that regress and I'm not fully confident it works. Please also include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks: mine is about whether infants and dogs are conscious on my view, but tell me if there's a worse one I haven't thought of.
