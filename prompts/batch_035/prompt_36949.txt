I've been going back and forth (ha) on the two envelope thing and I think I've finally mapped out what my gut actually says, but I can't find the rule behind it. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of versions:

1. Two envelopes, one has twice as much as the other, no other info. Before opening I can switch for free, and I can keep switching as long as I want, back and forth. My gut: it obviously doesn't matter, and anyone who switches more than once is being silly.

2. Same setup but I open mine and see $100, and a reliable person tells me the other envelope has $50 or $200, each 50/50. Fine, switch, that seems clearly right.

3. Same but I open mine, see $100, and know literally nothing except that one envelope has twice the other. If I do the same math as in 2 — the other is $50 or $200, "must be" 50/50 — I get that switching is worth $125 in expectation vs my $100. My gut says this math is somehow cheating, but I can't point to were the step goes wrong. Verdict: switching doesn't actually help here, or at least I wouldn't do it.

4. I get to watch the envelopes being filled: mine gets $100 no matter what, and the other one gets $50 or $200 depending on a coin flip I can't see. Switch, obviously.

5. The doubling game: they start with $1 and flip a coin, doubling on heads, stopping on the first tails. Whatever they ended up with goes in my envelope, and the other envelope gets double or half of that by yet another coin flip. I open mine and see $8. Here the 50/50 half-or-double thing seems to actually be true, so switch? Verdict: switch, but I'm not comfortable about it, something about there being no cap feels bad.

6. I see $4,096 and I happen to know the amounts are always powers of 2 between $1 and $4,096. Keep — the other one has to be $2,048.

7. I see $1 and I know the amounts are always at least $1. Switch — the other has to be $2.

8. Me and a friend each take one envelope, we both open them privately, and we each reason "mine is either half or double the other guy's, gotta be 50/50, so switching favors me." Verdict: we can't both be right, at most one of us can actually come out ahead, so at least one of us is reasoning badly.

So is there one principle that gets all of these? The one I keep circling is something like "only switch when you have real evidence about how the amounts were made," but that lets in 3's math way too easily for my taste, and it doesn't explain why 1 and 3 feel the same to me while 2 and 4 feel completely different even though the calculation is identical in all four. What I'd love is: the principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new case; what it says about a case I haven't listed — say I see $100 and I know the pair was either ($50,$100) or ($100,$200), but I'd bet 2:1 it was the first; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it just can't save, so I know which one to give up. Also, if the principle can explain what exactly is illegitimate about the calculation in 3, that would settle a standing argument with my brother, who is convinced 3 is airtight.
