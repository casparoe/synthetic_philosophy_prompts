I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on the philosophy of international law, centered on the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, and I keep running into what seems to me a deeper version of the familiar victors'-justice complaint. The standard worry is easy to state: the victorious Allies sat in judgment over the vanquished for conduct — above all, the waging of aggressive war — that no positive law of the time unambiguously criminalized at the level of individual responsibility. The prosecution had to lean on instruments like the 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact, which had renounced war as an instrument of national policy but said nothing about prosecuting individual leaders, and on tribunal charters drafted after the fact by the occupying powers themselves. At Tokyo this was, if anything, more visible: the tribunal exercised broader temporal jurisdiction than Nuremberg, stretching back to the 1931 invasion of Manchuria, and the convictions rested heavily on an allencompassing charge of conspiracy to wage aggressive war — which is one reason judges like Pal and Röling dissented in whole or in part. But I don't want to re-litigate whether these tribunals were really courts at all. I want to think structurally about the retroactivity problem, and I'd like your help.

Here is the framework I want you to work with; I suspect the Nuremberg debate quietly imports it from a different controversy. In the famous 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller over the postwar German grudge-informer cases — a wife, wanting to be rid of her husband, reports him to the Nazi authorities for derogatory remarks he had made about Hitler, and after the war a West German court must decide how to treat her — the possible routes were essentially these: let her go unpunished; convict her under a frankly retroactive statute; or, what the court actually did, convict her under the pre-existing criminal code by reading the Nazi denunciation provisions against their plain grain, on the ground that no statute so contrary to decent conscience could justify her act. Hart regarded this last route as smuggling retroactivity in under the guise of interpretation, while Fuller argued that on a properly moral conception of legality it was no smuggling at all but the truth. My question is whether this set of options transfers to Nuremberg and Tokyo — and I suspect the transfer is imperfect, because at Nuremberg there was no domestic statute to strain and no standing legislature to pass a retroactive one, so the tribunals may really have taken the third route dressed as the first: crimes against peace were treated as already criminal because the Pact had made war illegal, with individual liability quietly added on top.

Please write an essay that does three things. First, assess whether Hart's menu of options is even the right frame for international tribunals, or whether the options must be reinterpreted when the only legislature in sight is an occupying coalition issuing charters for ad hoc courts — perhaps the charter itself should be counted as legislation, which changes the analysis of what is retroactive and what is not. Second, treat the Tokyo complications separately: does the broad conspiracy charge and the pattern of judicial dissents show that the retroactivity problem was worse there than at Nuremberg, or merely that it was more honestly exposed? Third, and most important to me, reach a verdict on the question underneath my paper: setting aside whether the individual defendants deserved their fates, was the retroactivity at these tribunals the kind that can be redeemed going forward — can a conviction route that pretends the law was already there generate genuine law for the future, a precedent that really binds successor tribunals — or does it merely settle the past? On this last point, please don't end on a fence-sitting note: defend a definite answer, even if it is a conditional one.
