I write novels for a living, which means that characterological stability is not an academic question for me but a professional wager: readers accept that my protagonist lies on page three because she lied in chapter one, reviewers praise the "psychological realism" of a figure who is brave, petty, and loyal in roughly the same proportions across two hundred pages, and my whole craft depends on the thought that people carry something stable with them from situation to situation. A few years ago a philosopher friend — we were both teaching at the same artists' colony — pressed John Doris's situationist critique on me: the Milgram experiments, the seminary students who stepped past a slumped stranger in a doorway because they were told they were running late, the older honesty studies in which children's truthfulness varied almost arbitrarily from one classroom task to the next. His summary was that empirical psychology has quietly dismantled the global traits — honesty, courage, compassion as robust, cross-situational dispositions — that Aristotelian virtue ethics, and my kind of fiction, both presuppose. I assumed this would be a live dispute; instead, when I followed it into the literature, I found a debate that has been running since the early 2000s and appears thoroughly stalled. The situationists say the psychological evidence rules out robust traits; the defenders reply that virtue ethics never needed traits that strong, or that virtues are rare and achieved rather than routine, or that the relevant traits are local and socially structured rather than cross-situational, or that studies designed to manipulate subjects can't speak to capacities exercised by agents who are actually paying attention. Everyone has said all of this to everyone else, and nobody has moved.

Here is my suspicion, offered as a suspicion from someone outside the field: the two sides may not be disagreeing about people at all. They seem to mean different things by a trait — the situationists counting frequencies of similar behavior across situations, the defenders counting a structured sensitivity to reasons that need not show up on any particular occasion — and they also seem to disagree about what counts as data, with one side treating laboratory contrivances as revealing and the other treating them as artifacts designed to catch people at their worst. If that is right, the dispute is partly verbal, a fight over the word "trait" that would dissolve under better definitions, and partly a deeper clash about whether philosophy or experimental psychology gets to set the terms. But I may be missing a substantive empirical core — perhaps the dispute really turns on whether longitudinally stable individual differences exist at all, which is a testable question that for some reason nobody has tested to the disputants' satisfaction. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, or about standards of evidence, or about which cases count, or two inquiries running under one name? And tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis — a better taxonomy, a longitudinal study, a distinction nobody has yet drawn — or whether the standoff is itself the finding. I would also, frankly, like to know whether the answer leaves my professional wager intact: can a novelist keep writing consistent characters even if the situationists are right about what people are?
