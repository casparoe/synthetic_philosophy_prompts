I run a philosophy-of-perception reading group, and our last two sessions covered Molyneux's question: a person blind from birth, newly granted sight, is shown a sphere and a cube before being allowed to touch them — can they tell which is which? The students have now read the early modern exchange, but they have come away thinking the only options are a plain yes and a plain no, and they are already pattern-matching to whichever label they find congenial. I want a decision-tree handout that sorts them by what they actually believe, before they know the names of the positions they are being sorted into.

The tree's single entry point is the concrete case: tomorrow morning, a congenitally blind adult recovers sight; before any touching, pointing, or exploring, they are shown a polished sphere and a matte cube side by side. From there, every internal node must be a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads — a question about cases and about what they would predict or allow, never a question about which view they favor. I leave the exact branching structure to you, but the tree must somewhere separate on at least the following fault lines, since the real disagreements live there:

1. Whether the question is answerable by experiment at all, or whether the answer is fixed in advance by what we mean by "seeing" and "knowing."
2. If experiment is relevant, whether the question is about an idealized subject with fully functioning restored vision, or about actual patients recovered from congenital cataracts — whose acuity, contrast sensitivity, and oculomotor control may be too degraded for the results to count either way.
3. What counts as telling them apart: an above-chance forced-choice guess, a confident identification, or the ability to justify the identification in words. A subject who says, "I can't name them, but that one looks the same from every side, so it must be the sphere" — does that settle the question, and if so, in whose favor?
4. Whether the answer is required to be immediate, or whether a subject who reasons, hesitates, and revises is still answering "by sight alone," and what the difference is between an inference from visual input and a learned association formed over minutes or hours.
5. Whether the verdict for the classic pair — uniform curvature versus sharp vertices — is expected to extend to harder pairs: a sphere with one flat spot versus a cube with slightly rounded edges, or irregular objects sharing no obvious geometric signature.

The leaves must include at minimum: the strict empiricist no; the mediated yes on which the subject can infer, though not immediately perceive, the correspondence between the seen and the felt; the immediate yes on which some spatial content is available cross-modally from the start; the empirical dissolution, on which the historical and modern cataract-reversal outcomes cannot in principle answer the question because the test conditions never instantiate the case as posed; the skill-based dissolution, on which a newly sighted subject does not yet see in the relevant sense at all, so the question is premature rather than mistaken; and the conceptual dissolution, on which the answer falls out of a distinction between receiving visual input and knowing how to deploy it, whatever the experiments show.

Beyond the tree itself, I want three things. First, name each leaf, but only after the branches have located it — the taxonomy should be built from the logical space, not pasted over a list of positions. Second, note any combinations of branch answers that occupy no leaf, and diagnose each briefly: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has thought to defend it? Third, and most important to me, flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate. I have a specific worry here: I see no question that distinguishes the mediated-yes from an empiricist no with a fast learning curve, since both predict failure at time zero and success shortly thereafter — if your tree inherits that blind spot, say so, and if you can dissolve it with a well-chosen case, tell me what the case would have to show.

Constraints: the handout should fit on one page, so cap the tree at roughly ten internal nodes and eight leaves. Where you need diagnostic vignettes, invent them — the subject with three minutes to think out loud, the sphere with a flat spot, the child tested months after surgery — but do not present any invented case as an actual experiment, and keep whatever you say about the real surgical history to what is common knowledge. The audience is advanced undergraduates: assume they know the question and its principal discussants, but nothing about the contemporary literature.
