I am a final-year PhD candidate in philosophy of cognitive science, and my viva is in three weeks. The penultimate chapter of my thesis — the one my supervisor calls the load-bearing wall — argues that Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality is not the metaphysical boundary he takes it to be, and that Dennett's two-bitser example points to a better picture: the contents attributable to a coin mechanism are fixed not by the designers' intentions but by the evolving practice of use, so that the line between genuine and merely as-if content becomes a matter of degree fixed by the predictive and explanatory work the intentional stance does. I then extend the argument to a machine-learning content-moderation classifier of the kind my industrial sponsor built during my placement: its states, I claim, carry genuine but derived content in exactly the two-bitser's way, and this matters practically for how the sponsor explains false positives to regulators.

My examiners will not be a friendly audience. The external is a long-time defender of Searle on intrinsic intentionality and the Chinese Room; the internal examiner works on enactive and embodied approaches and is openly suspicious of anything that smacks of analytical functionalism. My own supervisor is a Dennettian of the strictest observance who nevertheless thinks my extension to learned classifiers is a bridge too far — he has told me the viva will be his last chance to get me to retreat to coin machines. Since I am the first in my family to sit a viva and the first in my cohort to defend a chapter that leans on an industrial collaboration, I would rather over-prepare than discover mid-defence that my three audiences object for three different reasons.

Please prepare my defence sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by the damage they would do, and separated by source — the objections the external will raise, phrased the way a Searlean would put them (for instance, that the two-bitser's beliefs are no more than a useful fiction because there is nothing it is like to be the machine, or that derivation without an original source is a pointless regress); the objections the internal examiner will raise in enactivist vocabulary (for instance, that content grounded in a practice of use still presupposes interpreters, so I have smuggled original intentionality back in at the level of the community); and the objections my supervisor might raise about the classifier extension specifically (for instance, whether a system whose internal structure no engineer can fully articulate can have content fixed by use at all, given that the users cannot tell which features it is responding to). For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede rather than contest. Include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks — I believe it concerns whether my moderation classifier's derived beliefs differ in any principled way from a thermostat's — and tell me how to survive it if it comes.
