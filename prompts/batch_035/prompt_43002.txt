I've covered science and technology for nearly fifteen years, and somewhere along the way I accumulated settled gut verdicts about which forms of automation involve a genuine loss in the meaning of work — as opposed to mere displacement, which is an economic problem. A colleague has challenged me to figure out whether my verdicts add up to anything, and I have to admit they may not. Here they are.

Spreadsheets wiping out the bookkeeping clerk in the 1980s: no meaningful loss. That work was drudgery, and the world is not poorer for its passing. CAD replacing the manual draftsman: also fine, on balance — the drafting skill seemed to get absorbed into design judgment rather than discarded. Robotic warehouse pickers and self-checkout machines: fine, at least where the workers land on comparable tasks; the bad outcomes there look like a distribution problem, not a meaning problem. A carpenter I profiled whose hand-planing was replaced by machine-planed moldings the customers could not distinguish, at the same wages: a genuine loss, and I have never been able to say exactly why. An AI system at a regional paper that now drafts routine obituaries and earnings summaries, with the reporters reassigned to longer features: this one troubles me more than the spreadsheets ever did, though I suspect a bookkeeping clerk would call obituary-writing drudgery too. And the hand-loom weavers displaced by power looms: a loss at the time, since the craft was their identity, yet I don't think the long run owes them an apology.

Two pairs look outright inconsistent to me. The draftsman and the carpenter both had hand skills replaced by machines producing indistinguishable output, yet only the carpenter's case reads as a loss. And the clerks and the obituary writers both had routine cognitive work automated away, yet only the reporters' case does. My tentative repair is that the loss tracks whether the skill was a source of identity and pride for the worker, not how routinized the work was — but that makes the badness of automation hostage to contingent preferences, which seems wrong: it would imply that automating a task is harmless whenever the people doing it happen to dislike it, and harmful whenever they happen to love it.

So I'd like help with three things. First, is there a principle — or a short list of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough to apply mechanically to new cases? Second, what does it say about the cases I haven't resolved, especially the piano tuner displaced by tuning apps whose trained ear was his life's achievement, and whether the spreadsheet verdict should flip if some clerks genuinely loved the tidiness of their ledgers? Third, an honest accounting: which of my verdicts cannot be saved by any principled repair, and ought simply to be abandoned?
