So I'm a novelist, currently stuck in the weeds of edits on a book with an unusual structure, and somewhere in the middle of it I got obsessed with Escher's Drawing Hands — the one where the two drawn hands each appear to be drawing the other — and with Hofstadter's point about it in Gödel, Escher, Bach: the loop between the hands is only intelligible because something stays outside it and untouched, what he calls the inviolate level (Escher's actual hand, the paper, whatever). The tangle downstairs is bought and paid for by the untangled upstairs.

Here's my problem: whenever I try to think through this myself I default to lazy metafiction — novelist dreams up character who starts writing the novelist back, narrator realizes she's in the book, etc. My editor has seen that a hundred times and so has everyone else. What I want, and what I'm asking you for, is a thought experiment built to order, that I can use as the structural spine of the book.

Construct a realistic scenario — everyday setting, no sci-fi, no supernatural, no dreaming, no actual Escher prints on walls — in which two levels genuinely create each other's conditions: what A does sets up what B is able to do, and what B does in turn sets up what A is able to do, so neither level is cleanly above the other. AND, at the same time, a third element must remain inviolate: outside the loop, unvisited by it, but doing the grounding work that lets the loop run at all.

Constraints, and I mean these seriously:

- The mutual dependence has to be generated by the ordinary causal facts of the scenario, not stipulated. "A and B define each other" or "each is the condition of the other's possibility" is cheating — I want each direction of dependence to be independently visible in the story.
- No symmetry that's just the same fact described twice. If reversing the labels A and B changes nothing, it's not a tangle, it's a mirror.
- The inviolate third element should be doing real work — I want to be able to point at it and say the loop runs because it doesn't get drawn into it.
- Then give me a variant where you tangle that third element too, and show exactly what breaks. Infinite regress? The levels collapse into one? The whole thing stops being intelligible? Be precise about the failure mode.
- Finish by telling me which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens if you remove it, and how someone who insists that strange loops go all the way down — no inviolate level anywhere, Hofstadter at his most radical about consciousness maybe being such a loop — would attack the case.

You're a philosopher of mind who has read GEB much more carefully than I have, and you've also spent enough time around working novelists to know that a conceit that can't survive being asked "wait, why does this happen?" is worthless in a manuscript. Think carefully and start with some brainstorming — a few candidate scenarios before you commit, with a quick note on why you're rejecting the ones you reject — then build the winner out properly and walk through the checklist above one item at a time. And write it so my editor, who has never read a page of Hofstadter, can follow the whole thing: the scenario first as a plain story, then the analysis. One more thing: don't make it about writing a novel. I'm serious. That's the trap and I know it's the trap and I'll fall into it anyway if you leave the door open.
