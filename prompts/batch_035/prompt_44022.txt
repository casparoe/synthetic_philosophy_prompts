I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on levels of explanation in cognitive science, and I want a prep sheet for the question period. The audience is about a dozen people: roughly half are cognitive neuroscientists who regard belief/desire talk as a temporary bookkeeping device that mature neurobiology will eliminate; three or four are philosophers of mind, including one who takes a realist, Fodor-style line on propositional attitudes (belief ascriptions state robust internal facts, and either they are mostly true or mostly false) and one pragmatist who thinks Dennett settled all of this in the Real Patterns paper and will be irritated by any apparent rehash; and two machine-learning researchers invited for the modeling perspective. My thesis: the intentional stance is vindicated by a real-pattern criterion — beliefs and desires are real in the way any predictive pattern is real, namely when tracking the pattern yields compression relative to not tracking it. The novelty I'm defending is a modification: the relevant compression should be assessed relative to a specified class of predictors, rather than relative to human users simpliciter, and I argue that the class should include non-human predictors — statistical models, idealized agents. This is what makes intentional patterns objective rather than a courtesy extended to creatures enough like us. The consequence I am bracing for is that, on my criterion, patterns that well-trained machine-learning systems detect more efficiently than humans do — including patterns no human user ever exploits — can count as real intentional states of the systems they compress, even where nobody is disposed to take the intentional stance toward them. I take this bullet publicly; I'd rather defend it than have the ML people force it on me.

Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by expected damage (likelihood times severity), each phrased in the idiom of whoever will raise it — the eliminativist neuroscientists will press one way, the realist philosopher another, the pragmatist another, and the ML researchers will find the corner cases. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, with my best available answer even so. Finally, tell me which single concession, if volunteered early, buys the most goodwill with this room — and which one I must never make, because the paper collapses if I grant it.
