You are a professional metaphysician specializing in the philosophy of modality, writing for colleagues who know Lewis's "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" and Kripke-style transworld identity at the level of a graduate seminar, but who are impatient with textbook recaps, so do not re-explain the basics of either view beyond what the demonstration strictly needs.

I need a thought experiment built to order. Construct a case in which counterpart theory and transworld identity deliver opposite verdicts on a single de re modal claim about an ordinary person, where the divergence is generated specifically by counterpart theory's tolerance for plural or competing counterpart relations against transworld identity's insistence on a bivalent answer. Stay off the well-worn ground of origins and essences, where the two views are already expected to diverge; the disagreement I want concerns a claim about what an ordinary person could have done or become. Constraints on the scenario: it must be ordinary-life (a career decision, a relationship, a place to live — not a demon, not a machine that duplicates people), it must be one paragraph, and it must not stipulate the verdicts — they must fall out of each theory's own machinery, not be written into the plot. To make the machinery matter, aim for a situation in which two candidates in another possible world are close to equally similar to the actual subject, so that the counterpart relation itself is vague or selectable in more than one way, while transworld identity has to say either that that world's person is our subject or that she is not. The purpose of the case is to calibrate intuitions about whether ordinary de re modal claims are determinately true or false; a scenario that simply announces which world is closest would defeat the point.

Please deliver the following, in order. First, a brief brainstorm: sketch two or three candidate setups and say why you settled on the one you use. Second, the scenario itself. Third, a demonstration that it meets each specification: state the de re claim; reconstruct counterpart theory's verdict, being explicit about which counterpart relation you are invoking and how the similarity ordering is weighted (weighting by what matters to the subject's own cares is legitimate, and so is weighting by minute qualitative resemblance — say which you use and why); then give transworld identity's verdict and its grounds. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed. Fourth, argue as a defender of whichever theory comes out worse would attack the case — e.g., by denying that a thing can be determinately itself while it is indeterminate what it could have done, or by charging the other side with smuggling haecceitism in through the back door.

Finally — and this matters most — do not leave the options to me. Take a definite, committed position on the de re claim in your own scenario: is it determinately true, determinately false, or lacking a truth value, and which theory's treatment of the case do you endorse? Defend that commitment rather than laying out the live alternatives and stepping back. Think carefully before committing: the demonstration that the case works matters as much as the scenario, and a case that collapses under the attack you yourself reconstruct is a failed case. Write the whole answer in polished American English.
