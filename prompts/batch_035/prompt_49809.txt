I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though a stray encounter with the extended mind thesis — the idea that tools a mind relies on constantly can count as parts of the mind itself — has gotten under my skin. My work makes the question feel less abstract than it might: I write code I could not reconstruct from memory, I often understand a bug only after writing a test that reproduces it, and my editor's autocomplete regularly finishes my thoughts before I have consciously finished them.

Please write a dialogue between two characters: Dana, who defends the extended mind thesis, and Marcus, who holds that cognition is strictly a matter of what happens inside the skull. Set the scene in the break room of a software company on the afternoon the build servers and developer tooling go down for maintenance, leaving everyone to work, as Marcus puts it, "with their actual brains."

A few requirements:

1. Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman. Dana should not win by rhetorical fiat, and Marcus should not be a fuddy-duddy who merely distrusts technology.

2. Dana should argue primarily from cases specific to software engineering rather than the stock examples from the literature: a developer's muscle-memory keybindings that have to be consciously recalled on an unfamiliar keyboard; the practice of debugging by scattering log statements rather than by introspecting one's own intentions; a version-control history functioning as a kind of external autobiographical memory one consults without hesitation; a failing test as something one literally thinks with. She should also connect these to the general parity-style argument — that if an external process functions the way an internal one does, denying it the status of cognition is just skin-and-skull prejudice.

3. Marcus should press the strongest objections available to him: the worry that something being causally necessary for a cognitive process does not make it a constituent of that process; the distinction between having access to knowledge and possessing it; and the observation that when our tools break we describe ourselves as hampered or slowed, not as having lost a part of our minds — whereas no one describes a stroke that way.

4. The dialogue should have genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the specific point the other just made, concede what ought to be conceded, and re-frame rather than repeat. At least one exchange should trouble Dana (for instance, what happens to the parity argument when the external process is not always available, or when its outputs are endorsed only after checking), and at least one should trouble Marcus (for instance, whether his own distinction between memory and access survives the fact that he cannot say how he knows things he demonstrably knows).

5. Since I have no philosophical training, technical terms and distinctions should be introduced naturally through the conversation rather than assumed on the reader's part.

6. The dialogue should end unresolved, but with the disagreement sharpened into a precise question the two of them would need to answer to make further progress.

Please aim for roughly 900 to 1,100 words.
