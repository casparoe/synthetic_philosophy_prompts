At a departmental drinks evening, I asked a physicist friend whether time could, in principle, run the other way in some distant patch of the universe — whether an observer there might find their entropy decreasing toward what we would call the future. She waved this off with the remark that entropy is time's arrow, delivered with the air of settled doctrine. Since then I've noticed the same one-liner doing very different work in different mouths. In a popular documentary it seemed to mean nothing more than that entropy tends to increase as time passes. In philosophy of physics discussions it seems to carry something far stronger: that the direction of time just is the direction of entropy increase, or even that any observer embedded in a region of decreasing entropy would thereby remember what we would call the future and anticipate the past, so that their time would not be running backwards at all. Elsewhere the phrase is used to gesture at the low-entropy initial condition of the early universe, as though the slogan named a cosmological fact about boundary conditions rather than a local lawlike trend. And I take it there is a further complication: in small isolated systems entropy fluctuates downward all the time, so the slogan must be read statistically, and I would like to know what that statistical reading costs the grander interpretations.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that could be compressed into that one sentence — definitional, lawlike, anthropic, constitutive, cosmological — and present them as a numbered menu I can bring back to my friend. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive but defensible, or whether it is actually false, and give a one-line justification for the verdict. Finally, address the question that started this: which of the readings, if any, would license treating my original question about a patch with a reversed entropy gradient as confused rather than merely unanswered — and which readings would instead leave it as a live question that physics has to answer case by case?
