I'm the non-academic member of my university's dining services advisory committee — I run the campus bookstore, and by some quirk of committee math the "general staff" seat fell to me. We're under pressure from the administration's climate plan to cut dining-hall food emissions 30% over five years, and the dietitian's headline proposal is to replace beef with chicken in most default hot entrees, since poultry has a much smaller carbon footprint per kilogram. At our last meeting this ran into an objection from a graduate student in the animal-studies program that I haven't been able to shake: producing a kilogram of chicken requires the deaths of vastly more animals than a kilogram of beef, and she thinks a switch that saves carbon by multiplying animal deaths by a large factor is a moral disaster, not a win. She also pointed out something I'd never thought about at all — that both chickens and feedlot cattle eat grain, that growing and harvesting grain kills field animals (mice, snakes, insects, earthworms) in ways nobody counts, and that once you start counting those deaths, the arithmetic between beef and chicken gets genuinely confusing rather than simply favoring the beef-adjacent option, because it takes several kilograms of feed to make one kilogram of chicken versus far more per kilogram of beef.

Since then I've been trying to work the problem out on my own, and I keep going in circles. My tentative picture: roughly 200 chickens die per cow-equivalent of meat, which seems to swamp everything else; but if pasture-raised beef requires little cultivated feed, maybe the "invisible" crop deaths tilt back toward beef; but then again, is a field mouse or an earthworm or an insect morally comparable to a chicken at all, or does sentience matter in degrees that make counting bodies naive? And there's a further knot the grad student raised almost as an aside: climate change itself destroys habitats and presumably kills enormous numbers of wild animals, so refusing the emissions cut also has victims, just ones no one can photograph. I don't know how to compare suffering across species, whether harms humans cause by harvesting differ from harms we allow by changing the climate, or how a committee is even supposed to act under that much uncertainty.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with applied-ethics experience who has sat on real advisory panels, and I'd like a document I could actually circulate. Please first think through the relevant considerations — moral and empirical — before committing to a conclusion, and then draft a short decision memo for the committee recommending a concrete course of action: adopt the beef-to-chicken swap outright, reject it, or adopt it with specified conditions or modifications (you're free to invent reasonable ones, like sourcing standards or phased implementation). The memo should make its moral assumptions explicit rather than smuggling them in, should show its arithmetic about deaths in a way a non-specialist can follow, and must engage the strongest version of the argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it. I also want intellectual honesty about the seams: in a closing section, state plainly how confident you are in the recommendation, identify the single empirical or moral premise the recommendation is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would flip your bottom line.
