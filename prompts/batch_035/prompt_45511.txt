Working title: "Bertrand's problem is about where you were standing"

The standard story: Bertrand's chord problem (1889) asks for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you can get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on how you set the problem up. Endpoints uniform on the circumference gives 1/3, midpoint uniform over the disk gives 1/2, midpoint uniform along a diameter gives 1/4. The textbook moral is that the principle of indifference is broken: same description ("a random chord"), different answers, so indifference underdetermines probability.

My claim: the moral is backwards, or at least misleading. The problem was never really about indifference over descriptions. It's about underdetermination of the sampling procedure. Each of the three answers is a perfectly good answer to a different, fully specified physical question: how would you actually *produce* the chord? Toss two darts at the circumference, vs. toss one dart at the disk, etc. Once you know the generative process, there's no paradox at all. So indifference isn't refuted so much as revealed to be a stopgap we reach for when we don't know the mechanism. Keynes saw this in 1921 and people keep rediscovering it, and Jaynes made it sharper with his invariance argument (he thought scale/rotation invariance singles out 1/2 as the answer when you genuinely know nothing about scale).

The new part, and this is what i really want your opinion on: i want to argue that Bertrand's problem is a scaled-down model of the anthropic problem, and that this is why it stays interesting instead of being a solved puzzle from 1889. A chord "chosen at random" without a mechanism is structurally like an observer "sampled at random" from a world of observers without a sampling story. When cosmologists or Sleeping Beauty people argue about the self-sampling vs self-indication assumptions, i claim they are having the Bertrand fight without noticing: they're imposing indifference on an indexical ("this chord," "me, now") precisely where the generative process (how did *i* come to be located at this position in the circle/world) is the thing we don't know. The halfer/thirder dispute maps onto the choice between sampling procedures in a way i haven't seen anyone make explicit. So the essay ends by saying the principle of indifference dies twice: once as a naive rule and once as anthropics, and the autopsy is the same both times.
