I teach a seminar on the philosophy of history, and in last week's session a colleague proposed an argument I have been unable to shake. It concludes that the familiar claim that the world is becoming less violent is not a factual claim at all, and while I am confident it fails, every repair I attempt seems to spread the damage to cases I want to keep.

(1) A trend claim such as "the world is becoming less violent" is answerable only relative to some way of measuring violence. (2) The two natural measures — the absolute number of violent deaths and violent deaths per capita — can come apart, and in the long run they do: absolute numbers have grown along with population, while per-capita rates have fallen. (3) When the measures disagree, nothing in the world decides between them; the choice reflects our purposes only, in the way the choice between nominal and inflation-adjusted figures reflects the purpose of a forecast. (4) So there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent; there are only facts of the form "violence is declining on such-and-such a measure," together with a decision about which measure to headline. (5) Therefore works that announce a long-term decline in violence as a discovery are, at the decisive step, adopting an accounting convention — and so, by the same reasoning, are works that announce that the modern era is the bloodiest in history.

I cannot accept (5), but my usual objections each prove too much in turn. If I object that per-capita normalization is conventional because it discounts each death by the size of the background population, I appear committed to holding that a given person's risk of dying violently — itself a per-capita quantity — is likewise conventional, which is absurd. If I instead defend (2) by insisting that the two candidate measures are exhaustive, I find I do not believe it (what about deaths per person-year of exposure, or years of life lost?), and if I attack (3) by appeal to the "right" measure, I cannot say what makes it right beyond its fitness for our purposes.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked secure, and what rejecting it costs — including, if you keep the argument intact, whether you can stomach the conclusion that quantitative trend claims across whole disciplines are conventions at the decisive step. If you think the argument is sound and the conclusion merely surprising, say so and defend it.

Please write your answer in American English.
