I host a small philosophy podcast that goes in for long-form interviews, and our next guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending Scanlonian contractualism — specifically the so-called individualist restriction, the thesis that in judging whether a principle of the general regulation of behavior could be reasonably rejected, we may set one person's complaint against another's, but we may never sum complaints across persons. She is on record arguing that this refusal to aggregate is not an embarrassment to be patched but the very heart of the view: what the restriction protects is the separateness of persons, and most alleged counterexamples trade on conflating genuine aggregation with some neighboring phenomenon.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for her. I want the sequence to build rather than hop around, and each question must turn on the actual content of her position. In particular, make sure I have at least one question on rescue cases, where nearly everyone's intuition is that numbers matter — we ought to throw the rope to the group of five rather than the lone swimmer, and the guest, if consistent, seems barred from saying why. Include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, namely that each of the five has a complaint of the same strength as the one swimmer's, and that there being many such complaints is different from there being one big complaint — and press on whether counting complaints without weighing their sum can really explain why the lone swimmer loses rather than merely describing it. I also want a question on the transitivity worry critics have pressed on the pairwise comparison: a chain of choices, each defensible by the restriction's lights, can allegedly take us from an alternative that harms no one to one that harms someone, and she needs to block the slide somewhere; ask her where, and what principled cost the block carries. Finally, end with one question about what evidence could count against the restriction itself — what intuition, if it survived full information and reflection, she would concede refutes her view.

A few constraints on tone and content. No generic questions about her career path or writing habits; my listeners already know the shape of contractualism from earlier episodes. She is practiced at deflecting aggregation worries with two moves in particular: appealing to the relevance restriction, under which complaints too weak to be worth considering at all drop out, and insisting that a given phenomenon is a matter of personal vs impersonal reasoning rather than aggregation proper. At least three of your questions should be engineered so that those deflections come out weaker than they were on the previous question, and the final one should be hard to deflect with either. Where you reference the literature, keep it to the well-worn landmarks — Taurek's case against letting the numbers count, the kind of World-Cup-broadcast scenario Scanlon uses where one person's serious burden is set against millions of trivial enjoyments — and describe the cases in your own words rather than attributing precise wording to anyone.
