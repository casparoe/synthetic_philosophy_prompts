I write novels, and I have found that my judgments about morally exemplary characters — my own and other novelists' — form a pattern I cannot quite state. When a character anonymously donates a kidney to a stranger, I find her admirable, full stop. When a character has given away nearly everything she owns and lives in voluntary poverty, I admire her, but I also find I cannot make her the center of a novel; she comes out flat, and I quietly demote her to the margins of someone else's story. When a character is relentless in the service of others but retains one blazing nonmoral passion — for jazz, say, which he guards jealously and will not sacrifice even for causes he weeps over — I find him not just vivid but more admirable, somehow, than the tireless gentle soul with no inner life at all. Yet when a father abandons his family for humanitarian work abroad, I judge him not admirable at all, even though his conduct is impersonally better than staying home would have been. And when a character is perfectly honest, perfectly kind, never laughs at anyone's expense, I admire her while feeling I would cross the street to avoid befriending her. My tentative thought is that there is a principle here having to do with the place of personal projects in a good life, but I cannot make it cover all my verdicts. Please tell me whether some principle — or small set of candidates, stated precisely — captures this pattern, motivate each clause by the case that requires it, tell me what the principle predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on (a character who is nearly morally perfect but keeps one hour of solitary birdwatching a day that no argument about the world's suffering could move him to give up), and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle forces me to abandon.
