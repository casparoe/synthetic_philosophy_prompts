Two of my colleagues have been arguing for close to two years now and I've basically been keeping score. The topic: free-floating rationales — reasons for things that no one ever represented, understood or decided; the rationale is sitting there in the design of the wing, so to speak, but no mind ever held it. (Dennett's term, though half their fights are about whether they even mean it the way he did.) One of them is a rationale realist: she thinks that when we say the wing is shaped as it is because that shape reduces drag, we're stating a fact about the world that was true before any engineer noticed it, and that these reasons are genuinely out there, as discoverable as mountains. The other is a deflationist: he thinks that's predictive convenience, full stop — the intentional stance applied wherever it compresses well, and 'reasons' without any reasoner is a metaphor we've collectively talked ourselves into taking literally. The frustrating thing is how much they agree on: both are dyed-in-the-wool Darwinians, both think reverse-engineering evolved structures is excellent science, neither thinks there's a ghost or world-soul hiding anywhere. So the disagreement is narrow and stubborn, which is exactly why it never resolves.

I've finally talked them into trying an adversarial collaboration, and since I'm the neutral party I'm meant to draft the joint statement they'll both be shown for sign-off. Please write that document. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both could sign, including claims about the specific cases that keep coming up in their arguments — the digger wasp that re-checks her burrow in a fixed loop that looks like deliberation but isnt, the independent evolution of camera eyes in octopuses and vertebrates, and termite mounds — plus the one they got genuinely heated about, whether a self-organising pattern with no selection history at all (the walking lanes that form in crowds, the branching of a river delta) could carry a rationale in the same sense; (2) the residual disagreements, each written twice, once in wording the realist would accept as a fair statement of her view and once in wording the deflationist would accept for his — no slanted phrasings that smuggle in a verdict; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the indispensability of 'because'-talk in mature biology commits you to rationales being real rather than merely useful, and another is whether etiology matters — whether a rationale needs a selection history behind it or whether any stable functional organisation will do. (That second one is where the trained neural network got dragged in: a network that lands on a solution we'd naturally describe in reason-terms, and the two of them flatly disagree about what that shows.) And (4) for each crux, a case, argument or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — what we should find, and what each would concede counts against her or his view.

If you think part of what's left over is merely verbal — e.g. just a fight over the word 'reason' — say which part and why, since they'd want to know whether to keep arguing or just split the vocabulary between them. Do the last section as a table (crux | the agreed test | realist's prediction | deflationist's prediction) if that reads better. And British English please — the department is in the UK and the deflationist is pedantic about it, the irony being he's a deflationist about everything except spelling.
