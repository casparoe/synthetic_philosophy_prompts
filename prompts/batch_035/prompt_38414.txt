I write a column for a general-interest magazine on science and ideas, and I'm currently finishing a piece on probability puzzles that turn on an observer not knowing when she is. My running example is the classic one: Beauty agrees on Sunday to sleep through an experiment governed by a coin toss — heads and she is woken once, tails and she is woken twice, with her memory of the first waking erased — so that on any given waking she cannot tell which day it is or how the coin landed. A decision theorist I interviewed walked me through an argument that Beauty's credence in tails, on a waking, should be two-thirds rather than one-half, and I planned to present it as the piece's centerpiece. The trouble is that when I extend the argument the way he himself suggested, it seems to license conclusions no one at the interview would accept, and I can no longer tell whether I've found a refutation or just failed to understand the original.

Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it. (1) Credence is revealed by betting behavior: an agent who is exactly indifferent to a single bet that nets W on some proposition and costs L otherwise has credence L/(W+L) in that proposition. (2) Suppose that at each waking Beauty is offered a bet that costs one stake on heads and nets half a stake on tails, and that her policy is to accept at every waking. (3) Then she finishes one stake ahead if the coin lands tails (she collects at both wakenings) and one stake behind if it lands heads, so with a fair coin the policy has expected value exactly zero; these, then, are her indifference odds. (4) By (1), an agent indifferent to a single bet at those odds has credence two-thirds in tails — so Beauty's waking credence in tails is two-thirds. (5) The reasoning generalizes: if the protocol wakes her N times on tails and once on heads, her indifference odds come out at 1 to N, revealing a waking credence of N/(N+1). (6) Nothing in the reasoning cares that the coin is fair. So let the coin be one known to land tails only once in a thousand, and let tails trigger a million wakenings: her waking credence in tails still comes out at roughly 0.999. (7) But Beauty is then nearly certain of an outcome she knows to occur one time in a thousand, and her "credence" is hostage to how many wake-ups the experimenter chose to schedule — a technician's calendar decisions, not the coin, now determine what she should be nearly sure of. I gather that the people who defend the two-thirds answer are generally unmoved by this, but I also can't get anyone to tell me exactly which step they're declining.

Since my deadline is Friday, I need a committed verdict rather than a tour of the literature. Name the single step you would reject, explain why it looked innocuous when first stated, and tell me concretely what giving it up costs — including whether the friends of two-thirds can keep their answer in the ordinary fair-coin case while disowning the argument that got them there, or whether the rigged-coin variant forces a package deal. For what it's worth, I would very much like to keep (1), and I would also like to believe that a known thousand-to-one bias survives an arbitrary number of scheduled wakenings; I'm aware that I may be entitled to neither. If you think the argument is in fact valid and that Beauty really should be nearly certain of a thousand-to-one event, I'll print that too — but you'll have to say plainly what kind of quantity her waking credence then is, if not a probability of the coin landing tails.
