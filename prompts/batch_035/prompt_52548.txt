Here is the debate as I understand it, correct me where I garble it. Around 1920, Ludwig von Mises argued that a socialist economy cannot rationally allocate capital goods, because without markets in the means of production there are no genuine prices for them, and without prices there is no economic calculation — only guesswork about what anything costs. Later, Friedrich Hayek sharpened this into an argument about knowledge: the information needed for allocation is dispersed, tacit, and tied to particular times and places, and market prices are the mechanism that compresses and transmits it. Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner replied, roughly: a central planning board can set prices by trial and error — raise them where shortages appear, lower them where gluts do, let managers respond to the adjusted prices, and repeat until things clear. I'm also told Lange, late in life, suggested the market is essentially a pre-electronic computing device that modern computers might replace outright.
