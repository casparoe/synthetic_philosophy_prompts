I've been going through my gut verdicts on absolute vs relative motion, Newton's bucket territory, and I honestly can't tell if they add up to anything coherent. Here's my list:

Water climbing the sides of Newton's bucket: that's real rotation, full stop. And the reason Newton's case is so good is that when the surface is most curved the water is roughly at rest relative to the bucket, so the rotation obviously isn't rotation-relative-to-the-bucket.

Newton's two globes tied by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty universe: if the cord is taut, they're rotating. I feel pretty good about this one even though there's literally nothing else in the universe to rotate relative to. The taut cord is doing something.

Uniform velocity: totally meaningless in isolation. There is no fact of the matter about whether I'm moving at 30 mph or at rest, not even a hidden one. But acceleration is different -- you feel it with your eyes closed, in a rocket or an elevator.

A single point particle spinning in an empty universe: meaningless, I think. Spinning about what axis, oriented how? Nothing to say.

A single spinning hoop in an empty universe, though: I want to say this is meaningful? The hoop has internal tension trying to fling it apart, and I feel like that's observable from inside, somehow, in the way the point particle's spin isn't. But I can't really defend why size matters here, and it bugs me.

Two particles alone in the universe whose distance apart is oscillating: meaningful, they're really accelerating (each one can feel it). Fine.

The whole universe rotating together -- every galaxy, everything, "rotating" as one: my gut says this is meaningless, relative to WHAT. But here's the thing: I vaguely remember that in general relativity a rotating universe would actually have observable effects, pendulums swinging weird, that kind of thing. So my gut might just be wrong there, or general relativity is being sneaky about what "rotation" means in that context. Not sure.

My tentative principle was something like: a motion is real/meaningful when, and only when, it shows up as a difference from inside the universe -- internal stresses, tidal effects, things you could in principle measure without looking outside. On that principle the bucket, the globes, the spinning hoop, and the two oscillating particles all come out meaningful, uniform velocity comes out meaningless, and the point particle comes out meaningless because a point has no internal structure to stress. That's... most of my verdicts, which is suspicious. But it also seems to make whole-universe rotation meaningless (nothing changes relative to anything else) while Newton's own machinery, which got me the globes, makes whole-universe rotation very meaningful, centrifugal forces everywhere. And the globes verdict came from the same guy, from the same argument!

So: is there one principle that captures all this, or close to it? What does it say about cases I haven't listed -- like a universe with exactly two clusters of matter far apart, rotating relative to each other, where the clusters aren't equal in size? Does the small one orbit the big one, do they orbit a midpoint, and does it even make sense to ask, if there's nothing else to compare against? And be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn -- I suspect the spinning hoop one, or the globes one, is where the trouble is, but I'd like to know which and why. I'd also take a small set of candidate principles if no single one works, but then tell me what settles the choice between them.
