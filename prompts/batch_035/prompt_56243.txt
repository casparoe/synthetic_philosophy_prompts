Last night my friend D. and I got into a three hour argument about whether you can compare how well off different people are, and somewhere in the middle I sketched what I think is a genuinely different framing of the whole interpersonal utility comparisons debate. Now I want to write it up as an essay but I'm not sure any of it survives daylight. Here are my rough notes, tell me what you think:

1. Everyone agrees each person's vNM utility function is only defined up to positive affine transformation — u and 3u+2 represent the exact same prefernces. Call this per-person scaling freedom.

2. The standard move is to treat interpersonal comparability as a further substantive question: is there a fact of the matter about whether Alice is better off than Bob? I want to say this sets the problem up wrong. Interpersonal comparability isn't a missing fact, it's an unfixed choice of representation. Once you have a bunch of people each with their own utility function, the composite object has a whole group of symmetries (rescale each person independently, and nothing intra-personal changes). "Making interpersonal comparisons" just IS fixing that freedom — choosing a gauge.

3. Consequence: asking whether interpersonal comparisons are "true" or "meaningless" is like asking whether the universe's metric signature is +--- or -+++ without first fixing anything. Wrong kind of question. The real questions are which gauge choices are usable for which purposes, and whether there's a principled way to pick one. D.'s response was "no, it's arbitrary, anything goes" — I want to argue that's too fast, because gauge choices in physics aren't arbitrary either: they're constrained by convenience plus the underlying structure, and nobody concludes from the existence of unit freedom that lengths don't exist.

4. Where I think this earns its keep: Harsanyi-style aggregation theorems and veil of ignorance arguments stop looking like they're smuggling comparability in, and start looking like they're implicitly doing gauge fixing. Which might explain the weird dialectical situation where the theorems "work" but people still feel comparisons are suspect.

5. The objection I can't fully answer: D. says I've just relabeled the problem. If there's no fact about which gauge is right, then the total utilitarian's "sum of utilities" isn't merely underdetermined, it's completely unconstrained — with the right choice of scalings you can make basically any redistribution come out optimal. I have a hand-wavy reply involving the affine structure being shared across people (common zero points, common units) but honestly I don't love it.

So: (a) is the gauge analogy doing real work here or is it a metaphor I've fallen in love with, (b) is there a good answer to the "conventionalism about units makes aggregation unconstrained" objection that I'm not seeing, (c) does point 4 actually hold up or am I misremembering what the aggregation theorems need to assume — that's the part I'm writing from memory, and (d) if you had to tell me to cut my losses on one part of this, which part would it be.

Also, and I mean this: say how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point of my argument you'd least trust. I'd much rather hear "this specific move is broken" now than after I've written 5000 words around it.
