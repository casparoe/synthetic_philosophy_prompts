I serve as chief of staff to a state legislator, and over the last two sessions our office has repeatedly faced a category of decision we handle badly and inconsistently: whether to recommend that she do something that is, by ordinary moral lights, wrong in order to secure a political outcome she's responsible for delivering. Trading a vote on a bill she doesn't care much about to protect one she does; declining to correct a false rumor about an opponent because the correction would cost us the district; letting a committee colleague believe we'll support his amendment when we've already agreed to kill it; misrepresenting, by silence, how a bill we need passed will actually affect a constituent group that trusts her. Philosophers call this the problem of dirty hands, and the literature gives us the pieces — the thought that a political actor can do the right thing and still do wrong; the distinction between an ethic of responsibility and an ethic of conviction; the idea that some deontological constraints yield at catastrophically high stakes without evaporating entirely; the claim that there's a moral remainder, a residue of guilt the agent ought to feel and not launder away. What it doesn't give us is anything my staff can run in the forty-five minutes between the whip call and the vote.

Write me that procedure. Requirements:

1. The procedure must be usable by two legislative aides with no philosophy background, in under an hour, under pressure, with a paper trail I'd be comfortable showing the legislator. Phrase every node as a question about the case at hand, in plain English, with an explicit statement of what counts as enough of an answer to move to the next node and what sends the decision upstairs to the legislator herself.

2. The procedure's backbone should be the dirty hands framework, but it must contain a genuine fork for the view, held by one of my deputies, that no wrong is ever done when the outcome is good enough — the position that says the politician who "does what needs doing" has clean hands, not dirty ones. She needs to see where the procedure would let her record that position and where it refuses to, and why. Don't stack the deck against her; make the strongest version of her fork the procedure can honestly accommodate.

3. The procedure must distinguish at least these three, which we have confused in practice: (a) doing a genuine wrong justified by outcome; (b) doing something merely unpleasant, ugly, or disliked that is not wrong at all; and (c) doing something wrong with no sufficient justification, dressed up in advance as category (a). Include concrete tests for telling them apart in the moment, since that's exactly the confusion pressure exploits.

4. The procedure must handle the moral remainder, not just the go/no-go decision. This is where I want some invention from you: the office needs something operational for the residue — Walzer's thought is that the leader who gets his hands dirty ought to know it and feel it, and I want to know what that becomes in a staff office, where the hands doing the recommending are mine and the aides', not the legislator's. Propose a mechanism (a written record, a disclosure rule, a standing cost, whatever you can defend), and defend it against the obvious objection that ritualized guilt is just a laundering device — a way of feeling clean about staying.

5. The procedure must have an iterated-case warning system. The single-case literature mostly assumes one grave decision in a career; we face a dozen small ones a session. Say explicitly how the procedure detects accumulation — the way a habit of category-(a) claims slides into category (c) without any single decision looking different from the last — and what it does about it, even if all it can honestly do is flag.

Then run the procedure on three of our real recurring cases: the vote trade, the uncorrected rumor, and one case of your own construction where the procedure returns an answer I won't like — I mean one where the procedure says our hands would be clean and my instinct says dirty, or the reverse — and show the trace node by node. Finally, tell me plainly where the procedure cannot do my thinking for me: which nodes are judgment dressed up as paperwork, which cases it will systematically misclassify, and what it is structurally blind to. Write the whole answer in American English.
