I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I can't figure out how to handle. Every few weeks a source pitches me a story where the stakes are effectivly unlimited: a tech safety person saying a certain kind of AI system, if developed, could kill everyone; a biosecurity guy claiming an engineered pathogen could end civilization; one guy who's dead serious that population decline means hundreds of billions of potential people never get born, which he calls the biggest story of the century. The pitch always has the same shape. The probability is small and everyone admits its a guess — but the harm is so enormous that even a tiny chance swamps everything else on my beat. The math they use is just "huge number times small number still equals huge." And the thing is, if I actually followed that math, I'd run extinction stories basically every week and nothing else, because nothing else has stakes in the same league. Climate, antibiotic resistance, actual wars — all rounding errors next to "everyone dies." When I don't take the bait, I need a reason, and "the number is too big to believe" doesn't seem like one. My editor says just use news judgment, but news judgment is exactly the thing the math claims to override. So my question: is there a principled line between covering a genuinely underrated risk and getting pushed around by someone who noticed they can always name a bigger number than the last guy? If so, where does the line sit — in how they know, how big the claim can get before it stops counting for more, whether it can be checked at all, or somewhere else entirely? And if it turns out there's no principled line and I'm just going to be arbitrating these by feel, I'd honestly rather hear that too.
