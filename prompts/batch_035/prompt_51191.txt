I've got a running list of my gut verdicts about heritability, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them or if I'm just holding a bunch of ideas that can't all be true. Here they are.

Height: within any recent Dutch cohort, heritability is something like 0.8 — yet Dutch men gained something like 15-20 cm over the 20th century while their genes obviously didn't change. My verdict: both things are true at once, so heritability can't be a measure of how fixed or unchangeable a trait is.

Phenylketonuria: a single gene, so about as genetic as a trait gets, and yet a change to the diet in infancy basically prevents the outcome entirely. Verdict: "genetic" clearly doesn't mean "immune to an intervention."

Same trait, different populations: some studies find that heritability of cognitive test scores comes out lower among kids from poor families than rich ones, and lower in poorer countries than rich ones. My verdict: this makes sense, because heritability is measuring something about the spread of environments in the sample, not about the trait itself.

The seedling setup where you grow two batches of seedlings, one flat with good soil and one with bad soil: within each flat the differences in growth are 100% due to genes, but the difference between the flats is entirely the soil. Verdict: within-group heritability tells you nothing about the cause of a between-group difference, even when the within-group heritability is literally 1.0.

Someone says "Sarah got 60% of her intelligence from her genes, that's what the heritability studies say." Verdict: that's a category error. Heritability is a statistic about variation in a population, and it doesn't assign a percentage to any individual person.

And here's my tentative positive claim, which the next case then wrecks: I think high heritability does license a modest conclusion — namely that differences in environments, as they currently vary in that population, account for a minority of the trait differences. So if you ran a program that gave kids environments of a kind that already existed somewhere in that population, you shouldn't expect it to flatten the differences much.

The case that wrecks it: some new intervention appears — a teaching method, a food, whatever — that nobody in the population has ever experienced. I can't tell, from the heritability number alone, whether this new thing is "inside" the range of environments that population has already sampled (in which case my modest conclusion holds and it won't do much) or "outside" it (in which case all bets are off, like the Dutch nutrition changes). Heritability seems to tell me about the map I have, not the territory I might move to.

So: is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly whether my wrecked conclusion about interventions can be repaired or has to go — and if it has to go, what am I allowed to conclude from a high heritability figure at all, if anything? Also, where would it come down on a couple of cases I haven't listed — say, whether heritability of a trait should go up or down in a society that equalized environments (same schools, same neighborhoods), and whether a breeder selecting on a trait in one environment can assume the ranking holds in a different environment?

On epistemics: please separate what's genuinely settled among people who know this stuff (I assume the definition of heritability as a variance ratio and the population-relative point are settled) from what's actually contested — for instance whether those heritability differences across social class replicate cleanly, since I gather that's been argued about — and don't dress up a contested view as the consensus. Please write in American English. And keep it reasonably plain — my cousin is a breeder and I want to send him the summary, he'll definately have opinions about the "range of environments" part.
