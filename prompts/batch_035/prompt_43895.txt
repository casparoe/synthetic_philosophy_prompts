I'm a third-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and a chapter of my dissertation tries to reconstruct how working scientists actually draw the line between science and non-science when they're forced to. My advisor — I'll call her V. — has been issuing demarcation verdicts in seminars for years, and I've been quietly keeping a list. Her record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- Astrology: not science, and not because it fails but because it can't fail — every missed horoscope comes with a built-in escape hatch (the native's will, an unaspected planet, an uncertain birth time).
- Intelligent design: not science.
- Freudian psychoanalysis as a body of theory: not science — she's careful to say this isn't a verdict on therapy — because its explanatory machinery could accommodate any patient whatsoever, and in her view did.
- Homeopathy, after the high-dilution trials came in: not science, chiefly because its defenders answered negative results by inventing new mechanisms rather than by making anything riskier.
- The Neptune episode: exemplary science. When Uranus's orbit refused to cooperate, postulating an unseen perturbing planet and then finding it was, on her telling, exactly how a theory deserves to be rescued.
- Ptolemaic astronomy: science — wrong, and baroque by the century, but science; epicycles had to answer to planetary positions, and their defenders cared when they missed.

Next term a new case lands in her lap, and I'd like to predict her verdict before she gives it. A group in our own department — mathematical history, roughly — fits systems of coupled equations to the rise and collapse of states: variables for elite overproduction, popular immiseration, fiscal strain. Their papers report impressive fits to thoroughly documented outcomes: the third-century Roman crisis, the timing of the English Civil War, the Taiping Rebellion. Each failed fit is met by adding a variable or re-windowing the data. No paper contains a prediction of anything not already in the historical record when the model was built.

I can read the first four verdicts as straightforwardly falsificationist and the last two as already a step beyond that, but I'm honestly not sure that's the only consistent reconstruction of her, and the new case sits right where my candidate readings come apart. So here's what I'd like from you: lay out the rules her record is jointly consistent with, including at least two that agree on everything above; say exactly where they diverge on this case — including whether she'll distinguish science done badly from pseudoscience at all; give me your single best prediction of her verdict with your reasons; and tell me what further case would most cleanly separate the rules that happen to agree on this one. Please commit to a prediction rather than presenting me a menu — if you think her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say which way you'd bet anyway and how confident you are.
