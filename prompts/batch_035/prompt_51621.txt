I'm a late-stage PhD student in political theory, and in three weeks I'm presenting at a workshop on legitimacy where I'll be defending an unorthodox reading of Pascal's scattered fragments on justice and force in the Pensées. The fragments I mean are the ones observing that justice without force is impotent, force without justice tyrannical, and that since we cannot make what is just strong, we end up justifying what is strong so that the two travel together; the companion jottings about custom being the whole of equity because it is accepted, and truth lying on one side of the mountains and error on the other. The standard seminar gloss treats these as either cynicism or a warm-up act for Hobbes: law is just organized force, justice is whatever your latitude happens to enforce. My paper argues that reading badly misses Pascal's point. On my reading, Pascal is not claiming that justice is force, but that our political concepts are structurally unstable: we cannot make the just strong, we cannot make the strong just, and the impossibility is not practical but conceptual — the demand for a rational grounding of political obligation undermines itself in Pascal's hands in a way that is closer to a diagnostic argument about the limits of legitimation than to a theory of law at all. Part of the paper claims that this is a distinctive position that doesn't map onto any of the familiar buckets (realism, positivism, Hobbesian contractarianism, ideology critique), and that Pascal's theological framing is doing work that can be profitably secularized.

The workshop audience is the problem. It's about twenty people: roughly half Kantians and Rawlsians who will hear "reason cannot settle political obligation" as a category error about practical reason; a Hobbes scholar who knows the contractual machinery cold and will want to know why Pascal's despair isn't just Hobbes minus the good parts; one legal positivist who will say I've rediscovered the thesis that law's authority is social fact and dressed it in seventeenth-century prose; and a historian of early modern philosophy who has spent her career warning people off reading the Pensées — an apologia assembled posthumously from fragments — as a systematic political treatise, and who regards my whole genre of paper as a category mistake. There will also be a couple of political realists who are friendly in principle but, in my experience, are the ones most likely to turn on me for getting their tradition's relationship to Pascal wrong.

I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from these specific people, ordered by how much damage each would do if handled badly, each phrased in the vocabulary that Kantian, the Hobbes scholar, the positivist, the historian, and the realist would respectively use rather than in generic philosophy-speak. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — the one that would expose the weakest joint in my reading if pressed. For the epistemics: I'd like you to tell me explicitly which of your recommended replies you think would actually collapse under a good follow-up rather than the first pushback, and what argument or consideration from any of these camps would, if pressed hard enough, force me to give up the central claim that Pascal's position is distinctive rather than a degraded version of something already on the menu. If your judgment is that the historian's objection, say, is the one I should be planning around most seriously rather than the Kantian's, say so plainly instead of hedging.
