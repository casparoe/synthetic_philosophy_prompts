After a career in condensed matter physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I have hit a puzzle about the norms of my old profession that I cannot resolve on my own. The community I came from celebrates Einstein's explanation of the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion — a discrepancy known since Le Verrier in 1859, sitting in the textbooks for half a century before 1915 — as among the strongest supports general relativity ever received, and it still counts in the theory's favor in every retrospective assessment I know of. Yet the same community, along with the statisticians and machine-learning practitioners who now dominate the funding agencies, treats data that was available while a hypothesis was being built as unable to confirm it: hence the near-sacred status of the train/test split, the disdain for post-hoc curve fitting, and the premium placed on genuinely novel predictions. So the profession both prizes a retrodiction of fifty-year-old data and legislates against counting old data as confirmatory. What view of evidence does this double practice presuppose, if any? I gather philosophers call the general issue the problem of old evidence, and I can see the crude worry myself: if I already gave Mercury's behavior probability one, then conditioning on it should leave my credences untouched. But I would like the implicit epistemology reconstructed honestly — say whether the two halves of the practice actually conflict or whether they only seem to, given different things "confirm" might mean; say whether the practice strictly requires one account of evidence or is compatible with several, including non-Bayesian ones; and tell me what would have to change in how physicists and statisticians go about their business if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly probabilistic one — for instance, whether the train/test distinction would lose its point, and whether anyone would still have a reason to run a new experiment rather than squeeze confirmation out of the data we already have.
