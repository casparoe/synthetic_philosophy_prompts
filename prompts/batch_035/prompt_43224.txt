I was promoted to engineering manager eight months ago, and one of the first things I introduced was a blameless postmortem process: after every significant outage, the team involved writes a document laying out the timeline of what happened, under the standing rule that no individual is ever named as blameworthy. The framing we use is that a human error is a symptom rather than a root cause — the question the review asks is why the system made that error easy to commit and hard to catch, and corrective actions are expected to target tooling, alerts, runbooks, and staffing rather than people. I think it has genuinely worked: engineers volunteer information in review meetings that they used to withhold, and the resulting action items are better than what we got from the old finger-pointing reviews. But two things nag at me. First, the organization has not abolished accountability in general: the same engineer who is shielded in a postmortem can still be put on a performance improvement plan, or quietly let go, for repeated sloppiness, and everyone can see that the boundary between an excusable mistake and negligence worth punishing is drawn somewhere even if no one says where — skipping a required checklist because you were rushing to a demo seems to land on the wrong side of it, while misreading a genuinely confusing dashboard does not. Second, I have been reading a little philosophy on the side, and it struck me that what the ritual seems designed to do is single out one moral emotion and starve it: the postmortem makes room for guilt (you acknowledge the specific thing you did, help repair it, and are done with it) while trying to prevent shame (the incident becoming something you are rather than something you did).

So I would like to ask what theory of moral responsibility and moral emotion this practice presupposes, if any. Does blamelessness require a particular view — say, that blame attaches only to choices made under sufficient control, and that a serious look at how our systems work shows that most of what we call bad choices were not under sufficient control — or would several quite different theories of responsibility license the same ritual, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined? Is the negligence carve-out a presupposition of the practice, or just an incoherence, or is there a principled control-based line between the skipped checklist and the misread dashboard, and can you say what it is? Relatedly, does the practice need its implicit theory of the moral emotions (guilt dischargeable by acknowledgment and repair, shame corrosive and to be engineered away), or could a defender of shame as the better moral emotion — someone who thinks the whole self should be on the line, because that is what motivates real change — accept blameless postmortems while dissenting from the rationale?

And then the question I keep circling: my postmortem process is an explicit attempt to change, by policy, which moral emotion dominates a small subculture — mine. Does anything about how this experiment has gone bear on whether a whole culture could choose between guilt and shame, or does the answer turn on features an engineering team has and a society does not: shared and legible goals, the ability to redesign the environment its members act in, voluntary membership, and the fact that failure is frequent, low-stakes, and informative? Please answer in continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — write it as you would a letter: begin with the presuppositions you think the practice rests on, then say which rival theories would license the same ritual, then address the negligence boundary, and close with what my experiment does and does not suggest about whether a culture can choose its moral emotions.
