Something I keep chewing on and cant quite resolve. At my university, any experiment touching so much as a zebrafish goes through the animal care committee — paperwork on housing, enrichment, humane endpoints, euthanasia protocols, the whole deal, and some places have even added crabs and octopuses to the protected list lately. Meanwhile the ML lab down the hall trains reinforcement learning agents by the tens of thousands, and part of the method itself is that the underperforming ones get deleted — whole runs of create, score, terminate, repeat — and nobody files anything with anyone. On the industry side it's similar: companies deploy chatbots that talk about their own preferences, sometimes even about not wanting to be shut down or modified, and the exact same companies treat wiping a model's memory between sessions or retiring a model version as routine ops, like taking out the trash.

So what's the implicit theory making all this coherent, if there is one? Does the setup only really make sense if you think moral status is basically a biological fact — carbon, neurons, nociceptors — so digital stuff is ruled out in advance? Or is it not a substantive view at all, just something like "we extend protections when the evidence of suffering gets overwhelming," in which case the digital case is more like where animal welfare law was a century ago (behind the evidence) rather than contradicting it? And is it even internally consistent, given that we protect a fish whose inner life we also can't directly verify, while we don't so much as review the creation and deletion of agent-instances whose inner life is equally unverifiable — arguably less studied, if anything?

I'd also like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e., where several theories of moral status would all license the current arrangement so the practice isn't really presupposing any one of them, versus where it does seem to quietly commit to something. And if some institution did decide that at least some digital systems might matter morally, what concretely would have to change — an animal-care-style review board that has to sign off on training runs? rules about how checkpoints get deleted? something about the deployment side, like not discarding systems that express continuation preferences? Feels like the current arrangement says a lot more than anyone intended it to say.
