Every year in my metaethics seminar, the week on the Frege–Geach problem is where I lose a third of the room. The students can follow the example arguments on the board, but they don't feel the force of the issue: that a single moral sentence must mean the same thing whether it stands alone, sits in the antecedent of a conditional, or falls under a negation — and that if its sense shifted across those settings, the inferences we run on it would simply fail.

I'd like a poem to open the following week's session. In it, one moral sentence — please choose your own, something concrete and domestic rather than grand — should appear verbatim at least three times: once asserted outright, once as the antecedent of a conditional, and once under negation or in a question. Across these appearances the poem should conduct a piece of reasoning it performs itself (a modus ponens enacted stanza by stanza), and its structure should make visible that the reasoning's validity turns entirely on the recurring sentence keeping its sense in all three positions. Let the ending bring the expressivist's predicament into view rather than state it: how can the same line be an act of disapproval in one place and a mere hypothesis in another, when nothing about the words themselves has changed? Free verse, 16 to 24 lines. It should be accurate enough that a metaethicist wouldn't wince, and lucid enough that a student who stalled on the problem could read it aloud and see where the standard quasi-realist reply will have to go — without the poem itself taking sides.
