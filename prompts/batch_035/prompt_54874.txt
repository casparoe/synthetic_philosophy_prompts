In the historiography of the Third Reich there's a debate that has been running since the early sixties and, as far as I can tell, has long since hardened into a fixed ritual: the intentionalists say remove Hitler and there's no Holocaust, and probably no war in 1939 either, because the radicalization was driven from the top; the structuralists (or functionalists) reply that the Weimar collapse, the conservative elites who thought they could use him, the economic crisis, Versailles, and anti-Bolshevism were the load-bearing structure, and that some other demagogue would have played a similar role on the same stage. Both sides have been citing the same archives for decades and neither has moved an inch. My working hypothesis, coming at this from the causality literature rather than from history, is that this isn't an empirical disagreement at all: it's a disguised dispute about which possible worlds count as close — a choice of similarity metric over counterfactual histories — where the intentionalists hold everything fixed except Hitler's absence while the structuralists treat the background conditions as the essence and the individual on the stage as swappable. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine, in-principle-settleable factual disagreement hiding here about difference-making — something like the question of whether Hitler's node carries a high average causal effect in the causal graph of interwar Germany — or is it bedrock intuitions about historical inevitability that neither side can argue for? And whatever your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a sharper causal model of the period, an agreed convention about what a counterfactual historical question holds fixed, a distinction nobody has drawn yet, or nothing — plus, if it's the last one, why the debate feels so productive to its participants anyway.
