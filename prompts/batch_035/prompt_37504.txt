I've been into stoicism for about two years now — the ancient texts, but also a lot of the modern revival stuff (the pop books, the online community, the therapy crossover), and next month my little discussion group is doing a session where everyone writes up their actual position and everyone else pokes at it. So here's mine, and I want to know if it hangs together before I embarrass myself:

1. The dichotomy of control, stated strictly: some things are up to us (judgments, assent, impulses) and some things aren't (health, wealth, reputation, and crucially the outcomes of our actions), with no third category.
2. At the same time I really like Irvine's trichotomy move: some things are completely up to us, some are completely not, and some — like winning a tennis match, or getting a job — are partially up to us. For that middle category you should "internalize" the goal, i.e. define success as having prepared and tried properly rather than as winning.
3. Virtue is the only genuine good; everything else is indifferent, though some indifferents are preferred (health, friendship) and some dispreferred.
4. A Stoic can and should grieve when someone they love dies. The first shock isn't a judgment and isn't up to us in the relevant sense — the sage feels the sting and lets it run its course without adding on the judgment that something bad has happened.
5. The CBT lineage makes me think Stoicism is basically empirically vindicated: Ellis and Beck drew straight from Epictetus, CBT has decent evidence behind it, so the core Stoic picture of how emotion works is just true.
6. Given all the above, I think Stoicism actually supports full worldly engagement — career ambition, political activism, trying to change institutions — because preferred indifferents give you rational grounds for big projects even though outcomes don't ultimately matter.

I'm aware 1 and 2 look like they clash, but I genuinely can't tell whether the middle category is a friendly reformulation or a real departure — if you think it's a departure, tell me what work the middle category is doing that the strict dichotomy can't do on its own. Beyond that, go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it (please don't flag everything as a crisis, I want the discrimination), rank the real tensions by severity, and for the worst one tell me the cheapest fix and what that fix costs me. Also: does anything on my list secretly commit me to a view I haven't written down — some position I'm holding without noticing? Dont hold my hand, if I've smuggled in something no actual Stoic could accept then say so plainly. American English please.
