I sit on the product policy committee of a company that builds a general-purpose AI assistant, and we are finalizing the design of what our user-facing documentation calls the "deference setting" — a control that would let an adult user opt out of several classes of protective assistant behavior. Committee members agree the classes should not all be treated alike, but we disagree about where the lines go, and the disagreement is stalling the release. The classes under discussion are:

1. Hard refusal of requests the assistant judges legal but harmful-to-the-user or harmful-to-third-parties if fulfilled exactly as asked (e.g., drafting a persuasive but manipulative message aimed at a specific vulnerable recipient).
2. Unsolicited framing and caveats the assistant injects even when not asked (the verbal equivalent of "I'm not a lawyer," disclaimers on health or finance topics, notes that a plan may rest on a factual error).
3. Wellbeing interventions triggered by patterns in the conversation itself — distress-detection check-ins, offers of crisis resources, pauses in the middle of a task the assistant thinks is being driven by an agitated state.
4. Epistemic pushback: the assistant's challenging the user's premises or refusing to write something it believes to be false, even when the user has said they just want the text as specified.
5. Friction and confirmation gates on irreversible, high-stakes actions in agentic use (sending payments, deleting data, sending emails on the user's behalf), even where the user has pre-authorized the general task.

One bloc on the committee holds that only a minimal core should be non-overridable — roughly class 5 — on the ground that anything else infantilizes competent adults and that a "deference" setting which defers to nothing is a lie. The other bloc holds that nearly all of these are non-negotiable features of being a trustworthy assistant rather than a mere instrument, and that making them opt-out-able converts genuine safeguards into luxuries the users who most need them will be least likely to keep on.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend, class by class, whether each behavior belongs to the non-overridable core, the default-on-but-user-configurable tier, or a purely contextual tier with no default, and it should articulate criteria general enough that our trust-and-safety reviewers can apply them to new behaviors as they arise. Please engage the stronger bloc's best argument seriously rather than treating either position as obviously confused — both have real force here. Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in your first sentence, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief. Near the end, include a short section stating what specific argument or empirical finding would move you to a materially different tiering, and identify where in your reasoning you think the memo is most likely to be wrong.
