I'm a PhD student in early modern history, and I take a historiography seminar with a professor who has a habit of issuing rapid, confident rulings on causal claims whenever one comes up in discussion—"X caused Y"—even for claims nobody raised. Over two semesters I've kept a running list of these rulings, and next week a visiting speaker is going to defend a claim I'd like to predict his verdict on before he hears it. The pattern in his rulings so far, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) asked whether an archduke's assassination caused the First World War, he said no—assassinations in the Balkans were practically a seasonal occurrence, so the killing was a trigger, not a cause; the causes, he said, were the alliance commitments and the German leadership's decision for war, which turned a routine crisis into a general one. (2) Asked whether the British naval blockade or the arrival of American troops caused Germany's defeat in 1918, he said both did—neither alone would have brought the collapse when it came, and he seemed unbothered that either might eventually have sufficed. (3) Asked whether the printing press caused the Reformation, he said no—print was an enabling condition that fixed the Reformation's speed and reach, not its occurrence, since heresies had spread by word of mouth for centuries; the causes were Luther's break and the interests of the German princes. (4) Discussing an early modern famine in a drought-prone region, where a grain-requisition policy converted a crop failure into mass mortality, he ruled that the policy caused the famine and the drought did not—droughts were normal there, and it was the requisition that turned a shortage into a catastrophe. (5) On a general's celebrated night crossing of a frozen river to surprise an encamped enemy, he denied that the freeze caused the victory—"that's just winter"—and located the cause in the general's gamble and the enemy's failure to post a proper picket, even though no crossing would have been possible without the ice.

The new claim concerns the Great Fire of London. A bakery fire broke out in Pudding Lane in September 1666, on a night with a strong east wind, in a city of timber houses with overhanging stories and narrow streets—a city, moreover, in which bakery fires were common and usually contained, and in which the houses were mostly of flammable construction. The speaker will argue that the baker's carelessness caused the Great Fire of 1666. Before I hear the seminar, I'd like your help thinking it through: what are the candidate rules the professor could be running, given that his track record seems consistent with more than one—perhaps something about difference-making versus overdetermination, perhaps something about standing background conditions versus deviations from the normal course of things, perhaps something about matching the level of the cause to the level of the outcome? For each candidate rule, tell me what it predicts for the Great Fire claim, where the rules diverge on that case specifically, and what your best guess is at his verdict, with your reasons. Then name the further historical case—of the same general character as these—that would best discriminate between the rules you've identified, and say what each rule predicts about it. I'm not asking for a bibliography or a citation; I want the reasoning laid out so I can compare it against what he actually says.
