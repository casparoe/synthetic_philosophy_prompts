Last month, during the question period after a seminar in my department, I watched an objection get manufactured in real time. The presenter had just finished, and the first two questioners pressed the same criticism of a passage in the paper. By the fourth questioner, everyone was elaborating on it; by the sixth, it was simply the thing the room believed. Over coffee afterwards, it came out that most of the later questioners hadn't actually read the section in question — they had assumed the earlier ones had. I'm a first-year graduate student, and I've spent the weeks since reading about information cascades, because I gradually realized that what I'd witnessed was the textbook case: each questioner behaved rationally, weighting two public signals over their own unread private opinion, and the aggregate effect was that a room full of economists collectively suppressed the private information the room contained.

I'd like a sestina about this, because I think the form is almost suspiciously well suited to it. In a sestina the same six end-words recur through a fixed permutation, each line ending on a word we have already seen — which is roughly what a cascade does to the participants' private signals. I'd like you to choose the six end-words so that each can be read two ways: as something said aloud in the seminar room, and as a fragment of a questioner's unvoiced thought. And I'd like the permutation to do conceptual work rather than just formal work: as the poem progresses, each recurrence of an end-word should carry less of its original, private sense and more of its role as received opinion, so that by the last full stanza the words function almost as ritual — repeated correctly by people who no longer remember what they mean.

The envoi, which should fold all six end-words back into three lines, is where I'd like the turn to live: the moment after the seminar, over coffee, when someone finally admits they never read the passage. The private signal resurfaces all at once, condensed — which is to say the envoi should do for the poem what that admission did for the room: reveal that the information discarded along the way was there the whole time. Someone who knows the literature on rational herding should be able to recognize the structure underneath the poem; someone who doesn't should still come away with a good poem about a room talking itself into a conclusion. Please keep the language plain — no jargon, no mention of the literature — and let the recurrence of the end-words carry the argument.
