I'm writing a paper for a moral psychology seminar that reads the Pierre–Dolokhov duel in War and Peace through Appiah's account, in The Honor Code, of how dueling ended. Appiah's story is a macro one: dueling did not die because the arguments against it improved — those had been lying around for centuries — but because the connection between dueling and honor rotted from the outside. The class whose opinion conferred honor stopped conferring it on duelists, the ritual became an object of ridicule in the press, and what had been a credential turned into an embarrassment. Here is the twist I want to test. Tolstoy, I think, shows the same rot happening inside a single participant. Pierre is the offended party — the jealous husband — yet he drifts into the duel half by inertia, wins it, and then feels nothing the ritual promises: no honor restored, no satisfaction, only nausea, pity for the man he has shot, and a vertiginous crisis about who he has been all his life. Beside him stands Dolokhov, a practiced duelist for whom the emotional economy of honor still works perfectly: cool, precise, genuinely aggrieved. So the scene splits the institution into a man it still fits and a man it no longer fits, and neither of them argues about it for a moment.

I want two things from you. First, a judgment on my thesis: is Pierre's hollow victory a dramatization of Appiah's mechanism at the level of one breast — a preview of the social collapse — or does it expose a limit of Appiah's story, since dueling plainly kept running on men like Dolokhov long after Pierres stopped feeling it, which suggests that the participants' feelings were never what held the institution up? Second, a verdict on a Williams angle I'm toying with: does Pierre's crisis support the treatment of shame in Shame and Necessity, on which shame is an internalized judge rather than mere sensitivity to the eyes of onlookers? Pierre leaves the field haunted not by what society will say but by what he can no longer respect in himself, and I'd like to know whether that distinction does any real work here or collapses on inspection. Please be honest about where my reading strains — my worry is that Tolstoy cares about Pierre's spiritual biography, not about the fate of an institution, and that I'm turning a conversion scene into a case study it isn't. And if you think the scene teaches something about how honor institutions die that neither Appiah's social story nor Williams's internalized judge captures, I want to hear that most of all.
