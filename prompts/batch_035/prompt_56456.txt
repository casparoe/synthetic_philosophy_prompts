I am drafting a paper on luminosity — roughly, the thesis that certain mental states (feeling cold, being in pain, it seeming red to one) are such that, whenever one is in them, one is in a position to know one is in them. Before I engage any particular theory, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory in this area must deliver. Please write that list of desiderata.

As shared background, the theory must confront the following commitments and puzzles:

1. The gradual change case: a subject is cooled imperceptibly slowly over the course of an hour. At the start they know they feel warm; at the end they are cold without ever having noticed a change. Safety-theoretic opponents of luminosity reason, in brief and in my paraphrase: if the subject knows they are cold, their belief-forming method must be reliable in nearby counterfactual cases; but for any such case there is a nearby case in which they are just slightly warmer yet in all introspective respects indistinguishable, so the same method would have produced the same false belief; hence in the borderline region they never know they are cold; hence feeling cold is not luminous.
2. Failures of KK seem compelling in some cases and perverse in others. With a fair lottery, I can know my ticket will lose while seemingly not being in a position to know that I know it. Yet it seems absurd to deny that I know that I am in pain right now, or that I know that I know it.
3. Introspection is fallible in some respects: blindsight, confabulation under divided attention, sincerely disavowed implicit attitudes, and misremembered motives all suggest the inner eye is not infallible — but the fallibility looks different in kind from the fallibility of perception.

The deliverables, in order of importance:
