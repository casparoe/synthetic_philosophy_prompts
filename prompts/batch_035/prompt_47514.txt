I'm on the council of a 28-household cohousing community, and I need a decision memo drafted before our vote next month. American English please, and I want it casual-ish in tone but usable as a real memo the council can circulate.

Background: our founding bylaws contain what everyone here calls the "own-hearth rule." For a defined list of personal choices — your front garden, whether you keep pets, whether your unit hosts the shared tool library, and crucially whether you join the weekly communal dinner rota — the household itself has final say, and the council can never overrule. It's basically the reason half these people moved in. It's sacred.

The problem: the dinner rota is dying. We ran a survey, and the pattern is kind of bizarre when you write it out. Nearly every household says they get real value from the dinners existing — they want them to continue with decent participation. But nearly every household also says they'd personally rather skip their own turns and let others host. Under the own-hearth rule, each household decides only its own participation, so households keep quietly dropping out, and we're now approaching the threshold where the whole thing collapses. And here's the kicker: surveyed in pairs of outcomes, the households unanimously say they'd be better off if everyone were simply required to take one turn a month than if the dinners dissapeared entirely. An outcome everyone can veto their way into, that everyone says is worse than an outcome that requires overruling them.

A council member has proposed an amendment: the own-hearth rule stays in force, except that where the council finds literally every household prefers the compelled outcome, it may overrule a personal choice. Her critics on the council say this swallows the rule — it converts a right into something the community grants and can revoke whenever revoking is collectively convenient, and a right you hold only until it's inconvenient isn't much of a right. There's also a practical worry: once the exception exists, people will start misreporting their preferences strategically, and "everyone prefers" becomes uncheckable. A friend who did econ as an undergrad told me this whole mess is an instance of Sen's liberal paradox — apparently you can't have both a minimal respect for individual spheres of choice and the principle that unanimous preferences should win out, and our amendment is one way of picking a side.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action — adopt the amendment, reject it and let the dinners go, reject it and try something else entirely (restructure the choice somehow, buy the right, shrink the scope of the rule — your call), or adopt with conditions — with the reasoning laid out so the losing side could actually engage with it rather than feel steamrolled. I want an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the option you're rejecting, including whether the "rights can't be overridden by unanimity" position is really as strong as its defenders here think, or whether it depends on treating these personal choices as the kind of thing a right protects in the first place. If you think the amendment is a hack that "solves" the problem only verbally, say so and say what would actually solve it. Roughly 700-1000 words, structured like something a council chair would actually read.
