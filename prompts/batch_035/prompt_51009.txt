I'm a law student who ended up in a graduate statistics elective, and a pattern from that course has been haunting me ever since, though I can't find the right vocabulary for it. In class we were told, in effect, that there are two different questions one can ask about a candidate cause X and an outcome Y: what would we expect to see if X had been different, versus what would happen if we somehow reached in and made X different. These sound interchangeable, and in most everyday cases they give the same answer, but my professor showed us cases where they come apart. If the barometer reading had been low this morning, that would have been evidence a storm was on the way — but if I had opened the case overnight and moved the needle to low, nothing about the weather would change. Same X, same Y, same question mark, completely different answer depending on which of the two questions I actually meant. My epidemiologist friend says her whole field runs on this gap: if Alice had gotten the promotion she'd probably be calmer, in the sense that people who get promotions are a different population from people who don't; whether giving Alice the promotion by fiat would calm her down is a separate matter, and it's the second one her department is paid to answer.

Here's why it's not just the correlation-versus-causation slogan, at least as far as I can tell. That slogan, as usually taught, is about drawing conclusions from limited data — about mistaking association for causation when you haven't controlled for confounders. But the barometer case doesn't seem to be an epistemic problem at all: even a perfectly omniscient being who knew the complete joint distribution of everything in the world, with no sampling error whatsoever, would still need to distinguish the two questions, and could still get the wrong answer by conflating them. So the phenomenon I'm trying to name is something structural, not something about our ignorance. What's more, my legal training keeps bumping into it from another direction. The but-for test in tort law asks, roughly, whether the harm would have occurred had the defendant not acted as she did — and I've started to wonder which of the two readings courts are using, because in some fact patterns it really matters. And there seem to be intermediate cases my professor skipped: interventions that aren't clean surgeries on a variable but instead change the whole system or the person (if you had been more intelligent, you would have passed the exam — what would it even mean to reach in and set that?), and cases where nobody can intervene at all and yet we still confidently make causal claims, as historians do.

So: does this distinction between the observational and the interventional question (my phrasing; I don't know the field's) have an established name, and what exactly is the name for? I won't be satisfied with a bare term — I want to know which cases fall under it, whether the barometer, the promotion, and the but-for test are all the same phenomenon or subtly different ones, what happens at the edges (irrealizable interventions, interventions that alter the agent herself, claims about the distant past where no intervention is even coherent), and whether there are cases where the two readings coincide by necessity rather than by accident. If the legal but-for test turns out to be ambiguous between the two readings, I'd want to know which reading tort law arguably should use, and why. If no single established concept covers all of it, tell me where the standard treatment draws its own boundaries, and coin something for whatever residue is left over.
