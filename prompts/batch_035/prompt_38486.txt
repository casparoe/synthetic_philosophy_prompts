You are a poet with graduate-level training in ethics and a working command of meter — the kind of writer who could be trusted to put a philosophical argument into iambic pentameter without bending either the argument or the pentameter. Take the task below seriously as both a craft problem and a conceptual one, and think carefully before you draft.

**The commission.** A philosophy magazine is running a feature on thought experiments and wants a poem to close it. The editor wants a dramatic monologue in blank verse — thirty to forty lines of unrhymed iambic pentameter, a single uninterrupted speech with no narrator and no stage directions — in the voice of Nozick's utility monster. (In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, Nozick asks us to imagine a being that gets enormously more utility from every unit of a resource than anyone else does, so that a utilitarian calculus seems to require, in his phrase, that we all be sacrificed in the monster's maw in order to increase total utility.) The monster speaks on the eve of the final transfer, addressing the small delegation empowered to hand over the world's remaining goods.

**What the argument in the poem must actually be.** The monster's case has to be the real one, not a caricature, and it must be seductive: every unit of the world's goods yields vastly more wellbeing in it than in anyone else; the total, not its distribution, is what matters; therefore anything kept from it is, in the strictest sense, a waste — each apple fed to an ordinary appetite is a hundredfold loss. The horror of the poem should be that the reasoning is hard to fault given its premises. A utilitarian reader should be unable to point to a line where the monster cheats.

**What the form must do.** This is a dramatic monologue in the Browning manner: the speaker never breaks character and never realizes what his own speech reveals. The poem's real content should be what the monster cannot afford to notice — that the theory that feeds him has no place for the persons he displaces, only for their locations as sites of utility; that the same argument that empties the world on his behalf leaves him alone with everything. Do not editorialize: no outside judgment, no aside, no closing sermon. Let the reader's dawning discomfort be the volta. The ending should be quiet and sated rather than grand.

**Constraints.** The poem itself must not use philosophical jargon or name anyone — no "utility," "utilitarianism," "aggregation," "Nozick." The thought must be carried entirely by voice, image, and argument. Someone who knows the theory should find the monster's reasoning exactly right and the irony tracking the genuine objection to aggregation; someone who doesn't should simply find a chilling, persuasive, well-made monologue.

**Process.** Before writing, brainstorm briefly: three or four things the monster can say with perfect sincerity, and the one thing he cannot permit himself to notice; keep this working out to a few lines. Then write the poem. Afterwards, add a short note of two or three sentences explaining your key formal choices — where the irony is carried, and why you ended it where you did.
