I am currently taking a course in philosophy of psychiatry, and I am drafting the term essay for it. The assignment is an argumentative essay on Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness is a myth, and I have chosen a thesis that goes somewhat against what our instructor presented in lecture. I would like comments on my draft before I commit to it, and I would especially like you to take a definite position on whether the central argument works, rather than giving me a menu of possible objections to choose from myself.

Some background on where I am coming from. Our instructor treated Szasz's position as historically important but refuted: Szasz, in his 1961 book, argued that disease requires a bodily lesion, that minds cannot be diseased since they are not bodily things, and that calling mental conditions illnesses is a category error that dresses up moral and social disapproval in medical vocabulary. The standard reply, which we covered, is that Szasz relied on an outdated lesion-based concept of disease, since modern medicine recognizes plenty of diseases defined functionally rather than anatomically. My instructor then presented Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis from 1992 as the polished version of this reply: a disorder is a condition in which some mental mechanism fails to perform a natural function for which it was designed by evolution, and the failure is harmful by the person's own social values. This is supposed to combine a factual component with a value component and thereby preserve a genuine fact of the matter about disorder while conceding the evaluative element that Szasz was right to insist on.

My thesis is that Wakefield does not refute Szasz so much as relocate the myth. The core of my essay is what I call an evidential circularity problem. For any physical disorder the dysfunction claim is established independently of the harm: we can see that the heart's pumping capacity is compromised before we ask whether the patient suffers, because we know what hearts are for and can measure their performance directly. But for the mental realm, we have no independent access to the supposed mechanisms. Take prolonged, incapacitating grief. We say the grief mechanism is malfunctioning because the sadness has gone on too long and is ruining the person's life; but we have no way to tell, from the inside of the case, whether the mechanism is broken or is functioning exactly as designed in response to a genuinely terrible situation. The only evidence for the dysfunction is the very behavior that the harm judgment was based on. So for mental conditions, the dysfunction judgment collapses into the harm judgment, and the analysis yields not two components but one value judgment that has been run through the machinery twice and come out wearing a lab coat. That, I want to argue, is precisely Szasz's myth reproduced within the theory that claimed to bury him.

The essay's structure, in brief. Part one separates two Szaszian theses that I think get run together: the conceptual thesis that mental illness is a category error, and the political thesis that psychiatric commitment is coercion under medical cover. I claim the functional conception of disease kills the first thesis but leaves the second standing, and that commentators on both sides keep defending or attacking the wrong pairing. Part two is the circularity argument against Wakefield described above, which I use to argue that the conceptual thesis, suitably restated, survives: it is not that minds cannot be diseased, but that our mental disorder attributions contain no component that is conceptually independent of our evaluations of the person's life. Part three is constructive and the part I feel least secure about. I argue that psychiatry should drop the dysfunction requirement and openly reconceive mental disorders as harmful conditions, full stop; that this makes psychiatry a normative enterprise akin to applied ethics operating under conditions of distress; and that involuntary treatment can still sometimes be justified, but only by the ordinary paternalistic arguments we would use to justify taking away someone's car keys, never by appeal to illness.

Here is what I want from you. First, tell me directly whether the circularity argument in part two actually holds up, including against the most serious objection you can construct for it; I know Wakefield has defenders who distinguish between the concept of dysfunction and our evidence for it, and I suspect that distinction matters, but I have not been able to figure out how much damage it does. Second, tell me whether part three follows from part two or whether I have smuggled in a further evaluative premise, since it is a standing embarrassment for my thesis that harm is itself an evaluative notion, and my official complaint against Wakefield was evaluative double-counting. Third, give me your own verdict on the essay's overall thesis, defended with reasons, so that I can either steelman my draft against it or revise in its direction. If you think a specific move in my argument is simply wrong, say so plainly rather than presenting it as one live option among several; I would rather learn now that the core argument fails than after the essay is graded.
