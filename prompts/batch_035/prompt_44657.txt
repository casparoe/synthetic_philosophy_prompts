I'm in a college reading group on international criminal law, and this week we're doing the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. The discussion keeps getting shut down by the phrase "victors' justice" — someone says it, everyone nods, and the actual question we were trying to answer evaportes. It came to a head last session when we were arguing about whether a defeated state's leaders can legitimately be prosecuted by an international court afterwards. My friend said the whole precedent is tainted: only the losing side was ever tried, the Allies sat as judges over things they arguably did too (the firebombing of cities came up here), and the most aggressive charges — especially crimes against peace at Tokyo — were pretty much invented after the fact. So for her "victors' justice" meant: the tribunals were a sham, and we shouldn't lean on them as precedent for anything today. I'm pretty sure that last leap doesn't follow, but I also suspect the phrase isn't *just* wrong, so I want to know what's actually in it.

Can you take "victors' justice" apart into the distinct precise claims it could be standing for? I want a menu I can bring back to the group: for each reading, say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and — this is the part that matters — which of those readings, if true, would really support my friend's move of dismissing the tribunals' legitimacy, versus which ones are just true but don't get you there on their own. Like, if the claim is only "the victors ran the court," does that show the verdicts were wrong, or only that the process had a structural bias that might or might not have affected outcomes? Please cover the retroactivity issue specifically: the nullum crimen idea, whether the charges at Nuremberg and Tokyo were genuinely ex post facto or were arguably already criminal under pre-war law, and whether that story differs between the two tribunals (I've heard Tokyo was on much shakier ground than Nuremberg on this, but I don't know how much of that is real).

One more thing: be upfront about what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who actually know this area. Some of this is just history no one disputes; other parts — like whether the retroactivity objection defeats the tribunals' legal legitimacy entirely, or only shows the legal reasoning was retrofitted — are live debates among legal philosophers and historians, and I'd rather have that labeled than have one contested take presented as the consensus. That's the whole sickness in my group: people assert a slogan as if it were settled law. If some readings of the phrase are contested precisely at the point where my friend would need them to be solid, please say that plainly.
