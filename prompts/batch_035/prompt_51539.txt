I am preparing a seminar unit on whether famines should be classified as political crimes rather than natural disasters, using Amartya Sen's entitlement approach as the organizing framework. Sen's core observation is that famine mortality is typically governed not by how much food exists in a region but by who is able to command it — through own-production, exchange, labor, or transfers from the state or from others. I would like you to work through three cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Great Famine in Ireland in the late 1840s, and the Chinese famine that followed the Great Leap Forward. For each case, assign two numbers on a 0-to-1 scale. The first is an explanatory score: how much of the pattern of mortality is accounted for by collapses in specific groups' ability to acquire food, as against an aggregate shortfall in food availability. The second is a culpability score: how far those entitlement collapses trace to deliberate and foreseeable policy choices made by agents who had both the information and the power to act otherwise, as opposed to ignorance, institutional inertia, or binding external constraint. Then give an overall ranking of the three cases by how cleanly the entitlement explanation converts into a charge of political crime, and — this is the part I most need — identify for each case the single weakest link in the inference from Sen's explanatory framework to the criminal classification. In the seminar I want to insist that the causal question and the moral question remain distinct, so please make explicit where, if anywhere, your scores for a case pull apart, and treat any divergence between the two scores as the interesting result rather than a defect to be explained away. I am less interested in comprehensive historical detail than in whether the reasoning keeps the two scales independent.
