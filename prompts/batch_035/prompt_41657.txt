I got into a really long argument over coffee with my friend Maya about supererogation — you know, acts it would be good of you to do but not wrong to skip, like throwing yourself on a grenade to save the squad or donating a kidney to a total stranger. I went in thinking this was just a boring, obvious part of commonsense morality. Maya walked me out genuinely stuck, because she claims the concept is flat-out incoherent, and her argument is short enough that I can't see where it bends. Best I can reconstruct it: (1) A supererogatory act is one that's permissible to do and also permissible to omit — it's "beyond the call of duty," so neither doing it nor skipping it leaves you at fault. (2) If you're not at fault, morality is done with you — there is nothing further to be said against you from the moral point of view. (3) So for the soldier who stays put while the grenade is in the air, no moral criticism is available. (4) But then there can't be any moral reason favoring jumping on it either, because a moral reason that never makes the alternative forbidden isn't really a reason at all — on Maya's picture, morality works like a rulebook that only ever requires, forbids, or stays silent. (5) So jumping on the grenade is not morally better than ducking; at most it's admirable in some other, wierd non-moral way. (6) And if it's not morally better, then calling it supererogatory — morally beyond duty — is a confusion, and every act is either obligatory, forbidden, or morally indifferent.

Maya knows (6) sounds extreme but says she'll bite the bullet: moral praise and moral blame come as a package, so if the soldier who ducks deserves no blame, the one who jumps deserves no distinctively moral credit, and what we call heroism is just something else we happen to like. For me (6) proves way too much — it seems to also imply that giving $500 to charity is no morally better than giving $50, when surely both are permissible but one is just better. But I can't locate the bad step. My instinct is that something slides somewhere in the middle — maybe between (3) and (4), the move from "no criticism of omitting" to "no moral reason to do it" — but I can't say what's fishy about it without just stamping my foot and repeating that heroes are good.

Can you sort this out for me? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with nothing before or after the list: (1) the step you'd reject, with a precise statement of what's wrong with it; (2) why that step looked so natural in the first place; (3) what rejecting it costs, i.e. the slightly odd view of moral reasons or moral criticism you end up owing Maya; (4) the strongest comeback Maya could still make against your diagnosis; and (5) your bottom-line verdict on whether supererogation survives.
