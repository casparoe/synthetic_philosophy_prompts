I work on numerical software for a living — bignum arithmetic, primality tests, that sort of thing — and I've gotten tangled in an argument about what my code is actually about. I have no philosophy training, so I've written it out as carefully as I can, as if it were a proof sketch.

First, the observation that motivates everything: when a colleague on another continent implements arbitrary-precision arithmetic from scratch, and I do the same, our implementations agree on primality, on modular inverses, on where the carries go. It feels like we are both probing one fixed object that neither of us built — like two observatories measuring the same star. Philosophers apparently call the view that numbers are real, mind-independent, non-spatial, timeless objects "platonism," and it matches my working intuition. But now the argument:

1. A belief counts as knowledge only if the connection between the belief and the fact that makes it true is not accidental — the fact, or something it involves, has to help explain why I believe it.
2. Helping explain, in this sense, means being part of a causal chain terminating in my belief: photons from the star hit the sensor, the sensor causally affects the computer, and so on.
3. If platonism is true, numbers are causally inert — the number 7 causes nothing, including my belief that 7 is prime.
4. So if platonism is true, none of my mathematical beliefs are knowledge; they are right the way a stopped clock is right twice a day.
5. But some mathematical beliefs plainly are knowledge: I know there is no largest prime, and Euclid's argument for it is the most certain thing I own.
6. Therefore platonism is false.

I could accept the conclusion if the argument stayed contained. What alarms me is what happens when I rerun it with other things substituted for numbers. Put "the laws of logic" in place of "numbers" and premise 4 tells me I don't know that modus ponens preserves truth. Put in "the fact that my proof checker is sound" and I lose the very assurance I'd need to trust my formal methods. Worse: premise 1 is itself a belief, and if it's true, the fact that makes it true is arguably not a cause of my believing it — so by its own standard, the argument's foundation is not knowledge. I have the strong sense the argument is proving far too much and possibly indicting itself, but I can't localize the failure.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. For the record: I want to keep 5 — my entire profession presupposes it — and I'd like to keep 3, since causal inertness seems like the one thing about abstract objects I actually understand. So I expect I have to break something in 1 or 2, and I want to know precisely what I pay: does weakening 2 leave me unable to rule out knowledge by crystal ball, where a clairvoyant reliably guesses tomorrow's lottery without any causal contact with the draw? And is there a way to keep the argument's insight about planets and stopwatches while letting numbers, logic, and soundness back in? Please be concrete about which trade-off is cheapest.
