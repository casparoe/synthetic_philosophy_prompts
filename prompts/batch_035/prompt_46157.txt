For my English class final, I have to read the ending of Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men through the philosophical distinction between doing and allowing, killing versus letting die, and I want to get past the obvious discussion. At the end, George shoots Lennie in the back of the head while distracting him with the story of the farm they'll never have, rather than letting Curley's mob reach him first — and the book also keeps a third option alive throughout, the state asylum. Here is the complication I want you to take seriously: assume some grave harm comes to Lennie whatever George does, since the mob is already in pursuit and the asylum would mean lifelong confinement. Some philosophers hold that when the bad outcome is coming anyway, the only difference left between killing and letting die is whether the harm passes through the agent — through George's hands and will — or merely through the world. Does Steinbeck's staging speak to that, especially given that George had long before told Lennie to hide by the river if trouble ever happened, so the meeting that made the killing possible was arranged in advance? And when Slim reassures George afterward that he had no choice, does that hold up? Please take a definite position, with reasons: was George's act right, and does the doing/allowing distinction do real work in reaching that verdict — or is what ultimately matters something else, such as mercy, or the fact that Lennie, given his disability, could not meaningfully consent?
