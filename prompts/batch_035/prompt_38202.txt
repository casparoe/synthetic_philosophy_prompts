I'm a program officer at a family foundation that gives away around $15M a year across three buckets: global health, local education, and a "wildcard" fund for research and pilot projects. I'm the one who reads the proposals and writes the recommendation memo the board votes on, so everything runs through me first. That's maybe twenty decisions a year, and it's the most money I'll ever help direct, so I'd like to not fumble it.

Here's my problem. I came to this job from a program-evaluation background, and I keep hitting a wall that I think philosophers actually have a name for. For any given grant I can trace the near-term effects well enough: kids tutored, clinics supplied, papers written. Everyone here agrees the distant effects dwarf the near ones and nobody can trace them. A tutoring program might bend a kid's whole life trajectory or do nothing; a cheap medical pilot might get picked up and scaled by a government or quietly forgotten. And when I try comparing across buckets — local vs global, present people vs future ones — I'm not just unable to add everything up, I can't even say whether my uncertainty would wash out on average or whether I'm missing an entire direction of effect, good or bad, that I haven't thought of. I read somewhere that this is called cluelessness, and that someone (Greaves? I honestly forget) splits it into cases where you're overwhelmed by too many individually traceable effects vs cases where you can't even agree on the signs. That distinction felt like it was written about me, but I've lost the reference and don't need it back.

So what I want from you is a procedure. Something I can run alone in about 90 minutes per proposal, before writing my one-page memo. Concretely it should tell me: which questions to ask about a proposal and in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the honest output is "this is a values call, hand it to the board as such" rather than me dressing it up as analysis; and where the procedure deliberately sends me back to comparing near-term effects even though we all agree those aren't what matters most. On that last one, I want to understand why it isn't just cowardice — or be told honestly that it is.

Then run the procedure on three proposals so I can see how it behaves:

(a) $1.8M to a deworming program with a decent track record in one country, expanding into a neighboring country with much weaker health infrastructure;
(b) $500K to a local afterschool literacy program, with a small but real local RCT behind it;
(c) $2M to a tiny team doing research on institutional decision-making under deep uncertainty — i.e., precisely the thing that makes my job hard — with no track record whatsoever.

Two more things. First, at least one of the three should come out with a recommendation I'll personally hate, and I want to see it happen rather than have the procedure mysteriously bend to what I'd want. Second, tell me what the procedure can't do: where following it faithfully would still produce the wrong answer, and how I would even notice that it had. My board chair is allergic to philosophy jargon, so whatever you give me should be runnable without me having to explain what a consequentialist is at the next meeting. I don't need a reading list — I need something I can actually run on Thursday.
