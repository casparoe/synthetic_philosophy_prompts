After a career in experimental biophysics, much of it spent measuring how neurons actually behave, I have retired and am working through philosophy of mind with more care than I ever had time for previously. Before I develop any of this into a longer essay, I want to know whether the following positions, each of which I hold with some confidence, can all be true at once:

1. The brain literally computes. This is not a metaphor or a modeling convenience: neural systems carry information about the world and transform it according to lawful operations that can be stated precisely, and that is a fact about the organ on the same footing as the fact that the kidney filters blood. It does not depend on our choosing to describe the brain that way.

2. Computation is substrate-independent. What makes a physical system a computer is its formal organization — the abstract pattern of state transitions — and not its material composition. In principle the very same computation could be realized in silicon, in hydraulic valves, or in neurons.

3. The Chinese Room argument is right, or at least it points at something real: syntax alone is never sufficient for semantics, and a system could manipulate symbols in perfect accordance with the rules while understanding nothing. The recent language models strike me the same way — fluent prediction without comprehension.

4. Not everything computes. A rock, a wall, the solar system: these do not implement arbitrary automata under some sufficiently generous mapping, and I regard pancomputationalism as a reductio of any theory of implementation that entails it.

5. The brain is an analog, continuous, noisy system embedded in a body, and the body in an environment. I doubt its operations can be discretized without loss, and I am skeptical of the claim that the brain is a digital computer in any straightforward sense.

6. Two verdicts on cases. First, a computer simulation of a hurricane, however detailed, produces no wind and wets nothing: simulation is not duplication. Second, a sufficiently faithful emulation of a person's brain would be a person, with a mind; I would grant functional duplication in the case of the brain while denying it in the case of weather.

I can see that some of these must be causing me trouble, but I honestly cannot tell which. Please audit the set: identify which pairs stand in genuine tension and how badly, which only appear to conflict, and for each genuine tension say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and indicate which single revision would clean up the most. I would particularly like to know whether my two verdicts in (6) can be held together, or whether doing so requires support I have not supplied elsewhere on the list.
