For about a year now I have been keeping a notebook of situations where I either do or don't find myself treating something mental — a decision, a pain, a fear, a mood — as a genuine cause of something physical, and my verdicts refuse to line up. I have no philosophy training; I repair industrial boilers for a living, and I started thinking about this after a coworker said the thermostat on one of our units "decided" the water was too cold. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind the verdicts below, or whether I have to give some of them up, and I would rather be told that honestly than be flattered.

My verdicts:

1. Yesterday I decided to wave at my neighbor and my arm went up. I believe the deciding genuinely caused the waving — not merely accompanied it — even though I understand that at the level of nerves and muscles there is a complete physical story about why the arm moved.

2. When I touch a hot pan and snatch my hand back, I think the pain caused the withdrawal, even though the reflex is faster than any decision and the nerve signals would tell the whole story on their own.

3. When the room gets cold and the thermostat clicks the furnace on, I am happy to say the coldness of the room caused the furnace to run. But if someone said the thermostat noticed the cold and acted on it, I would call that a manner of speaking — nothing mental caused anything there.

4. When my chess app beats me because it supposedly wanted control of the center, I treat that as shorthand. The numbers it computes caused the moves; the wanting caused nothing.

5. But when my cousin, who has a serious fear of heights, turns down a good job on the eleventh floor, I think the fear really did cause his refusal — it is not shorthand the way the chess talk is. And I cannot see what the difference is, since both are presumably patterns in circuitry producing behavior.

6. When a drunk driver hits a tree, I say the alcohol caused the crash, and I also say his decision to drive caused it. I am comfortable holding both causes at once and feel no need to choose.

7. When someone has a seizure and falls down the stairs, only the physical story is a cause; no mental item is involved, and nobody is tempted to say otherwise.

8. The one I keep going back and forth on: after my friend started on medication, her mood lifted and she rebuilt her whole life. I want to say the hopelessness had been causing her paralysis and the lifting of it caused the change. But if you traced it, the hopelessness is brain chemistry, and if the pill changed that chemistry directly, I cannot tell whether the mood is doing any causing or whether the chemistry is doing all of it and the mood is just the name for how it feels from inside.

Here is what I want. Is there one principle that separates the cases where I count the mental item as a real cause (1, 2, 5, and sometimes 8) from those where I do not (3, 4), and from the one where I happily accept two causes at different levels at once (6)? The principle cannot just say "only minds count," because I refuse to count the thermostat's noticing even though it does something recognizably mind-adjacent, while I am quick to count my cousin's fear even though I can't say what minds are either. It also has to face the fact that in my own cases, 1 and 2, there is by my own admission a complete physical story at the level of nerves and muscles — so what, if anything, does the mental description add as a cause? Please state the principle precisely enough to apply to new cases, tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a robot guide dog steering its blind user around an open manhole; a parrot that shrieks whenever the toast is about to burn; a person under hypnosis who opens an umbrella when told to; a patient whose brain scans show activity initiating a movement a fraction of a second before any awareness of deciding anything — and be honest about which of my eight verdicts, if any, the principle cannot save. If you think no single principle saves them all, say which verdict I should give up and why that one rather than another.

Please give me the whole answer as a JSON object with these fields. "principle": a short statement of the best principle you can find, or null if you think none exists. "clauses": an array of objects, each with "clause" (one component of the principle in plain language), "motivated_by" (the case number or numbers that force it in), and "what_it_does" (what verdicts it is there to protect). "verdict_review": an array over all eight of my cases, each an object with "case" (the number), "my_verdict" (my position as you understand it), "principle_verdict" (what the principle says), "overturned" (true or false), and "reason". "new_cases": an array over the four unconsidered cases, each with "case" (a short name), "prediction" (what the principle says the mental item causes, or does not), and "confidence" (high, medium, or low, with a sentence why). "residual_conflict": the single hardest pair of my verdicts to reconcile, and, if you had to break the tie, which one you would overturn and at what cost.
