So, bit of background: I'm a retired seismologist. Thirty-some years of earthquake hazard assessment, first with the government survey and later as a consultant, and now that I'm in my seventies I've gotten heavily into philosophy — go figure. I never took a philosophy course in my life, so forgive me if I use the terms wrong.

I recently ran into what the philosophers call the reference class problem, and it hit me like a truck, because I've been living inside that problem for four decades without knowing it had a name. The issue, as I understand it: when you want to put a probability on some individual thing (a person, a quake, a business), you first have to answer "a member of what class of things?" and different classes give different numbers, and nothing tells you which class is the one.

I've been sitting with my coffee going through cases where I have a gut verdict about the right class, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle underneath all my verdicts or whether I'm just making it up as I go. Here they are.

1. When we estimated the chance of a big quake (say magnitude 8+) on a particular segment of the Cascadia margin, my verdict was always: use the narrowest class that still has real data behind it. "All magnitude 8 earthquakes worldwide" is too coarse. "Similar subduction-zone segments worldwide" is better. Best of all is the segment's own prehistoric record, which you get from buried soils and offshore landslide deposits — I'd take that over any global average. Narrow wins, as long as the data holds up.

2. A cardiologist friend once asked me how he should think about a patient's risk of a bad outcome from a procedure, and I told him the same thing: don't use the rate printed in the brochure, which pools all patients; use patients matched on age and comorbidities. Narrow again. Except — and here's a wrinkle — when the matched group turned out to be only six or eight patients, I told him to fall back on the pooled rate, and I honestly could never decide whether that was sound statistics or just cowardice.

3. But then, a few years back, my grandson asked me how much longer our family's lake cabin would probably stay in the family, and I immediately answered from a broad base rate — how long family properties like this stay in one family across the country, from survey data. And I'm still confident that was the right call, even though our family is quite unlike most families in ways I could name if pressed (no kids fighting over it, for one). Broad wins here, and narrowing would have made the answer worse, not better. This is more or less the opposite verdict from cases 1 and 2, on what feels like the same kind of question, and I can't say why.

4. And then there's the doomsday argument, which I gather is due to a Brandon Carter and a J. Richard Gott, though I may have that garbled. My verdict there is different from all of the above: something like "using 'all humans who will ever live' as the class is cheating," though I can't for the life of me articulate why. And I notice that when I shrink the class to "humans born after 1900" or "humans in industrial societies," the conclusion about how long humanity has left moves around an awful lot, which makes me suspect the whole exercise. My gut says no class is right there at all. Whether that's a real verdict or just an escape hatch, I don't know.

So that's my set of verdicts. What I want from you is a principle — or a short list of candidate principles — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you. It has to tell me: when to go narrow, when to go broad, and when to refuse to play. For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save: I'd rather give one up knowingly than have you contort the principle into a Rube Goldberg machine to rescue all four. And please run the principle on a case I didn't list — say, estimating whether a specific clinical trial's positive result will replicate — and show your work, so I can see how the principle actually gets applied rather than just being asserted.

Two requests on how you should go about this. First, if you know the actual philosophical literature here (I gather there's a fellow named Bostrom who wrote about this, and something about Reichenbach), weave it in, but don't turn the answer into a bibliography — I want the principle, not a reading list, and I can't check citations from my armchair anyway. Second, and this matters to me as an old scientist: end by telling me where your reasoning is most likely to fail. What argument or worked example, if someone produced it, would change your verdict on the principle — and on my cases 3 and 4 in particular, which are the ones I'd bet money are the weak spots? I've been wrong about plate boundaries before; I can take it.

Oh, and one small thing: write it in American English — I've got enough trouble with terminology without translating spellings too. Sorry for the length, this is what happens when you retire.
