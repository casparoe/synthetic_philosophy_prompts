Quick question that's been bugging me ever since our reading group got onto distributive justice. Everyone kept saying the leveling-down objection is what sank egalitarianism — no theory can call a situation an improvement just because the gap between top and bottom shrank, when literally everyone is worse off and nobody gained anything. Fine, I get that. But then someone said prioritarianism is the safe harbor precisely because it escapes that objection, and honestly I don't see the escape at all. If prioritarianism says benefits to the worse off count for extra, doesn't it follow that a world where the better-off lose something (and the worse off get nothing in return) is at least slightly better than before, since the worst off are now relatively closer to everyone else? My friend says no, the weighting is supposed to be about people's absolute level of welfare, not their position relative to others, so making the rich poorer without helping anyone just makes the total worse, full stop. But if that's the move, then isn't prioritarianism just utlitarianism with a fudge factor — a discount rate applied to welfare for whoever happens to be low? What's philosophically interesting about a theory whose only difference from utilitarianism is arithmetic? And if the real answer is "it matters who the benefits go to, not just how much welfare there is," then I don't see how that's any different from the pattern-valuing that the leveling-down objection was supposed to rule out — you've just smuggled the pattern back in through the weighting. Sorry if this is a dumb question, I'm coming at this from economics and I keep feeling like I'm missing a step that everyone else finds obvious.
