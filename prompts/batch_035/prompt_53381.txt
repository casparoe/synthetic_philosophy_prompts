Six months ago I left a career in corporate law after a serious burnout, and I now face a decision that the debate over the agricultural revolution has, if anything, made harder. I can return to a reduced version of my old career in the city, or my partner and I can commit to a semi-subsistence smallholding we've recently inherited: a few acres, livestock, a large garden, with income topped up by occasional contract work. In working through this, I've been reading around the old question of whether the transition to agriculture was, for those who lived through it and their descendants, a catastrophe: the marked decline in health and stature visible in the remains of early farmers, the emergence of hereditary inequality and organized warfare with surplus and storage, Marshall Sahlins's idea of an original affluent society in which foragers were rich in time because their wants were few, and Jared Diamond's polemic that farming was the worst mistake in human history. What I've noticed is that my decision keeps getting hijacked by claims from this literature that, on reflection, may not follow from it at all.

What I want from you is a disciplined triage, not a survey. Please rank the following putative implications of the hunter-gatherer-affluence debate by how much they should actually bear on a choice like mine, and defend the ordering:

1. That humans are adapted to a foraging mode of life, so my burnout and general malaise are a form of evolutionary mismatch that a more agrarian or simpler life would cure.
2. That because foragers devoted far fewer hours to subsistence, the modern workweek is a cultural artifact I am entitled to opt out of.
3. That since hereditary inequality begins with farming and stored surplus, a life organized around household production is structurally less hierarchical than one organized around a firm.
4. That the earliest centuries of farming were so bad for the farmers themselves that anything resembling small-scale agrarian life should be avoided on their evidence.
5. That because forager affluence was a matter of limited wants rather than abundant means, the real lever available to me is wanting less rather than producing more.

For each item, tell me whether it follows from the evidence and arguments in that debate or is a romantic extrapolation; identify which one or two are genuinely load-bearing for my decision and which are overrated; and explain what consideration would move an item up or down your ranking — for example, whether the celebrated leisure estimates for foragers would survive careful reanalysis, whether the early-farming health collapse was an effect of crowding and sedentism rather than agriculture as such, or whether the strong claims only ever concerned foragers in unusually favored environments and marginal foragers complicate the picture. If there is an implication I have not listed that you think matters more than any of mine, add it and say why. I'm less interested in the history than in which parts of this debate a person standing at a fork in the road can legitimately lean on, and which parts I should stop leaning on. Please reason it through rather than pointing me to literature; I'm after the argument, not a bibliography.
