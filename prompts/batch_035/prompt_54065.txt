I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and my supervisor has strong and recurring views about which futures are "open" and which are "settled." We meet Thursday to go over a paper draft of mine, and I'd like to be able to predict his verdicts before he gives them. His track record so far: a frictionless pendulum, settled; a double pendulum, settled, on the grounds that chaos is a limitation on us rather than on the world; a coin flip, settled; weather three months out, settled, though he concedes no physically realizable observer could predict it; the decay time of a single radioactive atom, open; the outcomes of a spin measurement on an EPR pair, open; the output of a pseudorandom number generator, settled, since in his words it is settled by the seed. The cases my paper turns on are: (1) a macroscopic chaotic circuit whose initial conditions are fixed by sampling quantum shot noise, so that indeterministic events are amplified into classical chaos — is its future open or settled, and settled from what moment onward?; (2) a fully deterministic cellular automaton whose evolution admits no predictive shortcut, so the only way to know its state at time t is to simulate it to t; and (3) whether, on his view, my decision about which version of the paper to submit is open, given that neurons are largely classical systems with ion-channel noise at the bottom of the causal story. Which rule is he implicitly running — "open" means no fact of the matter before the event, "open" means unpredictable even by an ideal physical predictor, or "open" means quantum indeterminism somewhere in the causal history? Several of these rules seem to fit his past verdicts equally well, so tell me which do, what I should expect him to say about the three cases, with your best guess and reasons, and what further case I could raise that would genuinely discriminate between the rules instead of leaving him room to equivocate.
