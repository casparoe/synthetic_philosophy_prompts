I've spent years reading the rationalist blogosphere on decision theory, free will, and the Newcomb problem, and there's a cluster of issues where I think our community's confident talk papers over genuine open questions. One of them is Frankfurt-style cases. I want your credence — a real number — that moral responsibility for an action is compatible with that agent having had no genuine alternative to performing it. Not a survey of the literature; a probability with its derivation exposed.

Here is the specific setup I want you to reason about, since the details matter. Consider a standard Frankfurt scenario: Jones votes for a corrupt incumbent entirely of his own accord, moved by his own greed and tribal loyalty; a neuroscientist, Black, has implanted a device that monitors Jones's deliberation and would have forcibly intervened had Jones shown any sign of voting the other way. But now let me complicate it the way the decision-theory debates I grew up on would: suppose Black's device relies on a predictor of Jones's choice that is only 95% accurate rather than perfectly accurate. In the actual run, Jones votes corruptly on his own and the device never fires — that much is fixed. But it is not metaphysically or even probabilistically guaranteed that Jones couldn't have done otherwise: had the device's predictor been one of its unlucky 5%, and had Jones resolved to vote differently, Jones would have voted differently and the device would have learned nothing. So my question is really two nested questions, and I want a credence for each plus a combined figure: first, that in the classical perfect-predictor version of the case, Jones is morally responsible for his vote even though he could not have done otherwise; second, that the 95%-accurate variant changes the answer in kind rather than merely by degree — that the residue of alternative possibility, however improbable, is doing real work in our intuitions about responsibility, perhaps because evidential and causal considerations come apart here in a way that resembles one-boxing versus two-boxing.

Decompose the reasoning into the sub-claims your overall number rests on: whether the 'flicker' objection — that some tiny alternative always remains in these cases — can be dismissed as resting on a trivial notion of alternative; whether the 'dilemma' line of attack, according to which cases like this presuppose either causal determinism or foreknowledge of the agent's choice, and each presupposition undermines the case in a different way, succeeds against at least one way of filling in the details; whether the distinction between requiring alternatives and requiring that the action originate in the agent — sourcehood rather than leeway — is deep or merely a relabelling of the dispute; and whether the phrase 'could have done otherwise' has a determinate enough meaning for the whole question to have a truth value at all. Assign each of these a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine to your overall figures, and tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument, thought experiment, or empirical finding would most move it. If you think the 95% variant is really a different case dressed up as the same one, say so and adjust the decomposition accordingly — but then tell me where between perfect prediction and coin-flip prediction the case changes character, and why there rather than elsewhere.

Please answer in British English, and give me the numbers even where you'd rather hedge — I find that a stated credence with its weak points marked is worth ten paragraphs of 'it depends on how you frame responsibility'.
