Two professors in my department have been arguing about responsibility gaps for something like six years now, and honestly it's gotten a bit sad — they keep citing each other in increasingly irritated footnotes and I'm pretty sure neither has changed their mind on anything since 2019. I've managed to talk them into an adversarial collaboration, and I'm the one who's supposed to draft the joint statement. I need your help writing it.

The two positions, roughly:

- Professor V holds what she calls the closure view: the moral ledger has to close. For any sufficiently serious harm, there must be some identifiable agent who is responsible for it — maybe culpable, maybe merely answerable, but someone. If deploying a system foreseeably produces significant outcomes that nobody is responsible for, then deploying it is pro tanto wrong, and that pro tanto wrongness is a real feature that can be overridden by benefits but never erased or explained away.
- Professor V's opponent (I'll call him R) thinks the whole framing is confused. Responsibility was never supposed to be exhaustive: earthquakes, cancer, wild animal suffering, market crashes, and plain old bureaucratic many-hands diffusion have always produced harms nobody owns, and nobody thinks that makes the weather immoral. Ex ante responsibility for the risk-imposition — designing, choosing, deploying — is all the ownership morality ever demanded. R's favorite line is that V wants moral bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is tidiness, not ethics. He also loves the phrase "moral crumple zones" for what happens when you force the ledger to close: you end up manufacturing a scapegoat, usually the nearest human who had nominal oversight but no real control.

They've agreed to anchor everything on a shared testbed case instead of talking past each other in the abstract, so please use this one: a hospital runs a closed-loop ICU sedation controller that titrates dosage on its own, with clinicians setting bounds up front but not signing off on individual adjustments (this is roughly how real ones work, and they've agreed on the description). One night the system makes a judgment-call adjustment — the kind where two competent anesthesiologists could reasonably disagree — and the patient suffers a serious harm. Post-hoc review finds no one did anything wrong: the bounds were set reasonably, the training data was fine, the clinicians were attending to other patients as assigned. V says this is a textbook responsibility gap and exactly what she's been warning about. R says it's just Tuesday in a hospital — patients have always been harmed by diffuse chains of judgment where no individual blundered.

What I need from you:

1. A numbered section of claims both would actually sign — about the case, about what responsibility does for us morally, about what would and wouldn't count as closing the gap (e.g., whether strict liability on the manufacturer, or a formal "answerability pool," would do it). Please don't make these vacuous; a claim like "autonomous systems raise important questions" would get me laughed at.

2. A disagreements table where each residual disagreement is worded so that its own side would accept the phrasing. This is the part I keep botching — e.g., I know V would sign "the harm was nobody's and that is a defect in the situation," and R would sign "the harm was nobody's and that is not itself a defect," but I can't get from those to neutral shared wording, and maybe I don't need to.

3. The cruxes. My guesses: (a) whether responsibility is fundamentally a retrospective ledger that should sum to full ownership of outcomes, or a forward-looking deliberative concept that just doesn't apply where no one deliberated; (b) whether ex ante responsibility for imposing a risk transfers any ownership of the token bad outcomes, or only of the risk itself; (c) whether the strategies for "closing" the gap (liability, answerability pools, mandatory human sign-off) restore whatever good responsibility does or just manufacture crumple zones — and notice that this one might be partly empirical.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Feel free to invent cases (e.g., variants of the ICU one where you tweak who knew what, or when), since that's actually what they've been doing in seminars.

If you think part of the dispute is verbal, please say which part and why, in wording that doesn't condescend to either of them.
