I teach an introductory ethics course that includes a short unit on philosophy of language, and I'd like a diagnostic quiz for my students, along the lines of "Which view of the lying/misleading distinction do you actually hold?" The debate it should sort them on: when a speaker says something literally true but designed to make the hearer believe something false — as opposed to coming out and lying — is there a morally significant difference between the two acts? Please write it in the voice of a professional philosopher working at the intersection of ethics and philosophy of language, and before drafting anything, spend some real effort working out the logical space: decide which axes genuinely separate the positions before you place any named view in it. I'd suggest the axes should at least include (a) where the wrong is located — in the effect on the hearer's beliefs, in the act-type the speaker performs, or in the speaker's relation to the hearer's trust — and (b) whether the act's moral status depends on whether the hearer is entitled to the truth in the first place.

The quiz itself: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. The point is to sort students by what they say about cases, not by which labels they like, so no question may simply ask whether lying is worse than misleading. The following cases must appear among the questions. First, the murderer (or secret police) at the door asking where the fugitive is, with answer options covering flat-out lying, technically-true evasion, and refusing to answer — the classic Kant/Constant exchange and its descendants, though please do not treat Kant's own verdict on evasion as settled, since what his reply to Constant permits is disputed. Second, a bald-faced lie, where the speaker knows full well that the audience knows the claim is false; whether this even counts as a lie is itself one of the axes, so the question should probe that without using the term. Third, a lawyer-style technically-true answer under formal questioning. Fourth, a case where the questioner has no right to the truth at all — an interrogator for an unjust regime asking whether you are sheltering anyone — so that the wrongness of deceiving him is already in question. And fifth, at least one case where misleading plausibly comes off as crueler or more manipulative than lying would have been, so that the quiz is not quietly stacked toward "lying is worse."

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. At minimum, the leaves must include: the deception-only view, on which inducing false belief is all that matters morally and the lie/misleading line is a distinction of verbal hygiene (roughly the consequentialist corner); the what-is-said or assertion-norms view, on which lying is worse because it breaches the sincerity norm governing assertion while misleading merely exploits conversational cooperation — Jennifer Saul and Andreas Stokke are the standard defenders here, in somewhat different versions, so distinguish them if you can, but say so plainly if the instrument cannot; the view on which misleading can be the greater wrong, because it parasitizes the hearer's own trusting interpretive work rather than openly violating a rule the hearer could at least recognize as broken; and the Kantian strict-prohibition view, on which lying is categorically impermissible while silence or evasion may be permitted. Where the logical space has a combination of answers that no one in the literature occupies, note it explicitly and say whether you think the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because no one has explored it. And flag honestly any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate with the cases available, rather than making the instrument out to be sharper than it is.

Finally, after the scoring key, add a short closing paragraph stating how confident you are in the claims you have made — attributions of named views to named philosophers especially — and singling out the one point in the whole deliverable you are least sure of.
