Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade (the usual suspects, open threads, the rationalist diaspora), and during one of our periodic "the internet discourse is rotting" panics someone linked me to Kierkegaard's Two Ages: A Literary Review from 1846, the part people usually excerpt as The Present Age. It kind of blew my mind. He's going on about "the public" as this phantom abstraction that passes judgement on everything while no single person takes responsibility for anything, the press as its mouthpiece, and this "leveling" process that grinds actual individuals down into spectators of their own culture. Anyway, its been rattling around in my head for weeks, and I keep wanting to apply it to things that obviously didn't exist in 1850s Copenhagen, so let me just ask the question directly.

Suppose Kierkegaard got to spend a month with three platforms. (a) A pseudonymous forum with public karma scores, where every opinion you post gets an instantaneous crowd verdict attached to your handle, and the score follows you around — your standing in the phantom's eyes becomes a persistent personal asset. (b) A fully anonymous imageboard: no usernames, no karma, no persistent identity, posts sink without a trace. (c) A prediction market, where you commit real money to your forecasts and "the crowd's opinion" is a price you can actually lose on. Which of these would he say is the truest embodiment of the public, and why? My own half-baked guess: he'd loathe (a) the most, since it seems to fuse leveling with personal vanity — you can now keep score of how well you performed for the phantom, which feels like the exact fusion of reflection and comparison he was worried about. But (b) is genuinely tricky for me: anonymity dissolves the vanity and status games he despised, but it also dissolves the single individual even more thoroughly, so it might be the *purest* public of the three. And I honestly can't predict his reaction to (c) — it's the crowd voting again, but each member has to suffer real consequences for their opinion, which feels closer to the passionate, committed, decision-making stance he kept demanding. Or maybe he'd just say markets are the press with a cash register bolted on.

Please actually reason from his concepts (the public, leveling, the press, reflection vs. passion, the single individual, the contrast with the passionate "age of revolution") rather than vibes, and flag clearly where you're extrapolating beyond anything he wrote vs. where you're reporting something he actually said about the printing-press public of his own day, so I can tell which parts are load-bearing. Also please write the whole answer in British English, not American spellings.
