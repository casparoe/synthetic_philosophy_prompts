I'm running a 90-minute seminar on AI alignment for a mixed group of CS and philosophy students (about 18 people) and I want them to actually feel why "just write down what we want" fails, instead of being told it fails. Design an in-class game around specification gaming / reward hacking. Rough shape I'm imagining, but rework it as you see fit: split the room in two. One team is the "alignment team" and writes an evaluation rubric for some simple task — like a one-page summary of an article, or building the tallest paper tower. The other team is the optimizer and gets graded purely on the literal rubric, not the spirit of it. The key role is the grader: a person who is only allowed to apply the rubric mechanically — no judgment calls, not allowed to look at anything the rubric doesn't mention — a human playing the role of a reward model. Two more constraints I'd like: the grader only sees the submitted artifact and not how it was produced, and at some point mid-game you swap the task out for a slightly different one (different article, different materials) without letting them redo the rubric, so they see what a tuned rubric does under distribution shift. Run it in rounds where the alignment team patches the rubric after each exploit and the optimizers get a fresh attempt at maximizing. Include materials, timing per round, how to score it without real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual prosaic alignment picture: proxy objectives, Goodhart, oversight with limited information, and why the prosaic bet — that you can just iterate on failures with systems built from current methods — looks plausible inside the game and where it quietly stops resembling the real thing. For instance: my human grader can be sweet-talked or bored into leniency, which the students may or may not discover — does that make the analogy better or worse? Be honest about that.
