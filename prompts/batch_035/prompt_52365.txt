For my dissertation I'm modeling how a declining shorebird population responds to sea-level rise, and I've hit a fork my methods training never prepared me for. I have a small set of candidate models ranging from three parameters to nine, and the standard practice in ecology is to rank them with AIC and BIC. The two criteria disagree: AIC charges about 2 points per additional parameter, while BIC's charge grows with the logarithm of sample size, so with my 2,000 survey records BIC strongly favors the lean three-parameter model while AIC favors the nine-parameter one, and whether I report the sharp simple story or the complicated rich one may shape a real conservation listing decision. I want to understand what each scoring rule presupposes about Occam's razor itself, because the disagreement doesn't feel merely technical. BIC is usually derived as an approximation to a Bayesian marginal likelihood, and its penalty can be read as the log of the volume a model's extra parameters occupy — the same idea, pushed to its limit, that makes Solomonoff induction assign exponentially more prior weight to simpler hypotheses, and that ties simplicity to description length in the Kolmogorov sense. AIC, in contrast, is justified on grounds of expected predictive accuracy on new data, not truth, and its flat per-parameter charge doesn't depend on sample size at all. Reconstruct for me the implicit view of simplicity and of induction each rule carries: what BIC presupposes about whether there's a true model among my candidates and why simpler theories deserve higher prior probability; what AIC presupposes about the aim of statistical work; and which rival conceptions of Occam's razor each quietly rules out. Then tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — whether an ecologist could accept the algorithmic, description-length view of simplicity and still have a principled reason to use AIC in some settings — and what my model-selection practice would have to look like if I took the Solomonoff-style picture wholesale.
