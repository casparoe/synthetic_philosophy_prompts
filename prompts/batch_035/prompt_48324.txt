A column I read in a popular philosophy magazine explained Moore's paradox roughly like this, and I want to know how much of it I can trust. Here is the gist: "Moore's paradox is the discovery that some sentences are contradictory even though they are not contradictions. 'It's raining, but I don't believe it' is perfectly consistent — it could easily be true, as when it is raining and I have mistaken the weather — yet no one can sensibly assert it. The same goes for 'It's raining, and I believe it isn't,' which is really just the same sentence in different words. Moore's explanation was that present-tense first-person reports of one's own beliefs do not state facts at all; they express the mind, which is why they cannot be asserted insincerely. The deeper lesson is that asserting p logically entails that the speaker believes p, so the Moore sentence is self-refuting in the same way 'I am not speaking now' is. And since our beliefs are transparent to us, the sentence could not be used truthfully by anyone, ever. Notice what follows: whenever someone says one thing while believing another, they have not really asserted anything at all — lying is not an act of assertion but a kind of performance." I have a feeling the article is doing something slippery, but I can't locate it: some of this sounds like what I've read about the paradox, and some of it sounds too strong. Go through the summary piece by piece and tell me which claims a philosopher of language would accept as stated, which are half-truths hiding a conflation or an overstatement, and which are just wrong — and, importantly, take a definite position rather than telling me it's "contested": for each claim, say whether you think it survives, and say why. I am especially interested in whether the final inference about lying follows from anything in the summary that survives correction — that is, whether there is a kernel of truth about assertion and belief here that really does do that work, or whether the columnist has smuggled in the conclusion. And if the pair of example sentences at the start are not in fact interchangeable, explain what turns on the difference, because the article's later moves seem to lean on treating them as one.
