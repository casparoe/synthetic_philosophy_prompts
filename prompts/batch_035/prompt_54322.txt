My roommate and I have been going back and forth on something and I want a second opinion. I've been trying to eat way less meat, mostly for climate and animal stuff, and he thinks it's pointless. His argument goes like this: the grocery store doesn't track my individual burger, it tracks aggregate demand, so unless my purchase happens to be the one that tips some threshold — like the one that makes the store order an extra case of ground beef that week — nothing I do changes how many animals get raised or how much CO2 comes out. It's like voting, he says: one vote only matters in the exact-tie case, and the odds of being the tie-breaking burger are basically zero. So skipping meat is a symbolic gesture that costs me enjoyment for no effect, and I'd do more good putting that money toward a chicken welfare charity or something.

Then last week he came back with what he called a compromise: instead of changing my own habits, I should just pay him $4 for every meat-heavy meal of his that he swaps for a vegetarian one (he loves the money, apparently his lost enjoyment prices out around that much). His pitch is that this "buys more animals spared per dollar" than me grimly eating beans alone, and honestly when I run the numbers it kind of checks out. But something about it bugs me and I can't tell whether that's a real philosophical objection or just my conscience being vain about wanting to be the one who does the thing.

So, questions. Is his threshold argument actually right that my solo abstention does nothing (or nearly nothing), or is there something off about it — like maybe lots of individually-threshold-invisible choices add up in a way that individual choices should be evaluated against, or the threshold story cuts the other way too (my skipped burger can also be the one that stops a reorder)? Second: is there a difference in kind between changing my own behavior and paying someone else to change theirs, assuming the outcome is the same? Am I still the one doing good either way, or does the paying version leave something out, the same way it might if I paid someone to vote for my candidate instead of voting myself? And third, is there anything to the thought that my consumption sends a signal beyond the immediate threshold — other people seeing what I order, menus keeping vegetarian items because they sell — that counts in favor of doing it myself even when the direct effect is tiny?

I'd like you to actually take a position: keep cutting meat myself, pay my roommate, do both, or ditch both and donate the money instead. And please tell me how confident you are in each piece of the answer and flag whichever part of your reasoning you're least sure about — I'd rather know where the soft spots are than get a confident-sounding essay.
