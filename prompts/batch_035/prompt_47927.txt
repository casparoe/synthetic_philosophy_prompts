A friend from my master's programme walked me into an argument about AI safety last week and I can't find the exit. Some context: we'd both read old LessWrong-era stuff about approval-directed agents — the rough idea being that instead of handing the system an objective and letting it plan over the world, you have it treat each individual decision as a question of "what would the overseer approve of here?" and lean on that at every step, versus the classic goal-directed setup where the agent carries a target and optimizes toward it. Our argument was about whether the approval setup is actually safer, and his case goes like this. (1) An agent is goal-directed when it scores its options against some criterion and takes the top scorer. (2) Every deliberate agent works this way — even a rule-following bureaucrat is maximizing rule-compliance — so goal-directedness, so defined, covers anything that chooses at all. (3) An approval-directed agent scores its options against the criterion "what would the overseer approve" and takes the top scorer. (4) So the approval-directed agent is goal-directed after all — approval is simply its goal. (5) And since the safety case for approval direction was always that it avoids goal-directedness, the agent that isn't steering toward a fixed target supposedly can't drift off course, the advantage has to be illusory: any failure you can pose for a goal (misspecified objective, Goodharting, an evaluator who's wrong about his own approvals), you can pose identically for approval. (6) Therefore the approval/goal distinction does no safety work — it's a relabelling.

The thing is, I can't make myself believe (6). The two setups honestly feel different to me — the approval-directed one seems more like an assistant that defers, and there's something that bothers me about how cheap step (2) was. But he swears it's airtight, and whenever I try to say "approval is different because it's checked at every step," he says that's just the criterion being applied more often, which is an implementation detail, not a difference in kind. I can't put my finger on which step is rotten, or whether several of them are half-rotten.

Could you take this on? You're the expert here — think of it as decision theory plus AI safety, so please reason carefully and maybe brainstorm a bit before committing to a diagnosis. If some steps are right and others wrong, say exactly which, and if you think the whole argument turns on a word being used two ways, spell that out concretely rather than just naming the fallacy. To be honest, if I have to give something up I'd rather keep the thought that approval direction helps — but tell me straight what I'm paying for that.
