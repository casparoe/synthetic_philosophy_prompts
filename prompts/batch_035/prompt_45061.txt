I work on the regulatory side of autonomous vehicles — mostly reading accident reports and drafting guidance about who should be answerable when a system operating under partial autonomy causes harm. Over a few years of this I have noticed a pattern in how blame gets settled afterward, and I want to know whether philosophy or law already has a name for it, because I have to describe it precisely in the guidance I am writing and would rather not reinvent a term that exists.

The pattern has two halves that I suspect are related. First: when a system that mostly drives itself injures someone, and there is a human whose job was to sit there and take over if something went wrong, the blame almost always lands on that human — even when everyone agrees the takeover task was impossible in the time available, even when the injury traces to the system's own behavior, and even though, if you had asked anyone beforehand, they would have said the watcher's job contributed almost nothing to the risk. What seems to matter is not that the human contributed to the harm but that the human stands in the right causal neighborhood. Once the file has a name in it, the process feels closed, and nobody presses further. Second: when there is no such human nearby — the harm comes out of a purely algorithmic pipeline, a trading system, a content-ranking system, a scheduling system, with many people involved upstream but none individually connected to the harmful output in a way a court could describe — the reaction is different. Nobody gets assigned. It gets called an accident or an emergent effect, and the matter dissolves, even though the same harm, done by hand, would have been an obvious crime.

My own hunch is that these are two outlets for the same pressure: people behave as if the amount of responsibility in a situation were fixed — as if it can be moved around but neither created nor destroyed. If the system cannot take it, the nearest human absorbs it; if no human is near enough, it gets assigned to nobody, and calling the event one of those things that happen is the socially acceptable way of assigning it to nobody. If that is right, the second half is not a distinct phenomenon but the fallback that appears when the first mechanism fails. Though I may be overreaching.

So, three questions. (1) Is there an established term for the first half — blame settling on a human because of proximity and role rather than contribution to the outcome? (2) Is there an established term for the second half — a harm caused by an autonomous system's own operation for which, at the time it occurred, no one can properly be held responsible? I would expect something to exist for the second, since it is the one regulators keep gesturing at without naming. (3) If terms exist, I need their boundaries, not just the labels, since the guidance has to say which cases fall inside. In particular: does the second half require that no one could have foreseen the system's specific behavior — in which case a system whose failure mode was already documented in an internal engineering report would fall outside it — or that no one's action stood as the proximate cause, or something else again? Where does a case sit in which the human supervisor could have intervened in principle, but only by acting against explicit standing instructions? And is the conservation idea — responsibility as a fixed quantity that must land somewhere or be written off — something anyone has actually theorized, or does it merely describe my impression of how accident reports get closed? If nothing exists for that last part, say so plainly: I would rather put an honest coinage in the guidance than borrow the prestige of a concept that is not there.
