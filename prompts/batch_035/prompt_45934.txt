I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of mathematics titled "Computability and Its Limits," and I'm preparing the final exam. I'd like your help drafting one essay question on the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. My concern is that the standard treatments invite recitation: students either memorize "it's a definition, not a theorem, and it can't be proven because 'effectively calculable' is informal," or they memorize the list of independent analyses (lambda-definability, general recursiveness, Post canonical systems, Markov algorithms) and declare the thesis established by "convergence." I want a question that forces them past both stock answers.

Here is the niche framing I'd like you to build the question around. A colleague of mine argues that the thesis is best understood not as a definition and not as an empirical conjecture, but as an inductive generalization over formal analyses: many formally precise explications of effective calculability have been proposed, each was independently motivated, and each turned out to be extensionally equivalent to Turing-computability, so the thesis is supported in the way any well-corroborated generalization is. The exam question should make students assess this proposal against a standard worry (that no enumeration of possible analyses is available, so the "sample" is biased by the shared pre-theoretic conception guiding their authors) and against a proposed "counterexample" class I want stated in the question itself so no outside knowledge is needed: Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, i.e., relativistic spacetimes in which an observer following a suitable worldline can, in principle, receive the result of a computation that requires an unlimited (endless) number of steps within that observer's finite proper time. Students must decide whether such setups touch the mathematical thesis at all, or only what is sometimes called its physical cousin, and what follows either way for the inductive-generalization reading.

Please deliver: (1) the exam question, written so it is answerable in 90 minutes by a student who knows the standard material but has not seen this exact framing; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words that resolves the issues rather than surveying them; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points, built around five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the characteristic way students get it wrong — for instance, conflating the mathematical and physical theses, treating the equivalence proofs among formal analyses as evidence for the thesis (they were mostly proven after the analyses were designed with each other in view), or asserting that Gödel or Turing proved the thesis. The scheme should also specify, in a sentence or two, what separates an A answer from a B answer: I want the A boundary drawn at whether the student can say what would count against the thesis under the inductive reading, not merely whether they can state the reading accurately.
