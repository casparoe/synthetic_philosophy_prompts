I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the later Wittgenstein and am writing the take-home exam. Draft one essay question on family resemblance and language games that a student cannot pass by reciting the stock summary — that games, unlike the Tractatus's unified picture of language, share no essence but only overlapping similarities, as Wittgenstein argues at around §§66–67 of the Investigations. The question's difficulty must come from a crux, not from breadth, and the crux is this: is the family-resemblance claim a claim about the extension of a word across a whole linguistic community, or about the grammar available to any competent speaker — and does the answer change whether the claim is vulnerable to empirical evidence about usage?

To force the issue, build the question around the following invented case study, which should appear as a paragraph of the exam paper itself: Dr Ines Halvorsen, a corpus linguist, has analysed a large corpus of everyday English and reports that uses of 'game' fall into three tight clusters — children's pastimes, organised competitive sport, and 'playing at' activities such as pretend tea parties — with almost no corpus instances bridging the clusters. Halvorsen concludes that 'game' is locally essentialist: each sub-community of speakers uses the word with a stable core, and the famous family resemblance appears only when one conflates their dialects, rather as a linguist who mixes dialects finds 'no common grammar'. Students must decide whether Halvorsen refutes Wittgenstein, vindicates him, or rests on a confusion about what kind of claim §66 was making — and they must connect their verdict to Wittgenstein's own insistence that he is offering reminders for particular purposes rather than a theory of concepts, saying whether that insistence is a defence here or an evasion.

Please reply in three sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths: 'The question' — the exam-paper text, including the Halvorsen paragraph, roughly 200 words; 'Model answer' — about 600 words, taking a definite stand; 'Marking scheme' — out of 25, with three or four named criteria, the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how upper-second answers typically go wrong under each, and a closing sentence stating what a first-class answer must do that an upper-second cannot.
