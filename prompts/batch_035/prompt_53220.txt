This one is from the essay question on Searle. The question I gave the students was: "Explain Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment, state precisely which claim about minds and computers it is meant to attack, and discuss one objection to the argument together with Searle's response to it."

Here's the student's answer, typed out:

"Searle asks us to imagine a man locked in a room who knows no Chinese whatsoever. He's given a giant rulebook that tells him how to respond to strings of Chinese symbols that come in with strings of Chinese symbols to send out. The rules are purely formal — they tell him which shapes to push out depending on which shapes came in, but never what any of them actually mean. From the outside, people who slip Chinese questions under the door get back fluent Chinese answers, so the room passes the Turing test in Chinese. Yet the man understands nothing — he's just shuffling symbols by their form. Searle's point is that a computer running a program is in exactly the same position: it manipulates symbols according to syntax without ever grasping semantics. So the argument shows that no machine can think, and more broadly that behavior alone is never enough for genuine understanding — it's a refutation of behaviorism as much as of the Turing test. And since the man is the only thing in the room doing any actual work, and he doesn't understand Chinese, nothing in the room does either — there's no other candidate for where the understanding would be.

The main objection is the systems reply: even if the man himself doesn't understand, the whole system — man, rulebook, room — might. This is basically the same point as Block's Chinese Nation thought experiment. Searle responds that the reply is just absurd: if the man memorized the rulebook and did all the symbol manipulation in his head, we'd still deny he understands Chinese, so putting room walls around him can't change anything. Overall the thought experiment demonstrates that thinking requires the actual causal powers of a biological brain, which programs by their nature lack."
