I'm a second-year radiology resident, and something about how my training works has been bugging me for months. When I started, chest CTs were basically gray weather to me — I could stare at a scan for ten minutes and see nothing but lung. Now, most of the time, a small ground-glass opacity or a subtle nodule just pops out at me the way a face pops out of a crowd. My attending says that's the whole point of training: with enough supervised examples, you stop reasoning your way to the finding and just literally see it, the way a birder hears one note in a chorus and knows it's a warbler. A friend doing a PhD in philosophy of science thinks that's sloppy talk — in her view nothing at the level of seeing has changed at all, I've just gotten very fast at unconscious inference from the same visual input, and calling it perception is me flattering myself.

The reason I care isn't just winning the argument with her. We had a case recently where the finding was genuinely borderline, and it got me wondering what kind of evidense my read of a scan even is. If a lawyer in a malpractice suit asks the expert witness "did you see the lesion, or did you conclude it was there" — does that distinction do any work, or is it a distinction without a difference? And there's another wrinkle I can't shake: the same friend claims that after learning which bottle is which, the $150 wine now actually tastes better to her than the $15 one — that sounds like the opposite direction, knowledge reaching in and changing the experience itself rather than expertise being trained into the eye. Are these the same phenomenon or two different ones, and if they're different, which one am I? Should the answer change how I think about what my reports are worth — are they observations, or judgments wearing an observation's clothes?
