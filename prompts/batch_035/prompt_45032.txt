I teach an evening continuing-education class for adults with no mathematics beyond school. Next term's session on Gödel's incompleteness theorems has a twist: the theorems themselves are only half the topic; the other half is their afterlife outside mathematics — the claims that minds outperform any machine, that arithmetic is somehow unreliable, that any system whatever (a legal code, a physical theory, even the internet) must contain truths it cannot capture. My students arrive half-inoculated, several having read popularizations that assert these things confidently. Don't explain the theorems to me; plan how I should teach the session. What has to be in place before the out-of-bounds uses can even be evaluated — I suspect it is the distinction between truth and provability in a fixed formal system, but tell me whether provability first needs to be presented as mechanical symbol-shuffling before that contrast can mean anything to this audience. What single example should carry the session, and is the liar paradox the right ancestor to invoke or a trap I should avoid? Which misconceptions they will walk in with versus form during the session, in the order they will surface, and where in the sequence each should be absorbed. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level — the construction of the Gödel sentence, the second theorem, non-standard models — and admit that I am refusing. And what closing exercise would show me whether they can now sort a genuine from a bogus application of the theorems, rather than merely having memorized that Gödel proved something about limits.
