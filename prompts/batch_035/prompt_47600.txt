ok so I'm a backend engineer, never took a single philosophy class in my life, but I fell down a rabbit hole last week. Got into an argument at work about whether our LLM-powered support bot "actually understands" the tickets it's triaging, someone name-dropped the frame problem, and I've been chewing on it since. From what I can tell (mainly from skimming the Stanford encyclopedia entry), it goes back to some McCarthy and Hayes paper from 1969 about representing actions in logic, and the core issue is: when an action happens, how does a system know which things in the world changed and, just as important, which things DIDN'T change? Without explicitly listing a billion frame axioms saying "painting this block red didn't change its location, didn't change the weather, didn't change the price of tea in China..."

And honestly? That sounds exactly like my day job. When a user updates their email address, I have to figure out which caches to invalidate, which downstream services need a webhook, which rows to denormalize, and — crucially — everything I DON'T have to touch. Get that wrong in either direction and you're either serving stale data or burning your whole weekend recomputing stuff nobody cares about. There's an old joke in CS that there are only two hard problems: cache invalidation and naming things. (Off-by-one errors is the usual third one, ha.)

So my ask: compare the frame problem (the AI one from that 1969 paper, and its fancier philosophical descendants) with the software engineering problem of cache/dependency invalidation. Are these literally the same problem in different clothes, or is there a real disanalogy hiding in there? A couple of things I want you to specifically poke at:

- in engineering, the "frame" is basically handed to us: we drew the dependency graph ourselves, it's finite, and invalidation rules are written by whoever built the system. in the philosophy version, the agent apparently has to figure out what's relevant without already knowing the dependency graph, which sounds... circular? like you'd need to already know what matters to know what to check.
- my team's current approach with the LLM bot is basically "just stuff everything relevant into the context window and let the model sort it out." is that a brute-force solution to the frame problem, or is it just refusing to engage with it (paying O(everything) instead of ever learning what actually matters)? or is it something else entirely?

And don't be mealy-mouthed about it — give me an actual verdict on whether the analogy holds, not just a list of similarities and differences with no conclusion. I get enough of that from architects.
