I am a PhD student in philosophy, and next month I present a work-in-progress paper at our departmental seminar. My thesis is that van Fraassen's Reflection principle, in its original diachronic form from his 1984 paper "Belief and the Will," is not a requirement of rationality on credences at all. What survives, I argue, is a synchronic deference principle: your current credence in A, conditional on the proposition that at some later time t you assign credence r to A, should be r -- but only when you regard your future self as epistemically competent on that question. My argument is that all the standard counterexamples to Reflection (knowing you will take a drug that makes you certain of falsehoods, knowing you will succumb to wishful thinking, knowing you will forget decisive evidence) are cases where the competence condition fails, and that the interesting normative content lives in the competence judgment, not in the time-indexed deference. My slogan is that your future self is just one more source you might defer to, no differently in principle than an expert or a weather service, and the fact that it is your future self is doing no work.

The audience is small but sharp: two formal epistemologists who know the Dutch book literature on Reflection inside out, including the known worries about whether those book arguments actually go through; a Bayesian statistician who regards most of these principles as either theorems of the probability calculus dressed up or else undefined odds; a Kantian who suspects the whole debate is covertly about self-trust and autonomy rather than probability; and four or five graduate students who will ask whatever the senior people's objections make thinkable.
