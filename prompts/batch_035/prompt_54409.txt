I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been given a popular-science book by a relative who knows I work on scientific realism. One chapter summarises structural realism like this, and I want to check how much of it I should correct before I pass the book back with comments:

'Philosophers used to argue about whether science tells us the truth about unobservable things like electrons. Then in the 1980s John Worrall showed that this whole debate rests on a mistake. His argument, based on the historical shift from Fresnel's wave theory of light to Maxwell's, is that when a theory is replaced, the equations survive but the things they were about don't — so what science really tells us about is structure, not objects. Structural realism therefore says that electrons, genes, and spacetime points don't exist in the traditional sense; there are only relations between things. This also solves the problem of how we can have knowledge of unobservable objects at all, since there's nothing left to know except the mathematical relations, which we can grasp directly. More recently, some physicists have taken the view to its logical conclusion: the world is literally made of mathematics, so physics is not discovering how the world is but computing which structures are possible.'

Something about this sits uneasily with what I recall from my coursework — in particular I suspect it runs together two quite different versions of the view, and I have doubts about the claim that knowledge of structure is somehow unproblematic, which was half the point of the original debate. But the book is by a professional science writer, so before I annotate it I'd like an expert's audit.

Please go through the passage part by part and tell me: which claims a working structural realist would accept as a fair popularisation; which are subtly wrong or conflated (for instance, does it blur epistemic and ontic versions, or misstate what the Fresnel-to-Maxwell case is meant to show — did the equations themselves survive, or something weaker?); and whether the closing slide into 'the world is made of mathematics' follows from anything the view actually claims. Where the summary is wrong, say what the correct statement would be and whether the error matters for the reader's takeaway. I'd be grateful if you could write in British English.
