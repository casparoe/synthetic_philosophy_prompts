I am writing a section of a paper on population-level analogues of Nozick's utility monster, and I need a thought experiment built to isolate a particular variable. The variable is this: whether the anti-monster intuition is driven by the monster's superior efficiency at converting resources into well-being, or merely by the dramatic shape of the original case — one enormous beneficiary, everyone else reduced to misery.

Construct the case as follows. The scenario must be one a health minister could actually confront: a scarce pharmaceutical resource (a drug, a gene therapy, a supplement program — your choice) that permanently raises the rate at which its recipients convert ordinary goods into well-being. The enhanced enjoy food, friendship, work, and leisure more intensely, and suffer less from illness and setback; they get, say, twice the welfare per unit of ordinary circumstance that an average person does. There is enough of the resource for one person in a hundred, and the beneficiaries are selected by lottery, not merit or choice. They are ordinary citizens — not greedy, not alien, not superhuman — and their psychology is otherwise exactly like everyone else's.

The design goal: the case should agree with Nozick's monster in the one respect that matters — total utility is maximized by giving the entire scarce resource to the few and none to the many — while removing the features usually blamed for the anti-monster intuition. Specifically: no single vivid beneficiary (the gain is spread across millions), no total deprivation (the unenhanced retain a decent floor of welfare, comparable to a moderately good life in a wealthy country), and no dependence of the enhanced people's well-being on anyone else's suffering. The enhancement is a natural fact discovered after the fact, and in principle the resource could eventually be produced for everyone — but present scarcity forces the allocation decision now.

After the scenario, which should be one paragraph and must not stipulate any intuitive verdicts (no phrases like "and this seems clearly wrong"), do the following:

(a) State what act utilitarianism says about the allocation and why, and then state what a prioritarian and a straightforward telic egalitarian would each say, identifying precisely which feature of the case each of the latter two positions is reacting to.

(b) Confront the disanalogy problem head-on: explain what, if anything, distinguishes this case from ordinary medical priority-setting, in which a doctor gives a scarce drug to those who will benefit most from it and no one thinks a monster is involved. If the distinction is thin, say so — the question is whether the fragmented monster is just triage with better lighting, and if it is, whether that shows the anti-monster intuition was never about efficiency in the first place.

(c) Run the re-introduction test: having built the case with fragmentation, a welfare floor, and non-viciousness, add each removed feature back one at a time — first concentration into a single beneficiary, then removal of the floor, then making the beneficiaries aware of and complicit in the exclusion — and report at which step, if any, the anti-monster intuition returns and which feature is therefore doing the work.

(d) Give the strongest utilitarian defense against the case, clearly distinguishing the "bite the bullet" reply (the allocation really is right) from the "deny the possibility" reply (the case is incoherent, e.g. because interpersonal welfare comparisons of this kind cannot be made), and say which of the two the case as you have built it pressures more — noting, in particular, whether the lottery selection and the eventual universality of the resource make the bullet easier to bite than it is in Nozick's original.

The deliverable is the scenario plus the demonstration that it meets each specification; a case that smuggles the verdicts into the description rather than earning them from the theories fails.
