The socialist calculation debate has a peculiar property: it keeps being declared settled, and it keeps reopening without either side moving an inch. Mises argued in 1920 that without private ownership of the means of production there are no genuine prices for capital goods, hence no common denominator for comparing alternative production plans, and therefore no rational economy at all — an argument about the impossibility of economic rationality, not about inefficiency. Lange's response in the mid-1930s, with refinements by Lerner, was to keep markets for consumer goods and labor while a central board sets accounting prices for producer goods and adjusts them by trial and error in the direction of shortages and gluts — converging, in principle, to the prices a Walrasian auctioneer would find, only by iteration instead of simultaneous solution. Hayek then moved the goalposts in a way he insisted was not a move: the real problem was never solving the equations but assembling the inputs, because the relevant knowledge is dispersed, largely tacit, and tied to particular times and places; and prices are a mechanism for communicating it. Since then, every technological turn has prompted a reopening. The cybernetics enthusiasts of the early 1970s thought telex networks and real-time statistics answered Hayek; in the 1990s it was argued that ordinary desktop computing already exceeded anything Lange's iterative procedure would have required; today the argument runs that enormous internally planned firms — the logistics operations of the largest retailers — demonstrate that planning at scale works, and that machine learning closes whatever gap remains. And every time, the anti-planning side replies that this all misses the point: the objection was never arithmetic, and firms that plan internally are islands in a market sea, inheriting their prices from the surrounding ocean, so their success shows nothing about planning the whole economy. I have watched this cycle repeat, in the literature and in online discussion, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: a static allocation question — given a list of ends, a stock of resources, and a set of techniques, can an algorithm converge on the prices and quantities a competitive equilibrium would produce? — and a dynamic question about where the lists come from, since the ends, resources, and techniques are themselves outputs of the process being modeled. On that diagnosis each side wins on its own question and is simply silent on the other's, and the recurring conviction that a bigger computer must settle it is a category error: the debate is conducted in the idiom of computation while what divides the parties is something else, perhaps a disagreement about what counts as economic rationality once ends are endogenous. Is that right, or is there a genuine shared empirical question here that is merely hard to test? Is the firm-scale point — planning islands inside market seas — a decisive objection, a confound, or an argument whose evidential value cannot in principle be settled? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper decomposition of the question, a specific empirical test that could get traction, a formal result about convergence or information aggregation, or an argument that no progress should be expected — and, if you can, an honest assessment of whether the periodic reopenings driven by new technology are a sign of life in the debate or a symptom that the question, as posed, is ill-posed.
