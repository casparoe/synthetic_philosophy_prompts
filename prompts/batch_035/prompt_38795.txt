At seventy-one, after a career in agricultural research, I have lately taken up philosophy, and my family has obligingly supplied me with a case study. My brother-in-law has been issuing rulings on food, which I have recorded as they came. He eats beef without hesitation and in any quantity. He drinks ordinary supermarket milk. He eats eggs, but only those from his neighbour's hens, which he can watch scratching about the yard. He refuses chicken — though I note every chicken he has so far encountered has come from a supermarket shelf or a rotisserie. Bread, lentils, and vegetables he eats without comment. In my working life I measured feed conversion ratios and harvest losses, and I keep reaching for numbers he has never offered; I take it his principle, if there is one, is not mine to assume.

A family dinner is now being planned, and three items are in dispute: a whole wild trout for each diner; chicken from a nearby farm that pastures its birds and gives them, by broiler standards, a long and seemingly pleasant life; and a batch of brownies the younger cousins propose to bake with cricket flour, which I am told can be bought by the sack.

I would like your prediction of his ruling on each item, but I am more interested in the rule he seems implicitly to be running. Two candidates occur to me: a ledger of animal deaths per meal, and a ledger of suffering while alive. Please tell me whether some third rule fits his record as well; say where each candidate already sits uneasily with verdicts he has given (I have one in mind concerning the milk); give your best guess on the three rulings, with reasons; and tell me which of the three items — or what further dish you would propose — would most cleanly separate the competing rules, so that I know what to serve him next.
