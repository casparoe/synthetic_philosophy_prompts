I'm a retired physicist (semiconductor research, mostly, so I know how slippery "measurement" can be) who got into philosophy about two years into retirement and lately reads a lot about effective altruism and cause prioritization. The importance-tractability-neglectedness framework appeals to me -- it's dimensional analysis for doing good, decomposing a messy question into factors you can at least argue about seperately. But I keep wondering whether the three factors really pull equal weight, or whether one of them is quietly doing all the work while the other two are mostly there to make the whole thing look rigorous.

So here's my ask: rank the three factors -- importance (scale of the problem), tractability (how much progress is actually possible), and neglectedness (how many resources are already pointed at it) -- from most to least decision-relevant when you're deciding where to direct serious effort, with a one-sentence justification for each placement. Then give the framework as a whole a score out of 10 as a decision tool, compared to just thinking holistically about a cause area.

And one more thing I'd like folded into the ranking: my own candidate fourth factor, which I call verifiability -- how easily you can tell whether progress is even happening. It seems to me a cause could score well on all three classic factors and still be a trap if feedback is so slow or noisy that effort and waste are indistinguishable for decades (some wild animal welfare interventions feel like this to me). Tell me honestly where verifiability belongs relative to the classic three, and whether adding it fixes something broken or just clutters the analysis.

Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on prioritization and which parts are genuinely contested -- I don't want to walk away mistaking someone's favorite weighting scheme for a consensus. And don't hold back on the suspicion I can't quite shake: that the framework is partly hindsight rationalization wearing physics clothing, and that its apparent rigor is doing social work rather than epistemic work. If you think that's too cynical, tell me why.
