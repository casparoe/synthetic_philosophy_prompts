Nearly every government facing a falling birth rate now pays for births — baby bonuses, per-child tax credits, subsidized childcare, the large lump-sum "maternity capital" schemes of the kind Russia and Hungary have used — treating the creation of a new person as a public good worth purchasing at public expense. Yet the same states treat the prevention of a birth as a harm to no one: a life that never begins, however miserable it would have been, appears in no victim statistics, grounds no compensation claim, and no government anywhere maintains a ledger of "lives not begun" as a policy loss. What theory of value does this pairing of practices presuppose? Does pronatalist spending require the total view, on which extra happy lives make the world better simpliciter, or can a person-affecting view — on which a benefit requires an existing or future beneficiary — license it as well? And is the treatment of prevented births merely the mirror image of the first half, or does something like Benatar's asymmetry, which holds that the non-existence of a sufferer is good while the non-existence of a happy person is not bad, actually endorse the official picture while reversing the policy? Reconstruct the implicit axiology as carefully as you can, say whether the two halves of the practice presuppose anything consistent with each other, and then tell me what a government that instead took Benatar's asymmetry seriously would have to change — noting, if you can, whether anything states already do, perhaps in animal welfare law where some jurisdictions regulate breeding partly for the sake of animals not yet born, quietly presupposes the opposite of what pronatalist policy does.
