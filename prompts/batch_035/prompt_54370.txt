I've been asked to circulate a statement of my considered views on interpersonal aggregation for a graduate seminar on social choice, and before I do I'd like to know whether the package is internally coherent. Here is roughly where I stand:

1. I accept Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem (1955) as very close to a proof of utilitarianism. As I understand it, the theorem says: if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ranking over lotteries also satisfies the vNM axioms, and Pareto indifference holds, then the social ranking must be representable as a weighted affine sum of the individuals' vNM utility functions. All of the premises strike me as plausible, so I accept the conclusion.

2. I accept Pareto indifference without reservation: whenever everyone is indifferent between two options, the social ranking must likewise be indifferent between them.

3. I accept the independence (substitution) axiom as a genuine requirement of individual rationality. Allais-type preferences are simply irrational, and I'm prepared to say so in print.

4. I'm a utilitarian in aggregation: the right social ranking is the unweighted sum of individual utilities, with everyone counting equally, and the theorem in (1) is the backbone of my confidence in this. I deny that equality of outcomes has any intrinsic value; distribution matters only through its effect on the total.

5. I am skeptical of interpersonal comparisons of utility. There is no fact of the matter about whether my utility loss from some policy exceeds your utility gain from it; each person's vNM utility is only defined up to a positive affine transformation, and nothing in reality pins down a common scale across people.

6. When a single indivisible benefit must go wholly to one of two identically situated people, I think allocating it by a fair lottery is better — better in itself, not merely instrumentally (say, as insurance against resentment, or for incentive effects across repetitions) — than awarding it to one of them outright, even in the idealized case where the two people have exactly the same utility function and so the same expected utility under either procedure.
