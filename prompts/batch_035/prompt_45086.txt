So full disclosure: I spent about 35 years as a geophysicist, and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy, mostly logic and truth stuff. The Liar paradox has basically colonized my brain the way a hard dataset problem used to. And I've noticed two camps online and in the books I'm reading who argue completely past each other: the "truth-value gap" people, who say the liar sentence (the one that says of itself that it's not true) is neither true nor false, and the dialetheists, who say it's both true and false. I want the document these two camps should have produced after a long, good-faith adversarial collaboration, the kind where you preregister your predictions like a proper trial.

Concretely, what I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign. I'd expect them to agree on things like: the naive truth schema (a sentence is true iff what it says holds) is deeply intuitive and can't just be thrown away; simply banning self-reference doesn't work because of the indirect/liar cycles; and the "revenge" versions of the liar (e.g., the sentence that says of itself that it is not just plain true) are the real stress test for everyone's machinery, not just the opponent's.

2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement has to be phrased so its own side would accept the wording — no slanting "the gap theorist thinks..." vs "the dialetheist realizes...". And please try to identify which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive, if any are. My scientist instinct says some of this might just be two conventions for handling the same formal object, but maybe that's naive — you tell me.

3. The cruxes. I suspect one is whether "not true" and "false" can genuinely come apart, and another is whether explosion is really so bad (i.e., whether from one true contradiction everything follows, and whether that's fatal). But there may be better cruxes I haven't spotted, like how much ordinary mathematics and reasoning each side's preferred logic can actually recover, or whether Curry's paradox (the one about "if this sentence is true, then pigs fly") is worse for one side than the other.

4. For each crux: a concrete case, argument, or test both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Like, if the test is "how does your apparatus handle the sentence that says of itself it's not true and also not false," write what each camp predicts their opponent's machinery does, and what they predict their own does. The point, to me, is the same as in science: if neither side can say in advance what would count against them, they're not doing inquiry, they're doing fan clubs.

Keep the philosophical content real — assume the reader knows the basic moves but write the cruxes carefully. And don't have them agree on some watered-down nothing statement just so the document can exist; if they genuinely can't agree even on what the revenge problem shows, say that. Format as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, with the cruxes section after that.
