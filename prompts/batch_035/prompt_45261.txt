I'm a high school junior enrolled in an elective on dystopian fiction, and we've just finished Brave New World at the same time that a philosophy seminar I'm auditing introduced us to Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion — the claim that, if what matters is total welfare, an outcome containing vastly more people whose lives are only barely worth living can be better than one containing fewer people with excellent lives. The overlap struck me immediately. Mustapha Mond's defense of the World State is essentially the total utilitarian's wager: stability, engineered contentment, and soma for everyone, purchased at the cost of art, science, religion, and deep attachment. Huxley seems to have constructed a laboratory for the exact trade Parfit later described, nearly three decades before Parfit described it. My teacher liked the comparison but warned me that the novel may be doing something other than dramatizing the argument, and I'd like to work out whether she's right.

I want a reading that takes both sides seriously. On the philosophical side, I need the distinctions kept straight: is the World State maximizing total welfare or average welfare, given that it deliberately caps and controls population? Are the citizens' lives genuinely pleasant under hedonism — rich in agreeable feeling — or are they barely worth living in Parfit's sense, which is a much thinner thing than Huxley's depiction of them? And is the Savage's climactic insistence on claiming the right to be unhappy an objection within the welfarist framework (that misery is instrumentally necessary for the goods that make lives excellent) or an objection to the framework itself (that dignity, authenticity, and the capacity for real love matter independently of how lives feel from the inside)? On the literary side, I want to know where the novel genuinely stages the debate — the confrontation between Mond and the Savage seems almost designed as a philosophy seminar — and where Huxley cheats, for instance by making his contented citizens seem shallow through narrative tone and by stacking the deck with Linda's degradation and the Bokanovsky-twinned masses, so that our revulsion may track a loss of personhood or individuality rather than a low level of well-being per life.

So here is what I'd like: a reading of Brave New World as a thought experiment in population axiology, saying plainly where the analogy to the repugnant conclusion holds, where it breaks down, and what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the formal arguments cannot — perhaps by letting us inhabit both the Savage's horror and the citizens' sincere, unperturbed happiness at once, something no premises-and-conclusions presentation manages. But I also want honesty about the limits: if Huxley is ultimately presupposing an objective-list or perfectionist conception of the good rather than refuting hedonistic total utilitarianism on its own terms, say so, because my essay will be much weaker if it pretends the novel settles what it merely assumes. I can't quote the text from memory, so please paraphrase any passages you refer to rather than pretending to reproduce them exactly.
