At a family dinner, my uncle argued against same-sex marriage using a complementarity analogy: marriage is like a lock and key — the parts are shaped for one another, and two keys or two locks, however fond they may be of each other, can't do what a key and a lock together do. My sister replied that marriage is more like a business partnership: the state licenses an ongoing arrangement of mutual care, shared property, and default decision-making rights, and the sexes of the parties are an implementation detail, not part of the interface. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so I confess I hear "type mismatch" in his version and "interface vs. implementation" in hers, and I can't tell whether that framing is illuminating or just my hammer making everything look like a nail. Could you audit both analogies properly? For each, lay out what maps onto what — what plays the lock, the key, the opening of the lock; what plays the partnership, the partners, the contract — then say where the mapping breaks. Infertile couples, elderly newlyweds, and adoptive parents seem like obvious stress tests for the lock-and-key version, and I'd also like to know whether it matters that locks have designers while marriage's "purpose" seems to have no author. Conversely, I suspect the partnership analogy quietly assumes what marriage is for, just as the lock analogy does. Please be explicit about what each analogy smuggles in, and end by telling me which conclusion, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you're least sure of.
