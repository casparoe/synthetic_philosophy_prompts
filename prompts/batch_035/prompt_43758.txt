So I'm a PhD student in econ, and I'm TAing a history of economic thought course where we just did Aristotle on exchange, and now an ancient philosophy grad student friend of mine has me stuck. Her argument, as best as I can reconstruct it:

(1) For Aristotle, an exchange between, say, a builder and a shoemaker is just only if what changes hands is equal in value — that's the proportional reciprocity bit from Nicomachean Ethics Book V, where a house has to equal some number of shoes, and money exists by convention precisely to be the yardstick for this.

(2) The value being measured comes from need or demand — as I read the passage, that's Aristotle's answer to how a house and a pile of shoes get compared at all: they're made commensurable through what people happen to need.

(3) But need is person-relative. The builder needs shoes, the shoemaker needs a house — that's the entire reason they're trading instead of keeping their own stuff.

(4) So in any voluntary exchange both parties value what they receive more than what they give up. That's not a bug, it's the point of trading — mutually beneficial trade just IS trading on unequal valuations.

(5) So from the standpoint of the valuations the parties actually have, no voluntary exchange ever exchanges equals for equals.

(6) Therefore, on Aristotle's own criterion, no voluntary exchange is just. Every time the shoemaker and the builder walk away both feeling like they got a good deal, they've wronged each other.

(7) And if you try to escape by saying the equality should be assessed in some objective value instead of the parties' needs, then (2) goes — and with it Aristotle's only story about how a house and shoes are commensurable in the first place.

This has to prove too much, right? Aristotle obviously didn't think ordinary exchange within the household economy was robbery — what he hated was the boundless money-making, chrematistike, the accumulation for its own sake. If (6) were right his whole distinction between natural exchange and the discredited kind would just... never come up, because there'd be no natural kind to be had.

My friend says the problem is that I'm smuggling in a modern subjectivist picture of value around (3)-(4), and maybe she's right, but if so I want to know where the equality is supposed to live if not in anyone's valuations — measured by whom, felt by whom? And honestly I'd rather keep (1) and (2), since they look like Aristotle's actual commitments and my section is in two weeks.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is "reject (1)," please tell me what's left of proportional reciprocity after that, because the medieval just-price crowd built a lot on it. And if the answer is that some step equivocates between two kinds of value, name the two kinds, because I suspect that's what's happening but I can't make the distinction do any work. (There's also a longer version of her argument that ends with "if money measures value, what measures the value of money" but honestly I can only take so much.)
