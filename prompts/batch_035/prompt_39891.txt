So I work on AI safety, interpretability-adjacent, and there's something that keeps buging me about how my field uses the word "mechanism," and I want to know what philosophical view the practice is implicitly committed to — because I don't think it's coherent as used.

Concretely, the practice. When people in my area say a model answered a question "using the same mechanism" as before, they mean something like: roughly the same set of components, doing the same jobs, produced the output. This notion does a ton of work in safety claims. Mechanistic anomaly detection flags an input as worrying if the model processed it with a different mechanism than the ones it used on training data — the thought being that a model that gives right answers for weird reasons will fail when you scale it or shift the distribution, and you want to catch that before deployment. And people call a model "just memorizing" rather than "really reasoning" when the mechanism producing the answer is a lookup-ish one, and this is meant to be a deep fact about the model, not just about that one input.

Here's what bugs me, though. The sameness relation gets used at completely different grains depending on whats convenient:

1. On the universality side, people treat something like induction heads as the same mechanism across models with totally different weights, different sizes, even different training runs. So there, "same mechanism" is individuated at some abstract functional level — basically "same job description." Two models share a mechanism if the same abstract computation is implemented in both.

2. On the safety side, the same people will say a fine-tuned model might have "new mechanisms" for producing harmful outputs, even when the fine-tune barely moved the weights. There, sameness is much closer to "same concrete parts," and any small difference in implementation threatens to count as a different mechanism — which is exactly what makes the anomaly claim do safety work.

Can both be right? What theory of mechanism-individuation does this practice presuppose? Is it a kind of functionalism about mechanisms, individuated by causal role, where multiple realizability is fine? But if so, doens't the safety side collapse — if a fine-tuned model implements the same abstract computation as before, then by the universality grain it's the same mechanism, and the anomaly detector has nothing to flag? Or does the practice maybe presuppose that mechanisms are more like biological species, individuated by historical origin and continuity, so a fine-tuned circuit is "the same mechanism, modified" while a re-implemented one is genuinely new? Or are the two strands just two different relations that both got called "same mechanism" and nobody noticed?

And one more thing. Does the practice also presuppose that mechanisms are natural kinds in the sense that would make "produced by the same mechanism" projectible — that knowing which mechanism fired tells you how the model will behave on inputs you havent seen? Because that projection is the whole justification for mechanistic anomaly detection over plain statistical OOD detection, and it seems to need a lot more than "there are circuits in there."

So: reconstruct the implicit view (or views), tell me whether the practice is coherent as it stands or whether it needs to pick a grain and stick to it, say where it's underdetermined — maybe several individuation views would each license the practice as it actually gets used — and tell me what would have to change in the safety claims if we made the individuation view explicit and, say, went with a strictly functionalist one. Assume I know the ML side but not the philosophy of science side: mechanisms, type/token, multiple realizability, natural kinds, whatever's relevant, explain what I need as you go.
