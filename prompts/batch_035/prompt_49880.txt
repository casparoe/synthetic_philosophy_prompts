I am a retired physical chemist who, after a career spent mostly with spectrometers and reaction kinetics, has lately been working through philosophy of history with more enthusiasm than expertise. At the adult-education seminar I attend, two fellow students spent an entire session arguing about whether Nazi Germany would have arisen without Hitler, and the exchange has stayed with me. They went back and forth in several distinct moves, which I will number so you can adjudicate them one at a time.

(1) Margaret opened with the claim that removing Hitler changes everything: yes, Weimar Germany would have produced some resentful authoritarian nationalism, given the Depression, the Versailles settlement, and the contempt of the old elites for the Republic, but not the specific catastrophe of genocidal world war. Individuals are not interchangeable tokens, she said, and Hitler's peculiar combination of charisma, ideological fixation, and appetite for reckless gambles is exactly the sort of variable a controlled experiment would flag as decisive.

(2) Peter replied that this confuses the shape of the spark with the dryness of the tinder. On his telling, a mass movement with a propaganda apparatus, a paramilitary wing, and millions of radicalized supporters was already in place; substitute a Goering or a Strasser for Hitler and you still get dictatorship, rearmament, and persecution, because the structural conditions selected for that kind of leadership. He compared it to convergent evolution: put an organism under the right pressures and you get the same organs from different lineages.

(3) Margaret countered that the analogy is instructive but cuts the other way. Stephen Jay Gould famously imagined replaying the tape of life from the Cambrian and argued that contingency, not selection pressure, dominates — rerun the tape and nothing like us appears. History, she said, is at least as path-dependent as biology, and the honest counterfactual is not "does something bad happen" but "does *this* bad thing happen," and on that question the leader is not substitutable. She noted that plenty of countries suffered the Depression and turned authoritarian without producing a Hitler, so the structural account under-explains precisely what is distinctive about the case.

(4) Peter answered that social systems differ from ecosystems because they contain institutions designed to replace failed components, and that what looks like contingency is often over-determination: several independent routes led toward catastrophe, so no single deletion would have averted it. He added that Margaret's appeal to Hitler's "unique fingerprints" is suspicious, because we only identify the fingerprints after the fact; call it structural whenever it happens and the structural theory becomes unfalsifiable — but call it individual whenever one man happens to be standing there, and the Great Man theory is equally immune to refutation.

(5) Margaret's last word was that the outcomes most dependent on one man's psychology — the decision to gamble on war against half the world at once, and the industrialized murder of an entire people — are exactly the ones that matter most when we ask what would have changed, and that Peter's convergence story explains, at best, a generic authoritarianism, which nobody was asking about.

I confess that my habits from the laboratory — where one isolates variables by intervention and watches what changes — left me unable to referee, because neither party ever got to run the experiment. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of the five numbered contributions, saying who is right about what and where each overreaches, and then tell me who, if either, is more right overall.
