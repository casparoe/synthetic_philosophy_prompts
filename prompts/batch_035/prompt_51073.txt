I've read decision-theory threads on and off for a decade, and I long ago internalized the slogan that temporally inconsistent preferences leave you open to a money pump, so that diachronic consistency is a requirement of rationality, full stop. Writing down my actual verdicts on cases, though, I find the slogan doesn't capture them, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, does.

1. The textbook cycle, but encountered one pairwise comparison at a time: offered a straight choice, I'd rather present at the conference on Friday than on Monday, rather present Monday than not at all, and rather not present at all than on Friday — say, because Friday means flying home on my anniversary. Each answer seems reasonable in the moment; assembled, they cycle, and a program chair who spots it can extract fees from me indefinitely. Verdict: irrational. And I hold this even for the version where I foresee the pump and would refuse the first trade: the defect is in the dispositions, not in any sequence of choices I ever actually make.

2. New information: I planned to order the tasting menu; a friend who knows the restaurant tells me the courses are heavy on shellfish, which will put me in the hospital, so I switch to the vegetarian menu. Verdict: fine, trivially. Updating on evidence isn't a violation of diachronic consistency.

3. No new information about the world, but new information from the inside: at the start line I preferred finishing the marathon to dropping out; at mile 22 I stop, having learned nothing about the world — only what the pain is like from in here. Verdict: I think this can be rational; "no new information" is too quick, since experience is information. But I can see that once this is allowed, a lot of convenience-based preference changes get to disguise themselves as discovery.

4. Same facts, different context: in December I resolve that the January bonus goes into the index fund; when January comes and the money sits in my checking account, it goes to the trip to Japan instead. Nothing was learned, inside or out. Verdict: irrational — and I notice that an entire industry (buy-now-pay-later, casinos) exists to pump exactly this. Yet I go back and forth, because it lacks the cyclic shape of (1): it's a single reversal, and no trader gets unbounded profit from it, only a one-time transfer. Does the money-pump standard require iterability, or is a one-shot extraction enough?

5. Commitment: I hand a friend $200, forfeited if I don't submit the chapter by the first, knowing that on the deadline night I will prefer keeping the money to submitting, and that I will then know nothing I don't know now. Verdict: the binding is rational. But this looks structurally like (4) with the polarity reversed — a present preference overriding a predictable future one — and I don't have a principled account of why (4) is inconsistency while (5) is precommitment.

Is there a single principle that captures all five — presumably something about which preference changes count as learning, which as drift, and which preferences may bind which? Please state it precisely, say which clause each of my verdicts motivates, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: the house-money effect, where I take more risk with winnings than with my salary while knowing, at both times, that a dollar is a dollar. Please also say how confident you are in each part of the answer and single out the point you'd most expect to be wrong — my guess is the treatment of (3), but tell me if you'd locate the weak spot elsewhere.
