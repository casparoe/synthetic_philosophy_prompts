In the United States today, a defendant sentenced to death does not face execution anytime soon: the average time between imposition of a death sentence and either execution, exoneration, or death by some other cause now approaches two decades, and a substantial share of condemned prisoners — in some recent years, more than the number executed — die of natural causes or suicide before the state can carry out the sentence. Someone condemned in his twenties will typically be confined, often in near-solitary conditions on death row, into his forties or fifties before any execution, and if the sentence is finally carried out, the state kills a person who has in many ways become someone else. Take the institution exactly as practiced, then — the sentence, the decades of appeals, the conditions of confinement while the case proceeds, the eventual execution, and the tolerated drift into death by other causes — and tell me what theory of punishment that whole package presupposes. A straightforward retributivism, on which the state inflicts the suffering deserved for the offense, looks poorly matched to several of the parts: it is unclear that a desert fixed at the moment of the crime survives twenty years of change in the person punished, and retribution does not obviously explain why the state spends enormous resources keeping a man alive for decades solely in order to kill him later. Deterrence theory has mirror-image problems, since a consequence postponed for twenty years and uncertain besides is presumably a discounted threat. Yet the delay is not an accident: the appellate machinery responsible for it exists largely because the system treats executing the innocent as a distinctive, near-absolute wrong, and each round of review is the price of reducing that risk. So the practice looks like a tacit compromise among retribution, deterrence, and error-avoidance — which may or may not amount to a theory at all. I want the implicit theory made explicit: what does each component of the practice — the sentence, the prolonged review, the death row conditions, the delayed execution, and the acceptance of death by natural causes — presuppose about the justification of punishment and about who counts as the person being punished? Are those presuppositions mutually consistent, or does the institution only make sense as an unstable equilibrium among incompatible theories, each licensing a different part? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. And finally, tell me what would have to change if any one theory were placed firmly in charge — would a pure retributivism have to compress the timeline or restrict eligibility, would a thoroughgoing error-avoidance view have to abandon execution altogether, and is there any principled stopping point short of abolition once the analysis is pushed through?
