so here's my situation. I'm on a small ML safety team and last week we greenlit a roadmap that's basically iterated amplification + distillation: humans (with the current model's help) decompose hard questions into subquestions they can answer directly, the model gets distilled on that whole process, becomes the consultant in the next round, repeat. the pitch is that competence compounds — and, the safety story goes, since every training signal ultimately traces back to something a human judged, the system never learns from a source no human could check.

our most annoying coworker (he's honestly great) walked out of that meeting with an argument I can't accept but also can't break:

(1) at every round, the model is trained only on material the humans could evaluate — that's what the pipeline is for, the signal is human-judgeable by construction.
(2) a human can only honestly evaluate something they understand.
(3) so everything the system can do after round n is built out of things some human understood at round n-1 (and the model at round n-1 was itself built out of round n-2 things, all the way down).
(4) therefore nothing the system ever does is more than a recombination of things humans understood first. competence never crosses the human ceiling, it only rearranges what's underneath it.
(5) so when the pitch says the system will beat the humans at something, whatever makes that happen has to come from somewhere other than the human-judgment channel — and that somewhere is precisely what our safety story doesn't cover.

here's why I can't accept it: run the same argument word for word on ordinary supervised learning and it proves no system trained on human labels can ever do anything the labelers couldn't do. and we're all standing next to chess engines that got trained partly on games humans labeled and then proceeded to crush their teachers. so a premise must be wrong somewhere. but every time I try to break it, the fix I reach for is "well, the model generalizes — it picks up patterns from the data that no individual human ever judged" — and if I go with that, step (5) walks back in through the side door, just in a weaker form. it's like the argument is wrong, but the standard correction is the conclusion he actually wanted, wearing a softer hat.

so tell me: which step do I reject, why did that step look fine when he said it, and what does rejecting it cost? then, honestly, does his conclusion survive in some weakened form anyway, and if so which part of it survives and which part dies? I have to write the risk section of the design doc by thursday and right now it's just this argument staring at me from an empty page.

at the very end, give me one line like \answer{reject (4) — recombination isn't additive} — the step number plus a short phrase, no hedging, so I can grep it out of the doc later.
