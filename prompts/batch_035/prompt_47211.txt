My husband and I have been circling the kid question for maybe two years now, and last weekend we finally watched Arrival together because a friend said it was secretly a movie about deciding whether to become a parent. It wrecked me, honestly. I'd read a bit of L.A. Paul's stuff on transformative experience before (the book came up in a seminar I audited years ago), so I went in expecting the obvious reading: Louise can't know in advance what having Hannah will be like, so her decision is exactly the kind Paul says decision theory can't handle. But the more I think about it, the more the film seems to cheat on that reading in an interesting way. Louise doesn't decide in ignorance at all — because of the way she experiences time after learning Heptapod B, she effectively already knows the whole story, including the divorce and Hannah dying young. She's not deciding in the fog; she's consenting to a future she has, in some sense, already lived or at least already perceived.

So here's what I want from you: a reading of the film (and, if you can manage it, the ways Ted Chiang's novella differs, since the movie adds some things) through Paul's framework that takes this wrinkle seriously instead of hand-waving it. Does foreknowledge dissolve the transformative experience problem, relocate it, or expose that the problem was never really about missing information in the first place? I keep half-forming the thought that even if Louise knows every fact about her life with Hannah, there's still a difference between knowing the script and having lived the performance — that maybe the movie is saying the gap Paul points at isn't epistemic but something about endorsing a life from the inside, which no amount of advance access closes. But I'm not sure that's coherent or just me being sentimental, and I'd rather you push back where the reading strains. Please also tell me honestly where the film resists this whole approach — e.g. whether the choice it dramatizes is so stylized (literal alien language, nonlinear time) that it stops being about actual prospective parents at all.

A few specifics I'd love you to hit: which scenes you'd point to as doing the philosophical work (the ending conversation with Ian about whether you'd change things, the visions themselves, maybe the scene where she calls General Shang); whether the film sides with Louise or with Ian, since Ian walks away and the movie never really says he's wrong; and whether the movie ends up giving an answer to Paul or just changing the subject to something like amor fati. I'm writing this up as an essay for a small philosophy-and-film reading group I'm in (all amateurs, one ex-philosophy PhD), so it should be rigorous but readable by non-specialists. Please write in American English, and keep in mind I'm the person in the audience who can't stop applying the movie to her own life — a reading that pretends that's not part of what the film is doing to its viewers would feel dishonest to me. Also if you could avoid treating the decision theory stuff as settled background, that'd be great, half my group doesn't know what a utility function is.
