Our journal runs a series on philosophical stalemates, and the next installment pairs two theorists who have been arguing past each other for the better part of a decade about what pain asymbolia shows, or fails to show, about the badness of pain. One, whom I will call the phenomenal internalist, holds that what makes pain bad is exhausted by its felt character: whatever attitude one takes toward a sensation, its badness is fixed by how it feels from the inside, so an experience that is genuinely painful in the full phenomenal sense is bad whether or not the subject minds it. The other, the constitutive attitudinalist, holds that the sensory-discriminative component of pain is badness-neutral and that badness is conferred only by the way a sensation is woven into aversion, alarm, and the subject's caring structure — so an experience that no longer registers as mattering to its subject has lost its badness, however vivid it remains. The asymbolia cases — where damage to or disconnection of the insular cortex and anterior cingulate leaves patients reporting pain they insist does not bother them, and the analogous dissociation sometimes reported under morphine or following cingulotomy — are the principal battleground. Write the jointly signed statement they should produce.

The document must contain the following parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, covering at minimum: the empirical picture of asymbolia and adjacent dissociations, as they would state it; the contrast between asymbolia and congenital insensitivity to pain, where nothing is felt at all; the distinction between nociception, pain, and suffering, so far as they can jointly fix it; and whatever both accept about the reliability of a patient's report that something hurts but does not bother her. Nothing goes in this section that either party would want to phrase differently.

Second, a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated twice — once in wording the internalist would accept as capturing her view, once in wording the attitudinalist would accept as capturing his — so that the disagreement is located precisely and neither side is caricatured.

Third, before the table, I want the two of them to take seriously a worry they have mostly evaded: that they have been running together several grades of not being bothered. Let them distinguish at least (a) mere absence of withdrawal and avoidance behavior, (b) absence of any felt unpleasantness in the episode itself, and (c) preservation of felt unpleasantness alongside a subject who registers it but disowns its significance for her. Both must say which grades asymbolia, morphine dissociation, and hypnotic analgesia most plausibly instantiate, and must state explicitly what hangs on the answer — the internalist should explain why cases of type (b) would be harmless to her view, and the attitudinalist why cases of type (c) would be harmless to his, so that the reader can see what each side is really betting on.

Fourth, a cruxes section. They must identify the two or three subquestions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each crux specify a case, argument, or observational program that both accept as bearing on it, recording each side's prediction in advance. Include at least the following two, both of which they have previously fudged: the restoration-choice test — suppose a person with asymbolia is offered a procedure that would reinstate ordinary aversive responses to her pain; what should she choose, what would she in fact choose, and does her choice bear on whether the pain was bad for her all along, with each side's prediction about what such choices would actually look like — and the reverse-dissociation question of whether anything counts as badness without pain (grief, guilt, or an itch one is ashamed to scratch), and what the existence or nonexistence of that dissociation would show about what the two camps are actually disagreeing over.

Fifth, a concessions paragraph: what each party would have to give up if the other's crux predictions came out as forecast.

Finally, a section stating candidly which parts of their residual dispute they jointly judge to be merely verbal — in particular, whether their disagreement survives once unpleasantness, badness, suffering, and mattering-to-the-subject have been kept scrupulously distinct — and, if some of it does survive, what its substantive residue is. Keep the register of a negotiated document both would actually sign: careful, undefensive, and free of any author's elbow.
