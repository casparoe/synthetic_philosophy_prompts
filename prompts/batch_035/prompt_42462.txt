I am a teaching assistant preparing a handout for an intermediate philosophy of language seminar on the rule-following considerations in Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language. Write a decision tree that starts from a commitment every student in the room will grant — that when I computed 68 + 57 just now, I was right to answer '125' and would have been wrong to answer '5' — and leads to a position on what, if anything, makes that so. Every node must be a question answerable without knowing the literature's labels: e.g. whether there is a fact of the matter about which function I meant all along by 'plus'; if so, whether it is a fact about inner episodes, dispositions, my community's practice, or something irreducibly normative; if not, whether the practice of rule-following still needs justifying, needs nothing, or whether the demand for a ground is itself the mistake. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names, then locate the actual positions as leaves — a Platonist view of meaning-facts, simple dispositionalism, the communitarian view, the skeptical-solution non-factualism, a natural-history view on which the norms are simply what we find ourselves going on with, and a quietist or deflationary exit. Use the test cases the positions genuinely divide on — quaddition, the deviant interpretation of one's own past usage, a solitary rule-follower — to phrase the nodes, so that students are sorted by what they say about cases rather than by which label they prefer. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, note combinations that come out unoccupied and say briefly whether they look incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and keep the tree to at most ten leaves. Append a short note stating how confident you are in each leaf's placement and singling out the single branch point you are least sure about.
