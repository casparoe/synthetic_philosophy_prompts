I've been defending a Libet-style argument against free will in a reading group, and it keeps winning — which now bothers me, because I've come to see that it proves far more than any of us wanted it to. Here is the argument:

(1) For an action to be free, the conscious intention behind it must be what originates the action: the intention cannot merely inherit its direction from unconscious processes that had already settled the outcome before the agent was aware of anything.

(2) In Libet's experiments, subjects flex a wrist at a moment of their own choosing and report, with the help of a spot revolving round a clock face, when they first became aware of the urge to move. The averaged readiness potential begins roughly half a second before the movement; the reported awareness of the urge, on average, about two tenths of a second before it. On the standard reading, the brain's preparation is well underway before the subject knows of anything.

(3) So in these experiments the unconscious preparation precedes and settles the movement before any conscious intention is on the scene; the awareness that arrives at W is a latecomer that does no steering. These acts are not free.

(4) What is true of the wrist is true of everything. Every conscious state is announced to us only after the neural work is done: perception, judgment, intention — each shows up in consciousness as a finished product, its antecedents hidden from us. By parity, no conscious intention is ever the originator of anything; every intention is downstream of unconscious processes that had already determined it.

(5) Therefore no action is free.

Now the proves-too-much part, which is what I actually want help with:

(6) If (4) is right, then the judgment I formed just now — that this argument is sound — was likewise settled by unconscious processes before I was aware of judging anything, and so is whatever judgment you form in response. Reasons and evidence do no work; they are feelings that accompany outcomes already determined.

(7) But an argument whose premises can be accepted only through a process that, by its own lights, is not responsive to reasons cannot give anyone a reason to accept its conclusion. So if the argument is sound, no one can rationally believe it is sound.

I want to refuse the easy exit here — declaring the whole thing self-refuting at (7) and leaving it at that, as though that disposed of it. Determinists can absorb that cost cheerfully, and I'd rather know where the chain actually breaks. The candidates I can see:

- Break at (1): then the Libet result never bore on freedom in the first place, and the decades of debate were a staging of prior commitments. That seems right but is depressingly deflationary — is it really true that nothing in the experiment speaks to freedom?
- Break between (2) and (3): the readiness potential is an average across trials, and what is being averaged may not be a decision signal at all, but background fluctuation that occasionally crosses a threshold — in which case the activity preceding W is not the movement being settled but the noise within which a later decision occurs. But then I owe an account of what the pre-W activity is, and "noise" seems to hand back less than anyone wanted.
- Break at (4): the parity step. Perhaps spontaneous wrist-flexing in a laboratory is special — nothing at stake, no prior deliberation — whereas ordinary decided actions involve conscious planning that Libet's paradigm never touched. But then (3) still stands, and spontaneous acts are unfree: a real, if modest, skeptical concession.

For each break, tell me what rejecting that step costs, and which cost is least. Separately, I want to know whether (7) really follows from (4). I suspect (6) trades on running together two different things: a state's being determined by prior causes, and its being insensitive to reasons. If determination by prior causes is compatible with sensitivity to reasons, then (6) fails without touching (5), the argument stops proving too much, and it proves only what determinists believed all along — which may be the honest outcome. But I cannot see how to make that compatibility precise rather than merely insisted upon. Can you?
