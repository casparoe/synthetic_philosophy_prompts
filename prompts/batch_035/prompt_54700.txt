I edit a small-circulation newsletter on ethics for readers with no philosophy training, and for the next issue I want a "what do you actually think about your own conscience?" quiz — one that sorts readers by where they stand on the line between moral judgment and bias: when is a moral reaction a calibrated assessment, and when is it noise with a confident voice? The quiz should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which labels they like, so please design the instrument rather than explaining the views to me.

The quiz: eight to ten questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options of one or two sentences each. It must be answerable by someone who has never read a word of metaethics, so no question may use the terms "objective," "subjective," "relative," "moral fact," or ask anything of the form "do you think moral claims are true?" — the sorting has to happen through cases alone.

To keep it from being a vocabulary test, at least three questions must be what I think of as flip cases: situations where a person learns something about the causal origin or situational trigger of their own judgment, and the reader has to say whether that undermines the judgment. For example: your ranking of two options reverses depending on the order you heard them in; your conviction that some practice is vile turns out to track which side your group benefited from; you learn you would have believed the exact opposite of what you believe had you been born in a different century. Alongside these, work in at least: a dumbfounding case (the consensual siblings with reliable contraception, where nobody can produce a harm), a question about whether getting rid of slavery was discovering something or just changing, and a question about an advisor who knows every non-moral fact — is any moral question still open for them, and if so, open in the way a puzzle is open or in the way a preference is undecided?

Then the scoring key, and here is where I want the real care. Build it from the logical space first: lay out the combinations of answers the questions actually allow, and only then locate the named positions in that space — sentimentalism in Hume's vein, expressivism with its quasi-realist ambitions, constructivism, naturalist moral realism, plain subjectivism, and error theory. Where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, say so and diagnose whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions fall on the same answers — sentimentalist and quasi-realist are the obvious risk, but check constructivism against certain realists too — the key must say openly that the quiz cannot separate them and propose the single follow-up question that would, rather than pretending the scoring splits them. Each leaf should carry one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment, and readers whose answer patterns are internally inconsistent should get not a leaf but the key's identification of the two most likely inconsistencies and what each one reveals.

Format: lead with your bottom line — one sentence stating which distinction does the most sorting work across the whole quiz and why — and then go straight into the quiz, the key, and the notes, with no preamble and no restatement of my request.
