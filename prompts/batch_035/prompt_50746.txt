Next month I'm giving the closing talk at a workshop on the politics of historical memory, and my thesis is deliberately contrarian for that room: that there is a genuine, if narrow, moral case for letting certain weighty historical memories fade rather than preserving them by institutional effort — archives, anniversaries, mandatory curricula — and that the widely assumed general duty to remember is far narrower than the commemorative consensus suggests. My argument runs in two stages. First, a conceptual stage borrowing from Avishai Margalit: duties of memory arise from thick relations — shared history, kinship, common language — and bind communities, not humanity at large; humanity as such has at most thin, universal duties, which are duties of morality and law, not of remembrance, and thin relations do not carry a duty to remember the dead of strangers. Second, a prudential stage in the spirit of David Rieff: mythologized memory is not inert; in divided societies it keeps old scores open, transmits grievance as inheritance, and can make reconciliation harder rather than easier, so the moral ledger of remembrance is not one-sided, and sometimes the right side of it favors fading. I want to hold both stages but am willing to trim the second if the audience forces me to.

The audience matters here, so let me describe it precisely. The workshop is mostly memory-studies scholars — historians of commemoration, people working on collective memory in the tradition of social memory studies, a couple of psychoanalytically inclined theorists of trauma — plus a handful of practitioners from transitional justice: people who have designed truth commissions and memorial museums, some of whom have worked with survivor communities. Almost all of them treat remembrance as a moral achievement; several see forgetting as the tool of perpetrators and states. At least one senior figure has built a career on the claim that memory work is a form of care for the dead. They know Margalit and Ricoeur well, are skeptical of Rieff, and will not be impressed by the Santayana line quoted back at them in either direction. Nobody in the room needs the basics explained, and they will detect any hedging dressed up as nuance.
