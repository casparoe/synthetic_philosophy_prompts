I'm part of a small reading group that works through effective-altruism-adjacent philosophy, and our most recent session took up moral trade — the idea, associated with the philosopher Nick Bostrom, that agents with different moral views often leave gains on the table because each spends all of her effort on her own priorities, when a deal could leave both better off by their own lights: you fund my animal-welfare projects and I fund your art museums, even though I barely value the museums and you barely value the animals. The textbook examples are apolitical and low-stakes — two donors swapping which charities they contribute to, legislators logrolling over pet projects that the other side merely shrugs at — and the crucial feature is that my side of the bargain is something you regard as neutral or a mild positive, not something you positively condemn. A genuinely disliked project is supposed to be untradeable: if I think your cause is evil rather than merely non-urgent, the mechanism collapses.

Our group got stuck on a case that seems to strain the concept: the Missouri Compromise of 1820. Missouri entered as a slave state, Maine was split off from Massachusetts and entered as a free state, and slavery was barred from the remaining Louisiana Purchase territory north of the 36°30′ line. On its face it looks like moral trade: each section gave up something it wanted less than what it secured, both preferred the deal to a union-breaking confrontation, and the two sides had different moral views about slavery to begin with. But the fit seemed off to several of us. Many Northern congressmen regarded slavery not as a defensible priority of someone else's but as an evil — so their concession was not the deprioritization of a neutral project but acquiescence in something they condemned. And unlike a donor swap, the compromise did not merely allocate resources; it wrote a geographic rule into federal law, which arguably legitimated and stabilized the institution's westward footprint rather than containing it. Neither dynamic appears in the standard examples, and it became unclear whether we were looking at a hard case of moral trade or at something the idea was never meant to cover.

Please analyze the Missouri Compromise as a case of moral trade, and then tell me where the model breaks.
