Last month my renter's-insurance claim was denied on the grounds that, given what I knew about an ageing washing-machine hose, I knew or should have known the flood was coming. My niece, a graduate student in formal epistemology, pointed out that this formula quietly enrolls me in the ideal reasoner of classical epistemic logic: if I knew the premises, then I knew every consequence of them, so the loss could not have surprised me and must have been, in the relevant sense, already known. The whole affair has left me with a peculiar ambition to see that idealization carried through without embarrassment.

Please write a satirical insurance-claim denial letter, roughly 450 to 550 words, issued by a fictional insurer — call it something like Mutual Assurance of Ideal Reasoners — that has formally adopted the ideal epistemic agent of Hintikka-style epistemic logic as its standard model of the policyholder. The letter should be deadpan throughout, in a polished corporate-legal register, and its humor must come from getting the logic right rather than from generic jokes about insurers: the denial should turn on actual features of the idealization — closure of knowledge under known implication, knowledge of all logical truths, and negative introspection — so that each step of the denial is formally valid as an application of them to an ordinary domestic mishap. It should contain a numbered section of findings, and an appeal clause demonstrating, by the company's own lights, that appeal is impossible, for instance because the policyholder, by negative introspection, already knew the appeal would be denied. At least one finding should reclassify an ordinary human failure to draw an inference as material misrepresentation: since the policyholder did not deduce the loss, the company concludes the policyholder cannot have known the premises after all, so the disclosure of those premises was false. Somewhere in the fine print, include a curt acknowledgment that actual humans are non-ideal agents, dismissed as a problem for the policyholder rather than for the model. The target of the satire should be the idealization itself — the quiet substitution of an ideal reasoner for a bounded one — rather than insurance companies as such, and a specialist in epistemic logic should recognize the letter as fair. Do not cite any real publication; the only authorities the letter may invoke are the company's own underwriting standards and policy provisions, which you are free to invent.
