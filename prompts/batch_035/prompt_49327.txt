Something about the duress defense in criminal law puzzles me. We excuse someone (or at least mitigate) who commits a crime because someone credibly threatened to kill them, but only if there was no reasonable avenue of escape — and the doctrine is full of language about the defendant's will being overborne. Meanwhile we never accept "I just couldn't resist" — addiction, greed, rage don't count, even in cases where the person arguably couldn't have done otherwise. What theory of responsibility does that pattern presuppose? The escape-avenue requirement looks like the principle of alternative possiblities: no duress if you could've just walked away, i.e., the excuse exists only where alternatives existed. But the overborne-will talk sounds more like a sourcehood story — the action didn't really come from the agent. So which is it: does the doctrine presuppose alternative possibilities, or is it really about whose will it was, and are the two parts even consistent with each other?

Then try this twist. Suppose the gangster who made the threat had also stationed someone to forcibly make the defendant commit the crime anyway if he'd resisted — a Frankfurt setup, so the defendant couldn't have done otherwise no matter what. On the doctrine's own logic, should the excuse collapse, since the threat no longer "did the work" of removing his options? And more generally, what would actually have to change in how courts reason about duress if we took the Frankfurt challenge to alternative possibilities seriously?

Last thing: for centuries the rule was that duress is never available for murder, because no threat can justify taking an innocent life. What does that carve-out presuppose, and does it sit consistently with the rest of the doctrine, or is it pulling in a different direction?
