I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar — 14 students, one 80-minute session, movable desks and a whiteboard. We're about to begin the unit on laws of nature, and before the students read anything, I want them to feel the difference between two pictures: on one, the laws are just the best summary of the totality of particular facts — a "lawbook" chosen by trading simplicity off against strength, with nothing enforcing it, nothing it constrains; on the other, the laws somehow govern or produce the particular facts, so that the direction of explanation runs the other way. I want the session designed so they run into the trouble spots of the summary picture on their own, not because I tell them.

Design the session around the following, or around better machinery of your own devising. First, teams receive a finite "mosaic" of particular facts — give me a concrete, physically realizable dataset (something I can hand out on paper), plus a fixed vocabulary in which their candidate lawbooks may be written. Second, the teams compete as systematizers: each produces a lawbook, and I score them on simplicity and strength under an exchange rate I announce in advance. Third, at least one round must be engineered to end in a genuine tie or underdetermination: two clearly different lawbooks scoring identically under the announced exchange rate, so that which laws there are comes out relative to a choice none of them made. Fourth, a "miracle" round: the mosaic I hand out contains a sparse handful of exception-events, and the teams must decide whether a simpler law supplemented by explicitly declared exceptions beats a messier law with none — I want them to discover for themselves that the summary picture buys simplicity by tolerating miracles. Then a debrief that maps each stage of the game onto the corresponding part of the dialectic, including where in the game each anti-Humean intuition was at its strongest.

One constraint I care about a great deal: the game must not secretly smuggle in a governing rule. If I generate the mosaic from a hidden "true" generative rule, the students will naturally treat their candidate lawbooks as guesses at that rule — which is precisely the governing intuition that the summary picture denies. Either design the session to avoid this trap altogether, or build it in deliberately and make it the debrief's punchline. Say explicitly which you have done and why.

I also want two honest sections at the end. In one, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including how likely the students are to keep asking, and refusing to drop, "but which one is really the law?", what to do when a team tries to game the scoring rather than systematize, and what to do if the tie I engineered doesn't materialize. In the other, be frank about which features of the real disagreement no classroom game can reproduce and I will have to supply by talking — for instance, the modal reach of laws into counterfactuals about situations that occur nowhere in the mosaic, or the claim that explanation runs from laws down to facts rather than from facts up to lawbooks.

Format: open with a three-sentence overview of the session's arc, then present the rules, materials, timeline, debrief plan, and the two honesty sections as separately headed sections; keep the whole response under 900 words.
