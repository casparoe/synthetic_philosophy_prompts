A colleague and I have been circling the same argument for months and have reached the point where we can each recite the other's lines: whether AI alignment research should aim at worst-case guarantees or whether that ambition is a category error. I come from the safety side, where the reasoning feels airtight: alignment is a problem you only get to fail at once, so what matters is the maximum loss over plausible deployment scenarios, not the mean; you should reason about deceptive misalignment and distributional shift precisely because they are rare-tail events that benchmarks will never surface; and a safety case is supposed to bound what a system can do, not report how it does on average. He comes from mainstream ML, where my framing sounds like refusing to state a hypothesis: he points out that 'the worst case' is not a well-defined quantity without a threat model, that worst-case reasoning over unconstrained adversaries proves too much (by parity of reasoning we should never deploy any software, or leave our houses), and that average-case evaluation with strong red-teaming, held-out generalization tests, and incident monitoring is how every other safety-critical field actually operates — aviation and pharmaceuticals don't demand proofs against adversarial physics, they quantify and bound expected harm.

What strikes me is how little the disagreement seems to be about any actual prediction. We agree current systems fail in ways that benchmarks underdetect; we agree a sufficiently capable misaligned system would be very bad; we even agree on the rough probability ordering of catastrophes. Yet he thinks my worst-case talk is epistemically empty and I think his average-case talk is practically reckless, and neither of us budges. I have started to suspect the dispute is not about evidence at all, but about something structural underneath: perhaps we are answering different questions under one heading — he is answering 'is this particular system acceptable to deploy' while I am answering 'what research programme deserves resources now' — and worst-case and average-case are the right answers to each respectively. Or perhaps it is a hidden disagreement about decision theory, specifically about whether the catastrophe-aversion my side exhibits is a rational response to certain kinds of uncertainty (e.g., about the tails of one's own distribution) or just a bias that happens to be culturally rewarded in my corner of the field. Or perhaps it is genuinely about the modality of the evidence: whether thought experiments about deceptive alignment count as data.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Using what each side actually claims, tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, empirical, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as the relevant data, or about bedrock intuitions about precaution that neither of us can argue for. If we are indeed answering different questions under one name, say so and show where the equivocation happens. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a class of deployment scenarios where worst-case and average-case reasoning provably diverge, or an argument that this impasse is just what you should expect when a field's threat model is underdetermined by its evidence. Please write in British English.
