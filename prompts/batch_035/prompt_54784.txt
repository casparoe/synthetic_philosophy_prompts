In the rationalist and LessWrong-adjacent circles I've frequented for years, the standard way to motivate an evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism is the sweetness analogy: evolution made sugar taste sweet because seeking out calorie-dense foods was fitness-enhancing for our ancestors, and nobody thinks sweetness is a perception of some objective, mind-independent property of deliciousness residing in the sugar; it's just what our reward system feels like from the inside. The analogy is then extended to morality: our moral intuitions grip us for the same kind of fitness reason, so their grip is evidence of nothing beyond our own constitution, and realism about value loses its footing. I recently watched a forum thread lean on this analogy hard — one commenter called moral intuitions "sugar by another name" — but there was a pushback I haven't been able to shake: thirst and fear are also products of selection for fitness, yet they track real facts (dehydration, danger) rather well, because in those domains accurate tracking itself promoted fitness. It looks like the sweetness analogy quietly assumes that being selected for fitness precludes tracking, when the perceptual case suggests selection sometimes makes tracking all but guaranteed.

Please audit the analogy as it's actually being used in that debate. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the analogy still support the debunking conclusion? Then lay out the mapping explicitly — what, in the moral case, plays the role of the sweetness, the sugar, the taste receptors, and the ancestral conditions that made sweet-seeking adaptive — and separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from the ones that don't. I'd specifically like you to address whether the analogy begs the question by choosing sweetness (a mere push toward an option) rather than thirst (a seeming that happens to track) as its exemplar; whether the real load-bearing issue is whether tracking moral truths would have been fitness-enhancing for Pleistocene humans, so that the analogy can't settle the matter either way; and what exactly a debunker would need to establish for the analogy to carry the weight it's being asked to carry. If you think the analogy fails, say whether there's a better one available to the debunker — or whether the debunking argument is better off without an analogy at all. Take a definite position throughout and give your reasons; don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me. No preamble and no restating my question.
