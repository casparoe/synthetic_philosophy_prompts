I serve on the committee that conducts the character and fitness review required for admission to the bar in my jurisdiction. The practice, in outline: every applicant must disclose past misconduct — academic dishonesty, fraud, offenses involving dishonesty, and so on; we solicit letters from people who have observed the applicant in professional settings attesting to traits such as honesty, diligence, and judgment; we treat a pattern of scattered episodes across different contexts as far stronger evidence of bad character than a single episode, which we are prepared to excuse as youthful error or as situational pressure; and we are empowered to deny a license, more or less permanently, on the ground that the applicant's settled character makes future dishonesty as a lawyer likely. Roughly the same design appears wherever institutions gatekeep on character: reference letters for academic hiring, security-clearance adjudication, and good-moral-character clauses in other professional licenses.

I also read social psychology, and it seems to undercut the whole design. In one famous experiment, seminary students who had just prepared a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan mostly walked past a slumped, coughing stranger in an alleyway; whether they stopped correlated only weakly with measures of their compassion and religiosity and very strongly with whether the experimenters had told them they were already running late. In studies conducted in the late 1920s, researchers found that a schoolchild who cheated on a test was barely more likely than any other child to lie or steal in a different setting; honesty predicted behavior within nearly identical situations and hardly at all across them. In Milgram's obedience experiments, ordinary people delivered what they believed were dangerous electric shocks largely because a calm authority figure in a lab coat instructed them to continue.

Here is my puzzle. The review seems to presuppose exactly what this research puts in doubt: that people have global character traits, stable across situations, that predict how they will behave in the novel professional situations a lawyer will face. But I suspect the presupposition is not that simple, for three reasons. First, the same legal system that licenses lawyers on predicted character also sentences criminal defendants with heavy emphasis on circumstances — duress, mitigating factors, the corruption of a bad workplace — and even we excuse a single lapse by pointing to situational pressure, which looks like situationist reasoning. Second, our pattern-versus-single-episode distinction might be an evidentiary rule about base rates rather than a metaphysical claim about traits: even a thoroughgoing situationist can hold that past behavior is the best available predictor of future behavior in similar situations, so more episodes simply mean more relevant situations covered. Third, we may need only traits that are local to professional-role contexts — something like honesty-in-the-role — which the psychology does not refute, since almost none of it was conducted on sustained performance in a professional role.
