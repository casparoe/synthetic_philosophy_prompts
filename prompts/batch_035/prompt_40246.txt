In my space-policy reading group we keep returning to an analogy that a lot of Mars-settlement advocates lean on: making humanity multiplanetary is like backing up your data. On this picture, Earth is a hard drive that could fail at any moment — asteroid impact, engineered pandemic, nuclear war, runaway climate collapse — and a self-sufficient settlement on Mars or in orbit is the offsite copy. The people using it concede that the copy would be worse off than the original in almost every respect — thinner biosphere, harsher conditions, smaller population — but say that doesn't matter, because the whole point of a backup is not that it's good to use, it's that it exists. The analogy is doing real argumentative work in our discussions: it's been used to claim that the value of settlement is essentially independent of the quality of life in the settlement, that we should prioritize self-sufficiency over habitability, and that spending enormous sums on settlement now is just rational disaster preparedness, like paying for insurance.

I'd like a proper audit of this. Could you set out what maps onto what — the drive, the data, the copy, the backup software, the act of restoring — and then identify where the mapping breaks? Some worries we've been circling without resolving: a backup is a static snapshot that stays inert until needed, while a colony is a living population that diverges, develops its own culture, and might not want to be a backup; "restoring" Earth after a catastrophe means recolonizing it with people whose values were formed elsewhere; and it's not obvious what the "data" even is — the biosphere, human civilization, conscious life, or something like the accumulations of culture and knowledge that a copy might not reproduce. I'm also unsure which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions the analogy was being used to license, and which are just loose fit that we shouldn't expect from any analogy.

Finally: does the insurance-style conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — is there a version of the "backup" argument that gives you a reason to build settlements even granting that a settlement is a community of people and not a file? And is there a better analogy for what settlement advocates are trying to argue — seed bank, species redundancy, lifeboat, second-language acquisition, whatever — that does the work more honestly? If it helps, the reading group ranges from fairly serious utilitarians to people with strong person-affecting intuitions, so I'd like to see where the analogy's fate differs across those views, particularly on whether a "backup" that only matters after everyone we currently care about is dead can carry any moral weight at all.
