I am a retired physical chemist who has come rather late to philosophy, and I would be grateful for your help with a question that has arisen in the free-will reading group I joined after leaving the laboratory. Please answer in British English.

Our group's most consistent member — I shall call her Margaret, a retired circuit judge with unnervingly steady verdicts — has been working through cases concerning the principle of alternative possibilities: the thought that a person is morally responsible for what she has done only if she could have done otherwise. Her verdicts so far:

1. The classic case: Jones votes Democrat of his own accord, having deliberated normally, while neuroscientist Black waits with a device that would have forced the vote had Jones shown any sign of wavering. The device never fires. Jones could, at most, have begun to waver briefly before the device would have cut in. Margaret holds Jones blameworthy.

2. Variant in which Black's device actually fires, so that Jones votes Democrat because of direct stimulation that bypasses his reasoning altogether. Margaret holds Jones not blameworthy.

3. The intoxicated driver, who could not have avoided swerving at the moment of the accident but freely chose earlier to drink heavily knowing what he was like. Margaret holds him blameworthy.

4. Jones locked overnight in a polling booth, unable to leave or revise matters, who votes in accordance with an intention he formed freely and uncoerced the day before. Margaret holds him blameworthy for casting the vote.

5. Plum, engineered from before birth — values, temperament and all — so that he deliberates smoothly, in a fully reasons-responsive way, towards voting Democrat, and so that he could never so much as waver towards any other conclusion. No device ever intervenes; his design simply guarantees the outcome. Margaret holds Plum not blameworthy.

Next week we present her with two new cases, and I should like to predict her verdicts before she gives them.

Case A: as in the classic case, except that Black's device monitors the earliest neural precursor of doubt, milliseconds before any conscious stirring, and suppresses it instantly. Doubt therefore never even begins. There is no waver, no hesitation, no flicker of an alternative — only Jones's own settled reasons carrying him smoothly to the lever.

Case B: exactly as in Case A, except that there is no Black and no device; the precursor-suppressing condition is congenital, discovered post mortem, and no one designed it at all.

What I want from you is this: identify the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with Margaret's five verdicts — I can see at least two, one making responsibility depend on some residual alternative, however slight or distant in time, and one making it depend on the character of the actual causal sequence, subject perhaps to some proviso about origins — and say what each rule predicts for Case A and Case B. Note that her fifth verdict seems to cost her the purest Frankfurtian position, and I should like you to be explicit about that. Then give me your best guess as to her two verdicts, with your reasons, and describe the further case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules, ideally one that does not simply collapse into A or B.

I will confess a bias that may be clouding my own judgement: I spent forty years among measurable quantities, and I confess I find counterfactuals — what would have happened had the device been needed — uncomfortably close to unobservable scaffolding. I suspect this tempts me towards reading Margaret as an actual-sequence theorist when her record may not warrant it. If you think my training is doing my philosophy for me, do say so plainly.
