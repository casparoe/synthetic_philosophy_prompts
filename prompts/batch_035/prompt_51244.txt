I've been reading around the two-dimensional semantics literature for a couple of years now, and I've reached the discouraged conviction that the central dispute has stopped moving. To set the stage as I understand it: the two-dimensionalist wants to explain why "water is H2O" is necessary yet epistemically contingent by assigning each statement two intensions — one tracking how things could turn out to be, given fixed actual reference, the other tracking how things could have turned out to be, given only what a competent speaker knows a priori. The familiar argument is then that a priori entailments from physical facts to phenomenal truths fail on the second dimension, so materialism is in trouble. And the familiar objection, pressed hardest by Soames and taken up by Stalnaker, is that the epistemic or "deep" intension is not a semantic notion at all: it reflects how a speaker comes to know what she's talking about, not what her words mean, so any necessity delivered on the second dimension is an artifact of pairing a metasemantic or epistemological device with a semantic framework that was never built to house it. The two-dimensionalists respond that the framework is precisely a theory of the interaction between meaning and epistemic access, and that the objectors' own "semantic" intensions presuppose facts about reference that a theory of understanding should help itself to.

What I find dispiriting is that both sides seem to have settled into a rhythm where the two-dimensionalist produces a new way of characterizing the primary intension and the objector produces a new counterexample in which the primary intension and the meaning come apart — a speaker who is ignorant of the kind, or uses "water" for something that's a mixture of H2O and XYZ, or a Twin-Earthling raised on XYZ who mistakenly believes she's had H2O all along — and each side treats its own treatment of the case as obvious. Nobody's position has visibly changed since, as far as I can tell, the early 2000s exchanges, and yet the literature keeps growing.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the disagreement is not really about semantics but about what counts as settling the reference of a term — that the objector is demanding a semantic fact of the matter fixed independently of any epistemic facts, while the two-dimensionalist takes the epistemic facts to be partly constitutive of the semantic ones, and that each side's counterexamples are transparently question-begging against the other's conception. If that's right, the debate is either verbal or a proxy war over metasemantics, and the question is whether making that explicit would dissolve it or merely relocate it.

So here is what I want from you: a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey of the positions. Is my hypothesis right, or is there a substantive formal or empirical question underneath that the rhetoric obscures — for instance, a disagreement about whether a priori scrutability holds, or about what an adequate account of understanding owes us, that could in principle be resolved by argument or example? In diagnosing it, please engage with what each side actually says about the cases that fuel the impasse, including the ones I mentioned. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction between epistemic and semantic readings of the framework that both sides could accept, a class of cases that forces one side's hand, a restatement of the physicalism question that doesn't route through two-dimensional machinery at all — or an argument that no progress should be expected because the dispute rests on starting intuitions about meaning that neither side can argue for without circularity.

And single out the single point of your diagnosis you are least sure of — the claim that, if it failed, would take the diagnosis down with it — and say what evidence or argument would bear on that point specifically.
