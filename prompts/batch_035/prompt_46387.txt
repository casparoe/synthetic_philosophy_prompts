I'm a novelist, and a chapter I'm drafting turns on the Grand Inquisitor's claim that the great majority of people cannot bear the burden of freedom and will, given the chance, exchange it for happiness. My draft currently just asserts this; I'd like a case built to order to stress-test it, and I want the answer to commit rather than merely illustrate. Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The surrender of freedom must be voluntary and revocable, repeated over years, and free of coercion, exhaustion, fear, and propaganda: what drives it must be something in the structure of choice itself that the Inquisitor's own diagnosis identifies — the misery of responsibility, the craving to be led — not hunger or terror. The people who surrender must sincerely report, and keep reporting, that they are happier for it. Keep the setting domestic and realistic — a workplace, a co-op, a neighborhood association; no dystopia, no miracle, no science fiction — and the scenario itself no longer than one paragraph. The freedom given up should be one the average reader would swear he would never relinquish, yet by the end of the case that swagger should feel less secure. After the scenario, show which elements of the Inquisitor's position the case vindicates and which it undermines, identify the single feature doing the most work and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and then take a definite stand: on balance, does the case confirm his diagnosis or expose a limit of it? Give your reasons and do not leave the verdict to me. Please write in American English, and flag any point where the case rests on a contested reading of Dostoevsky rather than on the text itself.
