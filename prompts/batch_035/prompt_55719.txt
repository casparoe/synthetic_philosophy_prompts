My ethics reading group has been stuck on trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect for what feels like forever, and I'm starting to wonder what kind of disagreement we're actually having. Everyone in the room agrees on how the cases go: flipping the switch feels permissible, shoving the big guy off the footbridge feels wrong, and the DDE defenders in the group say that's because redirecting a preexisting threat is morally different from using someone's body as a means, even when the numbers are identical. The consequentialists just roll their eyes and say these intuitions are noise — maybe evolutionary leftovers about up-close violence that don't track anything morally real — so the whole exercise is like taking our fear of spiders as evidence about arachnid ethics. And then we go in circles for two hours, and the next month we do it again.

Some context on where we've ended up. Foot basically invented the setup to probe the intending/foreseeing distinction, and Thomson ran with it, but even she eventually turned around on parts of it — the loop track variant, where the fat man's body is part of the causal chain even in the switch case, is what did it for her, which the consequentialists in my group love to bring up. One of my friends thinks the real fight isn't about double effect at all but about the epistemology: whether intuitions about cases count as data that a theory must fit, or as suspect outputs of a biased brain that we should expect a correct theory to revise. Another friend thinks the whole thing is verbal, that "intends" vs. "foresees" isn't carving anything at the joints and the debate only feels substantive because the cases are so vivid. And I half-suspect the problem is the cases themselves — tiny, artificial dilemmas with all the context stripped out, where it's no surprise that rival theories all "explain" the verdicts and nothing budges.

So I don't want a verdict on whether double effect is true, I want a diagnosis of the impasse: is the disagreement substantive, verbal, epistemological, or a symptom of the method itself being bankrupt, and what — a new kind of case, a distinction, some empirical finding about how people actually reason — would even count as progress here? Please give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each only a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: your diagnosis, your main reason for it, the strongest objection to that diagnosis, what would count as real progress if you're right, and your honest bet on whether this debate gets resolved in fifty years or just keeps re-running.
