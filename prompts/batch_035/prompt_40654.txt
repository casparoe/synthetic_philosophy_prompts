I keep running into the slogan that history is written by the victors, and I want to stress-test it on an episode that seems to break it immediately: the Lost Cause. The Confederacy lost the Civil War totally — armies destroyed, leadership in disgrace, its political project dead — and yet for the better part of a century afterward, the dominant (white, American) historiography of the war and of Reconstruction was written by the losers: the Lost Cause myth of noble defeat, the Dunning-school picture of Reconstruction as misrule by corrupt carpetbaggers and ignorant freedmen, the statues of Lee going up decades after Appomattox. So take this as a case study and run it both ways. First, does the slogan survive at all — is the obvious rescue ("the real victors were the winners of the peace, the Redeemer governments that ended Reconstruction in 1877, so the same side that won the historiography did win something") actually a rescue, or does it stretch "victor" until the claim becomes circular, i.e., whoever's account won was thereby the victor? Second, what does the episode show about what "power" has to mean for the slogan to say anything non-trivial — military power clearly wasn't enough, so is it publishing power, school-textbook power, monument power, something else? And be honest about where the mapping gets uncomfortable: W.E.B. Du Bois and other Black scholars were publishing counter-accounts the whole time and losing the mainstream battle, so it's not that no one wrote the history, it's that certain accounts couldnt win. Does that push you toward a stronger claim (it's not that victors write history, it's that history gets written for whoever controls the institutions that decide what counts as history) or toward dropping the slogan entirely? I dont need a victory lap for either side — I want to know what survives.
