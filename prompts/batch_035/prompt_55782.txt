I'm a student in a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and for my session on moral luck I want to hand the class a decision tree that sorts us into positions on the question rather than letting everyone just pick a label. Please draft it for me, with the following constraints.

Build the tree from the cases, not from the standard fourfold classification of luck types. The cases I want included: (a) two drivers who are equally drunk and reckless, one of whom kills a child who runs into the road while the other gets home uneventfully; (b) a person raised by cruel parents in a racist town who ends up with a warped character, contrasted with a temperamentally identical person raised kindly; (c) a German who emigrated to Argentina in 1932 and later claimed his own decency, contrasted with his equally nationalistic friend who stayed and joined the party; (d) a deterministic world in which every choice is the downstream product of heredity and upbringing. Each branch node should be a question about one of these cases (or a further question arising from it) that a classmate could answer without knowing which position it leads toward — so nothing like "do you accept circumstantial moral luck?"

Two structural requirements that I care about most. First, at some point the tree must ask whether a difference between a lucky and an unlucky agent is a difference in how blameworthy they are, or only in what else is owed — apology, restitution, being the object of grief and anger — and I want the branches to genuinely diverge there rather than reconverge immediately. Second, after the leaves are fixed, tell me which pairs of positions the tree cannot actually separate, and whether any of the leaves you end up with are unoccupied in the literature — and if so, whether that's because the combination is incoherent or just unattractive or unexplored.

I expect the leaves to include at least: the strict control principle (no moral luck anywhere), a position on which wrongness is luck-free but blameworthy assessment is not, a position on which our differential treatment of lucky and unlucky agents tracks epistemic access rather than moral facts, and a position that accepts moral luck outright and revises the control principle accordingly. You're free to add or split leaves if the cases demand it. Each leaf should carry a name if there's a standard one, or "unnamed" if not, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Keep the whole handout to about a page and a half — my classmates will get it five minutes before we start.
