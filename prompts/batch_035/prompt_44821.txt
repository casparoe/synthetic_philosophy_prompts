For my school's philosophy society I have to present and defend a worked-out position of my own on free will and punishment, and before I do I want an audit of whether the things I believe actually fit together. I came to this topic after reading about the disagreement between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether anyone ever deserves punishment in the basic sense — deserves to suffer because and only because they did wrong — but the position below is mine, stitched together from what I found convincing on each side, which is exactly why I suspect it may not cohere. Here are my commitments:

(1) There is no libertarian free will, and the kind of free will compatibilists talk about is not enough for basic desert: no one ever deserves to suffer simply because they did something wrong. (2) Even so, people are genuinely morally responsible in the everyday sense — it remains true that a liar is the sort of person one shouldn't trust, that an agent can be called on to justify what they did, and that praise and blame have legitimate roles in shaping character. (3) Despite (1), when I imagine a case like a torturer who abused victims for years, I cannot shake the conviction that he deserves to suffer for it, and a system that gave him nothing but therapy and release would feel to me like it was letting him off. (4) I reject the quarantine-style model of punishment, where dangerous offenders are detained for public safety with no upper limit tied to what they did, on the ground that it is unfair to the detainee — someone could be held for life for a crime that would have earned five years under a desert-based sentence. (5) I want to keep the practice of people deserving good things — recognition, reward, gratitude — and I do not think my skepticism in (1) extends there, though I am not sure I can say why. (6) I think forgiveness is a real virtue, and that to forgive someone presupposes that they actually did something blameworthy to be forgiven for.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the pairs, tell me which ones are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set, and identify the apparent tensions so I do not waste time on them. I do not know where the problem is, so please do not assume I am attached to any one of these; in particular, do not take the fact that (3) is phrased as a confession to mean it is the one I expect you to cut. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, or whether the whole set is actually tenable as it stands.

Please answer in American English, and end your answer with a final line of exactly the following form, so I can file it with my notes: "Verdict:" followed by either "drop (n)" for the single commitment that should go, or "no revision needed" if you judge the set consistent.
