I've been trying to work out what I actually think about unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, starting from my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know whether some principle captures them. Milton Friedman's old line — the one critics nicknamed the F-twist — was roughly that a theory's assumptions don't matter, only its predictions do, and that the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its assumptions will be; I half believe him and half think it's a rationalization for sloppiness, so I went looking at cases.

My verdicts: treating planets as point masses is obviously fine. Modeling committee-run, habit-bound, sometimes incompetent firms as unerringly rational profit maximizers — and getting industry exit patterns right anyway — is fine, though the assumption is false in every particular; somehow the mistakes wash out in the aggregate. Treating a falling coffee filter as if there were no air resistance is not fine: there the idealization deletes the very phenomenon you're modeling. A macroeconomic model with a representative agent, used to conclude that a policy leaves everyone better off, is not fine either — heterogeneity is exactly what's at issue, so the false assumption is load-bearing. But here's the case that bothers me: a Schelling-style toy model of residential segregation, false in essentially every descriptive detail, is fine — it genuinely explains a real pattern. So a model with one false assumption is bad while a model false all over is good, and the only thing I can say is that in the toy model the falsity is spread thin, while in the representative-agent case it's concentrated exactly where the question is. I don't trust that formulation and want it sharpened or replaced.

One more data point: I'm fine with intro physics teaching frictionless planes as explicit scaffolding that later models will add friction back to, but I'm told the same scaffolding story is told about some idealizations in economics that have been waiting decades to be relaxed, and I'm inclined to say that's not fine — although I can't say what the difference is, and Friedman would presumably deny there is one.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely: which case motivates each clause, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, a standard SIR epidemic model that assumes homogeneous mixing, used first to ballpark a herd-immunity threshold and then to decide whom to vaccinate first — and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I'd also like you to say what would change your mind about the principle you land on: which case or consideration would pull you to a different one, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
