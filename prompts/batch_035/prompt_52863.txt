I'm a genetic counselor at a fertility clinic that has begun offering embryo screening beyond single-gene tests — polygenic risk scores for diabetes, heart disease, depression, and more — and the volume of selection requests has outpaced our ad hoc way of handling them. Our clinical director has asked me to draft a decision procedure the counseling team can run on any request, and she wants it grounded in the principle of procreative beneficence: the claim that parents have at least a pro tanto moral reason, among the children they could have, to choose the one expected to have the best prospects for a flourishing life. Our staff disagree about whether that's a duty, a mere permission, or a pernicious idea, and the procedure has to be usable in a twenty-minute consult by nurses with no philosophy background, often under time pressure because decisions must be made before a transfer cycle. Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order (severity and age of onset of the condition, how informative the risk score actually is, whether the condition is treatable, how many healthy alternatives the parents have, whether the trait is identity-affecting — i.e., whether selecting against it means a different child or a better life for the same child), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends in a firm 'we don't advise that', and where it must hand the decision back to the parents because the question is one of values rather than clinical facts — and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: parents choosing against an embryo that will develop a fatal neurodegenerative disease in mid-life; parents asking to deselect embryos with elevated polygenic scores for depression despite having no family history; and a Deaf couple requesting selection for deafness so their child joins the Deaf community, which I suspect several of our counselors privately think the procedure should permit even though our director will hate that answer. Finally, say where the procedure misfires or underdetermines the answer — especially for polygenic scores, where 'best prospects' gets slippery — and what it cannot settle at all.
