For a philosophy of science seminar, I'd like a decision-tree handout that starts every student from the same place — they grant that for any finite body of evidence, it is possible in principle to construct multiple incompatible theories that all entail it — and walks them, branch by branch, to a named position in the underdetermination debate. The pedagogical point is that students should arrive at their position by answering concrete questions, not by picking a label they already like, so every branch node must be phrased as a question answerable without knowing where it leads.

Please include at least these branch questions (you may add others you think the logical space demands):

1. A question about real historical cases rather than the abstract possibility: are the empirically equivalent rivals that actually turn up in science genuine competitors, or merely notational or parasitic variants that quietly borrow the successful theory's content? Test this with the Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction versus special relativity as of 1905, and with Newton-plus-Vulcan versus general relativity on Mercury's perihelion — ask the student whether postulating an unobserved planet or a contraction of rulers is the same kind of thing as a genuinely distinct theory.
2. Whether the point of the argument is about confirmation (no evidence can confirm one member of an equivalent set over another) or about theory choice (the set may be broken by non-empirical means) — the student should confront this through a concrete question about whether a deliberately ad hoc patch, reverse-engineered to fit the data after the fact, should count as confirmed by the fit.
3. Whether simplicity, unification, and similar virtues are indicators of truth or merely pragmatic conveniences, tested with a curve-fitting case: infinitely many curves pass through any finite set of points, yet we prefer the simple one — is that a belief about the world or about us?
4. Whether the aim of science is truth about unobservables or only empirical adequacy.
5. Where the pessimistic induction enters, if you think it belongs on this tree at all — it is a different argument from underdetermination, and you should either give it a node with a justification or explain why it has no place here.

The leaves must include at least Duhemian holism, Quinean pragmatism, contrastive empiricism in the style of Laudan and Leplin, constructive empiricism, structural realism, entity realism, and the no-miracles scientific realist position. Build the tree from the questions first and then locate the positions in the space that results, rather than growing branches toward pre-chosen leaves; where the space contains an unoccupied combination — for example, someone who denies that underdetermination ever bites in actual science but accepts the pessimistic induction — note the empty corner and say in one sentence whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

Finally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate (structural realism and constructive empiricism may prove hard to split depending on your node choices; entity realism and no-miracles realism may likewise converge), and state what further question, applied at which point, would pull them apart. Keep theory-ladenness and Kuhnian incommensurability off the tree entirely — students in this seminar routinely conflate them with underdetermination, and the handout should make clear this tree is not about them.
