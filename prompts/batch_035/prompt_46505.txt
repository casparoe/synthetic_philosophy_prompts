so I do grantmaking-ish work for a small EA-adjacent funder that can put maybe $3M over three years toward insect welfare, and I'm trying to figure out where the marginal dollar actually goes. Everyone defaults to "more research on whether insects feel pain" but that seems way too coarse to me — "are insects sentient?" isn't one question, it's a bundle, and what to fund depends a lot on which sub-question we're even talking about.

Here's the framing I keep bumping into. The scale argument says: even if each insect has a low probability of sentience, there are something like 10^18 of them at any moment (plus vastly more coming if insect farming scales up for feed and protein), so under expected-value reasoning a small probability times a huge number dominates almost any other animal-welfare calculation. But the whole thing rests on assumptions nobody has really nailed down, and I want a prioritized list of the open questions, with reasoning about which are upstream of which and which are actually tractable.

Some candidates I've been kicking around, roughly in order of how confused I am about them:

1. Nociception vs valenced experience. Insects clearly have nociceptors and withdrawal reflexes; nobody serious disputes that. The open question is whether there's any principled account of when reflexive avoidance becomes "there being something it's like" to avoid it. Is that even a principled distinction, or a spectrum, and if it's a spectrum, does the expected-value framework handle it the way people assume — i.e., can you just multiply by a fractional "degree of sentience," or does that smuggle in stuff?

2. If we had to pick empirical markers to look for, which ones actually discriminate? Candidates people mention: trade-off behavior (an insect accepting a shock to reach food, and that trade-off shifting under analgesics), sustained grooming of an injury site, self-administration of painkillers, whether learning generalizes across contexts in a way suggesting an affective state rather than a fixed action pattern. Most of these have known confounds, and some could in principle be implemented with nothing it's like to be the insect. Which, if any, would move a reasonable person's probability estimate a lot, vs just generate ambiguous results we argue about for another decade?

3. The decision-theoretic layer. Suppose you end up at like a 5% probability that insects have morally significant experience. How does that interact with the rest of your moral uncertainty? Sub-issues: (a) whether "probability of full sentience, else zero" is even the right model, vs partial moral weight — some views give partial weight to partial sentience, others are threshold views where below a cutoff you get nothing; (b) whether threshold views deserve much weight given nobody has a worked-out one that doesn't look ad hoc; (c) whether, once you're already uncertain across moral views, adding another layer of uncertainty reorders your other priorities or just scales them all down equally; and (d) the annoying one — whether you should act on the EV calculation at all when the numbers are made-up enough that the calculation might just be laundering intuitions, and whether theres any principled way to decide when a probability estimate is too unstable to act on.

4. Even granting the scale argument, there's a prioritization question inside it: research vs interventions now. Insect farming is growing (black soldier fly larvae for feed, cricket protein), and there's an argument that shaping practices early — stocking densities, killing methods, whether the industry standard ends up being something like freeze-drying alive — is much higher-leverage than basic science that might take decades. But intervening before knowing whether the intervention target is even sentient has its own weirdness. How do you trade "act now while the industry is young" off against "acting blind"?

What I want from you: rank the open questions you think matter most for this funding decision, and defend the ranking. For each, say whether it's upstream of the others (in the sense that its answer changes what the other questions even mean), whether it's tractable for a team of empirical biologists plus some philosophers over ~3 years, and what would move it up or down the list. I care less about a survey of the literature and more about the dependency structure between the questions, since my guess is one or two of them are load-bearing for everything else and most of the rest, while interesting, wouldn't change the funding decision either way. Also tell me if you think my framing is off — e.g. if the nociception/experience distinction question is itself confused, or if the expected-value version of the scale argument has a problem I haven't listed.
