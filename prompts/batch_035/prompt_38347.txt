For the past few months I've been keeping a running argument going with my brother, and it started about as trivially as anything can: we were refilling my daughter's sandbox from bags of play sand, and he poured a little heap on the patio and asked when exactly it would count as a "pile." I've never studied philosophy and don't know the literature on this; I just find that my own answers, which I keep written down now, don't add up, and I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, lies behind them.

Here are the verdicts. A mound of ten thousand grains is obviously a pile; four grains scattered on the ground is obviously not. But when he sent me photos of mounds of 300, 500, and 900 grains, all shaped the same way, I found I couldn't answer at all — saying "that's a pile" felt wrong, and saying "that's not a pile" felt equally wrong, so I just said "kind of." If you ask me whether there's some specific number where n grains is a pile and n−1 isn't, that seems flatly false to me — no single grain makes the difference, ever. And yet I also won't accept either end of the argument you can build from that: I don't believe 3 grains is a pile, so I can't say it's a pile at every number, and I obviously can't say it's a pile at none. I seem to accept every step of "take one grain off a pile and it's still a pile" while refusing the conclusion that a pile survives all the way down. One more thing: he offered to bet me on whether the 500-grain photo was a pile, and I wouldn't take the bet in either direction — but when we needed a rule to decide who wins our sandbox game, I just picked 500 as the cutoff, and once I'd picked it, saying "this one counts" felt fine, even though I still don't think my picking it made anything true.

The part that really bothers me is a mismatch with a different case. Ask me whether someone with $1,000,000 is rich and I say yes without hesitation; $999,999 is also rich, no single dollar makes the difference, but $40,000 is not rich — the same pattern as the sand. Yet around $150,000 I catch myself saying "it depends on where they live," which never happens with the sand; a mound of 500 grains doesn't depend on anything. Worse, when I pressed myself on a person with $150,000 fixed in a specific city, I said there's a fact of the matter about whether they're rich, just a hard-to-put-your-finger-on one — whereas with the 500-grain photo I said the opposite: it's not that I don't know whether it's a pile, it's that there's nothing there to know. So I have a gap in one case, an unknown fact in the other, and no idea why the cases differ. Is there a single principle that captures all of this — the refusing-both-answers cases, the denial of any sharp cutoff, the context-dependence I feel with money but not sand, and my acceptance of every sorites step alongside my rejection of its conclusion? If one verdict has to go, which one, and what does the principle that remains predict about cases I haven't thought of — for instance, whether the boundary between pile and not-pile is itself blurry, or what I should say about whether a 64-year-old person is old?
