I am a novelist of some twenty years' standing — mostly literary fiction, with a fair amount of workshop teaching on the side — and I would like the Gould–Lewontin critique of adaptationism restated in the vocabulary of my trade as faithfully as it can be carried, followed by an honest residue report of what does not survive the translation. The vocabulary I mean is the one I use daily with students and editors: Chekhov's gun, the plant-and-payoff economy of a plot, backstory, texture, the distinction between what a detail does and what a detail merely is. I came to this oddly. At a writers' retreat last autumn, an evolutionary biologist friend described the spandrels paper over dinner, and I recognised at once a fight I have sat through in a hundred workshops: the reader — I have come to think of them as the paranoid reader — who treats every gun on the mantel as a plant that must pay off by page three hundred, and who assumes that in a well-made novel everything is there on purpose: every scar a loaded weapon, every surname chosen under selection. That reader, it seems to me, is the adaptationist. And I have long defended what I now suspect is the Gould position — that much of what the paranoid reader credits to design is a byproduct of the load-bearing structure of the book (a first-person narrator entails a delimited world; a locked-room plot entails a clockwork cast), and that the critic, like the adaptationist, spins a just-so story for each detail rather than asking whether the detail exists for any reason at all beyond its structural necessity.

I want this done honestly, not as a cute analogy. So restate the debate in my terms: translate the adaptationist programme, the just-so story, the spandrel, exaptation, and the notion of architectural or developmental constraint into the craft vocabulary above — plant, payoff, texture, structural entailment, and the gap between what I intended and what the book merely appears to intend. Where it helps, consider the difference between how I work as a discovery writer and how my wife works as an outliner: the finished book conceals which details were designed and which merely accumulated, and I have noticed that even I cannot always reconstruct, reading my own early drafts, which details were planted for a purpose and which simply survived the edit. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about: what in the biological debate has no expression in craft vocabulary at all, what had to be distorted to fit, and — here is the thing I most want your judgement on — whether the deepest disanalogy is that in my trade there really is an author, a genuine intentional designer, such that the paranoid reader is wrong about most books but right in principle in a way the adaptationist biologist never can be; and whether that fact makes this translation a cautionary one, telling me more about what my vocabulary cannot say about nature than about what it illuminates in it.

Please give the answer as exactly five numbered points — I would suggest roughly three of translation and two of residue, though you may distribute them as you judge best — each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and written in British English.
