I am a high school senior writing an extended essay for my school's philosophy prize on luck egalitarianism — the view that inequalities are unjust when they arise from brute luck but not when they result from choices the person can reasonably be held responsible for. The centerpiece of my essay is what I understand is called the harshness objection: that the theory, taken seriously, would refuse to aid people whose plight is the result of their own choices — the uninsured smoker with lung cancer, the gambler who wagered the house — and that these cold verdicts are a reductio of the theory rather than a policy anyone should accept. My supervisor has suggested that instead of merely defending one reply, I should compare the main ways a luck egalitarian might respond. To structure the essay, I need a considered ranking.

Please sort the following five replies into tiers according to how well each defuses the objection — top tier for replies that genuinely dissolve it, middle tiers for those that blunt it at a cost, bottom tier for those that make the position worse — and give a short justification for each placement, including the main price the theory pays for adopting that reply:

1. The two-tier view: guarantee everyone a sufficientarian floor (food, shelter, basic healthcare) regardless of their choices, and apply luck-egalitarian redistribution only to inequalities above that floor.

2. Choice-skepticism: argue that the smoker's or gambler's choices were not genuinely voluntary — addiction, deprived formative circumstances, or adaptive preferences undermine responsibility — so most apparent choice cases are actually brute luck cases in disguise.

3. Biting the bullet: concede that in these cases aid is not owed as a matter of justice, though it may still be owed as a matter of compassion or humanitarian duty — justice and beneficence simply come apart.

4. Converting to prioritarianism: give priority to helping people in proportion to how badly off they are, and stop asking how they got there.

5. Abandoning the distributive view for relational equality: what matters is that people stand in relations of equality — free from domination and hierarchy — which secures everything the luck egalitarian wanted without the harsh verdicts.
