I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I serve on the clinical ethics committee of our university's teaching hospital. A revised anesthesia protocol has deadlocked us. It would add a prophylactic step that, on our own statistician's estimate, would prevent the two or three catastrophic cases of intraoperative awareness we see each year, at the cost of some hours of moderate post-operative nausea for roughly one patient in five — about 16,000 mildly miserable recoveries across our 80,000 annual procedures. One faction holds that a certain minor harm imposed on many cannot be set against the prevention of grave harm to a few, and proposes a bylaw forbidding exactly that trade; our statistician replies that every cost-effectiveness analysis we already rely on aggregates across persons, that refusing to do so here is arbitrary, and that any de minimis threshold the other side proposes will simply be gamed at the margin. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: a clear recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions such as a consent disclosure or a compensating comfort measure — with reasoning the losing faction could recognize as honest, plus a proposed general rule (if you think we should adopt one) for future cases in which a certain, widely distributed minor harm is traded against a rare, concentrated, grave one.
