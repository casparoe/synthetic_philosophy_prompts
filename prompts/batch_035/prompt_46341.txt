My advisor is a vision scientist who thinks the mind reaches into perception all the time — desire making desserts look closer or more vivid, expertise letting chess masters literally see the board differently, language warping color categories, that kind of thing. Another professor in the department, more of a cognitive-science-methodology person, thinks basically every claimed top-down effect is really judgment, attention, or memory wearing perception's clothes, and she co-authored a long takedown of that whole literature a few years back that my advisor still grumbles about at lunch. After years of talking past each other they've finally agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration and — because I made the mistake of seeming organised — I've been asked to draft the joint statement they'd both sign, or, if they can't sign it, to at least make explicit exactly where and why they can't.

Draft that statement for me. Start with the claims both could put their names to — I'd expect several to be methodological, like what counts as a genuine behavioral test of perception rather than of responses about it, but also maybe about what's philosophically at stake, e.g. whether penetrable perception would lose some of its justificatory role or whether that worry is overblown. Then the disagreements, each phrased so its own side accepts the wording of its own view. My guess is a lot of it comes down to a criterion problem: what would even count as showing a cognitive effect on perceptual experience rather than on judgment, memory, or spatial attention, and whether the perception/cognition line is a real joint in nature or a category we impose. Then the cruxes — for each one, name the kind of finding or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each sides prediction written down in advance so it's on record. If part of their dispute strikes you as verbal — two people using "perception" to pick out different stages of processing — say which part and why, since both of them would rather know that than keep fighting about a word.

Two notes on how to write it. First, keep it in the weeds: they both know the modularity debate, the El Greco fallacy, the standard examples and counterexamples on both sides, so don't re-explain the basics, just use them and move. Second, and this is non-negotiable since the thing has to read like an actual signed document: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — claims can run as "first, ... second, ..." inside paragraphs, the way you'd write a long letter.
