I've spent years reading ethics arguments online, and one thing that almost never gets stress-tested is the popular caricature that retributivism is the harsh theory of punishment while consequentialism is the humane, reform-minded one. Build me a thought experiment that reverses it: a case in which the retributivist must recommend the lighter sentence and the consequentialist the harsher one. Constraints: a realistic sentencing decision in an ordinary courtroom, not a dystopia; the scenario itself no more than one paragraph; and no stipulating the verdicts — both recommendations must be generated by the theories from facts stated in the case, which means no assuming that deterrence works perfectly or that the offender is certain to reoffend. Plausible raw material would be salience effects on general deterrence (a trivial offense that happens to be massively publicized, so the expected deterrence gain from severity is large even though desert is small) or incapacitation of a persistent petty offender; but whichever you choose, the case must confront the obvious retributivist escape — that desert can be counted cumulatively over a whole criminal record rather than offense by offense — and close it off rather than ignore it. After the scenario, walk through what each theory recommends and why, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest consequentialist counterattack: can she recover the light sentence without smuggling in a proportionality side-constraint, and if she can't, is the resulting view still consequentialism about punishment, or a quiet capitulation to retributivism?
