My roommate (econ PhD student, let's call her Priya) and I (philosophy MA) have spent basically two years arguing about the common prior assumption, and I think it's time we cashed out what we've learned into the document we should have written all along. She's the defender: roughly, she thinks that if two ideally rational agents with common knowledge of each other's opinions still disagree, something has gone wrong — differing priors are like differing code, a bug to be debugged, and the Harsanyi-style thought is that once you trace any disagreement back through the evidence, you either hit common knowledge of different information or you hit a difference in priors that itself should be treated as a common-knowledge event to be modeled with a prior over priors, so the assumption keeps reasserting itself at every level. I'm the skeptic: priors are just where evidence runs out, two agents can start with different credences without either being irrational, and the agreement theorem is conditional on an assumption that does all the work — it tells you what follows IF you have a common prior, not that you must have one. And "you should distrust anyone whose prior differs from yours" seems to prove too much: it would make disagreement with a stranger a black mark on at least one of you, which feels absurd.

Draft the joint statement of our adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) the claims we can both sign — and I want them to include the math we both accept, e.g. about the agreement theorem itself and its scope, the no-trade-style results, what happens when information is genuinely asymmetric, and the boring fact that ordinary humans disagree for plenty of non-mysterious reasons; (2) the precise statements of where we still part ways, each phrased so the person holding that view would accept the wording (Priya should get to state "differing priors among idealized rational agents are a rationality failure" in words she'd sign, and I should get my side in words I'd sign); (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — and for each crux a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment that we BOTH agree bears on it, with our predictions written down in advance so we can't wiggle out later. One crux I suspect is whether there's any principled, non-question-begging procedure for setting "ur-priors" (indifference over which partitions? max entropy subject to which constraints?); another might be whether cases involving self-locating uncertainty — two agents who share all the third-person evidence but assign different credences because of when/where they find themselves — are counterexamples to the spirit of the assumption or just outside it's scope. Priya has a regress argument I half-buy (any common-knowledge prior disagreement can be re-modeled with a common meta-prior, so the assumption is sort of unfalsifiable), and my standing response is that this just pushes the mystery up a level without saying where the meta-prior's mass comes from.
