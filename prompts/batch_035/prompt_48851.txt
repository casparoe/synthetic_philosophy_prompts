Many religious communities make conversion deliberately costly: a year or more of supervised study, visible changes in dress and diet, sometimes a formal renunciation of one's prior life, all before full membership is granted. Lifelong members, by contrast, never have to demonstrate anything at all — they were born into the faith, and typically remain members in good standing however lukewarm their observance or belief. What does this asymmetry presuppose about belief and commitment? On one natural reconstruction the practice treats costly observance as a signal of sincerity: someone who pays a steep, non-refundable price up front must genuinely intend to keep the faith, exactly as the handicap principle says an honest signal must be costly to fake. But this reconstruction presupposes that sincere commitment cannot be faked cheaply, which looks shaky when the community tolerates born members who neither observe nor believe. There is also an alternative reading on which the costs are not screening devices but commitment-forming ones: the practice presupposes that paying the price creates the belief rather than merely revealing it. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind conversion requirements as concretely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; explain whether it genuinely needs honest-signaling logic or whether several theories of costly commitment would license it; and note where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — for instance, whether the two readings would give different verdicts on admitting a convert who pays every cost and observes flawlessly but reports no inner conviction.
