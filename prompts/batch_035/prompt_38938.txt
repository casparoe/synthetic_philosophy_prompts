I'm a master's student in political theory writing my term paper on the boundary problem: the question of who is entitled to belong to the demos entitled to make a collective decision, which arguably has to be settled before we can even evaluate how democratic a decision procedure is. My draft defends a somewhat unusual reformulation of the problem, and I'd like your comments before I revise. Here is the current draft:

"The standard answers to the boundary problem are the all-affected principle — everyone whose interests are affected by a decision is entitled to participate in making it — and the all-subjected principle — everyone subject to a regime's coercive authority is entitled to share in that authority. Each fails in a familiar way. All-affected is unworkably expansive: nearly every decision ripples outward indefinitely, and on a strict reading it makes the demos decision-relative, so there is no stable demos at all. All-subjected avoids this but inherits the state-centric frame that produced the problem: it leaves out those subject to power that is not formally coercive — undocumented residents, the structurally dominated, those governed by firms and platforms whose rules effectively set the terms of their conduct — and it says nothing about who draws the line around the subjecting authority in the first place. My proposal is that both principles fail for a shared reason: they treat the demos as a single, pre-political entity whose membership must be fixed in advance. We should instead say that subjection to enforced norm-giving generates a claim to co-authorship of those norms wherever it occurs — in the state, but also in the workplace, the professional association, and the platform that moderates one's speech. The boundary question is thus transformed: not 'who belongs to the demos,' but 'which demoi do you belong to,' answered functionally. You belong to every association whose enforceable rules govern your conduct, and to none other. Call this the plural-demos view. Its payoff is that the inclusion of immigrants becomes a matter of degree — one belongs more fully to a demos as one's life is more thoroughly governed by its rules — and that platform governance, corporate power, and global coordination problems become boundary problems of the same kind as the territorial state's, rather than awkward afterthoughts."

What I'd like from you: first, an assessment of whether my treatment of all-affected and all-subjected is fair or whether I'm attacking strawmen; second, and most importantly, your view on whether the plural-demos move actually dissolves the boundary problem or merely relocates it — my worry is that drawing the line around "enforceable rules governing your conduct" is nearly as hard as drawing the original boundary, especially for power that operates through exit options and background constraints rather than enforced rules. Third, tell me what you take to be the strongest objection to the draft that I have not anticipated, and finally, given that I have roughly a week and a page limit, give me a prioritized list of revisions — what to fix, cut, or expand, in order of importance to making the paper work.
