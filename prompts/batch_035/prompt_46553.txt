I help run a small philosophy-reading group that meets every other Thursday at our local community center. None of us has any academic background in philosophy — the members are a retired nurse, a schoolteacher, a machinist, and a former software engineer named Bernard — and I took on the job of organizing the sessions because I read the most and am the one with the free evenings. We have just finished a unit on machine minds, built around Turing's 1950 paper in Mind and a long discussion of Searle's Chinese Room argument from his 1980 paper. At the end of each unit I set a take-home question, the members submit a short written answer, and — partly because they asked for it to be taken seriously — I committed to returning each one with a grade out of a hundred plus written comments, as a proper assignment would get. My own philosophical education amounts to whatever I could teach myself, so I want to make sure my feedback on this one is fair, precise, and catches everything, including things a casual reader would let slide.

The question I set was as follows: Turing proposed replacing the question of whether machines can think with his imitation game. State precisely what Turing intended the test to establish — and be careful here: did he mean it as a definition of thinking, as evidence for it, or as something else? Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to the test's use for this purpose, and evaluate whether that objection succeeds against what Turing actually claimed, rather than against a stronger claim he never made.

Bernard's submitted answer reads as follows:

Turing opened his 1950 paper by admitting that the words machine and think are too vague to be settled directly, so he swapped the question for an operational one: can a machine play the imitation game well enough that an interrogator, conversing with it and a human through text alone, mistakes it for the human as often as the game's ordinary players fool each other? The judge never sees or hears either respondent; the machine's whole job is to be taken for a person at the typewriter. And Turing was explicit about what he meant by this. For him the test is not merely evidence of thinking — it is the definition. To think just is to produce the right sort of verbal behavior, and a machine that passes the test thinks in exactly the sense in which you and I do; there is nothing further to ask, just as there is nothing further to ask about the people around us, whose minds I also only ever know through their behavior. Turing even predicted that by the end of the century machines would pass, and that once they had, nobody would any longer consider the phrase machine thinking an absurdity — which confirms that he regarded passing as constitutive of thinking, not merely suggestive of it.

The strongest objection is Searle's Chinese Room. Searle asks us to picture a man sealed in a room with an enormous rulebook. Chinese characters come in through a slot; the man, who understands no Chinese at all, follows the rules and sends other characters back out; and to anyone outside, the room is a fluent conversationalist in Chinese. Searle's point was that no machine can actually pass this way — symbol shuffling will always give itself away sooner or later, because syntax can never generate semantics, and semantics is what the interrogator in the test is really reaching for.

Turing, however, anticipated this objection. His rejoinder is the one now known as the systems reply: the man in the room does not understand Chinese, but the whole system — man, rulebook, room together — does, and it is the system, not the man, that passes the test. Searle found this reply absurd, but it shows Turing had an answer ready in advance, so the objection does not defeat him.

Finally, there is a deeper limitation that no reply can meet. I can never observe any mind other than my own. Whatever the machine does at the typewriter, I cannot see its thinking any more than I can see yours — the test gives me nothing, and we are back exactly where we started, with the word think just as undefined as it was before Turing. Still, as an engineering target the test has aged remarkably well, and in practice the industry has organized itself around it.

Please give Bernard a grade out of a hundred, along with the specific comments that justify it. I would like the comments organized point by point, tied to particular passages of his answer, so that he can see exactly where he was right, exactly where marks were lost and why, and which of the missteps matter most for the grade — he takes this seriously, and I want the feedback to be instructive rather than a general impression.
