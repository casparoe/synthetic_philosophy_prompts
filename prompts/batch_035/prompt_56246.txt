Two of my coursemates, Sophie and Tom, got into it at the pub after our behavioural economics seminar, and I honestly couldn't follow who was winning. The lecturer had shown us the classic Allais choices. Situation 1: pick between (A) £1m for sure, and (B) £1m with 89% probability, nothing with 1%, £5m with 10%. Situation 2: pick between (C) £1m with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, and (D) £5m with 10% probability and nothing otherwise. Basically the whole seminar, me included, picked A over B and D over C. Then it kicked off.

(1) Tom said that pattern is flatly irrational. Strip out the 89% that's common to both options within each pair and the same comparison is left over in both situations: £1m for sure, versus a lottery that's 10/11 £5m and 1/11 nothing. Preferring A to B but D to C means answering that one comparison differently depending on an irrelevant background — the independence axiom says the shared part shouldn't matter, and flipping on it is like ranking x above y and y above x.

(2) Sophie said nothing was flipped; the axiom is just wrong as a standard. The certainty in situation 1 is worth something in itself — nobody offered a guaranteed life-changing sum gambles it away on a 1% chance of ending with nothing — and the independence axiom was, she said, reverse-engineered to make expected utility come out tidy, so citing it against her just begs the question. That's basically what Allais himself said at the time, apparently.

(3) Tom pushed back: if certainty has value over and above the outcomes themselves, then say where it lives. If it's lexical — certainty wins no matter the odds — you'll refuse staggeringly favourable bets. If it's not lexical, then either it's absorbable into the utilities (which cant reproduce our choices anyway, since expected utility obeys independence by construction), or you need a full non-EU story like probability weighting, and he'd want to hear which one she's signing up for, because each of those costs you something elsewhere.

(4) Sophie answered that Tom is demanding a replacement theory as the price of counting as an anomaly, which isnt how evidence works — you don't need correct physics in hand to know your hypothesis just failed a test. And, she added, the case for independence being normative at all rests on pump-style arguments that don't really bite here the way they do for transitivity: there's no actual mechanism out there that turns our pattern of choices into a guaranteed loss. So his axiom's "rationality" is just another intuition, and hers is at least as good.

Please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the four from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence of justification each — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And if you think I've set something up confusedly somewhere, or that neither of them is 'right' in the way my question presupposes, say that plainly instead of splitting it down the middle to be polite. I'd rather be corrected than soothed.
