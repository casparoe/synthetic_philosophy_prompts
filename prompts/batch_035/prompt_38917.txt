I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, and after two sessions on dreams I've realized my students can recite the dialectic without feeling it. They've read Norman Malcolm's Dreaming, with its claim that the very grammar of our concepts ties dreaming to the waking report, so that it makes no sense to ask what, if anything, was going on in the sleeper at the time, and they've read Daniel Dennett's 1976 reply, with its alarm-clock scenario and the cassette and composition theories, on which the apparent dream experience is composed and loaded into memory at waking, or never consciously undergone at all. On paper they understand that however long, coherent, and vivid a dream report is, nothing available to the dreamer himself distinguishes an experience that unfolded in real time from one assembled afterwards. But they don't believe it in their bones, partly because they take dream reports to be unusually privileged first-person data, and partly because the philosophical literature never lets them touch the phenomenon.

For our next meeting — twelve students, ninety minutes, a seminar room with a whiteboard and a corridor outside — I want an activity that stages this underdetermination rather than asserting it. My rough idea, which you should repair, refine, or replace: split the class so that half undergo a brief staged experience of a few minutes (say, an odd little scene I perform or a piece of music with a striking ending) while the other half, elsewhere, are given cue cards with the same raw ingredients and a few minutes to compose a plausible first-person report of an equivalent experience; then reunite the group, have the reports read aloud by a neutral party so delivery cues don't leak the answer, and have everyone — including the presenters, and crucially the composers themselves, asked about felt duration and the order of events — try to sort genuine from composed reports. If there's a way to interleave a delay so that the composers are eventually answering questions about an experience they know they invented, from memory of their own report rather than the composition act, I want that too, even if it means a few minutes of the activity spilling into our next session.

Please give me the full design: rules, materials, timing, what the cue cards should contain so the two pools of reports are stylistically comparable, a light scoring scheme that doesn't require real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific pieces of the Malcolm-Dennett exchange — for instance, where the judges' inevitable failures correspond to the underdetermination of dream reports, and where a composer's sincere answer about how long it felt corresponds to the composition theory. I also want an honest section on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I anticipate students treating it as lie detection, and composed reports being suspiciously polished), how to handle each failure, and which feature of the actual dream case the activity cannot reproduce no matter what I do, such as the fact that in the real case the composer knows he composed, so the first-person symmetry Dennett needs is only approximated, not achieved. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — I want to read it the way I'd read a long letter from a colleague.
