The exam question was:

"Reconstruct Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against the possibility of true moral responsibility. Make sure you explain why Strawson thinks the argument works regardless of whether determinism is true. Then present and assess one objection to the argument."

And here's the answer from one of my students (transcribed as written, typos and all):

"Strawson's Basic Argument starts from the principle that nothing can be causa sui, nothing can be the cause of itself, at least in the mental respects that matter for responsibility. To be truly morally responsible for an action you would have to be responsible for the way you are — your character, desires, values — insofar as those led to the action. But to be responsible for the way you are, you'd have to have somehow chosen it, and to be responsible for that choice you'd already have to have been responsible for the way you were when you made it. This generates a regress that could only be closed if you were ultimately self-created in the relevant respects, which is impossible. Hence true moral responsibility is impossible. Strawson develops all this at length in Freedom and Resentment, his main paper on the topic.

Importantly, the argument doesn't hang on determinism being true. It's really just the standard dilemma argument: every action flows from your character, and your character is either fixed by causes you didn't control or the product of chance, and neither option grounds responsibility. That's why Strawson isn't a hard determinist — even if quantum randomness seeps into our psychology, we're no more responsible for the random component than for the determined one, so indeterminism doesn't help either.

As for an objection: the Frankfurt-style hierarchical view says you're responsible when your second-order desires endorse the first-order ones you act on. You don't need to be responsible for the desires themselves, just for the endorsement. Strawson's obvious reply is that you're no more responsible for your second-order desires than for your first-order ones, so the regress just reappears one level up. I find this reply convincing — Frankfurt just moves the bump under the rug. So I side with Strawson: nobody is truly morally responsible, though we can still be responsible for things in the ordinary everyday sense that Strawson is happy to keep around."
