My roommate thinks she's derived dialetheism from the weather report, and I can't find the exact spot where it breaks. Her argument: (1) The regional weather service publishes about 1,000 forecasts a year, each stated with 99.9% confidence, and their multi-decade calibration data backs that number up. (2) Given that track record, it's rational for the service to accept each individual forecast. (3) Rational acceptance is closed under conjunction — if you accept p and accept q, you've thereby accepted p-and-q. (4) So it's rational to accept the giant conjunction of all 1,000 forecasts. (5) But the very same calibration data tells you to expect about one miss per 1,000, so it's equally rational to accept that at least one forecast went wrong. (6) Now the move she's proud of: if rationality lets you accept both the big conjunction and its negation, then 'never knowingly accept an inconsistency' can't be a genuine requirement of rationality. (7) And if inconsistent sets can be rationally accepted, there's no principled difference between an inconsistent set and a single accepted contradiction — so the service could rationally accept 'forecast #412 will hold and forecast #412 will fail'. (8) Acceptance aims at truth, so some contradictions are true. I can see this is basically the lottery and preface paradoxes stapled together, but she's pushed it past where the textbooks stop, and I don't want to just dig in my heels at (8). I suspect the cheat is somewhere between (6) and (7) but I can't name it. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to begin with, and what denying it costs — and if you think the real problem is earlier, at (3) or (4), I want to hear that too.
