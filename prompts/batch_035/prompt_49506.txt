I'm in a reading group working through genocide law and scholarship, and we've hit a wall that I'd like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. One side wants the definition kept tight: genocide means physically destroying a group, with the specific intent to destroy the group as such, and stretching the word to cover cultural destruction, forced assimilation, or wrecking a group's institutions cheapens it and makes the legal instrument unusable. The other side says the tight definition is an artifact of horse-trading at the 1948 convention, that Lemkin himself — who basically invented the concept — understood genocide to include cultural, political, and economic destruction, and that sticking to the narrow reading lets obvious evils escape the name: ethnic cleansing where you expel rather than kill, removing children from a community, killing a language. This argument has been running for seventy-plus years and neither side has budged an inch. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal — two camps who might agree completely on the facts and even on what should be done, fighting only about who gets custody of a word? Or does the word do real work, so the fight is substantive in disguise: 'genocide' triggers duties to prevent and punish that 'crimes against humanity' or 'persecution' don't, so the definitional dispute is a proxy fight over institutional consequences, and treating it as merely verbal would be naive? Or is there a deeper moral disagreement lurking about what the worst thing you can do to a people even is — killing the members versus killing the group as a group — that keeps the word-fight alive? My own hunch is the second one, that it's really about the consequences of successfully applying the label, but I'm not confident. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress: a new term for cultural destruction that takes the pressure off, an international court settling the interpretation, a conceptual distinction nobody has drawn yet — or nothing, because the fight is doing political work that no argument could settle?
