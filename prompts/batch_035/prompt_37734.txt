So I finally got around to reading Dennett's "Where Am I?" (the one in Brainstorms), and I think I've figured out the moral of the story, but it doesn't match what people usually say about it, so let me run it by you. The setup, as I understand it: his brain (Yorick) gets taken out and kept alive in a vat because the mission — going down into a tunnel to deal with some radioactive warheads — would destroy a brain but not a body, so his body (Hamlet) gets sent down the tunnel by remote control. Now here's my reading: during the mission, his body went into the tunnel, but HE didn't — he stayed back in the lab with Yorick. So the essay basically demonstrates that the self sits wherever the brain sits, and all the later stuff (the reveal that Yorick had been swapped out for a duplicate at some point without him noticing, etc.) is just fun sci-fi decoration Dennett added so the essay wouldnt read like a dry lecture. If I'm right, then personal identity looks pretty much definately settled: you are your brain, full stop. Which would also make any mind-uploading scheme that scans your brain and then destroys the original straightforwardly a way of killing you. So my question — if this was all established back in the 70s, why do philosophers keep acting like "where is the self / what survives" is still an open question? Is the field just slow to accept its own conclusions, or am I missing what the story is actually supposed to show? (asking because me and a friend are arguing about whether uploading would be survival or death, and I want to know if I can count Dennett as being on my side)
