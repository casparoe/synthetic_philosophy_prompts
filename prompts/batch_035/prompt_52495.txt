I am preparing a diagnostic worksheet for the closing section of a graduate seminar on naturalizing mental content. The students have read the canonical theories, but in discussion they self-identify by label rather than by commitment: someone will say they are "a Millikan person" and then, two cases later, give the answer a Fodor person gives. So instead of another prose comparison, I want a decision tree that sorts a respondent by what they say about cases, and I want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: choose the branch questions first, then locate named positions as leaves. Each branch question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the theories, and phrased so the respondent cannot tell which leaf an answer leads to.

The branch points should come from the cases below; you may add at most one further case of your own if the space needs it.

1. The frog. A frog snaps at small dark blobs passing overhead. In its lineage, most such blobs were flies; some were sinking pebbles or pellets. Today it snaps at a pellet. Is that a false belief, a malfunction, both, or neither?
2. The swamp-creature. Lightning assembles a swamp into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog, with no evolutionary history whatsoever. When its internal state causes snapping at a fly, does that state represent flies, or does the creature merely track flies without content in the strict sense?
3. The co-opted trait. A structure was selected for one job and only later, having already been doing a second job incidentally, was selected for that second job (feathers: first insulation, later flight). When it now performs the second job, is that its function operating normally, or a side-effect?
4. The learning case. Within one organism's lifetime, an internal state is reinforced because tokens of that type preceded food; the organism now has that state in the absence of food. Does the state mean food in virtue of that reinforcement history — can a lifetime of selection stand in for a lineage of selection?
5. The novel environment. A detector was selected, in one environment, to respond to a cue that reliably tracked something significant there. Transplanted where the cue tracks nothing, it fires. Is this misrepresentation even though the device is functioning perfectly by its etiology, and does the content get fixed by the selecting environment or the current one?
6. The twin histories. Two creatures with the same internal structure and current causal role but different selection histories — one selected for responding to X, the other for responding to Y, both now responding to X. Can they differ in what their states are about?

The finished tree should have between five and nine leaves, and must include, at minimum: a causal-informational view of the Dretske style; a proper-function or producer-consumer view of the Millikan style; a selected-effect view of the Neander style; an asymmetric-dependence or covariation view of the Fodor style; a deflationary leaf on which ascription of content is only instrumental; and at least one leaf labeled "unnamed" occupying a live combination nobody defends. Where I attach a name, treat it as marking a family; if any pairing is off by the standards of the literature, correct it rather than silently adopting it.

Please organize the response into the following sections, in this order:

1. The branch questions (about 250 words) — listed in the order a respondent meets them, each with one sentence on which positions it pulls apart.
2. The tree (about 150 words) — as an indented outline with the branch questions as node labels.
3. The leaves (about 250 words) — each named position placed at its leaf, with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
4. Unoccupied combinations (about 150 words) — answer patterns no leaf captures, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
5. Inseparable pairs (about 100 words) — pairs of positions the tree cannot tell apart, stated plainly rather than papered over.
6. Settled versus contested (about 200 words) — separate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested (the swamp-creature, whether learning substitutes for selection, and which environment fixes content are likely candidates), and do not present a contested view as the consensus.

Please write no citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; the worksheet has to stand on its own.
