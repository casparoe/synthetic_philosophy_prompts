I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and for the last four years I've helped run a LessWrong-adjacent meetup in my city — 14 to 18 regulars, mostly software engineers, a couple of neuroscientists, all comfortable with the standard canon of thought experiments. For our next session (two hours, a rented room with movable tables), I want to tackle computationalism: whether the brain is a computer, and specifically whether implementing the right computation is sufficient for understanding, or whether there's a gap between executing a symbol-manipulating process and grasping what the symbols mean.

Rather than have us read papers and argue in a circle, I want an activity where the participants themselves implement a system that appears to understand something, while each of them can honestly report they understood nothing. My half-formed idea is a staged, room-scale version of the Chinese Room: each participant receives a card holding one fragment of a rulebook or lookup table, a visitor outside the room submits questions as slips of paper, and the room's collective output — passed hand to hand, card by card — should be indistinguishable from an intelligent respondent, even though no individual ever sees more than their own fragment. Design this so it actually runs: what exactly goes on the cards, how the visitor's script is built so the room's answers come out looking smart, what rehearsal or seed knowledge has to be smuggled in beforehand, and how to keep the whole thing inside 80 minutes including the debrief.

One twist I want your honest evaluation of, because I suspect my design instinct is wrong here: a second round in which each participant is allowed to memorize and internalize their fragment until they can execute it without the card. The idea is to ask whether the room got any closer to understanding once the lookup table lives in heads rather than on paper — my intuition is that this is meant to pressure the difference between simulating a process and instantiating it, the way a weather simulation doesn't get anyone wet. But I'm not convinced the twist actually does that work rather than just changing the mood. If it fails, tell me it fails and propose what would work better, or say plainly that no variant can do the job.

Deliverables I need from you:

1. The full game: premise as presented to participants, rules, roles (including the visitor and what they know), materials, and the exact workflow by which a question slip becomes an answer slip.
2. A timeline table with these columns: minutes, phase, what participants do, what the facilitator does, and the conceptual work that phase is quietly doing.
3. A debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the live positions — the systems reply, the robot reply, the brain-simulator reply, the other-minds rejoinder — without lecturing, ideally by replaying one or two actual slips of paper from the session.
4. A frank section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (cards that bottleneck, participants peeking at the whole system, the visitor's script being too easy), with fixes.
5. An honest note on which feature of the computationalism debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself at the end.

After the design itself, please append a structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: "mechanic" (a string explaining how the room computes without anyone understanding); "aha_moments" (an array of objects, each with "expected_moment" and the "concept" it is meant to trigger, described as where participants should feel it); "twist_verdict" (an object with boolean "works", string "reason", and optional string "salvage" describing a fixed version or what to do instead); "failure_modes" (an array of objects with "what_goes_wrong" and "fix"); and "cannot_reproduce" (a string naming the one thing the activity structurally cannot show and why).
