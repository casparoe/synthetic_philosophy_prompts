Background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a recently retired physical chemist -- forty years in a spectroscopy lab -- and now that I have time on my hands I've been working through philosophy books, mostly badly. The latest one is a popular neuroscience book (I'll leave it unnamed, no point embarrassing anyone) that devotes a chapter to Molyneux's problem. I understood the problem well enough going in: Molyneux asked Locke, back around 1690, whether a man born blind who suddenly regains his sight could, by sight alone and before touching anything, tell a cube from a sphere, given that he already knows them perfectly well by touch.

But then the book wraps up the whole three-century story with this paragraph, and something about it smelled off to me:

"Locke answered no: since all our ideas come to us through experience, the blind man's tactile idea of a sphere and the visual appearance of a sphere are two entirely separate things, and only experience can teach him they go together. Leibniz answered yes: the mind possesses an innate geometry, so the newly sighted man could work out which shape was which. The dispute stood unresolved until 2011, when surgeons in India restored sight to children who had been blind from cataracts since birth and tested them within a day or two of the operation. The children scored barely better than a coin flip at matching what they felt to what they saw. So Locke was vindicated, and the mind has no built-in geometry after all."

Now, here's my problem. I've run enough experiments in my life to know that "barely better than a coin flip" two days after surgery is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and I also dimly remember that there was some famous 18th-century patient, Cheselden's boy I think, whose post-surgery reports everyone treats as anecdote and dismisses. But I'm honestly not in a position to know whether the modern study escapes the problems that sank the old case reports, or whether the philosophical conclusion follows even if the result is solid. Is this a fair summary?

Please go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a careful vision scientist sign off on, which parts are simplifications a philosopher would grumble about, and which parts are just wrong or overreach. I'm particularly interested in (1) whether it's accurate to say Leibnix -- sorry, Leibniz -- predicted the newly sighted man would succeed right away, or whether the book is flattening a more qualified position; (2) whether testing children a day or two after cataract removal actually answers the original question, given everything that could still be wrong with a freshly operated eye and a visually immature brain; and (3) whether "Locke was vindicated" and "the mind has no built-in geometry" follow from the finding even if the finding is rock solid. My instinct is that the experiment is fine and the philosophy gloss is where it goes off the rails, but I've been wrong before, and I'd like to know exactly what the negative result does and doesn't rule out.

One small request: please answer in American English (gray, analyze, etc.) -- old habits.
