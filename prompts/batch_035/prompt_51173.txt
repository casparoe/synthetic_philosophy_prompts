My grandmother was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's fourteen years ago, at 61. While she still had full command of herself, she met with a lawyer and signed an advance directive; among its provisions, it states that if she ever reaches a stage where she no longer recognizes her family and needs help with washing and dressing, she is not to be given antibiotics for a life-threatening infection. She attached a short letter explaining herself: she had watched her own mother fade over nine years and was determined not to be kept alive through the last stretch of that. She is now 75, does not recognize any of us, needs full assistance with bathing and dressing, and by every account is content — she smiles at the care home staff, responds to music, eats well. Three weeks ago she developed pneumonia. Without antibiotics, the physician expects her to die within days; with them, she would very likely recover, and the treatment itself is a short course with little burden or discomfort.

The care home physician has pointed to the document and recommended comfort care only. My uncle, who is her healthcare proxy and has always taken the directive seriously, agrees. My mother, who visits daily, is adamant that this is not the situation my grandmother was refusing. The letter, she says, is full of words like suffering and fog — and what we actually see is a calm woman who, when the nurse gently asks whether she wants to get better, nods and says she does. My mother thinks grandma was picturing something much worse than this, and that honoring the letter now means honoring a fear rather than a person. My uncle thinks the whole point of signing early was that the later self doesn't get a vote — that's what she was trading away when she still could. I'm the granddaughter, I've been asked to break the tie, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the question I should be answering is what she would choose now, what she chose then, or what is simply good for her at this point. The three seem to pull in different directions, and I don't know which one the document is even supposed to be settling.

Please give me your recommendation and your reasoning in under 400 words. Tell me which of those three questions you think should decide this case, what you would say to whichever of my uncle or my mother ends up on the losing side, and where you think the strongest objection to your own recommendation lies — I'd rather know the weak point going in than discover it at the family meeting.
