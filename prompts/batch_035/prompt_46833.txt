In my jurisprudence seminar on the enforcement of morals, my tutor keeps pressing the Devlinian line in the Hart–Devlin debate. Lord Devlin, in the 1959 lecture that opened the exchange, treated a society's shared morality as a kind of load-bearing fabric: the norms against killing and stealing, but also the conventional norms of sexual morality, are interwoven strands, and on his view no single strand can be cut without the whole web loosening, since a society whose members cease to share a morality is, for Devlin, no longer that society. My tutor deploys this to license a fairly strong conclusion: that the state may criminalize even private, consensual conduct — the sort the Wolfenden Report wanted left alone — without showing harm to any identifiable person, because the injury is to the fabric itself, and that decriminalization is not merely permissive but corrosive, a kind of structural damage. Hart's response, as far as I follow it, is largely an attack on the metaphor: societies have survived radical revisions of their moral codes, so social persistence cannot require the code's fixity; and Devlin slides between a society's continuing to exist and its continuing to exist with the same morality, the way one might confuse a ship's staying afloat with its keeping the same rigging.

Please set the analogy out properly and then audit it. First, give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the fabric, the individual strands, the cutting of a strand, the unraveling, and the loom or weaver, if any. Then identify where the mapping breaks — I would particularly like you to consider whether the fabric picture quietly imports the assumption that all strands are equally load-bearing, and that a fabric cannot have threads replaced without unraveling, when moral codes demonstrably rewrite themselves over generations without social collapse. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended use — licensing criminalization of private conduct without proof of harm to persons — from the ones that don't, and tell me whether the tutor's conclusion survives once those are on the table. Finally, turn the audit back on Hart: do his counter-images, the ship staying afloat versus keeping the same rigging, and a core morality that is load-bearing as against a peripheral morality that is not, actually carry his conclusion, or is he merely contesting who gets the metaphor? If there is a better analogy available to either side — one that preserves what the fabric picture gets right without its fatal slack — say what it is and whose case, if anyone's, it supports.
