I'm trying to decide, before I commit several years to it, what a method for the empirical study of consciousness actually has to deliver — not which existing method I like, but the specification any adequate one must meet. Please write out the desiderata.

Fix the purpose this way: I want a method good enough for a mature science of consciousness whose findings could be used in animal welfare policy and in clinical decisions about pain management in patients who cannot communicate. That purpose is deliberate, because it forces coverage of exactly the subjects the standard toolkit was not built for: non-human animals, preverbal infants, people with aphasia or advanced dementia, and humans in altered states where self-report is unreliable. So the specification has to cover subjects who cannot fill out a questionnaire or narrate their stream of experience, and it has to say what, for such subjects, plays the role that verbal report plays for the rest of us — or whether nothing does, and what that costs.

Beyond coverage, include whatever else you think is genuinely required: which phenomena it must capture (including the ones discovered by psychophysics and neuroscience, like masking, binocular rivalry, and blindsight, which it cannot contradict); which distinctions it must respect — in particular, between a subject's beliefs about their experience and their experience itself, and between treating a report as data and treating it as true; how it must handle the confabulation literature, where subjects confidently misdescribe what moved them; and what, if anything, it owes to first-person acquaintance as opposed to third-person evidence. With each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable for my stated purpose and which are prejudices of particular camps that I could waive.

Most important: tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect the demand for report-like data and the demand for coverage of the non-verbal may pull against each other, but I want your diagnosis, not mine. Then, briefly only, score heterophenomenology, a strict first-person introspective method, and neurophenomenology against the list, flagging where each fails a non-negotiable requirement. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is a check, not the deliverable.
