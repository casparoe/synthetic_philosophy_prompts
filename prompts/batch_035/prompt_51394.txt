In a seminar on Scanlon's contractualism we constructed an argument that seems to show the theory forbids nearly all risk imposition. Each step looked fine at the time, but the conclusion is far too strong, and I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) A principle is impermissible if someone could reasonably reject it, and what makes a rejection reasonable is the burden the principle would impose on particular individuals.
(2) In a choice between one person's death and any number of others' severe headaches, the person facing death could reasonably reject any principle permitting their death to relieve those headaches: a complaint of a given strength is never outweighed by any number of lesser complaints.
(3) A burden must be assessed from the standpoint of the person who bears it as things actually turn out; the victim's complaint is about being dead, not about what the odds were beforehand.
(4) Consider a policy that imposes a one-in-a-hundred-thousand risk of death on each of a million people who receive no benefit from it, in order to relieve severe headaches in a different population. One possible outcome of this policy is one in which some person bears exactly the burden described in (2): dead, while many others' headaches are relieved.
(5) By (2) and (3), that person could reasonably reject any principle permitting the policy.
(6) Nearly every risk-imposing policy — highways, medicines with rare fatal side effects, power generation — has some outcome of the same shape: one person dead, others retaining benefits far smaller than death.
(7) Therefore contractualism forbids nearly all risk imposition.

I want to keep (2): without it, contractualism loses what separates it from utilitarianism. I am tempted to give up (3), but I cannot see how to restate the test ex ante without letting expected values quietly back in. Please identify the step on which the argument turns, explain why that step looked innocuous, and state what rejecting it costs — including the costs of the alternatives, such as giving up (2) or biting the bullet on (7). End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the step you reject, or \answer{valid} if you accept the conclusion as stated.
