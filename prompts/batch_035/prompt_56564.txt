My reading group has spent this term on the mathematical infinite, and at the last session two members got into a drawn-out argument about whether Zeno's paradoxes of motion were ever actually solved, which I'm hoping you can referee.

Marcus, who is doing a PhD in analysis, opened by saying the matter was settled in the nineteenth century: the dichotomy turns on the series 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converging, and since it converges, Achilles traverses the distance in finite time; Zeno simply lacked the concept of a limit, and the habit of treating the paradoxes as deep is a myth told about mathematics rather than a live problem within it. Elena, who is a philosophy student, replied that this mislocates the issue: computing the sum of an infinite series is not performing infinitely many additions but taking a limit of finite sums, so the calculation presupposes rather than demonstrates that the process completes — the machinery is a description of the endpoint, not an account of how it is reached. To press the point she raised a lamp that is switched on and off at each of the halving intervals, at 1/2 a minute, 3/4, 7/8, and so on: the series of toggles converges, but nothing in the mathematics tells you whether the lamp is on or off at the one-minute mark, which she took to show that convergence is not sufficient for an infinite process to terminate in a definite outcome. Marcus answered that the lamp is merely an under-specified setup — the description simply fails to fix the state at the limit, a defect of that description, not a problem for infinite processes in general, whereas Achilles' motion is continuous and the limit state is fully determined. Elena's rejoinder was that this concession is her point exactly: where the limit state is fixed, it is fixed by a physical assumption of continuity that is doing the real work while the calculus takes the credit, so "solved by limits" is a compressed way of saying "solved once we added a premise Zeno never granted." Marcus closed by denying that the runner performs infinitely many tasks at all: the subdivision into tasks is our bookkeeping, one running described in infinitely many ways, and the paradox lives in a confusion between descriptions and happenings — to which Elena said that if every interval really does contain further subintervals, as the continuum view he invoked requires, then the bookkeeping is tracking something real, and he cannot have the continuum for his solution while denying it generates the puzzle.

They asked me to referee and I genuinely could not follow all the moves. Please adjudicate the exchange step by step — who is right about which specific point, whether either of them is making a move that only looks like a move, and what, if anything, both are missing — and then give a verdict on the headline question of whether Zeno's paradoxes of motion are solved, and by what. Please keep the whole adjudication to at most 600 words.
