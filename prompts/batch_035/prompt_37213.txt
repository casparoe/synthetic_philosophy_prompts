I teach a gen-ed course on ethics and public data — mostly non-majors, 25ish students, one 75-minute session. The unit is on whether the world is becoming less violent, and I don't want to lecture it at them. I want a simulation where they have to decide the question for themselves, but for a fictional civilization, so nobody can just parrot half-remembered podcast takes about Steven Pinker. The thing I want them to run into before anyone names it: whether "violence declined" comes out true depends on choices that look like boring measurement decisions — per capita or absolute, what even counts as violence, how you treat harms that only show up in the records halfway through the period because people just started writing them down.

Concretely: rules, something I can hand out, and an invented dataset. Make the population grow a lot over the invented time span so the two metrics genuinely diverge, and bury a couple of traps — a harm category that only appears in the data once it's criminalized, a category that quietly shrinks (public executions, say) while a new one quietly appears, something like that. I'd like it staged so they commit to some headline verdict early and then have to either defend it or eat it as more data arrives, and so that the disagreement between groups working from the same numbers surfaces before anyone says the words "per capita" to the class. Splitting them into commissions with different secret mandates would be great if you can make that work — but you know best whether that or something else gets the effect.

Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the real debate: the absolute-vs-rates fight, the visibility problem, and the question of whether there's a fact of the matter once you fix the definitions — and whether fixing the definitions is itself a factual question or a values one. I want the students to do most of the talking in the debrief too, so give me questions, not talking points.

Also tell me: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which part of the real debate no simulation with made-up numbers can reproduce. I have a feeling it's the stakes — nobody has an interest in the fictional planet's death toll, and in the real version of this fight everyone does — but I'd like you to actually think it through rather than just agree with me.
