I've been going down a Buddhist philosophy rabbit hole for the past year or so — no formal training, just videos, a couple of popular books, and a lot of late night thinking. The thinker I keep ending up at is this Nāgārjuna guy, and the emptiness stuff. Anyway, my friend Dave, who's read maybe one short book on it, tried to explain it back to me at a barbecue and I honestly can't tell if he nailed it or completely butchered it. Here's basically what he said:

"Nāgārjuna proved that nothing exists. Everything is made of parts and everything arises from causes, so when you break anything down you never bottom out at anything that's actually there — what you find instead is emptiness, which is basically a void, the true reality hiding behind the world of appearances. He also had this four-cornered logic where he denies every possibility — it exists, it doesn't, both, neither — which is how he shows the world is literally contradictory, meaning logic itself fails on ultimate questions and you have to go beyond reason. That's how he proves there's no self either. The payoff is that once you realize nothing is real, nothing can bother you anymore, which is why Buddhists are so calm about dying."

Some of this rings true to me. The parts-and-causes argument I've definitely encountered, and I'm pretty sure the "emptiness of emptiness" thing is real too, so it's not like he made it all up. But "nothing exists" and "a void behind appearances" sound suspiciously like the nihilism people always warn you the teaching is NOT supposed to be. And the "logic breaks down so reason is useless" part seems almost too convenient, like a get-out-of-jail-free card. Also I vaguely remember the two truths thing being about ultimate vs conventional truth, and it seems like Dave's "real world behind the appearances" picture might be sneaking that in somewhere wrong, but I can't put my finger on it.

Can you go through Dave's summary and tell me which parts a scholar of this tradition would accept, which parts are subtly off, and which parts are just plain misreadings? I'm especially curious about the four-cornered logic — did Nāgārjuna use it as a blanket proof that everything is contradictory, or was he doing something more local with it, like applying it to specific claims one at a time? And then the part I most want to know: if you correct the wrong bits, does his conclusion about the calm-and-unbothered attitude still follow, or was that riding entirely on the mistake? It kind of matters to me personally, since I've been half-treating "nothing is real, so chill out" as life advice.
