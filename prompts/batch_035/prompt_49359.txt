In forty years of epidemiological work I estimated probabilities by putting things into classes, and since retiring and beginning to read philosophy of science I have realized I never once had a rule for choosing the class. I would be grateful for help extracting a principle from my verdicts, which are these:

For the failure rate of a newly designed bridge, I would use the record of that design, not the record of bridges generally. For the chance that a seventy-year-old man reaches eighty-five, I would use the national life table for men his age, and I would distrust anyone who carved out a narrower class of health-conscious bridge players. For the ridership of a proposed light-rail line, I would use cities of similar size that built similar lines in the last two decades — not all infrastructure projects, and not just the three cities most like ours. For the half-life of a newly synthesized isotope, I would condition on everything known: decays of that isotope, and nothing narrower or broader. But for my own life expectancy I cannot decide whether the fact that my father and both his brothers lived past ninety should move my estimate. I refused to let my own jogging habit move it, on the ground that the life table already averages over men roughly like me, and I can find no principled difference between the two pieces of evidence — yet my gut treats them differently, which I suspect means one of the two verdicts has to go.

My tentative principle is: choose the narrowest class whose frequency is stable under its own further subdivisions. I cannot make "stable" precise, and I suspect it preserves my bridge verdict only by fiat. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; show what it predicts for a case I have not listed, for instance whether a colleague should estimate the risk of a novel virus from the class of all known viruses or from recent zoonotic events; and tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep.
