So I've basically been converted to the view that "better than" isn't transitive — not just that our judgments about it are fuzzy, but that betterness itself can loop, at least across long chains of tiny differences. The spectrum arguments got me: take two states that differ by one barely-noticeable unit of something, and there's no way that single unit flips the ordering in any way that matters, but string ten thousand of those units together and you get obvious awfulness versus obvious niceness — and transitivity then forces you to say the endpoints aren't meaningfully ordered at all, which seems nuts. My pet case: a ladder of futures for your later self, each rung with 0.1% more constant headache, starting totally headache-free and ending at chronic migraine. Rung 0 vs rung 1 is coin-flip territory, honestly. Rung 0 vs rung 9,999 is no contest. Say both of those out loud and transitivity starts to feel like a rule someone just made up.

But I keep bumping into things that should bug me and I want them laid out properly. What actually counts against the nontransitivity view? Please give me the defeaters sorted by how much they should shake me, and I want the real ones, not strawmen: the money-pump worry (if your betterness ordering loops, someone can walk you around the cycle and drain you — though does that show the ordering itself is wrong, or only that you'd be dumb to keep trading past the point where you notice what's happening?), the fact that decision theorists treat transitivity as close to definitional and get entire representation theorems out of it, the thought that maybe all my spectrum cases are just vagueness about where "barely noticeable" ends rather than genuine loops in the relation, and whatever else is out there that I haven't listed.

Also be straight with me about two things. First, which of these would a committed nontransitivist (is that a word?) concede is a genuine cost, and which would they shrug off as missing the point — I want the view defended by its best defenders, not dunked on by its worst. Second, is the view one of those that keeps quietly retreating as you push on it: first it's "betterness isn't transitive," then it's "judgments about betterness aren't transitive," then it's "in artificial ten-thousand-step cases the judgments aren't transitive," until whats left is compatible with transitivity holding in every situation you'd actually meet? If it has retreated that far, say so plainly, and tell me whether that retreat is fatal for a view of this kind or just what you'd expect from a view thats about the limits of a formal relation.
