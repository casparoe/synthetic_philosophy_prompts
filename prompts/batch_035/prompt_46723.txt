My book club has hit a wall. For the past year, a friend of mine and I have been reading about whether history can ever be objective, and we argue about it so much that the others have banned the topic from meetings. My position is roughly the old Rankean one: I was struck by Leopold von Ranke's insistence that the historian's task is to show what actually happened in the past rather than to judge it by the standards of the present, and that this can be done through painstaking work on primary sources, since the archive constrains what a serious historian can say. My friend takes the E. H. Carr / Hayden White line from the other side. He loved Carr's point in What Is History? (the 1961 book based on his Trevelyan lectures) that facts don't sit there waiting for the historian: they're like fish, he says — the ones on the fishmonger's slab are only there because somebody went fishing, and the historian is the one who decides what counts as a historical fact in the first place. From a recorded lecture he also picked up Hayden White's idea from Metahistory (1973) that historians smuggle a plot into their accounts — that the same chain of events can be emplotted as a tragedy, a romance, a comedy, or a satire, and that choosing the emplotment is an aesthetic or political choice rather than something the sources settle. So when I say a good history book aims at the past as it actually was, he says that phrase is either a pious myth or just means agreement among a clique of professionally trained historians, and that no amount of archival elbow grease changes that.

We've decided to stop trying to win and instead produce the document a proper adversarial collaboration would end with: something we could both actually sign, not a truce where we each hide our real views. I'd like you to draft it for us as a model, and we'll then check it against our own beliefs. It should have a numbered section of claims that both sides can endorse — things about primary sources, archives, footnotes, and the training of historians, where I'd concede the sources never simply speak for themselves, and where he'd concede that a historian who invents or ignores evidence is simply wrong, whatever emplotment they fancy. Then a disagreements table in which each remaining disagreement is stated in wording that the side holding that view would accept as fair to it — no slanted phrasing. After that, the cruxes: I suspect one is whether two equally competent and honest historians, working from the same archive but with different politics, should be expected to converge on the same account, and another is whether White's plot-types are merely descriptions of how histories are written or genuine constraints on what can be true. For each crux, please include a concrete test, argument, or case that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — for example, we've wondered whether a dispute over some well-documented episode (say, the causes of a particular war) narrows over time as the archives open, and my prediction is that it does while his is that new sources just feed new interpretations.

Neither of us has any academic background — he's a nurse and I'm a retired schoolteacher — so please keep the language plain and avoid citations or reading lists; what we want is the substance. Also, both of us suspect that at least part of our quarrel is verbal rather than real, probably something about what the word objective is doing in each of our mouths, so please examine that explicitly and say which part of the disagreement, if any, dissolves once the words are pinned down, and which part survives as a genuine difference that no clarification can bridge. Format the whole thing as: numbered agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with tests and predictions, then your verdict on the verbal-versus-real question.
