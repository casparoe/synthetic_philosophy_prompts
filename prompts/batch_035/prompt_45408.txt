Last night a friend and I argued well past midnight about Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" and what it commits you to, and by the end I realized I had been defending a package of views I'm no longer sure is stable. For a seminar write-up, I want an honest audit of the following, which is roughly where I stand: (1) I am a consequentialist about rightness, with no morally significant act/omission distinction and no cap on how demanding the demands can get; (2) I accept something close to Singer's principle, that I am obligated to keep giving to prevent serious suffering until what I would sacrifice is of genuinely comparable moral importance; (3) I found Wolf's argument convincing, that a person whose life was wholly structured by moral concern would be an unattractive and impoverished ideal of a human being, and that it is a genuine good that our ideals leave room for the cellist, the wit, and the obsessive chef; (4) I believe the supererogatory is a real category — some acts are admirable precisely because they exceed duty rather than fulfill it; (5) I think moral admiration should track the quality of a person's will and character, not how colorful or interesting they are; and (6) I see nothing wrong with spending real time and money on nonmoral projects of my own — learning the oboe, restoring an old motorcycle — even while preventable suffering persists. Please tell me which of these are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I should say that I don't know where the deepest problem is, and I'd rather not be told to abandon all of it — I want to know what the minimal stable remainder looks like.
