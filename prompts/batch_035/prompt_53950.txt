My constitutional law professor is obsessed with the Ulysses story. Whenever entrenchment comes up — rights that can't be touched, supermajority amendment rules, judicial review — he says a written constitution is just Ulysses lashing himself to the mast: the framers knew the polity would be tempted by the sirens (war fever, panic, persecution of minorities), so they tied the ship down in advance, and the ropes are supposed to hold even when the crew wants to steer toward the rocks. He leans on this to argue that strong judicial review isn't really "counter-majoritarian" at all, because the majority being overridden is just lashed-down Ulysses complaining about ropes it (or its earlier self) asked for.

Audit this for me. Lay out the mapping — who's Ulysses, who are the sailors rowing, what exactly are the sirens supposed to be, what plays the role of the rope vs. the wax earplugs, who (if anyone) is Circe handing out advice. Then the places the mapping breaks: Ulysses binds *himself*, but the framers mostly bind *other people* — later generations who never consented and can't untie themselves the way he eventually could; Ulysses knew precisely what temptation was coming, while framers face uncertainty about which ones will matter; and there's the awkward fact that the people lashed to the mast in his story are the same person who ordered the ropes, which doesn't obviously describe a living population versus a dead hand. Tell me which of these breaks actually matter for his conclusion about judicial review and which are just pedantic quibbles, and whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the mast story can't carry it, is there a better analogy for what constitutions are doing when they bind the future?
