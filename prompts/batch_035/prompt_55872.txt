My flatmate came back from a weekend binge of philosophy of mind videos and tried to explain the "special composition question" to me over dinner. Here's roughly what he said: 'It's basically the ship of Theseus thing — the question of when a bunch of little objects add up to one big object. Peter van Inwagen's famous answer is that this basically never happens. There's no such thing as a table, there are just tiny particles arranged table-wise, and the same goes for chairs, ships, mountains, all of it. The only composite objects that really exist are living things like you and me. But most philosophers nowadays go the other way and say whenever you take any objects and put them together you automatically create a new composite object, so there's literally an object made of my left shoe and three quarks in another galaxy. That's obviously crazy, so almost everyone agrees composition has to be restricted somehow, and the debate is about drawing the line.'

He seemed really confident about all this, but a couple of things bugged me. For one, I thought the ship of Theseus was about a thing staying the same over time while its parts get swapped, which sounds like a different question from whether there's a thing there at all — he waved that off as pedantry. Also I can't quite tell whether the "you and me" part follows: if living organisms are composite, what makes them so special that tables miss the cut? And the last bit feels a bit fast — does the fact that universalism has weird consequences (if it is one) actually mean most people reject it, or do people just live with the weirdness?

Could you go through his little speech more or less claim by claim and tell me what a metaphysician working on this would sign off on, what they'd wince at or call sloppy, and what's flat-out wrong? I'd especially like to know whether the last sentence — the one about almost everyone agreeing composition must be restricted — is a fair report of the field or just his gloss on it. And since he's the one who read up on this and I'm not: please flag the points where your own audit is most likely to go wrong, and what would make you change your verdict on them — e.g. if some claim turns on a contested reading of van Inwagen rather than on what he explicitly committed himself to, say so rather than grading it as simply true or false.
