I'm a backend engineer at a company that takes its blameless-postmortem culture pretty seriously, and a retrospective last week somehow turned into a two-hour argument about one of my teammates that I haven't been able to stop chewing on since. The subject was "Marcus," who is famous on our team for never sandbagging. When he says a migration will take six weeks, it takes six weeks; when something is off track, he says so immediately, even when it makes him look bad. Someone finally asked him how he manages it, and he answered, completely without embarrassment, that his credibility is his career — that a reputation for never lying about timelines is worth more to him than any single project, and that once you actually do the math, lying early is always the losing move. A second teammate, "Priya," pushed back hard: if Marcus would lie the moment the math changed, then his honesty isn't a virtue at all, it's a strategy that happens to line up with honesty today, and it deserves about as much moral credit as a well-calibrated pricing model.

Then a third colleague complicated things in a way that's really what's stuck with me. "Dana" is honest in a completely different way: she says she's constitutionally incapable of sandbagging — lying makes her so anxious that it's not really a choice for her. Everyone in the room, including Priya, instinctively gave Dana full credit as the genuinely honest one. But the person running the retro pointed out that Dana's honesty is at least as self-directed as Marcus's: she tells the truth because it's the only way to make her own anxiety go away, not because of anything about other people or the truth as such. So now we have a man who is honest because it pays, a woman who is honest because she can't bear the alternative, and a room that's confident about which one is the real thing but can't explain why.

I have no philosophy background — closest I've come is some blog posts I went looking for afterward — so I'd appreciate it if you could help me sort this out rather than just telling me who's right. What I keep noticing is that my verdict flip-flops depending on whether I ask "which of them deserves credit for being honest?" or "which of them would I trust if the incentives ever turned bad?" Those feel like they should have the same answer, but they don't: Dana's honesty would survive any incentive change I can imagine, but I'm not sure she's choosing anything at all, while Marcus is clearly making some kind of choice but the choice seems to have a market price. Are these two different questions, or is one of them the real question and the other a distraction? And is there anything to say about Marcus beyond Priya's take, or is she right that a motive with a market price isn't worth anything?
