Ana pushed the orthodox line: money, time, and effort already spent are gone no matter what you do, so the only question a rational agent faces is which option has the best future prospects, and letting the past tip the scale is just sloppy reasoning. Ben didn't deny any of that as far as bare decision theory goes, but said the fallacy only bites if you treat each choice as isolated. His line was that a person who abandons a project the moment its expected value dips below the alternatives becomes a certain *kind* of agent -- a shifty one with no history -- and that others can see this. So finishing your PhD even when the numbers look bad now functions as a costly signal that you're a finisher, which pays off in every future project you'll ever take on. Ana said this smuggles in the answer: if finishing signals persistence, then finishing has future value after all, so Ben has just redescribed her position, not resisted it -- the "past investment" itself still does no work. Ben fired back that he can't just relabel it like that, because the signal only works *because* it involves ignoring future-value considerations; a finisher who finishes only when it's instrumentally optimal can't signal anything, in the same way a threat isn't credible if everyone knows you'll never follow through on it. Ana's last word was that this proves too much: on Ben's logic, deliberately throwing away money would be rational too, since that's an even costlier signal of not caring about money. They both looked at me and I mumbled something about office hours.
