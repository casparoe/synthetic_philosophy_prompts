I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a large introductory course on free will, and I've been asked to build a diagnostic quiz for the course website: "which view of free will do you actually hold?" The versions floating around online frustrate me, because they sort people almost entirely by their opinions about determinism and physics, so a student can land on "compatibilist" without ever having been asked a single question about human beings. What I'd like instead takes its cue from Daniel Dennett's old question about which varieties of free will are worth wanting: the quiz should sort students by what they take the concept of free will to be for — self-control, the fitness of praise and blame, immunity to certain kinds of manipulation, being an originator of one's actions in some stronger sense, or nothing much at all — rather than by what they happen to think about causation at the micro level.

Please write eight to ten questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased in ordinary language so that no question requires knowing any technical vocabulary and no question telegraphs which position it feeds. The cases should be ones on which the positions genuinely come apart, and I'd like you to work in roughly the following situations, which you can modify as you see fit: someone coerced at gunpoint who does what they'd otherwise never do; a person who has been covertly programmed, by neurosurgery or otherwise, to want exactly what they in fact go on to choose; someone whose every choice is infallibly foreknown by a predictor, and who deliberates as carefully as anyone, yet never does otherwise than predicted; an addict who could have refrained only with a struggle most people would call heroic; and, finally, a chess engine or a superbly trained dog that reliably does the "right" thing, to probe whether mere reliable right-doing is worth calling freedom.

At the end, provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions — I'd expect something like hierarchical views in Frankfurt's vicinity, reasons-responsiveness or reactive-attitude views in the Strawson/Dennett neighborhood, sourcehood or ultimate-origination views, plain hard incompatibilism, and the deflationary position that the whole concept of free will earns its keep only for the practical business of advising, blaming, and crediting. Where two positions can't really be separated by any question you can ask without jargon, say so explicitly rather than pretending the quiz discriminates cleanly; and if some combination of answers corresponds to no standard position, note that too, with a sentence on whether the unoccupied slot looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The audience is first-year students with no philosophy background, so the questions should read like little stories, with all the labels quarantined in the key.
