My sister just got engaged, and the whole ring economics thing we got into at the family dinner afterwards is still bugging me. The practice, at least in the US and a lot of other places: when a man proposes, he's expected to spend a big chunk of money on a diamond ring, with the old guideline people still repeat being something like two or three months of his salary. Meanwhile everyone knows diamonds resell terribly (you'd be lucky to get a fraction of what you paid back), lab-grown diamonds exist that are chemically identical but way cheaper, and a lot of people still side-eye those specifically for engagements. And the same culture that enforces this also says it's the thought that counts, praises thrift in basically every other part of life, and insists that money can't buy love. Someone at the dinner brought up "costly signaling" and peacock tails, and that's roughly where the argument started.

So here's what I want to know. What does this practice actually presuppose about how the ring does its job? Is the implicit theory basically the handicap principle — the ring signals commitment (or income, or both) precisely because it's a wasteful, unrecoverable cost, so that only a man who's serious would pay it, the way a peacocks tail is honest advertising because it's expensive to grow and carry around? If that's right, the terrible resale value isn't a bug, it's the whole mechanism, which would explain why nobody much cares that it's a bad investment. But does the practice strictly need that theory, or is it compatible with rivals — say the ring as a deterrent, a sunk cost that makes the man think twice before walking away, or just a convention everyone coordinates on because everyone expects everyone else to? There's also a timing thing I can't quite figure out: the ring is handed over at the moment of proposal, before the other person has signaled anything back, so the practice seems to presuppose that the proposing side is the one who needs to prove seriousness — and yet the norm seems just as strong now that women have their own incomes and no-fault divorce is everywhere, which you'd think would make an old-style one-way commitment signal pointless. Is the practice presupposing something inconsistent there, or is it just underdetermined?

Please walk me through it in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — like a long email to a friend who knows some philosophy and economics. Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me what would have to change in the practice itself if the implicit view were swapped for the pure convention story or for a mutual-signaling one. Would the rings get cheaper, or would something else become the costly part?
