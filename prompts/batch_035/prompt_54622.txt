At last month's departmental colloquium I defended a view I have held for years, in roughly the spirit of Dennett: that a self is a center of narrative gravity, an abstract posit we introduce — the way physicists introduce centers of mass — to organize and predict the behavior of an enormously complicated concrete system, the organism and its brain, without being any concrete component of that system. In the pub afterward a colleague I have argued with since graduate school produced the following, and I have not been able to put it down since:

(1) A self is an abstract posit, not a concrete particular, and no abstract posit is identical with any concrete particular. (2) Each of us is a self; what I am is what my self is. (3) So no concrete particular is me — not this organism, not this brain, no part or stage of either. (4) Only concrete particulars can suffer, be wronged, deserve blame, or be punished; abstractions have no interests for anything to be done to. (5) Therefore no one suffers, no one is wronged, no one deserves blame; atrocities have bodies as perpetrators and none as victims.

I want to say the argument slides illegitimately from the claim that the self is an abstract tool for describing a person to the claim that the person is an abstract object, but I can't make the slide precise, and my colleague has a rejoinder I find uncomfortable: on my own view the organism is just more patterns all the way down, no more a concrete particular than the self-posit is, so if I retreat to the organism as the suffering thing I have abandoned the spirit of the position for a naive realism I spent the talk rejecting. He also notes that if his argument is sound, a cousin of it shows there are no hurricanes or economies — after all, no concrete particular is a hurricane — and he is happy to accept that too, which makes me suspect the whole thing is either valid and harmless or invalid in a way I should be able to name. There is also a bullet I am half-tempted to bite: that on Dennett's own understanding, centers of gravity are real enough to figure in true explanations, so perhaps (5) is true on a reading where suffering is as real as spin — though I doubt I could get away with saying no one is wronged in the same breath.

Tell me which premise to give up, name the equivocation or other flaw if there is one, and price the alternatives: what accepting my colleague's reading of the view costs, what retreating to the organism costs, and what biting the bullet on (5) costs. I would most like to keep (1), since it just is my position, and least like to accept anything close to (5). Keep it to 600 words at most, and close by saying how confident you are in the diagnosis and identifying the single step or pricing you are least sure of.
