As a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, I've been asked to help judge our annual young writers' essay prize, and this year I'm one of the graders for the philosophy category. Entrants in the 18–25 bracket answered a set question in a closed-book, ninety-minute session. The question read as follows:

"In his work on the structure of knowledge, Quine argued that beliefs are tested against experience collectively rather than one by one, that even the claims of logic are in principle revisable, and that there is no fundamental divide between analytic truths and empirical ones. Yet when he turned to moral values specifically, he insisted that ethics stands on a footing very different from that of the natural sciences. Explain the contrast Quine draws between the epistemic standing of ethics and that of science, and assess whether his own holistic picture of knowledge can consistently accommodate that contrast."

One of the finalists submitted the answer reproduced below. I'd like to give the other judges a graded, point-by-point assessment of it before we confer. Please read the answer as a piece of philosophy first and as contest writing second: what it gets right, what it gets subtly wrong, and where its reasoning stops tracking Quine. Then assign it a letter grade from A to F, using the full scale as warranted, and write a numbered set of specific comments — each tied to a particular sentence or step in the answer — that justify the grade, so that a hasty reader couldn't mistake the entry for better (or worse) than it is.

The student's answer:

"Quine's most famous argument, advanced in Two Dogmas of Empiricism, is that the unit of empirical significance is not the individual statement but the whole web of our beliefs. Our claims face experience collectively; we can, in principle, hold onto any single belief by adjusting others around it, though the web as a whole is answerable to observation. This applies to moral judgments as much as to anything else. Since Quine rejected the analytic-synthetic distinction, no moral claim can be insulated as a definitional truth; moral beliefs are part of the web and are revisable like all the rest.

The contrast with science comes from Quine's account of how theory is anchored to the world. Scientific claims are tied, however loosely, to observation sentences — sentences on whose truth competent speakers can be brought into agreement by shared stimulation — and this anchoring is what lets science improve over time through trial and error. Ethics has no comparable anchor: there are no observation sentences for value, no court of appeal that is common to all parties. That, for Quine, is why moral disagreement is so persistent.

However, Quine also held that moral deliberation can borrow the authority of science in a limited way. Once a moral standard is adopted, many of the disputes internal to it become straightforwardly empirical — questions about what maximizes welfare, say, are questions of fact. And on Quine's view the standard that our total web of belief most strongly supports is utilitarianism: a system of the world organized around the promotion of aggregate welfare is the best-organized one available to us, and Quine treats the adoption of utilitarianism as vindicated by exactly the same holistic virtues — simplicity, conservatism, scope — that vindicate scientific theory choice. So the appearance of a sharp contrast between ethics and science is misleading on Quine's own terms. Given the standard, moral questions are settled by observation just as scientific ones are, and the adoption of the standard is itself justified holistically. Ethics is therefore methodologically on a par with natural science, differing only in degree of anchoring, and Quine's contrast amounts to a modest sociological remark about the causes of disagreement rather than a genuine epistemological distinction."
