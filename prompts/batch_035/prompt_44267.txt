I have no academic background, but for the past couple of years I have been thinking in my spare time about how Gödel's incompleteness theorems get used outside mathematics. The pattern I keep meeting is this: someone invokes the theorems to show that no system can fully account for itself, or that there are truths no method can reach, and someone else replies that this is a misuse because the theorems concern only formal systems capable of arithmetic. What I cannot work out is whether that stock reply settles every non-mathematical use, or whether some uses retain a residue of truth once the arithmetic is stripped away. Please give me an example of a non-mathematical argument that appeals to the incompleteness theorems in which the error is instructive rather than crude — one where a reasonably careful person could be taken in, and where simply pointing out that Gödel was talking about formal systems does not by itself locate the mistake. Then say precisely where the argument goes wrong, and what, if anything, survives the correction. Please write your answer in American English.
