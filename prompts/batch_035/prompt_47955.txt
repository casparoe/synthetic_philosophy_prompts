I lead a small platform-infrastructure team, and I'd appreciate advice on a voting problem that has come up twice now and that I can't fully make sense of.

Our team charter handles major adoption decisions by majority vote of the three senior engineers. For the most recent decision — whether to replace our current build system — we had previously agreed in writing on two adoption conditions: (1) the new system demonstrably cuts our CI times by at least 30 percent on our two largest repositories, and (2) it can be rolled out incrementally, without a migration freeze that would block other teams for more than a week. The charter also states, as a general policy, that a tool is adopted if and only if the agreed conditions are met.

Each senior engineer ran their own evaluation and then we voted on each condition separately, followed by a direct vote on adoption. The results:

- Condition 1 (CI improvement): Priya yes, Marcus yes, Lin no — passes, 2 to 1.
- Condition 2 (incremental rollout): Priya yes, Marcus no, Lin yes — passes, 2 to 1.
- Adopt the new build system: Priya yes, Marcus no, Lin no — fails, 1 to 2.

Priya found the gains real and the rollout feasible. Marcus found the gains real but judged the rollout to require a freeze. Lin judged the rollout feasible but found the gains evaporate on our largest repository. So each person voted in a way that is internally coherent: Marcus and Lin each rejected one condition and voted against adoption accordingly, and our policy says adoption requires both conditions to hold.

Here is what troubles me. As a group, we have now gone on record saying both adoption conditions are met, while also voting against adopting the tool that our own charter says follows from those conditions being met. It looks like the team, taken as a single entity, contradicted itself — even though no individual did.

Two of my colleagues have dug in on opposite sides. Marcus argues nothing went wrong: every engineer voted consistently, the procedure was followed, and it is simply a fact about majority voting that this pattern can occur, so the direct adoption vote stands. Our engineering manager argues the opposite: since the condition votes passed, the charter's if-and-only-if clause settles the matter, and we should treat adoption as required rather than re-voting — the direct vote was the redundant one.

I'd like a considered opinion on several questions. Is there a genuine inconsistency here, and if so, where exactly does it live — in the group, in our procedure, or in the way I'm describing the group as if it had beliefs? Deciding the outcome by the direct vote versus deriving it from the condition votes are two different procedures that happen to conflict; is one of them clearly the right one, or is choosing between them itself a substantive choice about what the vote is for? And is there a better-designed process we could adopt going forward, given that this has now happened twice — or should I accept that any majority-vote process over multiple related questions will occasionally produce this kind of outcome?
