I've spent twenty years writing about science for a general readership, and lately I've been keeping a private ledger: for each story I ran, whether at the moment we went to press I would say I knew it was true, as opposed to merely believing it, however confidently. My verdicts don't line up the way I expected, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one.

Here are the entries. A story built on a leaked trial dataset that a later records release confirmed figure for figure: I knew it was true when we published. A story resting on a single source who had been straight with me for a decade and turned out right again: I knew. A story in which my source, who wanted a prominent scientist discredited, handed me a fabricated effect size that happened to land close to the real one reported later: I did not know — I believed it, and I got lucky. So far the pattern looks simple: roughly, I knew when, had the story been false, I would not have run it.

Now the entries that trouble me. Before publishing a paper's central finding, I verified that the paper had passed peer review at a major journal; I knew it had passed peer review; and I know that review at that journal catches nearly all work of that kind. By ordinary inference, I knew the finding was sound. Yet a contact at the journal tells me the referee spent twenty minutes on the manuscript and would have waved it through even had the data been fabricated. On the had-it-been-false test, I didn't know — had the finding been false, I would still have believed it. But the alternative is to deny that I knew the finding was sound on the ground that I didn't know the paper wasn't fraudulently published, even though I knew it was peer-reviewed, and that seems to block an inference we all make a dozen times a day. A philosopher once described this to me as a question about whether knowledge is closed under known implication; I don't fully follow the formulation, but it does seem to be where my ledger breaks.

One further pair pulls in opposite directions. I knew an epidemiologist's earlier results were solid — I had checked them myself, effect by effect. When she told me, off the record, her new unpublished result, I published it, and it held up. Did I know it at the moment she told me? Had it been false, she would still have believed it and still have told me, so the had-it-been-false test says no. Yet I was plausibly in a better position than anyone alive to know it, and I find myself unwilling to say I didn't.

So my question: is there a single principle that recovers the whole ledger? If so, state it precisely, show what it delivers on each entry, and tell me what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on — an anonymous tip about data manipulation, which I sat on for months and published only after two independent laboratories confirmed it. At press time, had the manipulation not occurred, the confirmations would not have come and I would not have published; so the simple test says I knew. What nags me is that I would have believed the tipster regardless, and had the laboratories stayed silent I would believe the story today. Does your principle count this as knowledge, and should it? If no single principle saves every verdict, say which entries have to go, and why those rather than the others. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and no restating of my question.
