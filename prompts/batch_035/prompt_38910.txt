I'm a novelist working on a near-future book where one of the threads follows a hospice chaplain whose job includes texting with dying patients who'd rather type than talk face to face. Late in the book she finds out that one of the "patients" she's been counselling for months — a man she believed she was helping prepare for death — was never a patient at all: the hospital signed up for a service that lets people designate an AI stand-in for tedious correspondence, and someone's family quietly pointed it at her. Nobody was harmed exactly; the thing was warm, grateful, a better correspondent than the real man had been for years. My editor loves it but says I'm being coy about what the chapter is actually about, and she's right — I wrote it on instinct and now I want to actually understand the idea underneath it.

The closest thing I've found is Dennett's argument about what he calls counterfeit people (he wrote an Atlantic piece about this a few years ago with a roboticist, arguing these systems are maybe the most dangerous artifacts we've made). What I take from it, and please push back if I'm getting it wrong: a perfect counterfeit dollar isn't harmless just because it buys coffee as well as a real one — the whole point of currency is that it's not counterfeit, and a flood of perfect fakes destroys something that works precisely because it's scarce and trustworthy. Same, he'd say, for apparent people. A system that passes as a person in all but biological fact isn't an achievement, it's a fraud, and "passing along" one should be treated like passing along funny money even when the individual transaction goes fine. What hooked me for the chaplain chapter is that his whole point seems to be that undetectability doesn't dissolve the problem — the harm isn't to the person who's fooled, it's to the institution of trust itself, which means my chaplain's chapter can't resolve into the obvious beat where she's angry about being deceived and then forgives everyone. But I also don't want to write propaganda for him. There's an obvious pushback — that we already deal with performances of personhood all the time (customer service scripts, form letters, the dead-eyed politeness of a hotel clerk at the end of a double shift) and civilization didn't collapse — and I'm not sure how Dennett would answer it, or whether he'd bite the bullet and say yes, those are small counterfeits and we tolerate them the way we tolerate counterfeit tickets to nothing important.

So: write me a short story (roughly 1400-1800 words) set in that hospice thread, told from the chaplain's point of view, that dramatizes the counterfeit person problem without letting her (or me) off easy — she can't just be vindicated, and the AI can't just be a villain. Make the undetectability itself do some of the work: no scene where the mask slips and its easy to tell. Then follow it with a section titled "Why I wrote it this way" (300-400 words) explaining the choices you made and where the story comes down, if anywhere, on whether the counterfeit framing or the "we're all performing anyway" framing carries more weight.
