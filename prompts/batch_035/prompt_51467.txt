At our astrobiology group meeting, a visiting speaker argued that the Great Silence settles more than most of us in the room were willing to grant. His case ran like this: the galaxy is a field of dry grass that has been standing for ten billion years; a single expansionist civilization is a spark; a burn front crosses a dry field in a negligible fraction of the field's standing time, just as a colonization wavefront at even modest sublight speeds should cross the galaxy in a few hundred million years; and since the grass is still standing — no obviously waste-heat-bright stars, no visible industrial traffic, no settling of everything we can see — no spark has ever occurred. So technological civilizations are either vanishingly rare or short-lived, and 'they exist but are quiet' scenarios deserve no serious weight. I found the fire picture rhetorically powerful but suspiciously tidy, and I'd like a proper audit of it. Please give the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the fuel, the spark, the wind that carries embers ahead of the front, the ash left behind — and then work through where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that genuinely matter for his conclusion from the pedantic ones. In particular I want to know what the analogy smuggles in about settlement dynamics: whether spread should be exponential or saturating, whether 'burned' territory stays burned or colonies can fade and revert, whether the front advances uniformly or hops like wind-blown embers to whatever is nearest, and whether it assumes that settlement leaves the kind of astronomical signature we would in fact notice. Finally, tell me whether the 'rare or short-lived' conclusion survives once those assumptions are relaxed, or whether the tidy picture was carrying the conclusion all along — and if there is a better analogy for interstellar expansion than a fire in a field, offer it.
