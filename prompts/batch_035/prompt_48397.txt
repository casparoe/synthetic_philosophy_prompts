National weather services attach probabilities to single, unrepeatable events — a 30% chance of rain tomorrow for this particular day — and audit their forecasters on calibration: across all the days ever labeled 30%, rain must have fallen on roughly 30% of them, and persistent miscalibration is treated as a forecaster having been wrong rather than unlucky. The conventions enforced by statistics journals and their style guides, by contrast, permit a researcher to report only the long-run error rate of a testing procedure, and discourage claims that a given hypothesis is probably true, as though probabilities could attach to procedures but not to the single hypothesis in front of us. Both institutions quantify uncertainty for a living. What theory of probability does each practice presuppose — long-run frequency, single-case propensity, or calibrated degree of belief — and is the pair jointly consistent? Reconstruct the implicit view underlying each practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say where each is compatible with several interpretations at once, and explain what would have to change in the journal conventions, and in the calibration audits, under the interpretations each practice excludes.
