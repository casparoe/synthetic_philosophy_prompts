I'm on the technical standards board for our national device-interoperability program, and we've got a genuinely nasty fight coming to a vote next month that I think is secretly a philosophy fight, so I want a memo that actually engages the philosophy. The case: about two years ago manufacturers started converging on the same magnetic charging connector without anyone decreeing anything — one big laptop vendor shipped it, accessory makers followed, then phone makers followed the accessory makers, and now something like 70% of new devices use it. It just happened, purely de facto. The question in front of us is whether to formally adopt it as the national standard, with certification marks and compliance testing and the whole apparatus, or leave well enough alone. One faction says ratifying reality gives small manufacturers the confidence to tool up and locks in the benefits. The other faction — led by a committee member who will not stop citing David Lewis's Convention at us — argues that the moment we write it down, the regularity stops being a convention in his sense: firms will conform because a certificate says so, not because everyone expects everyone else to conform, and we'll have traded a self-enforcing solution to a coordination problem for something that lives or dies by inspectors. To make it messier, a rival consortium is pushing a technically superior connector and has threatened to lobby the ministry if we bless the incumbent instead of theirs.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate ahead of the vote: adopt, decline to adopt, or adopt with conditions, with reasoning the board can actually act on and a time frame. Please take the Lewis guy seriously rather than brushing him off — salience and how the market apparently solved its own salience problem, whether a convention really is arbitrary in the way he claims (does it matter that the incumbent connector is slightly worse?), whether the common knowledge he requires survives codification or gets replaced by something else, and what he'd say about the fact that conventions can apparently be created by explicit agreement when agreement itself seems to presuppose a prior convention of keeping agreements — I half-remember there being something almost circular there that he has an answer to, so don't let the memo fake certainty on that point. But the memo also has to be honest about the stuff pure Lewisian analysis might miss, like what certification does for small manufacturers who fear the de facto standard getting quietly forked by the big vendor someday, and what happens to whichever convention loses if the ministry steps in and mandates the consortium's connector instead. End with a concrete bottom line and a fair treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. I took a philosophy of language seminar ages ago so I'll notice if the Lewis material is hand-wavy, but keep it readable — half the board are engineers who think all this is beneath them.
