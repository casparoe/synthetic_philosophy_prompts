Write the joint statement that a scientific realist and a historical pessimist could both sign at the conclusion of a year-long adversarial collaboration on the pessimistic meta-induction. The realist endorses the no-miracles argument — the novel predictive success of mature science would be miraculous unless its central posits are at least approximately true — and her preferred response to the historical record is selective realism (the so-called divide-and-rule defense): past theories were wrong overall, but the posits genuinely responsible for their successes (mass in Newtonian mechanics, the wave character of light in Fresnel) were carried over into their successors, while only the idle ones died. The pessimist presses the meta-induction: phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether each anchored decades of successful research before being discarded as radically false, and he sees no principled reason to expect our situation to differ. He regards the selective realist's working/idle distinction as a retrospective exercise conducted with the answer key in hand.

The document should contain three sections: a numbered list of agreed claims (roughly six to ten), a disagreements table, and a cruxes section. The agreed claims should go beyond truisms. Note that both can accept, for instance, that the historical record is not a tidy list of clean falsifications — the historian Hasok Chang has argued that the phlogiston theory was never straightforwardly refuted and that its abandonment owed as much to disciplinary politics as to experiment — and that this complicates the pessimist's base of cases at least as much as it complicates the realist's. The disagreements table should state each residual dispute in wording the relevant side would itself sign: about whether "approximately true" retains substantive content across theory change; about whether the ether's reference was preserved by later field theories, or whether a term for a mechanical medium in which nothing is detectably at rest refers to nothing; about whether the continuity in the equations from Fresnel to Maxwell, which Worrall made the centerpiece of structural realism, supports realism about structure, and whether structure is enough to be a realist about; and about whether the meta-induction suffers a selection effect, since only theories that were actually replaced are available to be counted as refuted.

For each crux, record the case study, historical investigation, or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. At least one crux must be prospective: the two should agree now on how to classify the central posits of some current theory, so that future theory change could in principle score their predictions differently. Finally, if any part of the dispute strikes both of them as verbal — for instance, how far "approximate truth" can be stretched before the realist's thesis becomes unfalsifiable or the pessimist's becomes trivial — say which part, and why that disagreement is about words rather than substance.
