The literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia seems to proceed case by case, each author leaning on a single example that the rival rule mishandles. Before I commit to a side, I would like the requirements written out: what a policy for adjusting first-order credences in response to evidence about those very credences has to get right — the cases it must handle, the patterns of reasoning it must forbid, and the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as the line between higher-order evidence and simply more first-order evidence.

The cases, to fix the target: (a) you form a belief on the basis of a study, then learn that studies of that kind get cases like this one wrong more often than right; (b) a source announces that it will keep telling you that p until you believe it, so that the cost of holding out rises with each refusal; (c) the very process that produced your confidence in p is what you now receive as evidence against it — the brainwashing shape; (d) two agents share their first-order evidence while disagreeing about what that evidence supports. The patterns to be forbidden, as far as I can tell: believing p while judging your total evidence to tell against p; bootstrapping, by which any conclusion is licensed once paired with a claim about one's own reliability; and rules that, applied to belief in themselves, instruct you to abandon them.

What I care about most is the interaction among the requirements: which pairs cannot be honored together — I suspect that unrestricted deference to total evidence and the ban on akratic belief part ways somewhere near case (b) — and which requirement excludes the most candidates, the familiar rules being defer to higher-order evidence always, ignore it whenever the first-order case is strong enough, and settle at the point where first- and higher-order assessments agree. Please leave out any scorecard; the criteria are what I need, not rankings of particular proposals.
