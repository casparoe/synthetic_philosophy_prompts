I spent my working life as an experimental scientist — first running randomized field trials in agronomy, later in clinical drug development — and I have lately, in retirement, taken up philosophy. I have just encountered the problem of moral luck, in the essays of Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, and I want to find out how much of it I genuinely understand by having it restated in the vocabulary I have used for forty years: experimental units, treatments, covariates, estimands, exogenous noise, realized versus expected outcomes, confounding, heterogeneity across units. I am fluent enough in that vocabulary to be corrected, but only a newcomer to philosophy, so I want the translation kept honest — please do not quietly build your verdict into the terms you hand me.

As I understand the position to be translated: the so-called control principle says that we are morally assessable only to the extent that what we are assessed for depends on factors under our control. Yet, first, two agents can perform what seems the very same act with the same intentions and differ in moral standing merely because of how things turned out — the stock example being two equally reckless drivers, one of whom happens to have a child run into the road. Second, what we do at all is shaped by circumstances we did not choose: the situations that happen to confront us, the talents and temperaments we were born with or had formed in us before we could consent to any of it. Nagel, I gather, sorts these into resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck, and argues that as the principle is applied it erodes — nearly everything we might be assessed for is infected by luck, so either the principle is false or its scope collapses to almost nothing. Williams's example, if I have it right, is the painter Gauguin abandoning his family for his art, and it is meant to show that in some cases the very justification of a decision is only available retroactively, through the success of what the decision risked.

Now the translation I would like, as faithful as you can make it:

1. Restate the control principle and its erosion in my vocabulary. It is natural for me to think of an agent's choice as the assignment of a treatment, the agent's character and circumstances as covariates the agent did not select, and the outcome as a noisy realization in which many causes besides the treatment participated. In that vocabulary, judging someone by results rather than by what they did looks like confusing the realized draw with the estimand — precisely the mistake we beat out of first-year students who overinterpret a single trial's point estimate. Say whether that rendering is faithful, and where it bends the original.

2. Translate each of the four kinds of luck separately. Where does resultant luck map onto noise in outcomes, circumstantial luck onto differences in experimental conditions, constitutive luck onto heterogeneity across units that precedes any assignment? Flag whatever you had to distort to make a kind of luck fit a slot in my vocabulary, and whatever has no slot at all.

3. The residue report, which is what interests me most. In my vocabulary, the two drivers received the same treatment with different realizations of the noise, and the rational response is to judge the treatment, not the draw. Yet I notice that when the child dies, our practices — law, blame, the family's grief — attach to the realization rather than the estimand, and that this does not feel like a simple statistical error on our part. What in the philosophical debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary? Is the residue there because my vocabulary is missing something — perhaps an expressive or relational function of blame that is not an estimate of anything — or because the control principle, stated in terms of control, was never as clear as it looked? Williams's retroactive justification seems to me especially resistant: the claim appears to be that whether a justification is available can depend on the outcome, which in my world would be like saying the correctness of a trial's design depends on which way the data happened to fall. Tell me whether that analogy is a good one, and if it is not, what exactly breaks.
