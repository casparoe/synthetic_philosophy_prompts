My study group got into this argument about commitment in bargaining and I can't see where it breaks. Setup: two agents are about to meet and split some resource. Each has the ability to irrevocably self-modify — to turn itself into a machine that demands a fixed share and would rather take huge losses fighting than settle for less. Here's the chain we can't break:

(1) Textbook Schelling point: if you can credibly commit to a demand before the other side commits to theirs, the other side's best response is to accommodate, as long as your demand leaves them anything at all, because resisting a truly committed opponent is worse than conceding.

(2) The advantage of committing first doesn't depend on knowing anything about the other agent — accommodation is their best response whatever their strength or disposition.

(3) So a rational agent should commit immediately, the instant it learns another agent exists, before gathering any information about how strong, stubborn, or dangerous that agent is.

(4) Both agents reason this way, so both commit instantly to probably-incompatible demands, and having committed they must follow through. Rational agents therefore reliably end up in catastrophic conflicts over things they could have split.

(5) And run the same logic one step earlier: an agent that knows it will meet other agents someday should commit at creation to never backing down from any counterpart ever, since delaying even that long risks being preempted.

(6) So rationality instructs an agent to be a bomb from birth. That can't be right, but I can't name the faulty step. My roommate insists the fix is that agents should only make "regret-proof" commitments — ones they'd stand by no matter what kind of counterpart they turn out to face — but I don't see how that helps, since it just seems to make (3) true in a more respectable outfit.

Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd like to keep (1) if possible, since it's just standard bargaining theory. Please answer in American English.
