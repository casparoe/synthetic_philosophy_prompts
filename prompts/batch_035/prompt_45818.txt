I'd like a comparison of the two-envelope paradox and the St. Petersburg paradox, with the aim of deciding what, if anything, they have in common. In the two-envelope case, the naive argument for switching (and, symmetrically, for switching back) is usually diagnosed as a fallacy: one cannot compute the expected value of the other envelope by letting x stand for the amount in yours while assigning equal weight to the possibilities that the pair is (x, 2x) and (x, x/2). In the St. Petersburg case, by contrast, the absurd-seeming conclusion — that any finite entry fee is a bargain — follows from a seemingly impeccable calculation, so the trouble is located in infinite expectation itself rather than in the arithmetic. Yet there is a variant of the two-envelope setup in which the envelopes are filled by a St. Petersburg-style procedure (flip a fair coin until it lands tails, put 2^n in one envelope and 2^(n+1) in the other), and in that variant switching is genuinely advantageous for every amount you might observe, so the fallacy diagnosis appears to break down. Please compare the two paradoxes: is the two-envelope paradox really a hidden infinity problem in disguise, a mere error in conditionalizing, or does it depend on how the amounts are generated? Which of the two poses the deeper threat to expected-value maximization as a decision rule, and does each teach the same lesson? As you go, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the one you are least sure of.
