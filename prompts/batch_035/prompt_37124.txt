You are a professional philosopher specializing in theories of well-being, and you write with the rigor of someone who both publishes on the literature and designs teaching materials for it. I need you to construct a thought experiment built to order, and I want to see your design reasoning, not just the finished product.

Most experience-machine-style cases are engineered to make audiences distrust mental states: the thought of floating in a tank while believing you are accomplishing things is supposed to generate the gut feeling that something is missing, and hence that well-being is not just a matter of how life feels from the inside. I want a case that runs the intuition in the opposite direction: a scenario in which the audience's spontaneous reaction sides with the hedonist — where the pull is toward the thought that how life seems from the inside settles how well it is going, and the worldly facts appear not to matter. The point is to test whether the experience-machine intuition is really evidence against hedonism or just an artifact of how the cases are constructed.

Specifications the case must satisfy:

1. Realistic setting: a career, a family, a town, ordinary stakes. No machines, no simulations, no demons, no elaborate deception.
2. One paragraph, no longer.
3. The reversal must be generated by the structure of the case, not stipulated. You may not simply declare that the subject's life went badly, and you may not smuggle the verdict in by making the worldly side of the life secretly rotten or by having the subject discover something that retroactively poisons it.
4. The worldly achievements in the case must be genuine goods by objective-list lights: real accomplishments, real relationships, real knowledge. The pressure on the list theorist has to come from the audience's intuition against those goods, not from the case quietly devaluing them. This is the hardest constraint, so check your draft against it explicitly.
5. The subject's hedonic profile should be bad and, in some appropriate sense, available to them throughout — no last-minute reveal.
6. The scenario itself must be readable by a first-year student with no philosophy background: no technical vocabulary inside the paragraph.

Deliverables, in order:

(a) Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios and testing each against specifications 3 and 4. Briefly report which candidates fail and why before committing to one. A case that only appears to satisfy the constraints is worthless to me.

(b) Present the final scenario.

(c) Walk through the verdicts: what hedonism, desire-satisfaction theory, and an objective-list theory each imply about the life in the case, and identify where each theory has to strain to deliver its verdict. Pay attention to whether desire-satisfaction theory can be made to agree with the hedonist here or only forced into an unattractive bullet-bite.

(d) Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the intuition when that feature is removed.

(e) Give the strongest available reply to whichever theorist comes out worst — a reply that explains the intuition away rather than accepting the verdict — and assess honestly whether that reply is principled or ad hoc.

(f) Finally, be frank about whether your case genuinely reverses the experience-machine intuition or merely neutralizes it, and explain why that distinction matters for what the case can legitimately be used to argue.

Think carefully about each specification before drafting; the tension between them is the point.
