You are a professional philosopher, specializing in metaphysics (persistence and identity over time) but comfortable working at the interface of philosophy and heritage conservation law. I want you to think carefully and rigorously: before committing to an answer, brainstorm the candidate reconstructions and test each against the details below rather than reaching for the first theory that fits the label.

Consider the following body of practice, which I'll describe concretely.

International heritage doctrine since the Venice Charter has been organized around the "authenticity" of original fabric: interventions should be minimal, repairs should be distinguishable from original material at close range, conjectural reconstruction is disallowed, and a monument's credentials were long tested against its design, materials, workmanship, and setting. Yet the same institutional world, and the public it serves, routinely treats wholesale replacement as identity-preserving in other cases. Warsaw's Old Town was systematically blown up after the 1944 uprising and then meticulously rebuilt between 1947 and 1953, partly from salvaged bricks and partly from Bellotto's eighteenth-century paintings, and it was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 as the "Historic Centre of Warsaw" — not as a replica of the old town. Japan's Ise Grand Shrine is dismantled and rebuilt from new timber on an adjacent site roughly every twenty years, and both Japan and UNESCO treat the building as a continuously existing thirirteen-hundred-year-old shrine; the resulting friction with Western fabric-based doctrine was one of the pressures that produced the 1994 Nara Document on Authenticity, which widened the notion of authenticity to include the continuity of craft, technique, and tradition. And after the 2019 fire, Notre-Dame was rebuilt with new oak and new vaulting and reopened in 2024, with no serious public dispute that it is the same cathedral that stood there before. Meanwhile, in ordinary national heritage law, a listed building keeps its listing and its legal identity while windows, roofs, timbers, and eventually nearly every visible part are replaced over a century — the practice quietly absorbs the "grandfather's axe" case.

My question: what theory of identity over time does this whole apparatus — doctrine, inscription decisions, and everyday practice — presuppose? Please do the following:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories. Does the fabric-based wing of the doctrine presuppose that a building's identity supervenes on its matter or on spatiotemporal-continuity-with-material-overlap, while the Warsaw and Ise wing presupposes something like a type/token view, a form-continuity view, or a continuation-of-social-function view? Is there a single metaphysics under which both halves are correct, or does the practice only look coherent because "the same building" is being used equivocally — with the equivocation itself doing useful work?

2. Distinguish strict presupposition from good fit. Say which parts of the practice any adequate theory of persistence must license, and which parts are compatible with several rival views (endurantism with overlap, perdurantist worm theory, a "best-candidate" or replica-friendly account, and a thoroughgoing conventionalism on which institutional identity is whatever the listing and inscription bodies say it is). Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — where would two metaphysicians who disagree about persistence nevertheless both shrug and accept the Warsaw inscription?

3. Assess whether the practice actually needs any theory of identity at all. One live possibility is that "authenticity" in heritage discourse is not tracking identity over time but something else: evidential value (original fabric as a record of the past), witness value (a thing that "was there" at an event), or the transmission of skill and intention. If that is right, explain whether the Ship-of-Theseus framing is a mistake imported from the seminar room, or whether the doctrine still commits itself to persistence claims it cannot avoid — for instance in the sentence on a plaque that says this is the shrine founded in the seventh century.

4. Finally, tell me what would have to change in practice under an explicit replacement of the presupposed view. If heritage law openly adopted the best-candidate or replica-friendly view, would full-scale recreations elsewhere (a rebuilt Palmyra, a fabricated "medieval" quarter) become inscribable, and on what grounds would the line still be drawn? If it adopted outright conventionalism, would any inscription decision retain the power to be a mistake — and is a practice that cannot err still making a claim about identity, or merely recording a designation?

Please be concrete: anchor each general point to one of the cases described above, and flag explicitly wherever you think the practice is internally inconsistent rather than merely underdetermined.
