I'm a third-year PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and this year it's my turn as the grad rep on our university's graduate funding committee (nobody else wanted it, lesson learned). We have an actual mess on our hands: central admin announced a mid-year shortfall in the graduate support budget, and we have to find roughly $180k in cuts to the PhD stipend pool before the next admissions cycle. Two live options, since the dean has already ruled out touching anything besides stipends. Option A: rescind funding from three incoming students, i.e. tell them their funded offers are now unfunded spots — saves the whole amount in one move. Option B: spread it — every funded PhD student takes a stipend cut of a bit under 4% plus loses one guaranteed summer. There's also a wrinkle: an alumni donor has offered to restore exactly one of the three rescinded packages, with the recipient to be chosen by lottery if we go with A.

The economist on the committee is pushing A hard, and honestly her strongest point isn't stupid: the total hardship under A is smaller — three students whose plans get wrecked versus a few hundred students who each eat a smallish cut — so any straightforward welfarist math says A, and she asks why we should pretend the 4% doesn't matter just because each instance is small. Meanwhile I just finished TA-ing intro ethics, where we taught Scanlon, and I keep coming back to the aggregation problem: the one student who loses her whole livelihood has a massive complaint, while everyone else's complaint under B is like "I eat out a bit less" or "my summer research trip is gone." The whole contractualist apparatus seems built to say that weak complaints can't just be summed up to defeat a strong one — nobody could reasonably reject the principle behind B, whereas the student defunded under A can obviously reasonably reject a principle that lets her degree (and for at least one of them, her visa, since funding is tied to her status) be sacrificed for tiny gains to others. And the old Taurek-flavored intuition that numbers just shouldn't count is in the back of my head too. But I also don't want to hand the committee hand-waving.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete bottom line — and you're allowed to mix elements, like partial cuts plus some targeted protection for the three, or A-with-lottery if you actually think that's defensible — with the reasoning spelled out in a way committee members who aren't philosophers can follow. Take the economist's aggregation point seriously rather than strawmanning it: address whether some weaker sense of counting numbers (tie-breaks, procedural fairness, threshold effects where small burdens start to matter) can be made compatible with the contractualist framework, or whether I should just concede that the framework is doing less work here than I'd like. It's a committee document, so no footnotes and no name-dropping paragraphs, but I want the actual philosophy load-bearing, not just a gesture at fairness at the end.
