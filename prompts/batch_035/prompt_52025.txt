WHY WORDS ARE LIKE CHEAP CAMERA LENSES

Everyone knows the sand pile thing — take away one grain, still a pile, take away another, still a pile, so by that logic it stays a pile forever, which is absurd. Philosophers call this a sorites argument, I learned that much from a youtube video. Most people meet it with grains of sand and heaps, or hairs on a head and baldness. I meet it every day at my desk, because I restore old photos, and photos are actually the perfect example, better than sand, and I'll explain why.

Sand is vague because nobody bothered to count. But a photo is made of pixels, and pixels are countable — you can point at pixel number 4,502,197 and say exactly what it is. So if there were ever a place where the sorites puzzle should break, this is it. And it doesn't. If I flip one pixel in a portrait of my grandmother from grey to slightly-less-grey, it's still her. Flip another, still her. Flip 200,000 pixels with a blur filter and it's a smudge of a person — arguably not her at all, maybe not even a person. But which pixel was the one? There's no pixel. I've literally watched for it. It's not there.

My theory, which I call the resolution theory, is that this happens because words are like cheap camera lenses. A cheap lens can't resolve detail below a certain size — it just mushes it together. Words are the same. The word "pile" has a certain resolving power, and below that power it genuinely cannot tell the difference between 4,999 grains and 5,000. So when we ask "is 4,999 grains a pile?", the question feels deep but it's actually like asking a cheap lens to read the fine print on a contract — the instrument just isn't built for it. The mush is real, but it's a property of the lens, not of the world. The world itself, I'm pretty sure, has no mush in it whatsoever.

If that's right, then here's my main claim: vagueness is always and only a language problem, never a world problem. There is no object anywhere in the universe that is "sort of" a pile the way there are objects that are sort of wet. Wetness is in the world; pile-ness is in the lens.

And this has a practical upshot I like. Since the mush comes from us, we can decide where to put the edge. When the law says you're an adult at 18, that's not an approximation of a hidden natural fact — 18 is fine, it's just where we decided to focus the lens. Some people find that scary, that there's no fact of the matter, but I find it freeing. The alternative, that there's some real boundary out there in nature between adult and non-adult, seems way weirder.

One more thought and then I'm done. My resolution theory suggests that words with more resolving power should have less sorites trouble. And that checks out! "Grandmother" doesn't have this problem nearly as much as "pile" — either someone is your mother's mother or they aren't, and a blurry photo of a grandmother is still exactly as much a grandmother as a sharp one. Compare "bald," where one hair matters in the argument but not at all in reality. So words come with different pixel counts, basically, and the sorites puzzle is just what it feels like when you push a low-resolution word past what it can resolve.
