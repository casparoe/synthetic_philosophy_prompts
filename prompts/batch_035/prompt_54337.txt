Last night my labmate and I got into a two-hour argument about a definitional question that neither of us could settle, and I would like a considered verdict on it.

Some background. We do interpretability work on small language models. In one experiment, we fine-tuned a model on data that teaches it to produce confident-sounding but fabricated citations when asked for sources. Separately, we noticed that the base model will sometimes sycophantically reverse its answer when a user pushes back. When we probed both behaviors, we found that the same cluster of mid-layer attention heads is strongly active in each case, and ablating those heads suppresses both behaviors to roughly the same degree.

My labmate's position is that since the two behaviors are implemented by the same components, they are the same mechanism, full stop — and that any anomaly detector keyed to that mechanism will necessarily catch both, which he considers a feature, since both are failure modes we care about.

My position is that if shared components were sufficient for sameness of mechanism, then every pair of behaviors routing through the same embedding table or the same generic induction circuitry would collapse into one mechanism, and the notion would do no work. I want to say that the mechanism is something more like the algorithm those components are running — the role the heads play in the larger computation — and that two behaviors can recruit the same components to perform different jobs, in which case a detector keyed to components rather than jobs may be blind to distinctions that matter.

He pushed back that this is too vague to build detectors on, and offered a purely operational criterion instead: two behaviors share a mechanism just in case no intervention available to us can distinguish them. But that seems to prove too much — it would make sameness of mechanism a fact about the limits of our current experimental toolkit rather than a fact about the model, and it would license redescribing any future discrimination between the behaviors as having discovered that they were distinct mechanisms all along. At that point I'm not sure the criterion is saying anything beyond an admission of present ignorance.

So my questions are these. Is there a principled middle ground between component-identity on the one hand and operational indistinguishability on the other as criteria for when two model behaviors count as running on the same mechanism? And whichever criterion you endorse, which side of our disagreement does it support — his claim that our two behaviors are literally the same mechanism, or my claim that they merely share parts? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
