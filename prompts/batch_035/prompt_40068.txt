I've co-taught a grad seminar on language acquisition with a linguist colleague for six years now, and every year we hit the same wall around week five, when we get to poverty of the stimulus arguments. The room splits in a way that's become predictable enough to be depressing. On one side the linguistics students, who think the argument is basically sound: kids don't get enough data to fix structure-dependent rules like auxiliary inversion, or the binding constraints, so something about the prior has to carry the load. On the other the ML students, who think the whole thing was a complaint about 1960s learning theory that transformer models retired: a big enough model trained on corpora statistically rich enough learns plenty that Chomsky thought had to be innate. Then we spend three weeks at cross purposes and the semester ends with everyone exactly where they started, just more annoyed.

The thing is, I've stopped being able to tell what the disagreement actually is, and I'd rather not teach it as a settled historical episode either way. Some of it might be verbal -- nobody in the room actually disagrees that a learner needs some bias to get from finite data to a grammar, and if "innate" just means "prior," the argument evaporates into something trivially true. Some of it might be a hidden factual fight about what the actual input to children looks like, where the corpus people have genuinely moved things even though each side keeps citing the same few papers as if the other hadn't read them. And some of it, I suspect, is two questions under one heading: what the child's grammar ends up looking like versus what class of learners can learn what from what data, which the ML students treat as the only real question and the linguistics students treat as a non-question. There's also the nagging sense that the disagreement is really about burden of proof: who has to show what, and what would even count as a demonstration. A distributional learning result showing the input is statistically rich enough feels decisive to half the room and like changing the subject to the other half, and I want to know whether that's because they're using different standards of evidence or because one side is actually wrong on the merits.

So, as a diagnosis exercise and not an adjudication: what kind of disagreement is this, concretely, given what the two sides actually say? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a learnability result, better corpora, a cleaner distinction between contentful and contentless nativism, something I haven't listed -- or should I expect the wall to still be there in year twelve? I'd like to know whether to redesign the seminar around the diagnosis or just stop teaching the unit.
