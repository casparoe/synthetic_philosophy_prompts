Longtime lurker on the rationalist forums here. I keep getting into arguments in the comments about whether you can assign a probability to an "if" statement — you know the genre, someone says "p(if we get AGI by 2030, we're all dead) = 20%" and someone else replies that "if" statements don't have probabilities at all. So I finally went and found a blog post that explains the whole thing via David Lewis, and it mostly made sense, but something about it smells off and I can't put my finger on what. Here's the relevant passage, roughly as it appeared:

"Lewis proved back in the seventies that indicative conditionals can't have probabilities. The idea he was attacking — called Adams' Thesis, and it seems obviously right on its face — is that the probability of an 'if' statement should equal the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent: 'if it rains tomorrow, the picnic is off' should have exactly the same probability as P(picnic off | rain). Lewis showed that if you insist on this, the only way it can work is if nothing anywhere has a probability strictly between 0 and 1 — everything has to be either certainly true or certainly false. Since that's absurd, conditionals just aren't the sort of thing that can be more or less likely, which is also why Bayesians treat 'if' statements as inference rules or dispositions rather than claims, and why you can never coherently bet on an 'if'."

Is this a fair summary? Please go through it bit by bit and tell me what a philosopher working on conditionals would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong. I'm most suspicious of the last two claims — treating conditionals as rules rather than statements sounds like a philosophical choice someone might make, not something a theorem forces, and the betting thing seems off given people bet on "if X then Y" all the time. But I genuinely don't know whether the middle part — the triviality result itself — is being described accurately: is the "nothing between 0 and 1" thing what Lewis actually showed, or a garbled version of it, and is the result supposed to hold for one probability function or for all of them? That distinction seems like it might matter a lot but I'm not sure I've even got it right. Finally: if parts of the summary are wrong, does the comment-section conclusion — that writing "p(if X, Y) = 20%" is always meaningless — follow from the correct version of Lewis's result, or is there a sensible reading of that sentence that survives it?
