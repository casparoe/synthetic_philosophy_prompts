Last month I attended a workshop on long-term risks from AI, and during the evening session two of the researchers, Lena and Marco, got into an extended argument about which suffering risk (s-risk) deserves the most attention and whether moral advocacy can reduce it. By s-risk they meant risks of outcomes involving astronomical amounts of suffering — for example, a powerful actor permanently locking in values that are indifferent to suffering, or large-scale conflict between actors with incompatible moral views. Their exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Lena argued that the dominant s-risk is value lock-in under competitive pressure: if some actor — a government, lab, or coalition — gains a decisive strategic advantage and settles the future's values early, and those values are indifferent to suffering, then vast suffering gets locked in by ordinary neglect rather than malice. The remedy is to spread concern for suffering broadly before that critical point, so that whoever ends up shaping the future already cares about it.

(2) Marco replied that aggressively spreading one view is itself a route to the s-risk he worries about: value conflict. If the future contains blocs with rival moral priorities — say one that treats total welfare as what matters, another that treats suffering as dominating the moral calculus — each bloc can come to regard the other's preferred future as a moral catastrophe, and the suffering-focused bloc in particular might one day accept creating suffering (as a deterrent, or to avert what it sees as a still worse outcome) as the lesser evil. On his view, the more successful advocacy is at entrenching a strong version of suffering-focused ethics, the more likely that configuration becomes.

(3) Lena said this threat requires the suffering-focused side to be both powerful and willing to trade created suffering against a perceived catastrophe — an unusual combination, since people who center suffering in ethics are typically also strongly averse to creating it, so the view is largely self-limiting. Lock-in through indifference, by contrast, needs no such unusual psychology: indifference is the default state of value systems that simply optimize for something else.

(4) Marco answered that "self-limiting" is doing the same work as "the market will correct itself": it assumes the temperament of today's small community survives being scaled up and reinterpreted by strangers. What matters is not what the founders of a movement meant, but what the most strategically placed reader of the spread doctrine concludes when combining the principle that suffering dominates the calculus with an actual strategic situation.

I couldn't tell who was right and didn't want to pick a side on the spot. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is sound, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. End your response with a final line of the form "Verdict: ..." containing one short phrase naming who is more right overall.
