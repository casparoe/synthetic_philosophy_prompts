is there an established name for this thing I keep noticing? When a well-practiced multi-step routine gets interrupted partway through, the person (or machine) doesn't resume where they left off — they get yanked back to the very beginning of the routine and rerun the whole thing, including the steps that are already done. Examples: I drive the same route to work every day, and if I actually need to stop at the pharmacy on the way, I'll drive straight past it unless I make a deliberate effort, sometimes only "waking up" when I'm parking at work with no memory of the drive. My wife, if one step of her coffee ritual gets disturbed (someone asks her a question mid-pour), will sometimes dump it out and start over. At work there's a woman in our lab who, if step 6 of a prep fails, redoes the entire prep from step 1 even though steps 1-5 succeeded and nothing about them changed — and she says basically everyone in the lab does this. Even the self-checkout machine at my grocery store seems to do a version of it: if the scale glitches mid-transaction it restarts the whole order rather than continuing.

The interesting bit to me is that this doesnt seem to be a dumbness thing. My lab coworker is genuinely brilliant, very flexible in arguments and in emergencies, but these grooves are still there. So it's like the routine gets compiled into one indivisible unit: the trigger starts the whole loop, and the sub-steps aren't individually addressable, only the finished product is. Interrupt the middle and there's no "resume" instruction, only "run."

So my questions: does this have an established name anywhere — ethology, psychology of skill, robotics, philosophy of action, wherever? If it does, how exactly is it distinguished from plain habit strength, from compulsion, and from the everyday "autopilot" metaphor? And if nothing fits exactly, I'd want you to coin a term, define it, and draw the boundary cases — e.g. when does rerunning-from-the-start stop being this and become just sensible rechecking of your work, or normal caution? Also, do you think the phenomenon says anything about free will arguments? The part I can't stop chewing on is that a system can be maximally flexible everywhere else and still have these rigid blind grooves, which seems like it complicates the usual picture where rigidity and flexibility trade off against each other.
