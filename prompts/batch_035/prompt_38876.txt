I teach an intro ethics course and I'm getting sick of the "is morality just convention?" discussion going nowhere because students use the word "convention" to mean like six different things. So I want to build a sorting instrument for a specific slice of this: everyday local conventions like tipping, queuing, jaywalking, dress codes, removing shoes indoors. The question I want the instrument to sort on: are these things genuine moral obligations, and must visitors/immigrants follow the local version of them even when their own society does it differently?

Please write me a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz, 8-10 questions, each a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, plus a scoring key. The cases should be specific, like: a tourist in a country where tipping is actively seen as insulting who tips anyway out of habit; queue-jumping in a totally empty store where nobody will ever know; jaywalking at 3am on a completely dead street in a jurisdiction where it's illegal; a local who refuses to tip in the US on principle because tipping culture itself is unjust; someone who follows a table-manners convention in a way that visibly signals contempt for people who don't know it. Stuff like that. No question should just ask "do you think conventions are morally binding" -- the whole point is that the cases do the sorting, not the labels.

For the scoring key, build it from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names, but do place the recognizable positions in it as leaves. The distinctions I care about are at least: (1) whether the source of any obligation is the convention itself, the effects on people, the unfairness of free-riding on others' compliance, or something about respect/disrespect toward the community; (2) whether the obligation holds even when violation harms no one at all (the dead street, the empty queue); (3) whether the obligation on visitors is different in kind from the obligation on locals, or just different in strength; (4) whether an unjust convention can still generate an obligation (the tipping one). Leaves should include at least a pure coordination view (conventions generate no moral obligations whatsoever, only prudential reasons), a harm-based view where the obligation tracks effects, a fairness/free-rider view, a respect-based view, and a "mere etiquette, always" view. Also do this: after the scoring key, note which combinations of answers don't map onto any leaf and say whether you think nobody holds that position because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your questions can't actually separate no matter how someone answers.

Keep it usable by freshmen -- no assumed background, don't lean on any particular philosopher's terminology beyond maybe a word or two defined inline. If you want to throw in one question that's a deliberately hard case where two of the views give the same answer, that's fine, but mark it as such in the key.
