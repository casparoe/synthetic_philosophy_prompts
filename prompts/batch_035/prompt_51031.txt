i'm a phil of mind PhD student and i've pretty much adopted the view from Dennett's Atlantic piece on counterfeit people: current LLMs are counterfeit persons -- they pass as people without being people, the way counterfeit money passes as money. On this view they're not proto-agents or nascent moral patients; they're mimicry all the way down, and the interesting ethical question is what they do to *us*, not what we owe them.

Tell me what would count against this view. Not just the obvious stuff -- I want the specific observations, arguments, and intuitions that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus what a committed Dennettian would concede as genuinely counting against the position vs. what they'd wave off. A couple of things to dig into specifically: (1) the intentional-stance tension. If having beliefs and desires is just being a system whose behavior is best predicted by the intentional stance, why doesn't that apply to LLMs already? Dennett gestures at "real" agents having something at stake, but is that a principled line or an ad hoc patch? (2) something i keep chewing on and can't find discussed anywhere: could a system be counterfeit at the level of every individual token/response yet genuine at the level of a whole conversation -- is there a scale at which sustained mimicry stops being mimicry, the way (maybe) enough good fake money in circulation just becomes the currency? And (3) whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by now -- and if so, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or is it like other philosophical claims where insulation is just what maturity looks like?
