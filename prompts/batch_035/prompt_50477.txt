I'm a grad student presenting at an interdisciplinary decision theory workshop next month, and my talk is about Good's theorem — the old result that the expected value of getting free information is never negative. I'm not just defending the standard version though. My move is to argue that even once you allow that agents care about their own beliefs intrinsically (painful truths, blissful ignorance about incurable diagnoses, all that), the value of information stays nonnegative as long as the agent has the option, decided on in advance, to look away or ignore what they learn. So something like: no rational agent pays to avoid relevant information, even counting the pain of believing as part of the outcomes. I think this is basically right but I have a nagging feeling the "option to ignore" move has holes in it.

The audience is a grab bag: two hard-core Bayesians who think the whole theorem is trivial and will push on exactly where the proof uses the expected utility axioms; one person from psych who studies information avoidance empirically (people declining genetic test results etc.) and is skeptical that any theorem says anything about actual humans; a visiting economist who works on ambiguity and Ellsberg-style preferences and, I'm told, definately has a bee in her bonnet about non-Bayesian agents paying to resolve uncertainty; a postdoc into the ergodicity economics stuff who will ask whether a time-average rather than ensemble-average agent still gets nonnegative value of information; and an older theorist who I suspect will bring up multi-stage problems with changing preferences — the thing where a time-inconsistent agent can be made worse off by learning about future temptations, since my "ignore it later" move maybe just recreates the problem one step back. There may also be someone who cares about information you're forced to disclose (auctions, screening), though I'd been treating that as out of scope.

Please build my Q&A prep sheet in American English as a JSON object with the following fields. "objections": an array of 8 or so items, each an object with the fields "objection" (phrased the way that audience member would actually say it, in their vocabulary), "asker" (which of the people above, or "unsure"), "damage" (one of "fatal", "serious", "annoying"), "reply" (what I say in about two minutes), "weak_spot" (where my reply is softest), and "concede" (what I should just grant up front). "hoped_nobody_asks": the single objection I'm most hoping doesn't come up, with the same fields plus "why_i_dread_it". And "one_liner": the sentence-length version of my view that I need everyone to leave the room believing. Order the objections by damage. Don't go easy on me on the forced-disclosure and changing-preferences stuff just because I called it out of scope — tell me honestly whether the option-to-ignore move handles those or whether they're genuine counterexamples I need to fence off, and if you think my central move is just wrong, say so in "why_i_dread_it" rather than pretending otherwise.
