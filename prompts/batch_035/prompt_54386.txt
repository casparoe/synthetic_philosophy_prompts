Been thinking about when someone "has a reason" to do something, and I've got a list of cases where I have pretty firm gut reactions, but I can't find the pattern behind them. Here's the list with my verdicts, and I want to know if there's one principle that captures all of it, or whether some of my verdicts just have to go.

1. My aunt is eating soup that's been poisoned, and she doesn't know. She has no desire whatsoever to stop eating — she's enjoying it. Gut says: she has a reason to stop, right now, even before anyone tells her about the poison.

2. My coworker Dave has smoked for twenty years. When you press him he admits he wishes he could quit, and he's tried twice, but day to day he tells himself he likes smoking and doesn't want to stop. Gut says: Dave has a reason to quit.

3. An old boss of mine cared about exactly one thing, getting promoted. He genuinely didn't care whether the people under him suffered — not pretending, just absent. Humiliating a report in a meeting got him ahead and he never felt a thing about it. Gut says... I honestly go back and forth. Some days I say he had a reason not to do it, other days I say there was just nothing there to hang a reason on.

4. A friend has never listened to opera and says she'd hate it. I'm pretty sure that if she actually went she'd become obsessed with it. Gut says: she has a reason to go, but it feels like a weaker kind of reason than the poisoned soup one, and I can't explain why.

5. During a bad depressive episode my sister cared about nothing at all — not in a "had other priorities" way, just flat. Did she have reasons to take a walk, call a friend, things she couldn't feel at the time? Gut says yes, but when I say it I suspect I'm just describing the reasons she'd have once the episode lifted, and I don't know if that's the same thing.

So is there a single principle here? My half-formed thought is something like: you have a reason when you'd want the thing under better information or sounder thinking. But then case 3 is the problem — does "sounder thinking" include coming to care about other people? And if it does, isn't that just me smuggling my own values into what counts as sound? I'd rather you tell me straight which verdict to drop (case 3 is the one I'd sacrifice first) than build something baroque that saves all five. And if you do end up with a principle, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — e.g. someone who, no matter how much they reflect, will never come to care about their own future self: reasons now, or not?

One more thing. I know there's a real live debate in philosophy about whether reasons can come from outside anything you'd ever want or come to want, and I do NOT want you to pick a side in that debate and hand it to me as if it were common sense. Please mark clearly which parts of your answer are pretty much settled among people who know this stuff and which parts are genuinely contested, and if my verdicts themselves force a contested choice, say so instead of pretending it's obvious. Also, small thing, if you could keep it plain-language — I've never studied this, I just think about it on my commute.
