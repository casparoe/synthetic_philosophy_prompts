Write a dialogue between two philosophers — call them Ada and Ben — who disagree about how to resolve the paradox of fiction. The paradox arises from three individually plausible claims: (i) readers and audiences feel real emotions toward fictional characters — they fear for the heroine and grieve when a character dies; (ii) emotions of these kinds ordinarily require belief that their objects exist and that the relevant events are actually occurring or have occurred; and (iii) audiences do not believe that fictional characters exist or that the events they are witnessing are really happening.

Ada defends a make-believe view: while engaging with a fiction, audience members make-believe that they believe its content, and what they undergo are quasi-emotions — genuine physiological stirrings and felt experiences, but not full-fledged emotions, because they lack the belief component and the associated action tendencies. Ben defends the thought theory: emotions can be generated by evaluative thoughts alone, and belief in the existence of the object is not a necessary condition of emotion, so the tears shed for a fictional heroine are genuine grief, no different in kind from tears shed at a funeral.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's actual arguments, pressing, conceding, or refining where appropriate. Neither should be a strawman; each should be given their strongest lines.

The characters should test their views against the following cases:

1. The non-intervention datum. No one in the audience jumps on stage to warn Othello, and no one calls the police during a crime drama. Ada offers this as evidence that fictional emotions lack the action tendencies of real ones; Ben must explain why genuine fear would be compatible with remaining seated.

2. Physiological and behavioral responses. Audiences cry, grip their armrests, and exhibit elevated heart rates. Ben argues these are the signature of genuine emotion; Ada replies that startle responses at horror films show only that quasi-emotions can mimic emotion's physiology.

3. A retrospective-revision case. A listener follows a serialized audio drama for months, is moved to tears by the narrator's loss in the final episode, and only afterward learns that the drama was a lightly fictionalized account of real events — the narrator a real person, the loss a real one. She reports that her tears do not feel retroactively upgraded; if anything, she is disturbed that she had treated something real as a toy. Each theory must say what her tears were at the time she shed them, and whether the new information changes their status.

4. A symmetry case. At a film festival, two viewers watch the same dramatic reenactment of a mining disaster. One knows it is a reenactment; the other, having missed the disclaimer, takes it for documentary footage. Their reactions appear identical. Ben argues that if the two emotional responses are identical in kind, and the second viewer's are genuine, then so are the first viewer's. Ada must say whether one viewer had a quasi-emotion toward a real disaster while the other had a quasi-emotion toward a fiction — or whether the difference collapses — and what that implies for her view.

5. A knowing-spoiler case. A reader who has been told how a novel ends reads the final chapters with a dread she describes as stronger than any she has felt at genuinely bad news in her own life. Both characters should consider whether this intensity comparison — weighing putatively fictional dread against remembered real dread — is even intelligible, and if so, what it shows.

The dialogue should end not with a winner but with each character stating, as precisely as they can, the strongest surviving objection to their own view. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. Do not cite or name specific philosophers; the characters should argue from the cases and the reasoning alone.
