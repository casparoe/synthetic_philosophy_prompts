I've drafted notes for a short essay on the two-envelope paradox, and I'd be grateful for comments, particularly on whether the central argument is sound. The notes are as follows.

One of two envelopes contains twice as much money as the other; you don't know which is which. Standard presentation of the paradox: you open your envelope, see some amount x, and reason that the other envelope is equally likely to contain 2x or x/2, so its conditional expectation is 5x/4 — you should switch. But the same reasoning would have applied whatever x you saw, and by symmetry it can't be that switching benefits you regardless of which envelope you initially hold.

The standard resolution I sketch first: the 50/50 conditional cannot hold for every x under any proper prior with finite mean. Correctly computed, the expected gain from switching conditional on holding x is positive for small x and negative for large x, averaging out to zero; the fallacy is treating the pair (x/2, x) and (x, 2x) as equally likely for all x simultaneously.

The essay's central claim, on which I most want comments, is that this resolution is incomplete. If the prior distribution of the smaller amount has infinite mean, there are proper priors for which the expected value of the other envelope conditional on this one containing x exceeds x for every x that could occur. In that case the conditional argument for switching, far from being an arithmetic error, is correct at every value of x, and the tension between it and the unconditional symmetry argument becomes genuine rather than illusory. My notes suggest this shows the paradox is not merely a botched calculation but a breakdown of the assumption that unconditional and conditional valuations must cohere, and that the philosophically interesting question is what normative force the symmetry argument retains once the conditional case for switching is everywhere correct.

My specific questions: (1) Is the infinite-mean claim right — can such priors really exist, and does the conditional advantage at every x indeed follow? (2) Does this observation strengthen the standard resolution (by showing exactly when it applies) or undermine it? (3) Is the diagnosis in terms of a clash between unconditional and conditional valuations the right framing, or is the infinite-mean case better described as one in which expected value simply ceases to guide action, so that no real conflict remains? I'd also welcome comments on whether the essay would be more interesting if reorganised around question (3).

Please respond in British English. Conclude with a final line of the form "Verdict: sound", "Verdict: unsound", or "Verdict: partially sound", giving your overall assessment of the essay's central claim about the infinite-mean case.
