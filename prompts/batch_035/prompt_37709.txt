I'm on a hiring committee and I also write reccomendation letters for students, and it just hit me how philosophically weird the whole institution is. We ask someone who watched a person in one narrow context (our classes, our lab, one semester) to predict how they'll behave in a totally different one (some other employer's workplace, five years from now), and we treat a strong letter as genuine evidence. Meanwhile I've been reading about the situationist stuff in social psych — the Milgram experiments, the seminary students who stepped over a guy slumped in a doorway because they were told they were running late for a talk, and the old Hartshorne and May studies where kids who cheated on one test barely cheated more than anyone else on the next one. If behavior is that context-dependent, what theory of character do reference letters actually presuppose?

Like, are we implicitly committed to robust global traits — "honesty", "conscientiousness" — that transfer across wildly different situations? Or could something weaker do the work: maybe traits exist but are indexed to context, so the letter only tells me about the slice of the person that shows up in a classroom-shaped situation, and I'm extrapolating on vibes? And then the part I really want your read on: letters have inflated so much that nowdays "good" reads as mediocre, so the real signal is in deviations from the inflation baseline. Does that arms race presuppose anything further, like a shared background understanding of which traits matter and how they're distributed, or is it just noise on noise?

Also try to seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits well with — e.g., maybe it works fine under global traits AND under dense-but-local traits as long as the writer's context resembles the target context, and only breaks under full-blown situationism. And tell me what would actually have to change in the practice if global traits turned out not to exist: do we ditch letters, or just ask different questions of the writers? Where you think the practice is underdetermined — compatible with several rival theories of character — say so explicitly instead of forcing one reconstruction.

Structure it however makes sense, but end with a single line starting "Verdict:" that gives your one-sentence answer to whether letters of recommendation can survive situationism as honest evidence, and if so under what reading of them.
