I've been reading a lot about societal collapse, mostly archaeology-adjacent stuff, and I've sort of assembled a set of positions on it over the past year that I want to stress-test before I write it up for our little history-of-civilization discussion group. Here's roughly where I stand: (1) I think the classic collapse narratives are mostly debunked at this point — the Maya didn't vanish, the southern lowland cities lost their kings but people stayed and the northern ones like Chichen Itza carried on (and then Mayapan after that), and on Rapa Nui I'm pretty convinced by the revisionists who say the palms were destroyed by Polynesian rats long before people got to the deforestation-ecocide story, and that the real demographic crash came with European contact, disease and slave raids, not with eating their own island. (2) I buy Tainter's framework in The Collapse of Complex Societies: societies invest in complexity, the marginal returns on each new layer of bureaucracy, standing army, or elite diminish, and collapse is often not a malfunction but a rational simplification — the load-bearing provinces basically deciding the empire isn't worth the taxes. (3) Relatedly, I think for a lot of ordinary people in a lot of cases, the aftermath wasn't worse: the health and nutrition data after the western Roman pullout in some regions arguably improves, and "collapse of states" often just means "collapse of the elite's ability to extract." (4) Methodologically I think "collapse" should be defined neutrally — loss of population, of institutional continuity, of complexity, however you want to measure it, but not loaded up with doom language, since whether a transition is a failure depends on who you are in the society. But then, (5) I also genuinely believe we today are in a different and more dangerous position, because globalization has synchronized everything — supply chains, financial systems, crop monocultures, everyone running the same institutional "software" — so that failures that used to stay regional can now cascade together, and the historical record of societies surviving or bouncing back from shocks doesn't transfer to us.

The thing I want to know is whether this package hangs together, because when I try to argue (5) to my friends, they throw (2) and (3) back at me: if collapse is usually adaptive rebalancing and often fine for the peasants, why is our coming collapse a catastrophe rather than the same overdue simplification? And if it's different for us, what exactly is the difference — is it the synchrony point, is it that we've nowhere to simplify *to* since there's no periphery left, or am I just doing special pleading for the present? On the other side, when I push my skeptical line on (1), someone said that if I'm right that Easter Island's real destruction came from outside contact rather than internal dynamics, that's actually evidence that isolated systems get wrecked by external shocks they didn't evolve defenses for — which, if anything, makes me *more* worried about (5), not less, since globalization is basically permanent contact with no buffer anywhere. I can't tell if that's a fair point or a sophism. So: go through (1) through (5), tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the tensions by severity, and for each real one tell me what has to give and what I lose by giving it. I don't want a lecture on collapse generally, I want an audit of my specific commitments. And be honest if the whole edifice is just "the past was fine, but we're doomed" dressed up in archaeology.
