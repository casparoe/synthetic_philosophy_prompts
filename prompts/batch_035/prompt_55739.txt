In a discussion forum on historical counterfactuals, a thread about whether the outcome of a particular battle would have changed had a single messenger been delayed was closed by another member reciting "For want of a nail the shoe was lost; for want of the shoe the horse was lost," and concluding that since history turns on such minute contingencies, asking "would it have happened anyway?" is pointless: the answer is always no, and anyone who calls a historical outcome "bound to happen" is making a category error. The leap felt wrong to me, but I could not pin down where. I'd like you to take the proverb apart.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses "for want of a nail" could be expressing, ordered from least to most demanding. At a minimum, consider the following candidates, and add others if you see them:

(a) that some historical outcomes have causally necessary small antecedents;
(b) that for most interesting historical outcomes, one can find small antecedents without which the outcome would not have occurred as it did;
(c) that macro-history behaves like a chaotic system, in the technical sense of sensitive dependence on initial conditions — small differences at one time grow, so that the trajectory of events is unstable at every scale;
(d) that small contingencies matter chiefly by selecting between discrete, self-sustaining equilibria that then lock in (path dependence), rather than by continuous amplification of the sort described in (c);
(e) that the specific course of history is dominated by contingency rather than by structural forces — geography, economics, demography — so that explanations of the form "the outcome was overdetermined by deeper causes" are generally mistaken;
(f) that counterfactual questions about history are ill-posed in principle, because the nearest possible world in which the nail was present diverges so far from ours that the question loses its target.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contentious, or false; and say for each whether it is primarily a claim about dynamics, about evidence, or about the semantics of counterfactuals. Note explicitly that readings (c) and (d) are empirically distinguishable — amplification implies that rerunning history from a slightly perturbed state yields a wide spread of outcomes, while lock-in implies that perturbations early on matter enormously and later ones barely at all — and address which pattern, if either, we should expect of history.

Then analyze my interlocutor's argument: which reading did his conclusion actually need, and does any reading strong enough to support it remain plausible? Flag explicitly any equivocation between adjacent readings that does the argument's work — I suspect the move from (b), which is nearly undeniable, to (f), which is radical, happens unnoticed.
