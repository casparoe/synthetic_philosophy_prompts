I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of public policy, and this term's capstone event is an extended interview with a visiting scholar who has spent her career defending a fairly austere version of libertarian paternalism. Her view, as she presents it, is that choice architecture is permissible only when three conditions jointly hold: the intervention is transparent at the moment of choice; it can be resisted at negligible cost; and it improves the chooser's welfare as the chooser herself would judge it on reflection, with full information and without the distorting effects of the very impulse the intervention targets. She is also — and this is what makes her unusual — willing to bite a bullet she calls menu trimming: she holds that it can be permissible to remove a tempting but welfare-reducing option from the menu altogether, provided the option is restored on request and the cost of asking is deliberately made trivial. My students will have read her work alongside the standard objections and are primed to be either converts or hecklers, and I want my moderation to model something better than either.

Draft ten to twelve interview questions that press on the actual content of her position rather than its slogans. I want at least one question in each of the following areas, and for each of those, a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

1. The welfare condition. Whether her third condition can be made determinate without either collapsing into the chooser's actual preferences (adaptive, ill-informed, or otherwise) or quietly substituting the choice architect's judgment for the chooser's — and if she appeals to reflection, what fixes the reflection's output well enough to license an intervention before it occurs.

2. Transparency. Whether her transparent-at-the-moment-of-choice condition can survive the empirical literature suggesting the most effective nudges often work precisely because they operate below the level of conscious attention; and whether retreating to something like transparency-on-inquiry — the nudge is discoverable if you look — abandons a constraint the view needed, or merely acknowledges that we never notice most of the architecture anyway.

3. Menu trimming. Whether restorable-on-request is a nudge at all rather than a soft shove with an exit hatch; what principled line, if any, separates her trimmed menu from an outright ban with an exemption process; and whether the trivial cost of asking is doing all the libertarian work while being exactly the thing no one in the states she targets will ever incur.

4. Political economy. Whether the machinery she designs can be kept out of the hands of governments and firms who will use it for ends that fail her own conditions — whether, that is, she is designing for a world that does not exist; and what her view says about the nudges actually deployed in the wild, which routinely fail her third condition. Are those abuses of the framework, or the predictable output of the incentive structure the framework legitimates?

Finally, close with two questions I can hold in reserve for the last stretch of the hour: one that asks her to name the strongest single objection she cannot fully answer, and one that asks what empirical or institutional development would make her abandon the view outright. Please keep the register professional and non-combative — the questions should be sharp enough that a good answer to them would be a real philosophical achievement, not gotchas.
