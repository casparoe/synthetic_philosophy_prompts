Something's been bugging me about how IP law treats people who come up with the same thing seperately. If two songwriters independently write the exact same melody, that's fine — copyright only punishes copying, so if you can show you never heard the other song, you both keep your copyright. But if I independently invent something that someone already patented, I'm still infringing, even if I never read their patent and genuinely came up with it from nothing. Same legal system, opposite rules. So what's the underlying picture of ideas there? It almost seems like copyright treats a piece of expression as something you make — as if there are plenty of possible melodies to go around, so whoever makes one owns it — while patent law treats an invention as something already sitting out there waiting to be found, more like land, where staking a claim first cuts everyone else off. Is that the actual presupposition? It seems to bake in an assumption about which ideas are scarce and which kinds of creative work are substitutable for each other, which feels more like economics than a theory of ownership.

Also I can't tell whether the rules really need this picture, or whether it's just compatible with it — maybe a straightforwardly utilitarian story about incentives would produce the same rules without any view about discovery vs. creation at all. And what would actually have to change in the practice if the presupposition were flipped — say, if we decided inventions really are more like authored works, or that expressions are closer to discoveries? Would an independent-inventor defense have to appear in patent law, or would the independent-songwriter defense have to disappear from copyright?

Whatever verdict you land on, spell out where your reconstruction is on the shakiest ground — which argument or consideration, if someone pushed back with it, would most likely make you change your mind about the implicit theory you're attributing to the system. Don't be confident just because the pattern looks neat.
