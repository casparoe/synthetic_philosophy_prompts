I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were being posted serially, and for the better part of a decade my standing debate partner — I'll call her Anna — and I have been arguing about uploading and personal identity. We've finally converged to the point where her verdicts on the standard thought experiments are stable, and before our next round I want to predict her verdict on a new case rather than simply re-litigating labels. Here is her track record, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

1. Neuron-by-neuron replacement with functionally identical silicon, at a rate of about one percent of the remaining biological neurons per week over a decade, with the brain awake and running throughout: you survive.
2. Destructive scan followed by whole-brain emulation, the whole procedure taking an afternoon: that is death. The emulation is a new person who wakes up sincerely believing she is you.
3. Non-destructive scan, with the emulation living on in silicon while you keep your biological brain: the emulation is not you, though she's a perfectly real person who matters morally, with your memories up to the scan.
4. Cryonics done right — brain vitrified with fine structure preserved, no activity whatsoever for a century, then revival in the repaired original body: you survive. Suspension is just a long night.
5. Dreamless anesthesia: survival, trivially.
6. The same slow replacement as in (1), except each replacement chip is printed from a destructive scan of the individual neuron it replaces — the neuron is scanned and destroyed on day one, the chip is fabricated overnight, and it's installed the following day, so each replaced neuron has a one-day functional gap while the rest of the brain continues running. She says you survive this too, and she has never cared whether the procedure is reversible.

Next month's argument concerns something someone floated on the forum, which I've been calling the lockstep migration. Over roughly three years, one neuron at a time is scanned while live, and its emulation is brought up on remote hardware running in perfect lockstep with the biological original. For one week, both run in parallel: the emulation is wired into the rest of the brain through the standard replacement interface, while the biological neuron is isolated in a support bath, metabolically maintained but disconnected. At the end of the week the biological neuron is destroyed. At every instant, exactly one copy of each neuron's function is connected into the whole; the transition is about as gradual as anything in (1); and at the end the mind is running entirely on remote hardware, the biological tissue having been dissolved piecemeal along the way.

Three requests. First, predict Anna's verdict on the lockstep migration — but the real work is in the rules underlying her track record. I can see at least two candidates that fit everything above: a rate-and-branching rule (survival iff the transition is gradual and never produces two successors at the whole-brain level) and a causal-takeover rule (survival iff each new component assumes its function within the still-running system, with the original system's overall operation never being handed off to a newly instantiated whole). I suspect there are others — perhaps something about token continuity of the physical system, which is what her cryonics verdict hints at — and I'd like you to lay out the full candidate set and what each says about the lockstep case, since I believe they genuinely diverge there, though you should tell me if I'm wrong about that.

Second, give me your best guess as to her actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring it over the rivals, and be honest about how confident the track record really lets you be.

Third, I get to propose the following argument after this one, so give me the single further case that would best discriminate between the candidate rules — ideally something that probes their real structure rather than a rerun of the lockstep case with one parameter dialed up. And if you conclude that her verdicts are jointly inconsistent, or consistent only with a rule she'd refuse to endorse once it was stated plainly, say so plainly yourself: I would rather learn that now than after another decade of threads.
