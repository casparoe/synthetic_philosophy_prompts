I'm taking a metaphysics seminar this semester and we just finished the unit on origins. My TA is basically a Kripkean about everything and keeps insisting origin essentialism is just obvious — if the sperm and egg that made you had been different, the result would never have been you, just some sibling-shaped stranger. I tried to push back and instead built an argument I now can't get out of, because its conclusion is either false or terrifying. Here's the chain. (1) I could not have developed from a different sperm and egg; anything originating from a different pair of gametes just isn't me. (2) But whether those particular gametes ever combine is wildly sensitive to circumstance — if my parents had met a year later, or even conceived on a different day, a different pair would have met. (3) So in nearly every nearby world where things go even slightly differently, I never exist at all. (4) Now zoom out: our climate policies shape the timing and circumstances of billions of future conceptions, so they determine which gamete pairs ever combine, and so by (1) they determine which future people exist. (5) The people alive in, say, 2150 under the high-emissions scenario each have that policy baked into their origin conditions — without it, those exact people never exist. (6) Nobody is wronged by an action that was a necessary condition of their ever existing, so long as their life ends up worth living. (7) High emissions leave the 2150 people with degraded but still worthwhile lives. (8) Therefore nobody alive in 2150 is wronged by our emissions. (9) And by the same reasoning, a couple who can only conceive a child with a painful genetic condition, know this in advance, and conceive anyway rather than using a donor gamete, wrongs no one either. (10) But it seems obvious to me that reckless climate policy wrongs the people who suffer under it, and honestly (9) seems wrong too, though I'm less sure about that one. Something has to give, and my TA swears it isn't going to be (1).

Where does this go wrong, if it does? My own instinct is that (1) is doing less work than the TA thinks — maybe "you depend on which gametes" quietly slides into "you depend on everything causally upstream of which gametes," and the slide is where the trouble gets in. But I can't quite nail down where the slide happens or whether it's even a slide rather than a legitimate consequence. And if I keep (1) at full strength, I have to pick between giving up (6), which seems to protect all sorts of cases, and biting the bullet on (8) or (9). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs me elsewhere. I'd rather keep the intuition in (10) than the one in (1), but I want to know exactly what I'm trading away.
