I'm a program officer at a small foundation that can fund roughly six researchers for three years on the problem of induction — work that could span philosophy, formal epistemology, and some statistical learning theory. Rather than scattering grants across whatever proposals happen to arrive, I want to set an explicit agenda, and I'd like your help ranking the open questions.

From where I sit, the candidate questions look something like the following, though part of your job is to tell me whether there's a more fundamental question upstream of all of them that I've left out:

1. Whether Goodman's own answer — that we project predicates that have become entrenched through a history of past projection — is an explanation of projectibility or merely a relabeling of the phenomenon it was supposed to illuminate. If it is circular, the question becomes whether anything non-circular can fill the gap, or whether the demand for such a thing is itself confused.

2. Whether the new riddle of induction and the underdetermination of curve-fitting in statistics are the same problem in different dress, or genuinely distinct. If they are the same, a fair amount of work gets unified; if they are distinct, treating them as interchangeable may have been corrupting both literatures for decades.

3. What the new riddle actually teaches us about the choice of priors and inductive biases. In particular, whether formal frameworks that build a simplicity preference into the prior dissolve the riddle or merely relocate it — since the choice of the underlying description language in such frameworks looks suspiciously like grue all over again.

4. Whether the intuition that we should prefer hypotheses positing fewer unobserved changes in the world itself — as opposed to fewer changes in our descriptions of the world — can be made precise and defended. Several commentators have gestured at this as the real content behind our reluctance to accept grue-like hypotheses, but I've never seen it stated carefully enough to evaluate.

5. Whether Hume's old problem of justifying induction and Goodman's new riddle of saying which hypotheses are confirmed by which evidence should be worked on jointly at all, or whether conflating them has muddied the treatment of each.

For each question you place high on the list, I want three things: why it matters downstream of other work in the area, how tractable it is for philosophers with formal training as opposed to machine-learning researchers, and what developments over the next few years would move it up or down the ranking. I'm less interested in the final list than in the reasoning that produces it, so please make the ordering criteria explicit and say where you'd accept a worse trade among them — for instance, where you'd take a neglected but hard question over a tractable but shallow one. Please don't respond with a reading list or bibliography; what I need is a defended agenda.
