I'm taking part in a seminar on the ethics of killing and dying, and the participants have been asked to circulate a short statement of their settled views in advance so that the others can interrogate them. I've drafted mine, but before sending it round I'd like an audit of whether the package actually hangs together — which of my commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and what the cheapest repair would cost.

Here is the set. First, I hold that there is a morally significant difference between doing and allowing at the level of permission: a doctor may withhold or withdraw a futile and burdensome treatment at a patient's request, but may not administer a lethal injection that would produce the very same outcome, and I think this asymmetry is not mere convention or medical etiquette. Second, and despite that, I find Rachels's bathtub pair compelling as a claim about culpability: the uncle who drowns his young cousin in the bath for the inheritance, and the uncle who, intending exactly the same, stands by and merely lets the cousin drown after an accidental slip, seem to me equally blameworthy — the bare difference between killing and letting die appears to make no difference there at all. Third, I accept the doctrine of double effect: harm intended as a means or an end is harder to justify than the same harm merely foreseen, which is why I think bombing a munitions plant with foreseen civilian deaths can be proportionate while terror bombing the very same civilians to break morale cannot be. Fourth, I take the footbridge/switch asymmetry to track something real rather than squeamishness: I would divert a runaway trolley away from five workers and onto one, but I would not push the large man off the footbridge into the trolley's path, and I do not believe this is status quo bias or an artefact of bodily contact. Fifth, I am sympathetic to Singer's claim that proximity and relationship are irrelevant to the stringency of the duty to aid: the child drowning in the shallow pond in front of me and the child dying far away whom I could save through a modest donation impose equally stringent obligations, other things such as cost and likelihood of success being equal. Sixth — and this is the one I half suspect I'll be pressed on — I also believe that most people who decline to give to distant relief, while perhaps not admirable, are not doing anything nearly as bad as killing; generosity beyond a certain point seems to me supererogatory rather than obligatory.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the set, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and order the real tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally; for each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I've noticed, for instance, that several of my other judgements seem to lean on the doctrine of double effect, so if that has to go I want to know what else falls with it; conversely, I'd want to know if some of the conflicts dissolve once one is careful about the level — permission versus culpability — at which the claims are made. Please also be candid about the fragility of your own diagnosis: say where your verdicts are least confident, what argument or worked-out position would move you to a different one, and where you would expect a competent defender of whichever commitment I abandon to push back hardest. Please write in British English.
