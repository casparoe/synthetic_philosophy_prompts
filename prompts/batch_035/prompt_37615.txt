I lead a workshop series for members of a county historical society—dedicated amateur genealogists and local-history researchers, most of them retired professionals, who have decades of experience using archives but have never been asked to think about how archives are made. Next month I have ninety minutes to teach the idea of archival silence: that an archive is not a neutral sample of the past, but the residue of what some institution had the power, reason, and habit to record, and that silence therefore has structure and can be read. Please plan the explanation for me rather than explain the idea to me.

Here are the constraints I'm working with. The concept I want to get across has three layers, and I suspect they have to come in a particular order, though I'm not certain what it is: first, that some absences are absences of recording (a life never written down at all, because no clerk, church, or bureaucrat had cause to notice it); second, that some absences are created later (records destroyed, deliberately or through indifference—tax rolls pulped, parish registers burned, or simply never accessioned by a collecting institution); and third, the subtlest case, where the person is present in the record but only as an artifact of someone else's category—an enslaved person listed as an item of value in a probate inventory, a wife appearing in a census only as a tally mark in a column, a pauper recorded as a cost. My working thought is that the third layer is the one that matters most, since it shows that bias is not just omission but the shape of what does survive, but I'd like you to tell me whether that ordering is right and why, or what dependencies I'm missing.

For the carrying example, I want a single concrete record that can be revisited at each layer, ideally something my audience would actually encounter in a county courthouse or state archive, and ideally one where the same person is legible in one way and silent in another. I have no settled candidate, so propose one and tell me what it must have and what it must lack.

The misconceptions I expect, in rough order: the complacent version of "archives are biased, we all know that," which treats the point as a disclaimer and ends inquiry rather than opening it; the romantic version, in which silence is read as heroic hidden history just waiting to be recovered; and the despairing version, in which the conclusion is that we can know nothing about the unrecorded. Tell me where in the sequence each should be headed off, and whether there's a misconception I haven't listed that is more dangerous than these.

Finally, I need a closing check: a question I can ask, or a small exercise I can pose, that would show me whether they can actually read a silence—say something substantive about what a given record's gaps imply—rather than merely having been made to feel guilty about the past.

Keep the whole plan under 500 words.
