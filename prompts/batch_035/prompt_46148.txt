I'm a retired physicist -- solid state, forty years in the lab, nothing glamorous -- and since retiring I've gotten seriously into philosophy. Started with the classics, then philosophy of science for obvious reasons, and now I'm a bit obsessed with a fight that seems to have been running in circles since the early nineties: is philosophy any use to working scientists? You know the cast. Weinberg has a whole chapter in his big essay collection from that era that's basically titled Against Philosophy, where he argues the stuff has been useless at best and actively harmful at worst -- his example being that the logical positivists' insistence that science is only about observable quantities kept physicists from asking sensible questions about what quantum mechanics is actually about. Hawking and Mlodinow open their 2010 popular book by declaring philosophy dead, saying philosophers haven't kept up with science and it's the physicists asking the deep questions now. And then there's Feynman, who I gather is usually credited with the crack about philosophy of science being as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds -- though I've read the attribution is shaky, so treat that one carefully.

The philosophers' replies are also well worn by now: that the physicists are doing philosophy themselves whenever they talk about interpretation, laws of nature, or what a final theory would even be, just doing it without the training; that the anti-philosophy stance is itself a philosophical stance nobody demonstrated experimentally; and so on. And every few years somebody restates one side and everyone applauds their own team and nobody budges.

Here's what I want, and it's not a rerun of the debate. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. I have a working hypothesis, and I'd like you to take it seriously but beat it up: I suspect the fight is at least partly verbal, because the physicists mean "philosophy" as the professional discipline that lives in philosophy departments, while the philosophers mean "philosophy" as the activity of conceptual clarification that nobody, scientist or not, can avoid. Under that reading, Weinberg's chapter and the philosophers' rebuttals are ships passing in the night. But I'm not sure that's the whole story, because there also seems to be a real substantive disagreement lurking underneath, something about whether conceptual analysis done in advance of the data has ever actually steered science in a useful direction, or whether the cases where it did (Einstein leaning on Mach? the reaction against positivism in quantum foundations?) are cherry-picked or even misdescribed by both sides.

So: is this verbal, substantive, or two camps answering different questions under one name? Where does my hypothesis break down? And is this the kind of impasse that signals the question is ill-posed, or just one nobody has bothered to make precise? Tell me what would count as actual progress here -- a sharper distinction, a case study done properly, a change in how philosophy departments and physics departments actually divide their labor -- or whether I should just accept that this one stays a spectator sport.

Two requests on top of that. Please write in American English. And since I'm an old empiricist and I know my own attachment to my verbal-disagreement hypothesis might be blinding me: tell me explicitly what consideration or argument would move you to a different diagnosis than the one you land on, and where your own reasoning on this is most likely to go wrong. I'd rather have a hedged answer that shows its soft spots than a confident one that hides them.
