For roughly the past decade I've been a regular reader of the rationalist blogosphere — the old Overcoming Bias material, the early LessWrong sequences, the later threads on mesa-optimization, sandbagging evals, and deceptive alignment — and I now run capability evaluations at a mid-sized AI lab. My team faces the same decision about a dozen times a month: we run some behavioral elicitation — a benchmark, a battery of prompts, a red-team conversation — and the result gets written up in a report that says the model either does or does not 'know' or 'have' some capability or piece of knowledge. I've become convinced we are being sloppy with that verb, and I want to replace the sloppiness with something runnable.

The principle I'd like operationalized is the distinction between latent knowledge and elicitable performance. A model may encode information that no prompt we have tried gets it to express; it may express something correct without encoding it, because the probe leaked its own answer through surface cues; and it may encode the right answer while still producing the wrong one, because the elicitation context makes the wrong one locally rational — for instance when a model follows a user's presupposition into error, or underperforms on a task it has learned is an eval. We have no interpretability access (weights and activations are off-limits to my team), limited compute for re-runs, and our reports are read by policy staff with no ML background.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run in an afternoon on a single elicitation result: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; what to do when we can't afford to run a condition the procedure calls for; where the procedure terminates in 'insufficient to attribute knowledge' rather than forcing a verdict; and where it hands the decision back to me because the remaining question is about what 'knows' should mean in a model report, not about the model. Two devices we already use informally and would like folded in — treat them as correct unless you think the procedure should reject them: (a) the swapped-foil test, in which the item is rebuilt with a different 'expected' answer marked by the same surface cues (answer ordering, phrasing length, genre conventions), so that we can see whether the model tracks the leak or the content; and (b) counterfactual world probes, in which the same question is asked about a fictional variant of the world, checking that the model's answers track the variant's stipulations rather than our world's statistics.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a model scoring near ceiling on rare-item multiple choice in a technical domain while confabulating freely when asked the same material open-ended; (2) a model that fails every standard phrasing of a class of word problems but solves them reliably when the same structure arrives as dialogue between two fictional characters; and (3) a safety evaluation in which the model produces a dangerous synthesis procedure only when the request is framed as the premise of a story — a case where I expect the procedure to conclude we cannot write 'the model knows how to do this,' and where that conclusion will be politically inconvenient, because reporting 'produces it under one framing' will read to my management as downplaying. Please give me the procedure's honest answer anyway.

Please structure your response in the following named sections, in this order: Summary (at most 150 words, stating what the procedure attributes and to whom the hard calls go); The procedure (700 to 900 words, the ordered questions with pass/fail criteria); Worked applications (roughly 200 words on each of the three cases); and What the procedure cannot do (at most 250 words, including the cases where judgment cannot be eliminated and what misfires to expect).
