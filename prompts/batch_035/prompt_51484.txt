Longtime lurker here, mostly LessWrong and adjacent decision theory blogs, and I just lost an argument to a slogan I'm not sure was ever cashed out. We were arguing about whether an AGI with mildly cyclic preferences — say it strictly prefers A over B, B over C, and C over A in some corner of its value structure — would be a big deal or basically self-limiting. My position was that cyclic preferences in a subregion don't obviously stop you from being a formidable optimizer everywhere else. My friend countered with the classic line: "intransitive preferences leave you wide open to money pumps" — i.e., any agent with knowledge of the cycle can extract unbounded resources from the thing, so intransitivity gets trained away or selected against, and in the meantime the AGI is harmless-by-exploitable.

He won the room with that, but walking home I realized I don't actually know what the slogan is claiming, and the posts I reread use it as a trump card without ever making it precise. There seem to be several distinct theses mashed together. Like, "there exists a sequence of trades the agent would accept that strictly decreases its holdings" is one reading, but doesn't that require the agent to be naive — to evaluate each trade one at a time, without noticing it's circling back to where it started? Can't a sophisticated agent with cyclic preferences foresee the loop and refuse the last trade, in which case the pump never runs? Or does the pump need some extra machinery — a fee at each step, or an assumption that the agent's choices don't depend on its choice history? And there's also a jump from "you can be pumped" to "your preferences are irrational" that seems to smuggle in a whole view about whether rationality is a property of moment-by-moment choices or of whole trajectories and precommitments, which feels like the actually interesting part and exactly the thing the slogan waves away.

So what I want is a menu: lay out the distinct precise claims that could be hiding inside "intransitive preferences leave you open to being money pumped," from the nearly trivial ones (given a naive trading rule, a bookie can pump you) through the substantive normative ones (having these preferences is irrational, period), to the versions that are just plain false or need heroic assumptions. For each reading say whether its plausible and, crucially, whether it gets my friend his actual conclusion — that the AGI self-corrects or is neutralized by exploitability. It'd help if you separated the behavioral claim (agents like this do get exploited in real environments) from the normative one (they're irrational) from the selection/evolutionary one (they won't persist long enough to matter), since those fail in different ways. And flag which readings survive if the agent is allowed to be sophisticated or to bind itself with precommitments, because that's where I think the real action is and where I want to push back on him.
