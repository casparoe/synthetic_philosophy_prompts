i teach intro ethics at a community college and every year I end up defending the universalizability test to my students, and lately I've been worried about whether the version I defend actually risks anything. The version: a maxim is impermissible when universalizing it undermines the very practice the maxim presupposes — lying only works against a background of trust, promise-breaking only works if not everyone breaks promises, so there's something self-undermining about willing it universally. Plus the second, messier tier where the maxim doesn't self-destruct but a world of it would undermine ends any agent with a body and a life has to have.

The objection I always get from the sharper students is gerrymandering. "I'll lie whenever I'm a 5'6" redhead named Linda on Tuesdays in Topeka" universalizes just fine, since at most one person satisfies it, so nothing self-undermines. My patch, which I gather Kant people have used in various forms: a maxim isn't whatever string of words you like, it's the description under which the agent actually acts, the consideration actually doing the work in her deliberation. Nobody lies *because* it's a Tuesday in Topeka; they lie because it's convenient or benefits them. So the gerrymandered maxim isn't a maxim at all, it's a lawyer's trick. Fine in class, but I've started noticing the patch has its own problems and I can't tell how much of the view survives.

So: what would count against the patched view? Specifically I want you to sort it for me. Some things I can already see — the patch makes a psychological fact (what actually moves you) do normative work, and agents are messy: self-deceivers, people with weird compulsions, people who really do act on considerations the rest of us find arbitrary. Does the view have an answer to those, or does it only handle tidy agents? And there's the determinacy problem: an agent acts under lots of descriptions simultaneously, so which one is "the" maxim, and who decides — the agent's self-report, the observer's best theory, something else? If different reasonable choices of maxim give different verdicts, what exactly has the test told us?

Also tell me what would count against the test itself, patch aside — cases where the practice survives universalization but the act still seems clearly wrong, cases where universalization forbids something that seems fine, that sort of thing. And the question I most want an honest answer to: has this thing been qualified into unfalsifiability? Every counterexample gets dissolved by redescribing the maxim, and every redescription is individually plausible, so I genuinely can't tell if I'm holding a moral test or a Rorschach blot. If it's insulated, say so — and tell me whether that's fatal for a decision procedure, or whether some insulation is just what you get in ethics. Rank the defeaters for me, fatal to merely awkward, and flag which ones a committed defender of this view would concede as real hits versus wave off. I'd rather know if my semester is built on sand.
