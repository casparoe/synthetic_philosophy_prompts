My friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether our city's school funding system is fair, and we kept sliding past each other in a way I now suspect is a general pattern in need of a name. He cared about whether families end up equally satisfied — some children, through no fault of their own, need tutoring, therapy, or wheelchair access to get the same benefit from school that others get for free — while I kept counting what each family was actually given, in money and services, and calling the system fair when those amounts matched. We eventually realized we could both correctly call the same arrangement equal by our own measure and unequal by the other's, and that neither of us had an argument for why our measure was the right one that didn't just presuppose it. There was also a stubborn case we couldn't resolve: a family that converts the same resources into much less benefit through sheer pickiness — expensive hobbies, a refusal to use what's provided. Counting their dissatisfaction as inequality felt wrong, but counting a child's medical needs on a par with those hobbies felt worse, and I couldn't say what the relevant difference is. Is there an established name or literature for the situation where two people can each truthfully call the same distribution unequal because they measure inequality in different currencies, and for the puzzle about which complaints a fair distribution is supposed to answer? I'm aware of the folk distinction between equality of opportunity and equality of outcome, but it doesn't seem to fit, since both of us were describing outcomes in some sense. I'd like to know what the nearest concepts are, how my pattern differs from each, and where it falls among them — or, if nothing established fits, I'd like you to coin a term, define it, and tell me where its edge cases lie.
