I'm drafting notes for an essay on whether "low-impact AI" — AI that avoids side effects without being told everything we care about — is a coherent research program, and I'd like your comments before I write it up. The notes are below.

The central question: Suppose we want a household robot to clean the kitchen, with the instruction understood as "clean the kitchen and don't do anything else that matters." The hope behind impact measures is that we can penalize the agent for its "impact" on the world — change beyond the task — using a definition that doesn't presuppose a full specification of human values. Two well-known proposals: penalize the agent for making previously reachable states unreachable (relative reachability), and penalize the agent for changing how well it could achieve any of a large class of auxiliary goals (attainable utility preservation). Both bill themselves as value-agnostic: the penalty is meant to track option value or power in general, not any particular thing we care about.

The thesis I want to defend: this value-agnosticism is an illusion; impact measures relocate rather than eliminate the value-specification problem, via three design choices.

First, the choice of feature map or auxiliary set. The penalty is computed relative to some representation of states, or some set of auxiliary reward functions. Two representations that both suffice for specifying the cleaning task can yield wildly different impact penalties for the same behavior. So the designer's choice of representation silently encodes which differences in the world count as differences at all — a value judgment.

Second, the baseline counterfactual. Whether the agent is penalized depends on what counts as what would have happened otherwise: the initial state, inaction from the current state, or a simulated rollout of "the agent does nothing from here." Each choice embeds a normative stance on counterfactual responsibility — e.g., whether the agent is at fault for effects it merely fails to prevent — which is structurally similar to the act/omission distinction in ethics.

Third, normalization and the exchange rate. Converting penalty units into reward units fixes how much task success we trade for how much side-effect avoidance; a valuation, compressed into a single number.

I then want a reframing: instead of asking whether impact measures escape value specification, ask how much coverage a cheap, low-dimensional proxy buys. The comparison I have in mind is a general-purpose tripwire. On this view, an impact measure is best understood as a partial, generic, biased-toward-simplicity value specification — a bet that the values we forgot to specify correlate with simple, measurable changes in an agent's power over the world. And the bet can fail systematically for values that are complex conjunctions of features (I'd like to gesture at something like preservation of ecosystems or of institutions as candidate failures).

Please respond with exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, conclusion, or headers. I'd particularly value comments on: (i) whether my three smuggling arguments are genuinely distinct, or whether the second and third reduce to the first; (ii) whether the auxiliary-goal approach can answer the feature-map objection by drawing auxiliary goals from a distribution broad enough to wash out designer bias; and (iii) whether my final reframing is a substantive concession or merely a relabeling. But comment on whatever seems most important to you.
