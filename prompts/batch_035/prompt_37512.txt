Last night my friend and I got into a three-hour argument about moral trade and I still cant let it go. Quick background in case it helps: moral trade is the idea that people with different moral views can strike bargains with each other -- e.g., I think eating meat is wrong and you don't, but you only mildly value eating meat while I care about it a lot, so I pay you to eat less of it, and we both come out ahead by our own lights. Each of us sees less of what we consider bad happening, at a price we're happy to pay. We both found this attractive at first, it's basically Pareto improvement applied to moral views. Then he turned it around and argued that moral trade licenses a whole extortion economy, and I couldnt find the flaw anywhere.

Here's his argument as he gave it: (1) If a payment leaves both parties better off, by their own lights, than the no-payment outcome, then the payment should be made. (2) So suppose I credibly announce that unless you pay me, I will do something you consider seriously wrong but that costs me little -- say I'll donate a chunk of my salary to a cause you find abhorrent, or eat meat at every meal for a year. (3) Compared to the no-payment outcome, paying me leaves you better off by your lights, and I'm obviously better off, so by (1) you should pay. (4) By the same reasoning, the smartest move available to anyone is to become a person who credibly threatens lots of wrongdoing and collects the payments -- the more bad things you're genuinely prepared to do, the more everyone else owes you. (5) So moral trade doesn't merely permit rewarding threats, it turns cultivating them into a rational industry. He concluded that moral trade refutes itself: no theory whose upshot is "become a credible threat" can be right. Something feels off to me somewhere between (3) and (4), but I can't name it, and honestly step (1) sounded so innocuous when we started that I'm reluctant to touch it.

Could you take on the role of a professional philosopher working on rational choice and ethics, and think this through carefully -- maybe brainstorm out loud for a few lines before committing to a diagnosis? Then tell me: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd much rather keep (1) than give up on moral trade altogether, so if you think the right move is to keep (1) and swallow some bitter pill about baselines, I want to know exactly what I'm paying. And if you think his argument is actually valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to abandon, please say so plainly rather than rescuing my preferences. One thing that keeps nagging me: in the ordinary case of moral trade, nobody deliberately manufactured the "no-payment outcome" that the payment is compared against, whereas in his case I did -- but I have no idea whether that difference can carry the whole weight, since he'll just ask what the counterfactual baseline is supposed to be once I've already made the threat. Does the comparison world become the post-threat one no matter what? That seems to let him define away the problem. Any help appreciated, this is eating at me.
