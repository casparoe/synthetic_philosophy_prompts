I'm trying to get clear on whether behavioral economics actually explains weakness of will or merely redescribes it, and I want a careful conceptual comparison of the two frameworks, not a survey.

On the philosophical side, there is the traditional problem running from Aristotle through Davidson: an agent judges, all things considered, that doing A is better than doing B, yet freely and knowingly does B. Aristotle already distinguishes impetuous akrasia (acting before deliberation completes, carried off by anger or appetite) from the weaker form in which deliberation occurs and is simply outvoted. Davidson's version sharpens the puzzle: if the agent acted intentionally, and held the judgment that A was better, what exactly broke — and why isn't the "break" just evidence that the judgment was never sincere or never really all-things-considered?

On the economics side, there is the hyperbolic discounting tradition of Ainslie and Rachlin: agents discount future rewards at a rate steeper in the near term than the long term, which produces preference reversals — the larger, later reward is preferred from a distance, but as a smaller, sooner alternative gets close, its discounted value overtakes the larger one, and the agent flips. Ainslie adds that agents partially solve this by bundling choices into personal rules, treating each choice as evidence about a pattern, which introduces something like intrapersonal bargaining and reputational dynamics with oneself.

My question is which of these is the deeper explanation, or whether they are orthogonal. Some specific sub-questions I'd like addressed: (1) The discounting framework seems to model akrasia as diachronic instability among time-indexed preferences, while the philosophical problem is framed synchronically, as a conflict between judgment and action at a moment. Does hyperbolic discounting explain the synchronic phenomenon, dissolve it as an artifact of mis-describing preferences as time-indexed, or simply presuppose the thing that needs explaining — namely why the "closer" reward speaks louder to an agent who can represent the trade-off perfectly well? (2) Aristotle's impetuous akratic arguably never performs the evaluative comparison, so nothing reverses; can a discount-curve story even model that case, and if not, does that matter for which framework is more general? (3) Personal rules look like they smuggle normativity back in: the agent treats a past or future choice as a reason in a way that mere preference reversal doesn't obviously license. Is the bundling mechanism a genuine account of self-control, or does its reliance on quasi-reputation show that you cannot get from curves to shoulds without importing a distinctively practical form of reasoning? (4) Where does this leave compulsive behavior and addiction, which the behavioral literature often treats as the paradigm case but which the akrasia tradition explicitly sets aside as not counting against one's better judgment at all?

I'd like you to take a position on whether hyperbolic discounting is an explanation of weakness of will, a mechanism of it, or a rival phenomenon that has been conflated with it — and to say where the conflation, if any, originates. Please be explicit about your confidence level at each stage, and identify which single argument or piece of evidence on the other side would most move you to reverse your verdict, as well as the step in your own reasoning you consider most likely to be wrong.
