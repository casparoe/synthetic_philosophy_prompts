I'm part of the clinical ethics consultation service at a big teaching hospital. Part of my job is that I constantly get people wanting us to defer to somebody else's moral judgment instead of forming our own: a daughter saying "we talked about this as a family for years, we know what's right for our mother," a veteran nurse saying "I've seen three hundred of these cases, terminating treatment here is the right thing, trust me," a chaplain with a verdict grounded in a tradition I don't share, and now — new this year — the hospital is piloting an AI decision-support tool that outputs tidy little "ethics recommendations" alongside each case summary, and some of my colleagues are quietly treating those as authoritative.

Two of my colleagues got into a fight about this at our last case review. One proposed what she calls a "no-borrowed-verdicts rule": the service should never endorse a recommendation on someone else's moral judgment alone, we have to reason to it ourselves, every time. The other thinks that's absurd -- we defer on medical facts constantly, why is morality different? And a third has this idea I can't stop thinking about, which she calls the "burden rule": whoever lives with the consequences gets the moral say, and the rest of us, including the consult service, are basically just taking moral testimony from them, which is fine, because that's what deference to the affected party is.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and epistemology, especially the moral testimony literature (the old pessimist worry that there's something specially wrong with relying on testimony about moral matters -- worries about moral autonomy, manipulation, responsibility without understanding -- and the optimist replies; Hills on the difference between merely knowing the right answer and understanding why; the general question of why moral deference should be treated differently from deference about, say, the weather or a drug interaction). Think carefully before you write, and maybe do a quick brainstorm of the pressures on the problem first. Then write me a decision procedure the service can actually run in under an hour before we finalize a consult write-up: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "deferring here is fine," where it says "we have to work it out ourselves," and where it has to admit the question is a judgment call that no procedure can settle -- please be honest about those instead of papering over them. The procedure should tell me something about all four sources of verdicts above (family, experienced clinician, chaplain, the AI tool) -- I suspect they shouldn't all be treated the same, but I'd like to see whether the procedure actually distinguishes them or whether that's my bias. Then run it on three concrete cases: (1) the daughter whose family deliberated for years about their mother's care, (2) the AI tool recommending against a requested withdrawal of treatment, and (3) a patient who flat-out asks me "you've seen all this before, just tell me the right thing to do and I'll do it." Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- I already know I want to say the AI tool gets less deference than the chaplain, so if the procedure says otherwise, don't soften it. Also tell me: does the procedure vindicate my colleague's burden rule, kill it, or turn it into something weaker and more plausible, and where exactly does the no-borrowed-verdicts rule go too far?

One more constraint: whatever you write has to survive being read aloud in a room with an attending physician, a rabbi, and a risk manager, so no unexplained jargon, or at least explain it in a sentence when you use it. I dont want a philosophy lecture, I want something we can actually put in the consult manual.
