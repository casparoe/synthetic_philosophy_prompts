I teach a graduate seminar in social epistemology and I want a case study that puts pressure on the peer disagreement literature in an unusual direction: the 2011 faster-than-light neutrino affair. In case the details have faded, here is the episode. In September 2011 the OPERA collaboration at Gran Sasso reported that neutrinos fired from CERN, roughly 730 km away, arrived about 60 nanoseconds sooner than light in vacuum would have — a speed excess of about 25 parts per million. Crucially, OPERA did not announce that neutrinos are superluminal; they held a public seminar, said they had checked their instrumentation as best they could, found no fault, and explicitly asked the rest of the community to scrutinize the result. The wider community almost uniformly declined to believe it: the received view was that this was a systematic error in the measurement, and that special relativity — by then corroborated by a century of particle physics and astrometry — should hold until fault-finding found the problem. In early 2012 the collaboration located two instrumental faults: a loosely seated fiber-optic cable carrying the GPS timing signal to the master clock, and an oscillator running off its specified frequency. The corrected timing brought the neutrino speed back into line with light speed, and the ICARUS experiment, timing the same beam, then confirmed a result consistent with c.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the peer disagreement debate between conciliationism and steadfastness. I want a careful mapping, not a retelling. Some directions I want you to take up (and, where useful, complicate or reject):

1. Who are the peers here, if the notion applies at all? One party is a 150-strong collaboration reporting an instrument reading; the other is essentially everyone else reasoning from the total evidence of physics. The conciliationist picture of two epistemic equals with shared evidence fits awkwardly. Say whether peerhood fails, or whether it survives under a refined notion — and if the community as a whole can count as the "disagreeing peer."

2. There is an independence puzzle. Most of the community's confidence in special relativity rests on a body of theory that is highly corroborated but never tested on superluminal neutrinos, and the OPERA measurement itself was supposed to be independent of theory. Does the standard requirement that the peer's opinion be independent of one's own evidence do any work here, or does the asymmetry between a single measurement and a web of indirect confirmation break the shared-evidence assumption in a way the literature hasn't really addressed?

3. The OPERA physicists hedged: they released the anomaly for scrutiny rather than asserting it. If the reporting party itself had only partial confidence in the result, what exactly was the disagreement about — the proposition that neutrinos are superluminal, or the proposition that this particular measurement was reliable? Does conciliationism give different advice depending on how the proposition is framed, and is that a defect in the view or a feature?

4. By everyone's lights, most physicists were steadfast and were right. Does the episode vindicate steadfastness, or is vindication normatively irrelevant — a case of getting the right answer for reasons that would, in other episodes (continental drift, bacterial ulcers), have made the steadfast majority wrong? Distinguish carefully between the claim that steadfastness was reasonable and the claim that it was correct, and say which one the disagreement literature is actually about.

5. Note that the episode was not resolved by anyone splitting the difference or by anyone deferring. It was resolved by fault-finding and replication — two institutions with no obvious analogue in the two-person thought experiments the literature runs on. Consider whether conciliationism and steadfastness are best understood as individual epistemic duties inapplicable to a distributed community, and whether some third view handles the case better.

Conclude by running the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about the theory — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the framework never anticipated? Be disciplined about saying where each view is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and be explicit about what facts of the episode the mapping depends on. Please write your answer in British English.
