I'm a novelist, some two hundred pages into a book whose central marriage may never have validly begun. The setting is coastal New England in 1958: my narrator, a thirty-two-year-old woman just finished with a decade of nursing in a charity hospital, marries a widowed doctor who courts her with the promise, explicit between them, of children — and who, as I reveal late in the book, had a vasectomy years earlier, knew she would have refused him otherwise, and said nothing. When she finds out, the question that consumes her, and me, is whether her consent to the marriage was ever real: whether she agreed to marry him, or agreed to marry a man who did not exist. A lawyer friend of mine, reading the draft, dismissed the whole problem over a bottle of wine with the maxim that fraud vitiates everything — consent obtained through deception simply doesn't count, he said, so the marriage was a nullity from the start, and I should write her as a woman who was never married at all. He added, as a flourish, that in equity fraud unravels all. It was offered as a plot solution; it struck me as a plot device, and I would rather interrogate it than build an ending on it.

What I'd like from you is a disambiguation rather than a verdict. Take the maxim my friend reached for — fraud vitiates consent, fraud vitiates everything — and lay out, as a numbered list I can pin above my desk, the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be expressing: the trivial readings, the substantive but defensible ones, and the radical ones. For each, say whether it is plausible, and — this is the part that matters to me — whether it actually delivers what my friend needs it to deliver, namely the conclusion that my character's consent was void. My own suspicion is that the maxim conceals a line between deceptions that dissolve consent (lies about what one is agreeing to: the nature of the act, its consequences) and deceptions that merely induce it (lies about the surrounding facts that made the choice attractive). I want to know where that line is customarily drawn, whether it can be drawn at all non-arbitrarily, and — since a novel is also an ethics — whether any reading strong enough to void her marriage would not equally void the ordinary self-fashioning of every courtship, hers included, for she has her own quiet concealments and I have not decided whether the book forgives them. If you can, close by saying which reading the people who repeat the maxim most likely have in mind, and which reading my ending should turn on.
