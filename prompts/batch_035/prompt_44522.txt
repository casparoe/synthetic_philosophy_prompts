I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in Nishapur in the years just after al-Ghazālī's death, and one of my central characters is a jurist who studied logic and the natural philosophy of Ibn Sīnā in his youth, suffered a crisis of certainty, and has since become a devoted partisan of what he takes to be al-Ghazālī's teaching. A pivotal scene has him debating an old classmate, now an unrepentant Avicennian, over whether fire really burns cotton, and I want the exchange to hold up under a knowledgeable reader's eye. To prepare, I read a popular history of science that a friend recommended, and it summarizes al-Ghazālī's position roughly as follows: he argued that nothing in nature causes anything else — that fire does not burn cotton and a knife does not cut — because everything that happens is God directly re-creating the world moment by moment, and what we call a causal connection is only God's habit of doing the same thing in the same circumstances. The book goes on to say that since nature has only habits and no laws, empirical science is impossible in principle on this view; that al-Ghazālī accordingly condemned the philosophers wholesale as infidels; that Averroës wrote a point-by-point rebuttal but the Islamic world sided with al-Ghazālī and rational inquiry was effectively shut down; and that it was left to Aquinas, defending the reality of secondary causes, to make the intellectual world safe for science.

The novelist's anxiety you might imagine: I do not know which of these sentences I can put in my character's mouth. Some of it squares with what I have read elsewhere, but the confident sweep of the last sentences makes me suspicious, and I have noticed that popular histories of science tend to compress medieval positions into heroes and villains. My character, being modeled on a man of his time, will also be shown doing respectable astronomical work and holding some doctrines of the philosophers in esteem, and I cannot tell whether that is historically incoherent given the summary above, or whether the summary has quietly run together two things that al-Ghazālī kept apart — the denial of necessity in causes and the denial of regularity in nature.

Could you go through that summary part by part and tell me what a careful scholar of classical Islamic philosophy would accept, what they would wince at as an oversimplification, and what they would flag as straightforwardly wrong? I would particularly like to know whether the claim that science is impossible in principle on al-Ghazālī's view follows from anything he actually held, whether he condemned the philosophers wholesale or drew some more careful line, and how the connection to the later fortunes of inquiry in the Islamic world should be softened if it cannot stand. Finally, given whatever correction emerges, advise me on what my jurist can plausibly say in the debate scene: can he coherently maintain that fire never truly burns cotton while still trusting the heavens enough to compute an eclipse?
