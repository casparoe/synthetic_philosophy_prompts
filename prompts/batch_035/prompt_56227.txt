Quick one from my historiography seminar. We had a guest lecturer, and at the drinks afterwards one of the older professors basically shut down a whole conversation about narrative in history by saying the historian's job, since Ranke, is just to show how it actually was -- wie es eigentlich gewesen -- and that anyone who doubts this (he was clearly aiming at Hayden White's whole emplotment thing, and maybe at Carr too) has given up on history being knowledge and is basically writing fiction with footnotes. It felt like the slogan was being made to do a LOT of different jobs at once and I want to know which of those jobs it can actually do. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one line could be making -- I imagine there's a trivial one (don't invent sources), a methodological one about the historian effacing themselves, an epistemic one about a past that exists independent of any historian, maybe a stronger ontological one -- and give a verdict on each: plausible, contentious, or plain false. Then for each reading, say whether Carr's selection point (the past doesn't tell you which facts are significant; the historian chooses) or White's emplotment point (the same set of events can be narrated as tragedy, farce, romance etc.) actually touches it, or only attacks a weaker straw version. Also: someone at the pub claimed the German word "eigentlich" can mean "essentially" or "properly" rather than just "actually," which would make Ranke sound closer to Hegel about each epoch having its own essence than to a fact-collector with good archive manners -- is that a real translation dispute, and if it is, does it change which claims survive? I want to know which subset of readings the professor's slam dunk actually depends on, since it obviously only works on some of them. Lay it out as a menu with verdicts, no reading lists, I cant get to a library before thursday.
