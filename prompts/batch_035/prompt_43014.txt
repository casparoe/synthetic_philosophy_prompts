At my local rationality meetup we got onto Hofstadter's superrationality (the idea from his old Scientific American columns, later collected in the Metamagical Themas book): in a one-shot prisioner's dilemma against someone who reasons the way you do, your choice and theirs are effectively two computations of the same function, so you should cooperate — not because you causally move them, but because your "cooperate" and theirs rise and fall together. Someone then said the obvious thing: this is just the categorical imperative in a decision-theory costume — act only on the maxim you could will universalized; if everyone like you reasons as you do, then universalizing your choice IS your choice. And the analogy is being used to do real work in two directions in our group. One guy leans on it to say superrational cooperation is really a moral duty all along, so decision theory has quietly proven Kant right. Another uses it the opposite way: if it's just Kantianism smuggled into rational choice theory, then it's ethics wearing math, not a theorem about rationality, and we should drop it from the toolkit entirely.

I'd like the full audit of the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the maxim, of universalization, of the rational being, of the contradiction-in-will test — and then where the mapping breaks. My own hunch is that the "everyone like you" clause works completely differently in the two settings: Kant's universalization runs to all rational beings no matter how they actually reason, while Hofstadter's link only connects agents who happen to reason like you at that node, which sounds more like a correlation claim than a moral test. Kant is also asking a hypothetical question (could this maxim be willed as universal law?), whereas superrationality seems to assert that your decision actually does co-vary with similar reasoners' decisions. So: does either of my friends' conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table — "rationality proves Kant" or "it's ethics not rationality" — or is the analogy carrying both conclusions the whole way? And is there a better analogy for what superrationality is actually doing, maybe something from statistics or physics instead of ethics? Don't be afraid to conclude that neither of them gets what they want out of it.
