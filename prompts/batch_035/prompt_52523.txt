I cover science and ideas for a general readership — the daily run of new findings, retractions, contested expertise, and the occasional cheerful claim that coffee is bad for you again — and I have been asked to write a seminar piece on what, exactly, my byline commits me to. Rather than interview anyone, I have tried to set down my own working views on assertion as they actually show up in the practice of science journalism, and I would like a serious audit of whether they can all be true at once. Here is the set:

1. The rule that governs assertion is a rule of knowledge: a claim is properly asserted only if the speaker knows it, and this binds journalists like anyone else.
2. Knowledge travels by testimony. If a scientist who knows that p tells me that p, I can come to know that p — and hence assert it — even though I could not evaluate the underlying evidence on my own.
3. When I write that a study "suggests" or "is consistent with" a finding p, or that researchers "suspect" p, I am not asserting p at all; I am asserting something about the study or the researchers, which I typically do know. Hedged science writing therefore cannot violate the knowledge rule. I take this to be confirmed by the fact that it is sometimes perfectly honest to report that a study suggests p while adding that I do not for a moment believe p.
4. It is nonetheless improper for me to assert a contested scientific claim outright — the conclusion of a small, unreplicated mouse study, say — even when I am genuinely confident of it.
5. I am a fallibilist: one can know that p on evidence that does not entail p, so the bare possibility that a finding fails to replicate cannot by itself disqualify me from knowing it.
6. When a credible scientist tells me her own preliminary result, it is sometimes good, straightforward journalism to report it as news — "scientists discover that p" — and not merely to report that she says so.
7. I hold myself answerable not only for the truth of what I assert but for what my readers come to believe on the strength of my story.

Some of these I suspect do not sit well together, but I would rather you find the fault lines than have me point at them. Go through the set, tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I care less about being vindicated than about knowing what I have actually signed up for.
