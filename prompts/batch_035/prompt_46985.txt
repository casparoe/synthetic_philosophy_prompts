I help edit a fake-news section for a philosophy department's grad-student-run humor page, and the next issue is about art forgery and why originals seem to matter so much. Write me an Onion-style article, headline plus roughly 450 words, reporting that a mid-size regional museum has confirmed its centerpiece painting -- the one on the gift-shop mugs, the one that saved them from a closure scare in the 90s -- is a forgery, painted in the 1950s by someone who then donated the money to buy it. The museum's official response is that nothing on the wall has changed, so nothing should change anywhere else: same label minus two words, same room, same admission price, and the director is genuinely puzzled why everyone keeps calling it a scandal.

The comedy has to come from the director being philosophically almost right rather than dumb. He should keep making the aesthetic empiricist case, and making it well: he can point out that every visual fact visitors claim to love is still there, that for sixty years the painting did all the work an original does (moved people to tears, inspired a local poet, got a school named after the artist partly because of it), that the docent scripts need only a pronoun edited, and that if knowing the provenance changes your experience that's a fact about your head, not about the paint. The joke is that he refuses to let his own logic carry him anywhere he doesn't want to go: he still won't lend it to other museums ("they'd be getting a forgery"), he quietly cancels the exhibition of the "same painter's" drawings, and when a trustee proposes commissioning the now-dead forger's estate to freshen up the varnish he's horrified. Somewhere in there, one quote from a visiting philosopher who makes the counterpoint in ordinary words -- that a painting is partly a record of something someone pulled off, and what got pulled off here was something different -- and one ordinary museum-goer whose reaction is the opposite of whatever the reader is braced for.

Two constraints: keep it deadpan and legible, no winking at the philosophy -- someone who knows the forgery literature (the perfect-duplicate intuition pumps, the idea that a fake misrepresents an achievement rather than an appearance, the retrospective-corruption worry that once you know, you can't see it the same way even though the photons are identical) should recognize you know it too, but the article itself never cites or gestures at any of that by name, and someone who knows none of it should still find the director funny. And by the end the reader should feel the actual pull of the question, not just a punchline: it should be at least a little uncomfortable to notice you don't fully know which side of the director's argument you're laughing at. No real museums, no real paintings, invented everything.
