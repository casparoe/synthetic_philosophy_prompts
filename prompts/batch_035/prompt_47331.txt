i'm a grad student working on philosophy of probability and something keeps bugging me about the objective chance literature. everyone argues best-system humeanism vs propensities as if we all agree on what a theory of chance is even supposed to DO, and i dont think we do. so before i commit to a dissertation topic i want the spec written down.

write the list of desiderata any adequate theory of objective chance has to meet. the ones i can think of, at least -- you add whatever i'm missing:

- it has to tell rational agents how to set their credences given the chances (the deference idea, the thing lewis called the principal principle). but note the hard part here is admissibility: deference is supposed to hold even when you know other stuff, except for "inadmissible" evidence, and the obvious definition of inadmissible ("evidence that would rationally change your credence despite knowing the chance") is circular. so if you count this as a desideratum, count it in a form that isnt trivially satisfiable by definition.
- chance has to underwrite the connection to frequencies -- long runs of coin flips, insurance tables, that stuff. does it have to explain why frequencies track chances, or just permit it? decide and say why.
- it has to say something about chances in deterministic settings. classical statistical mechanics gives us probabilities that look for all the world like chances, and there are deterministic interpretations of quantum theory too. is "allows nontrivial single-case chances in a deterministic world" a genuine requirement or a confusion -- and if it's a requirement, which camp gets to fail it?
- it owes us an explanation of why deference is rational at all, not just the assertion that it is. (this one might be doing a lot of work, tell me if so)

for each requirement: the case or consideration behind it, whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference one camp elevated into a principle, and -- this is the part i actually want -- flag any subset you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. i have a feeling there's one here, something like: if chances supervene on how the whole history of the world goes (including future stuff, as on humean views), then evidence about the future should change your credence about current chances, which fights with unconditional deference. if that tension is real, say so plainly, that's a result about the list itself, not just about the theories.

last thing: tell me which single requirement knocks out the most contenders if you take it seriously. my hunch is it's the explanation-of-deference one, but argue it either way. no scorecard against named theories, i'll do that myself -- i just want the requirements, clearly motivated, with the tension flagged. and keep it usable for someone like me: the theory is supposed to earn its keep in statistical mechanics and quantum contexts, not just in the seminar room. sorry for the rambling, its late here.
