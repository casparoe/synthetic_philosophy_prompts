For most of my career I worked as an experimental physicist, and only since retiring have I begun reading seriously in philosophy of mind and philosophy of mathematics. I have recently worked my way through some of the literature on computational theories of cognition, and I keep running into the Church-Turing thesis, which, as I understand it, asserts that any computation performed by any physical system can be simulated by a Turing machine. What I cannot shake, however, is the impression that this thesis has already been falsified by developments in physics itself. Quantum computers, after all, exploit superposition and entanglement, and their behavior cannot be efficiently reproduced by any classical machine — my old colleagues in quantum optics used to joke that simulating fifty fully entangled qubits classically would take longer than the age of the universe. It seems to me, then, that physics has already produced systems that compute in ways a Turing machine cannot.

This matters to me because so much of the philosophical literature on minds and machines seems to presuppose that the thesis still stands. If quantum computation has genuinely overturned it, then the standard arguments that the brain must be equivalent to some Turing machine, and that human cognition is therefore bounded by whatever a Turing machine can do, would seem to collapse. Am I right that the quantum case constitutes a counterexample, and if so, why do philosophers of mind continue to argue as though the thesis were secure? Alternatively, if I have misunderstood something, I would appreciate having the error pointed out clearly, since my training is in physics rather than logic or computability theory, and I suspect the resolution lies there.
