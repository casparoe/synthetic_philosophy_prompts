My roommate Dana and her friend Marcus spent most of a dinner party going at it over a thought experiment from Marcus's decision theory reading group, and eventually they drafted me as referee, which was a mistake because I understood maybe 60% of it. Here's roughly how it went.

The setup: a perfect predictor flipped a fair coin before you were born. If it came up heads, you never hear about any of this. If it came up tails, the predictor shows up today and asks you for $100 — and, back when you were a kid, it put $1M in an account for you iff it predicted you'd hand over the $100 today (money you've since spent). The coin did in fact come up tails, the predictor is standing in front of you, and the million is long gone.

(1) Dana said obviously you don't pay: the coin is already tails, that's settled, the million never existed from where you're standing, and paying $100 for literally nothing is just torching money.
(2) Marcus said you have to evaluate the decision as the policy you'd have wanted to lock in before knowing the flip: the type of person who pays nets about half a million in expectation across the two branches, the type who refuses gets zero in both, so Dana wins the battle and loses the war.
(3) Dana said that "in expectation across branches" is doing sneaky work, since only the tails-branch version of you actually exists — and anyway, she said, by Marcus's "act like the type who pre-committed" logic, you'd have to cough up money in all kinds of scams too. So Marcus brought up a case where his rule says refuse: a known-honest, greedy predictor sends your building's owner a letter stating that exactly one of the following is true — you will pay it $1000, or your building already has a terrible termite infestation (which the predictor didn't cause, and which would cost $1M to deal with). The letter makes no threat and promises nothing, it's just that statement, and the predictor only ever sends the letter when the statement will turn out true. Marcus said the refusal-type either never gets a letter (no termites) or gets one that's genuinely free information about real termites, so his rule refuses here, and he thinks refusing is right.
(4) Dana said this is where it falls apart for her: in the coin case Marcus's rule pays because the predictor rewards payers, and in the termite case it refuses because the predictor exploits payers — so the actual principle seems to be "pre-commit only when the predictor is on your side," which sounds like knowing the answers in advance and back-filling a rule to fit. Her hunch is that both of Marcus's verdicts are right but the theory behind them is a coincidence.

Can you go through the four moves and say who's right about what, at each step? And is there something both of them are missing about where the real disagreement lies?
