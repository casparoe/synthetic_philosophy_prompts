Last week I was at a family dinner going on about how I finally saw my first common raven (huge, croaky, totally different bird from a crow, don't get me started) and my brother-in-law, who's doing a philosophy PhD, said something like "congrats, that's one more piece of evidence, and so is your green jacket." He then explained Hempel's raven paradox and now it's living in my head rent-free.

As I understand it: every black raven I see seems to confirm that all ravens are black. But that claim is logically equivalent to "everything that is not black is not a raven." So if I look at my green lawn and see it's not a raven, I've supposedly confirmed that all ravens are black. Same for my coffee mug, a banana, basically every non-black object on earth, which is nearly everything. This feels obviously insane to me but I cannot find the flaw in it, and my brother-in-law just smirks when I try.

What I want to know is the most important implications of this for how I should actually think about my birding and about evidence in general. Is my life list doing any real evidential work beyond the ravens actually on it — should I in principle be "collecting" non-black non-ravens on my walks the same way, and if not, why not? Is there some real asymmetry between the two kinds of observations, or only a psychological one? And does whatever you say about ravens carry over to everyday induction outside birding — like, does every non-stubborn non-mule I meet really confirm that all mules are stubborn? Keep it under 300 words please, and don't just tell me it's a paradox philosophers worry about, I want the practical upshot for someone who spends his mornings counting birds.
