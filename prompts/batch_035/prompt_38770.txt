I've been cataloguing my gut verdicts about which acts are above and beyond duty, and I'd like to know whether a single principle can capture them. Diving into a flooded river to save a stranger, at real risk to my own life: admirable, and surely not wrong to skip. Donating a kidney to a stranger in ordinary circumstances: the same. Giving up my bus seat to someone who is clearly struggling: somehow not admirable in that way — it feels merely required. Whistleblowing on my employer when it will end my career: here I genuinely waver, since it seems both heroic and something I owe the people being harmed. If I am the only compatible donor and the recipient will otherwise die, giving the kidney still seems above and beyond, yet declining now strikes me as wrong in a way it doesn't when other donors exist — the same act at the same cost, but my verdicts pull apart. And a related wrinkle: donating a kidney to buy a dying stranger a few extra months strikes me not as admirable but as mildly irrational, so it can't simply be that greater sacrifice means greater virtue. Is there a principle that fits all of this? I'd like it stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it has to overturn and why, and its rulings on two cases I keep going back and forth on: an off-duty paramedic who happens upon a serious accident, and giving away 10% of one's income to effective charities. Finally, tell me what the principle predicts about a case I haven't listed — whether it turns out required, forbidden, supererogatory, or something else entirely.
