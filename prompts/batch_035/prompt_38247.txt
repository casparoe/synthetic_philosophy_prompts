I write novels — mostly close third person, but my current project is strictly first person: a woman narrating her own life in something like real time, and a lot of the texture of the book is her reporting her own experiences: the headaches, the taste of coffee, the exact bluish quality of the light in her kitchen at 6am. Here's what's nagging me, though. A friend who did a philosophy PhD read some of the draft and said, half joking, that the whole convention of first-person narration quietly presupposes that the narrator's reports about her experiences are caused by those experiences. My narrator says things like "I finally called the doctor because the pain had gotten unbearable," and the plot moves on that: the report is an effect of the pain and then a cause of everything that happens next. He then mentioned something called epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experiences are caused by underlying brain events but don't cause anything themselves — and some puzzle called the paradox of phenomenal judgment, about how the epiphenomenalist is still supposed to explain how our statements about experience respond to the experiences they're about. He wouldn't elaborate past that (he was leaving for a train), so I'm turning to you.

So here's my question, I guess, put as clearly as I can. What exactly does the narrative convention I'm using presuppose about the mind? If epiphenomenalism were true, would my narrator's claims about her sensations come out false, or true-but-not-caused-by-what-they're-about, or would the novel still work exactly as written because it's really tracking the physical chain and the experiences themselves do no causal work at all? Could I coherently write a novel narrated from inside an epiphenominalist mind — one where every mention of the blueness of the kitchen light is in fact caused by a neural event, and the blueness itself is causally idle, and the reader knows it? Or does that collapse into something unreadable, and if so, does that tell us something about the convention, or about the view? And is the convention really committed to the strong causal claim (experience causes the report), or only to something weaker, like the reports being reliable — and what would the narration have to look like if the weaker thing were all that held?

Dont worry about citations or who-said-what in the literature; if I need to chase down the actual papers I can do that myself later, and honestly I write better when a page isn't half names. What I want from you is the conceptual layout: what the practice of first-person experiential narration presupposes, which rival views of the mind it quietly rules out, whether its parts fit together, and what a version of the book built on the epiphenomenalist presupposition instead would have to change. Sorry if any of this is a mess, I'm typing it at 1am between chapters and the coffee's gone cold.
