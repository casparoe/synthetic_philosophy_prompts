A debate I keep returning to with a friend of mine, a physicist, has stalled completely, and I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is rather than another round of the arguments themselves.

The dispute is over whether chaotic systems vindicate any philosophically interesting claim about predictability and determinism. I hold that deterministic chaos establishes only practical unpredictability: given the exact initial state, the trajectory is fixed; the exponential growth of error is a fact about measurement and finite precision, not about the world; so Laplace's demon is untouched, and treating chaos as an escape hatch for determinism is a confusion of the epistemic with the ontic. He holds that this picture trades on an incoherent idealization: there is no such thing as an exactly specified initial condition in a quantum world, measurement noise is irreducible, chaos amplifies it exponentially, and so within a handful of Lyapunov times the future of even a classical-looking system is genuinely open, not merely unknown. He also tends to lean on this for something larger — the openness of the future, occasionally free will — and at his most ambitious he claims that chaotic orbits are algorithmically incompressible, so that even a being handed the exact initial state as an infinite digit string could not predict or compress the trajectory any better than a coin-toss sequence. Our standard exchange runs as follows: I accuse him of a bait-and-switch, since once he appeals to quantum noise he has changed the subject from deterministic chaos to ordinary quantum indeterminism, with chaos doing no work beyond amplifying it; he accuses me of defending a demon that no physical account of measurement could instantiate, and says an idealization with no possible instantiators is not a permissible idealization.

Neither of us has moved in two years of this. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is substantially verbal — that 'predictable in principle' means something like 'derivable from a complete state' on my usage and something like 'derivable from any state a physically realizable observer could occupy' on his, and that we have been talking past each other. But I'm not certain the dispute dissolves that cleanly, for two reasons. First, there may be a hidden empirical question doing quiet work: whether quantum noise is actually amplified to macroscopic scales in systems like weather or the solar system, and whether decoherence caps the amplification, does not look merely verbal. Second, there may be a genuinely substantive question about whether a system whose initial state can only be specified as a probability distribution should count as determined — whether an objective distribution over futures is different in kind from a single fixed future, or just a fancier way of describing our ignorance.

So: is this a verbal dispute about the phrase 'in principle', an empirical dispute masquerading as a conceptual one, a dispute about which idealizations are legitimate, or a real substantive question about the modal status of the future that would survive any verbal cleanup? Diagnose it using what we each actually say, and tell me honestly whether anything — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or simply an agreed definition — would count as progress, or whether I should expect us to be having the same argument in another two years. Finally, tell me whether his free-will and open-future talk is doing any argumentative work in the exchange, or merely explaining why he cares so much; I've never been able to decide.
