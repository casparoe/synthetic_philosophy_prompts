I work at a mid-sized nonprofit that helps people navigate benefits applications, so I end up staring at the weird architecture of the welfare state all day and some things I can't stop noticing. Unemployment insurance here requires documented job searches every single week and cuts people off for missing appointments, sometimes even one. Disability benefits have heavy gatekeeping up front (endless medical reviews) but once you're on them there are basically no behavioral conditions — nobody tells disability recipients to apply for jobs they might be able to do with accommodations. Child benefit goes to every family with kids regardless of income or behavior, no strings attached. And the enforcement is so lopsided: a single mom who under-reports a few hundred dollars of gig income gets a fraud referral and possible prosecution, while the tax authority treats the identical-sized mistake by a self-employed person as just an administrative adjustment to be repaid. Oh, and the agency claws back overpayments that were caused by its own clerical errors.

My poli sci friend says it's all just politics and path dependence, but it really feels like there's a theory buried in there somewhere. If I squint, the whole pattern looks like it presupposes some view about which needs are the person's own fault: you broke your leg, we pay no questions asked; you're unemployed, and we assume it might be your fault until you prove otherwise week after week. But then why do retirees get unconditional pensions while eating badly and never exercising, if the underlying theory is "fault matters"? And why does the fraud treatment flip when the same dollar amounts get misreported to the tax authority instead of the benefits agency — it's not like the deception is smaller in one case than the other.

So what philosophical commitments does this whole setup actually presuppose? Is it luck egalitarianism (compensate bad luck, don't subsidize choices), or a reciprocity / fair-deal view where benefits are payment for past or future contribution and that's why only working-age benefits get conditions, or just a deservingness heuristic the public applies inconsistently? Which parts of the pattern fit one theory but not the others, and where is the thing genuinely underdetermined — i.e., several views would license the same mess? And if a government took the luck-egalitarian reading seriously, what would actually have to change: would work requirements disappear, or would medical conditionality expand to cover smoking, obesity, risky sports? I'm less interested in the politics of it and more in what the implicit theory is, and whether the parts contradict eachother.
