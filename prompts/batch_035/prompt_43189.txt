A colleague explained Parfit's nonidentity problem to me as follows: a present policy choice, say one of rapid resource depletion, shifts the timing of conceptions, so the people who exist a century later under that policy would not have existed without it; and since you cannot be made worse off by the very act that brings you into existence, and since wronging someone requires making them worse off than they would otherwise have been, it follows that such a policy cannot wrong the future people it affects. If this is a straightforward implication, I am puzzled that philosophers keep insisting we can nonetheless wrong future generations through depletion or climate policy — is that just the same kind of reluctance that makes people resist the repugnant conclusion, which Parfit also proved? Separately, it looks to me as though the whole argument turns on a biological conception of personal identity, where a different gamete yields a different person. If we adopted a psychological criterion instead, so that delaying a conception by a few weeks would not change who comes to exist, the problem would never get started. Would that fix dissolve it, or have I misunderstood what the argument actually depends on?
