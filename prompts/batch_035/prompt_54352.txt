My friend Maya has spent the past semester letting me quiz her about a sorites series: conical piles of sand, all identical in shape and arrangement, differing only in grain count, from 1 grain up to 10,000. Her verdicts so far, recorded as faithfully as I can:

1. "10,000 grains is a heap" — true, and definitely true.
2. "3 grains is a heap" — false, and definitely false.
3. "Either 120 grains is a heap or 120 grains is not a heap" — true; she volunteered that this holds no matter which number we substitute.
4. "For every n, if n grains make a heap, then n−1 grains make a heap" — false.
5. Asked "Is 120 grains a heap?", she says: "I can't tell you it's true, and I can't tell you it's false." Pressed on whether she means there is no fact of the matter or that the fact is hidden from her, she refuses to separate the two: "Show me the stretch of numbers where there's no fact, or show me the pile where the fact flips. You can't, and neither can I."
6. "Is 120 grains definitely a heap?" No. "Is 120 grains definitely not a heap?" No.
7. Asked whether, if we walked her down the series one pile at a time, she would stop saying "heap" at some point, she says yes — but insists that neither she nor anyone could say in advance where, and that there would be no pair of adjacent piles at which she could tell that the change occurred exactly there.
8. On a different predicate: she is entirely comfortable saying that the same 5'10" man counts as tall among jockeys and not tall among basketball players, with both judgments correct.

At next week's meeting, our philosophy club plans to put a new question to her: "Is there some exact number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not?" I know enough to suspect she is implicitly running either epistemicism or supervaluationism about vagueness (or something else I haven't considered), but her record seems to fit more than one of these. Predict her answer to the new question. More important than the answer itself: lay out the candidate rules that fit her recorded verdicts, state for each what it predicts about the new question, flag which verdicts each rule explains easily and which it strains at, give your best guess with reasons, say where her "tall" verdict fits or does not fit, and describe the further question — a concrete case, not a request to state her theory — that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules.

Give the analysis in prose, then conclude with a JSON object having exactly these fields: "verdict" (your prediction of her answer to the cutoff question, as a short string); "confidence" (a number between 0 and 1); "candidate_rules" (an array of objects, each with "name", "easiest_verdicts", "strained_verdicts", and "prediction"); "best_guess" (the rule you favor, with a one-sentence reason); "discriminating_case" (the further question that best separates the rules, plus what each rule predicts she would say); and "mind_changer" (the argument or additional recorded verdict that would most move your prediction, and in which direction). Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
