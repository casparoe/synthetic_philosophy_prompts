I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on rational choice and decision theory, and I'm drafting an essay on what critics of expected value maximization call fanaticism: the claim that an agent maximizing expected value should always prefer a tiny probability of an enormously valuable outcome over a certainty of a modestly valuable one — for instance, choosing a one-in-a-hundred-million chance of saving a billion lives over certainly saving a thousand lives, since the expected value of the former is ten times larger. My draft's central move is an epistemic critique rather than the usual intuition-pumping one. I argue that probabilities like 10^-8 are not really measurements: they are artifacts of a formalism pushed far outside the regime where it has any calibration. When a probability is anchored in physical symmetry — die rolls, lottery drawings, quantum-randomized devices — expected value reasoning works fine even at extreme odds, and I accept what I call fanaticism about dice: if a genuinely random device offers a one-in-a-googol shot at a vast payoff at negligible cost, I claim you should take it. The pathology arises only when the tiny probability is produced by a long speculative causal chain (a stack of assumptions about future technology, institutions, or social dynamics), where our number-generating model has no calibration whatsoever. So my thesis is roughly: be a fanatic about dice, a skeptic about stories.

My TA said the idea is interesting but underdeveloped, and I have three specific worries. First, is the physically-anchored/speculatively-anchored distinction principled rather than ad hoc? A cynic might object that even a lottery drawing involves speculative steps — that the ticket will be honored, that the prize delivers what I value — so all probabilities have speculative tails, and the distinction collapses into a mere difference of degree. Second, does my view conflict with the standard axioms of expected utility theory, particularly continuity: if I accept fanaticism for some lotteries but reject it for others, am I forced into violations of transitivity or continuity somewhere, and would that be fatal to the view or merely an acceptable cost? Third, I worry about symmetry on the negative side: if expected value reasoning is unreliable for tiny probabilities of astronomical gains, doesn't the same argument make it unreliable for tiny probabilities of astronomical losses, in which case my skepticism about stories starts to look reckless rather than cautious, since it would counsel dismissing both the gambles and the precautions?
