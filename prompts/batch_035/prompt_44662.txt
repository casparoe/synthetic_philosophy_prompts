I'm a graduate TA running the discussion sections for a second-year philosophy of perception course, and next week's fifty-minute section is on Molyneux's problem. The main lecture beforehand will already have covered the canonical armchair dialectic — Molyneux's original negative answer as reported by Locke, Leibniz's dissent that sight and touch at least share the notion of extension, Berkeley's empiricist agreement with Locke, and Reid's odd conditional answer that a blind geometer might reason his way to the correspondence. So I don't want to re-teach that. What I want my section to do is examine what happens when the question is dragged out of the armchair: Cheselden's celebrated 1728 report of the boy whose congenital cataracts were couched at around age thirteen, which Berkeley took as vindication of his own theory (and about which there's a more recent suggestion, which I'd like to work in, that the report may even have been ghost-written by someone with an interest in that verdict), and then Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash, where children who gained sight late after cataract surgery in India were actually tested on just the matching task Molyneux specified — with initially negative results that changed rapidly over the following days.

Plan the section for me rather than explaining the problem to me. Specifically: what has to be established before the question can even be posed cleanly — I suspect the distinction between the metaphysical reading (is there a necessary connection between the senses?) and the psychological reading (what will this particular patient in fact do at first sight?) is load-bearing, so tell me whether you agree and where it should enter; the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what; what the single carrying example should be — I could hand out opaque bags with a sphere and a cube, have students identify them by touch, and then ask them to predict what they themselves would match by sight, but tell me whether that bit of theater earns its ten minutes or whether a worked case from the actual Prakash testing protocol carries more weight; and the misconceptions I should expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off — my guesses are "the experiment settled the philosophers' question," "Leibniz was simply wrong," "a negative result shows the senses are unconnected at any time rather than only at first sight," and the reflexive "it turned out to be empirical, so the philosophy was a waste of time," but I'm sure I'm missing some. Close with one question I can ask in the final five minutes that would distinguish a student who grasps why the Prakash results leave the original question more refined than settled from one who has merely memorized the outcome, and tell me what I should openly refuse to adjudicate at this level and say so out loud to the students.
