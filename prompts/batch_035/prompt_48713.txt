My grandmother died last month after about seven years of dementia. she was a novelist her whole life, and the hardest part of those years was watching my family split over whether she was still "really her" — some of my cousins basically decided she'd left the building years before the actual death, and treated visits that way, which I found cruel but also weirdly tempting, because some days it did feel like talking to someone's leftover body. I've been reading Dennett on the self as a center of narrative gravity, this character the brain keeps posting to hold the story together, and it genuinely changes how I think about those years — the self was never a thing that could be "in there" intact or gone, it was always more like a story being kept up, and different people were keeping up different editions of it at the end. But I can't figure out how to put any of that into a eulogy without standing there lecturing a grieving room. Write it for me: it has to comfort people who've never read a page of philosophy, it can settle accounts with the cousins gently or not at all, and it can't say anything about her watching over us or being whole again somewhere — she would have laughed at that, and I'd hear it.
