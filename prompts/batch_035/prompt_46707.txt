I'd like a specification before I wade into the literature on how contractualism should treat numbers. What I keep running into is that everyone argues about particular cases -- whether one strong claim can be outweighed by many weaker ones, whether a million people each suffering a mild headache can outweigh a single death -- while talking past each other about what a solution is even supposed to accomplish. So write the desiderata: what an adequate account of the aggregation of claims within Scanlon-style contractualism has to deliver, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. To my mind the list must at least cover: explaining why numbers seem to matter when a rescuer must choose between saving the one and saving the many, without simply counting complaints; respecting the individualist restriction that no one's loss can be balanced against another's gain -- so say explicitly whether that restriction is itself a desideratum or a camp prejudice to be discarded; handling the small-impositions problem, where rejecting a principle that imposes a tiny burden on each of a great many people would be unreasonable, and explaining how that can be so if we are forbidden from summing; dealing with transgression costs, where one person's very large claim against being killed can be outweighed by many people's claims not to have small costs imposed on them, and with the question of whether everyone affected or only those with objections matter in principle-selection; and staying usable when claims arise under risk or uncertainty rather than certain outcomes, since a principle exposing each of millions to a small chance of serious harm needs to be assessable somehow. For each requirement, say which camp it belongs to, if any, and whether you would treat it as non-negotiable. Then tell me whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- my suspicion is that the numbers intuition and the individualist restriction cannot both be honored, and if so, that result is more interesting to me than any rescue of Scanlon, so flag it clearly if you find it. Finally, indicate which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate accounts, and sketch in a sentence or two how a strict no-aggregation view, a levelling-down-averse aggregative amendment, and an appeal to transgression costs fare against the list -- I want the weight on the criteria, since the scoring is the easy part.
