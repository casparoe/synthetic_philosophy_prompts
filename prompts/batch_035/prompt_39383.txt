So I teach an engineering ethics course, and every year we do a unit on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems -- you know, the thing Andreas Matthias kicked off in 2004 with the claim that once machines learn after deployment, you can't pin their behavior on the manufacturer or operator in the traditional way anymore. The students are mostly engineers, sharp but allergic to philosophy jargon, and I've noticed they basically sort themselves by intuitions about cases long before they know any of the position names. So instead of lecturing the taxonomy at them, I want a diagnostic instrument they take first.

What I want you to write is a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz, and I want it built the right way around: start from the logical space of questions, not from a list of positions, and only afterwards locate the actual positions in that space.

Here's how I want the questions designed:

- 8 to 10 questions, each a short concrete case, with two or three answer options. No question may just ask "do you think there is a responsibility gap?" -- the sorting has to come from what they say about cases, not labels.
- Every question has to be answerable by someone who's never heard the terminology. If a student can't figure out what a question is asking without knowing which position it leads to, it's a bad question.
- The branches should be built from real fault lines, roughly: whether the classic conditions on responsibility (roughly, foreseeability plus control) leave any residue at all with learning systems; whether that residue, if real, is an epistemic problem (nobody could know) or a voluntariness problem (nobody could steer) or both; whether the gap is a metaphysical feature of responsibility itself or just a practical/legal/political artifact that institutions could close if they wanted; whether foreseeable-risk-only responsibility suffices, i.e. whether you're responsible for harms of a kind you foresaw even when you couldn't foresee the instance; and whether the fix belongs at design time (control baked into the system), deployment time (humans able to intervene in the loop), or aftermath (distribute, insure, compensate).

Some cases I specifically want in there, because our intuitions get strained in different directions:

1. A trading algorithm that goes haywire in a way nobody at the firm predicted, but the firm knew that class of algorithm sometimes does this and deployed it anyway.
2. A surgical robot that does exactly what it was designed to do, no bug, but kills a patient in a situation where the designed behavior turned out to be wrong -- no human "erred" in any ordinary sense.
3. A self-driving car that behaves differently in the same situation after an over-the-air update triggered by learning from the whole fleet, so its behavior in this instance traces back to statistical aggregation across millions of other drivers' choices.
4. A weapon system where the human commander nominally has veto power, but in practice only ever has seconds and mostly rubber-stamps.

For the scoring key: map answer patterns onto the actual positions in the literature -- the gap-realist position going back to Matthias; the gap-deniers who think it's just the "problem of many hands" or ordinary liability wearing a costume; the meaningful human control camp with their tracking and tracing conditions (Santoni de Sio and Mecacci's version); the collectivist/distributed responsibility people who close the gap ex post across the whole network of humans involved; the people who say design-time responsibility plus strict liability for foreseeable kinds of harm closes it before anything happens; and the hardliners who say if no one is responsible, the system shouldn't be used at all. Where a combination of answers doesn't obviously correspond to any defended position, say so and give me your diagnosis: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? And flag any two positions my instrument genuinely can't separate -- I'd rather know that than pretend the quiz is cleaner than it is.

One more thing: don't let the quiz silently conflate legal responsibility with moral responsibility, because that conflation is doing a ton of hidden work in this debate and I want at least one question that forces the distinction on them (answers can come apart between "who pays" and "who's to blame").

Please write it all in American English, and keep the question wording plain enough that a mechanical engineer with zero philosophy background won't bounce off it. I'll probably have them retake it at the end of the unit to see who moved where, so its worth having the scoring key granular enough to show movement between adjacent positions, not just a single label per student.
