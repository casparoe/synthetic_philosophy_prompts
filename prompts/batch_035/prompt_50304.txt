I teach a Saturday morning "clear thinking" workshop at my local library — a drop-in crowd of maybe fifteen adults, mixed ages, from a retired engineer who argues about trolleys on Facebook to a couple of college sophomores. Nobody has any philosophy background. This month I want to do a session on thought experiments: what I'm loosely calling intuition pumps — cases like the Chinese Room, Mary the color scientist, the trolley problem, Plato's cave, zombieworld — where a little story is doing the persuading, and how to tell when the story is doing real work versus when it's smuggling in the conclusion through loaded details. The session is ninety minutes, whiteboard but no slides, and the crowd actually shows up because they want to get better at spotting manipulation, so I want them to leave able to take apart a thought experiment they meet in a podcast or an op-ed, not just recite critiques of famous ones.

Don't write the session for me — plan it. What has to be established first before anything about "misleading" even makes sense (I suspect they need to see that the intuition being reported is data about *them*, not evidence about the world, and that this cuts both ways)? Which single example should carry the whole ninety minutes — I'm leaning toward the Chinese Room because I could have them vary its details live on the whiteboard, but talk me out of it or in: what must the carrying example have, and what must it not have (too famous so everyone has cached opinions? too simple so nothing to disassemble?). Give me the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form on the way, in the order they'll surface — my guesses are "thought experiments are just bad arguments, throw them out" and "my gut reaction to the case is the proof" — and exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off, not at the end. Include somewhere an exercise where the audience changes one detail of a pump and watches the intuition flip, since I think that live moment is where the whole thing lands, but tell me where in the order it belongs. Also tell me what to openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes with this crowd. And end with one question I can ask the room to check whether they got the real skill rather than just enjoying the show.
