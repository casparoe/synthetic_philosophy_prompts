I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, writing a thesis on the comparative ethics and law of the sale of sexual services — mostly on the tension between the Nordic model, the legalised regimes of Germany and the Netherlands, and the decriminalisation framework New Zealand adopted. Last month I gave a conference paper asking a fairly specific question: under decriminalisation, could ordinary labour law — collective bargaining rights, health-and-safety inspection, the employment-status tests we use for other precarious service work — be coherently extended to the sector, and what would have to change for that? A discussant cut the discussion short with 'sex work is work', delivered as though it disposed of my question, and the room clearly took it to have done so. I've heard that slogan deployed many times now, but almost always with a different argumentative job in view, and I've come to suspect it's not one claim but a whole family of them, only some of which would actually defeat a question like mine.

Could you disentangle the slogan for me? I want a menu of the distinct precise theses 'sex work is work' could be expressing, ordered as you see fit from the most modest to the most radical. To give you a sense of the distinctions I care about, here are some candidates, though I don't promise they're the right cuts: a purely stipulative or semantic claim about renaming and stigma; a descriptive claim that selling sex shares the economic structure of other service labour (wages for tasks performed for another's benefit, under conditions of market pressure); a normative claim that the transaction can be morally permissible on the same grounds as other labour; an anti-paternalist claim about the standing of sex workers to define their own interests; a labourist claim strong enough to entail extension of ordinary workplace protections — which, notice, would support my question rather than dismiss it; and a flattening claim that it is work *like any other*, with no features distinguishing it from, say, massage or domestic work. There may be readings I've missed entirely, including ones falling between these.

For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or outright false; whether it is widely held, contested, or held by almost no one once stated precisely; and — this is the part I most need — whether accepting it would license dismissing my question about the extension of labour law, or would instead either leave the question open or positively demand it. It would also help if you addressed an awkward wrinkle for the slogan: the Nordic model in effect asserts something like 'sex work is work' for the seller while denying it for the buyer, so at least one apparently monolithic reading is already split by an existing legal regime. If some reading of the slogan turns out to be compatible with all three of the major legal regimes I study, that's worth flagging, since it would suggest the slogan settles less than its users suppose.

Finally, two housekeeping requests. Please say how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the reading you are least sure you have characterised fairly — the one where you suspect proponents mean something you haven't quite captured — rather than presenting the menu as if it were settled. And please write in British English throughout. I'd like to bring the resulting list to the next of these conferences and ask my discussant, before anything else, which of the theses on it she actually holds.
