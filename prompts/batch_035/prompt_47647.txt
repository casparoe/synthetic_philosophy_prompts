retired chemist here — 38 years on polymer degradation before philosophy got its hooks in me. been reading about consequentalism and this "cluelessness" worry, the idea that we can never know the overall consequences of what we do because the effects ripple out into chaos. fine, I buy that as far as it goes. but here's something I want and haven't seen anyone build:

a thought experiment where the setup is inverted. the agent knows the very long-run consequences of her act (centuries out, say) with decent confidence, while being genuinely clueless about the short-run ones (the next few years). the case has to be realistic — a decision somebody could actually face at work, no superintelligent forecasters, no perfect predictors — and no longer than a paragraph. crucially, the far-future confidence has to fall out of ordinary facts about the situation, not be stipulated. if you just declare that the future happens to be predictable here, that's cheating and I'll know it when I see it.

after the scenario, three things. first, walk me through why the cluelessness worry doesn't apply in its usual form — what the act consequentialist actually faces in a case like this. second, identify the feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens to the inversion if you strip that feature out. third, argue the case back at me as a defender of the cluelessness objection: is knowing the far future in this special way actually enough, or does the residual near-term ignorance still poison the expected-value comparison between the options?

my hunch, for what it's worth, is that the real lesson is that cluelessness tracks something about the causal chain other than how far away the consequences are in time — but I want to see whether a well-built case actually shows that, or whether it just ends up a curiosity. prove me right or prove me wrong.
