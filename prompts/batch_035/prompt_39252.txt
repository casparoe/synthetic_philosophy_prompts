My husband and I just got our carrier screening results back and it turns out we're both carriers for cystic fibrosis, so I've gone down a philosophy rabbit hole and I need someone to sort out my gut reactions for me. Apparently this is called the nonidentity problem. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. The famous 14-year-old mother (Parfit's case): she has the baby now instead of waiting, the kid has a much worse upbringing, but a life worth living, and a completely different kid would have existed if she'd waited. My verdict: she does something wrong, but I can't say she wrongs the child. Wrong with no one wronged.
2. A society that depletes resources so that future people — literally different people than would otherwise exist, since the policy changes who meets whom — live worse but still worthwhile lives. My verdict: wrong, and I really want to say it wrongs those future people. Even though the same logic as case 1 applies. These two verdicts together make me nervous.
3. A couple who delay conception a couple of years for money reasons and thereby end up with a different child than they would have had: fine, harms no one.
4. A woman who drinks heavily through early pregnancy before she knows she's pregnant, and the child is born with lasting effects. My gut screams that she harmed that particular child, but I can't point to anyone made worse off than they'd otherwise be — the alternative was no such child at all.
5. Two deaf parents who knowingly conceive a child who will be deaf, because they want a child like them and they're confident a deaf life is a good life. I flip-flop constantly on this one. Swap deafness out for constant pain and I'd say wrong in the case-1 way (wrong, no one wronged), but at some point "wrong, no one wronged" starts to feel like it's not doing any work.

So my question: is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these, and if not, which verdicts have to go? State it precisely enough that it can answer our actual decision, which is this: conceive naturally and take the 25% chance, or do IVF with screening — knowing that screening means a different child than natural conception would have given us, so it's not like we'd be sparing our kid the condition. Does the principle say anything about which of these is better, or does it refuse to rank them? And be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overturn to do it. Under 600 words, I know thats tight, but I'd rather have your one best principle plus its verdict on our decision than a tour of the literature.
