I sit on the discretionary grants committee of a small family foundation with an endowment of about $4 million, from which we award roughly $300,000 a year. Our docket this quarter includes two items, and we are deadlocked.

The first is a routine application: $60,000 for a local food pantry with audited outcomes data, which our estimates suggest will meaningfully improve nutrition for a few thousand households over three years.

The second is from Dr. H. Veldman, a retired aerospace engineer who has applied (and been tabled) three years running. He requests $180,000 to build and install a device he calls a metric-stabilizing lattice. By his own calculations the device has roughly a one-in-10^25 annual chance of preventing a decay of the metastable Higgs vacuum — an event that, should it occur, would destroy all life in our future light cone. He values the stakes at around 10^45 future person-lifetimes, so that his proposal's expected value, on his numbers, exceeds the pantry's by some twenty orders of magnitude. He adds, in an appendix, that the committee's refusal to fund him in prior years has been evidence in his favor: the vacuum has not yet decayed, which he takes to confirm his model, and he has adjusted his probability estimate upward each cycle accordingly.

Our treasurer, who describes himself as a strict Bayesian, argues that declining the proposal is straightforwardly incoherent: if expected value is what we claim to maximize, we cannot let the sheer size of a number frighten us out of arithmetic. Three other members believe funding it would be a parody of philanthropy, but their objections so far amount to incredulous stares. One member has floated a compromise — funding it once, at a reduced amount, to make the problem go away — which strikes the rest of us as the worst of both worlds.

There is also a structural worry we keep circling. Veldman's latest letter notes, correctly, that if we reject him on the grounds that his probability is too low, we are committed to a threshold rule; if we reject him on the grounds that his probability estimate is unreliable, he asks what our rule is for deciding which applicant-supplied probabilities to trust, given that our pantry's figures are also applicant-supplied; and if we simply refuse to engage, he observes that anyone who can name a sufficiently large number will then dominate our entire docket, and that we are one persuasive letter away from handing him the endowment.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our meeting. It should (a) make a clear recommendation on the Veldman application — fund, reject, or fund under stated conditions — and (b) propose a general policy for handling tiny-probability, astronomical-stakes proposals that the committee could adopt in writing and apply consistently to future applicants, not just to this one. The memo must engage the treasurer's expected-value argument on its merits rather than dismissing it, and give an honest accounting of the strongest consideration on whichever side it comes down against. In developing the policy, we would like you to examine the escape routes seriously and say where each buys its consistency at a price: refusing to act on probabilities below some threshold (and what happens to the innocent applications caught by it); discounting or penalizing applicant-claimed probabilities in proportion to the stakes claimed (and how one justifies the penalty without double-counting); bounding the foundation's social welfare function (and what that concedes about how much we ever really valued far-future lives); and treating the reliability of probability estimates themselves as part of the decision problem (including what to do about Veldman's habit of updating in his own favor on the vacuum's continued stability). Aim for roughly 1,100 words, in the register of a real committee memo: numbered recommendations, reasoning a dissenting member could recognize themselves in, and a bottom line we can vote on.
