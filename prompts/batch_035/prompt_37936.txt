Got into this at the pub with my brother and neither of us can get out of it cleanly. He reckons he can prove that no country may restrict immigration at all, he thinks the arguement is airtight, and I keep having the feeling it proves far too much but I can't locate the exact broken step.

His argument:

1. A coercive exclusion is permissible only if it can be justified to the people it excludes — only if they couldn't reasonably reject the grounds.
2. Immigration restrictions coercively exclude people on the grounds of where they were born.
3. Where you're born is morally arbitrary — nobody earns or deserves their birthplace, any more than they earn their sex or their skin colour.
4. Nobody could reasonably accept being shut out of a country's labour market, welfare system, and so on, on the basis of a trait that arbitrary, with no compensation on offer.
5. So immigration restrictions cannot be justified to the people they exclude, and no state may restrict immigration — not "may restrict only somewhat", may not, full stop.

Here's my problem. If the argument works, it works everywhere birth is doing the sorting. Which family you're born into is exactly as arbitrary as which country. Parents who pay for tutoring, leave money to their kids, ring up an old colleague to get them an internship — every one of those shuts other children out of unearned goods, and the justification offered to the shut-out kids is "wrong parents", which is exactly as non-justificatory as "wrong side of the line". So either the argument also proves parents may not advantage their own children at all (which he cheerfuly accepted, mad lad), or something in steps 1–5 is wrong.

When I pushed him on the family thing, he said the cases are different because parents just fail to help other people's kids, whereas border enforcement detains and deports people — coercion versus omission, and the justification requirement in (1) only bites on coercion. Fair enough as far as it goes, but then I start worrying about (2) instead: is turning someone back at Calais coercing them, or merely refusing to hand over a benefit (membership of the club) they never had a claim on? If it's the latter, (1) doesn't even apply to borders and his conclusion evaporates before the arbitrariness premise does any work. If it's the former, the conclusion stands and maybe we really do have to say something uncomfortable about the family, or at least about inheritance.

So: which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd like to keep (3) — the arbitrariness of birthplace seems about as undeniable as anything in this area — and I'd rather not end up at "all borders must come down tomorrow", though I could live with something like "the burden of proof sits entirely on the excluder". Tell me where the argument actually goes wrong, or whether it doesn't — and be specific about the step and why it looked fine to the two of us.
