I've followed Robin Hanson's futarchy writing since the Overcoming Bias days, and after years of nodding along from the sidelines, I'm now in a position to try a small piece of it. I sit on the board of a grantmaking fund that deploys roughly £20 million a year, and at our last away-day the trustees agreed in principle to pilot the futarchic division of labour — "vote on values, but bet on beliefs" — in a limited internal form. Board votes would settle which outcomes we're trying to promote (lives saved, policy change, field-building); questions of fact about how our grants perform would be settled by something like internal prediction markets among staff, grantees, and invited forecasters, rather than by whichever trustee argues longest.

Before we spend political capital on this, I want a triage procedure our board secretary can actually run on any question that comes before us: a fixed sequence of questions that determines whether a given question goes to the market mechanism, goes to ordinary deliberation, turns out to be a values question in disguise, or is unfit for either because manipulation incentives swamp the information gain. The procedure needs to handle the failure modes the futarchy literature itself worries about: traders who bet in order to move a decision they personally benefit from rather than to express a belief; thin markets where a single motivated actor can set the "consensus"; and questions where the very act of marketising them changes what's being measured. It should specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it halts with a firm no, and where it hands the question back to the board because it's a matter of values rather than facts.

The practical constraints matter, so take them seriously: we make perhaps forty consequential decisions a year, mostly in quarterly cycles with a few weeks of lead time; the procedure would be run by one secretary and a part-time analyst, neither of them an economist; anything demanding more than a day of work per question will quietly stop happening within six months.

Please run the finished procedure on three questions we actually face: (a) how many of the bednets distributed under our flagship malaria programme will still be in use three years out, which effectively determines whether we renew it; (b) whether to accept a large donation from a cryptocurrency exchange with an ambiguous regulatory record; and (c) whether our grant-allocation process is systematically biased toward prestigious institutions — a question our CEO has raised, and which several trustees, myself included, would rather not see sent to any mechanism that might produce a number. If the procedure's verdict on (c) is that it goes to the market, say so plainly; that's the case where I'll learn whether the procedure is worth anything.

On epistemics: I'd rather have honest confidence levels than crisp-sounding rules, and I want every place flagged where the procedure mechanises what is really a value call, and every place where you're leaning on a contested reading of Hanson rather than something his proposal actually commits him to. And if you think the whole premise is confused — that a fund of our size is simply too small for futarchic machinery to do anything a well-run internal review couldn't, or that I've misdescribed what futarchy claims for itself — say that directly instead of building a procedure around a mistake I'd then have to discover the expensive way. I've seen enough enthusiasm for clever governance mechanisms curdle into sunk-cost defences of them that I'd count that as the most useful answer you could give me.
