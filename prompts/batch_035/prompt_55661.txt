I teach an upper-level seminar on free will and the sciences of the mind at a small college, and next week we reach the Libet experiments. The class is fourteen students and I have a single 80-minute session. The reading has already given them the vocabulary, but on the page the experiment feels inert: a graph, a few numbers, and then suddenly people are announcing that neuroscience has weighed in on free will. What I want is an activity in which they run a stripped-down version of the paradigm on themselves — the introspective half only, since we have no EEG — and run into its strange parts first-hand: trying to date the moment of their own decision, discovering they can't quite locate it, and noticing how much of the report seems to be reconstruction after the fact.

Concretely, I'm imagining pairs: an actor and a recorder, a rotating spot on a projected clock face (roughly one revolution every 2.5 seconds), and a spontaneous condition in which the actor, at some arbitrary moment of their own choosing, flexes a wrist or presses a button and afterwards reports when they first became aware of the intention to do it, while the recorder logs the actual movement time. I'd also like a preplanned condition — Libet ran one, where subjects are told in advance when to move, and in the real data it produces a differently shaped potential — and a veto round, in which actors form the intention and then sometimes cancel at the last moment, so they can try to date the cancellation the way Libet's subjects tried to date the "free won't." But I'm open to a better design. The fixed requirements are: participants must experience the difficulty of dating their own intentions before anyone names it as a problem, the rules must be explainable in five minutes, and it has to run on nothing more than a projector and a stopwatch.

Please give me the full write-up: rules and roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a way to compile the class's reports into something visible — I want them staring at the spread, the outliers, and anyone whose reported intention lands after the recorded movement. Then the debrief: map what happened onto the three times the real experiment coordinates (movement, reported awareness of intention, readiness potential), and be explicit that the readiness potential is the one thing this activity cannot produce — I will have to hand them the curve from the literature, and I want the debrief to dwell on precisely that hand-off, because it is where the free-will inference either lives or dies. The debrief should also raise, in plain language, the alternative reading of the early potential they haven't encountered — that it may be background neural fluctuation that occasionally crosses a threshold rather than the signature of an unconscious decision — and press them on whether their own noisy reports suggest that the moment they were asked to find is a natural kind at all. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how I should handle each failure.
