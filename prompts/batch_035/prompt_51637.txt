I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I've hit a fork I can't resolve on vibes alone. The book follows a plantation owner's son in the 1820s who turns against slavery over the course of maybe fifteen years, and the question I keep circling is: what actually turns a person like that? I've been reading around the moral revolutions stuff — Appiah's line that dueling and footbinding didn't die because anyone got argued out of them, but because they flipped from being honor badges to being honor stains, the move he gets from Bernard Williams' distinction between honor and morality. And then there's the cruder, older story, where abolition wins because sugar prices shifted, slave revolts made the system look brittle, and the interests lined up — with the abolitionist arguments as window dressing for a decision the ledger had already made.

So here's what I want from you: a real comparison of these two pictures. The honor-flip account versus the interest-based account of how norms like slavery and dueling actually die. Where do they genuinely disagree, where do they quietly agree (I suspect more than either side admits — an honor flip sounds a lot like an interest flip for status-hungry people, since standing IS the currency), and which one fits the timeline of British abolition better? It matters for my book, and this is the part I care about most: if the honor story is right, my character has to lose standing among people he respects before he's even capable of hearing the arguments — the argument lands only after the status fact on the ground has changed. If the interest story is right, he should be rationalizing a choice his pocketbook already made, and the pamphlets are set dressing. Those are very different chapters, even though the externals are identical.

I've also got a half-formed third idea I want you to pressure-test: that moral revolutions are basically narrative events — they need a memorable face before they can spread, and what I'm doing in miniature with one character is the same move a whole society performs. Which might just be novelist vanity, I honestly can't tell. Treat that one roughly.

Also, while you're at it: is there a way to write the character arc so the outcome feels genuinely undetermined from the inside, the way real moral change presumably felt to the people living it, rather than like an arrow the author fired from behind? Or does committing to one of the two accounts force you, structurally, into writing the change as if it was always going to happen? If you can adress that too I'd be grateful — its the thing that will decide whether I keep or cut the last hundred pages.
