I've been reading LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and the adjacent MIRI agent-foundations work for something like fifteen years, and I've noticed that my community has its own private stock of Gödeliana. We are fluent in Löb's theorem — if a sufficiently strong, consistent formal system proves that provability of P implies P, it proves P — mostly via the Cartoon Guide, and we treat the so-called Löbian obstacle as a working engineering problem: a proof-limited agent using a fixed formal system cannot prove the safety of a successor that reasons in the same system, which blocks any straightforward scheme of self-modification or self-trust. There's a whole vocabulary around this — tiling agents, Vingean reflection on successors you can't fully model, trust hierarchies between weaker and stronger reasoners — and the local doctrine is roughly: Gödelian self-reference is real, no system can certify itself, so stop looking for self-certifying agents and study which trust structures the mathematics actually permits.

Recently I finally read the philosophy I had only seen secondhand: Lucas's 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind (1989), plus chunks of the enormous rebuttal literature. And I was struck by how much the two conversations resemble each other, and how little they cite each other. Lucas and Penrose argue that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show human mathematical understanding cannot be algorithmic: given any consistent formal automaton strong enough for arithmetic, we can construct its Gödel sentence, which it cannot prove, but which we can see to be true. The standard replies turn on the human's own consistency being unprovable, on the difference between producing the Gödel sentence once the machine is specified and doing so from a standpoint outside every specification, and on the possibility that we are simply inconsistent or inconsistent-but-useful in ways we don't notice. Meanwhile my community has spent years asserting that Gödel's theorems are strictly about formal systems and prove nothing about minds, while also building an entire research program on the premise that real agents approximate formal systems closely enough that Löb-style limits bind them.

Here is what I want: restate the Lucas–Penrose argument, and the main lines of rebuttal to it, in the vocabulary I actually use — agents, proof systems, successors, self-modification, Löbian obstacles, Vingean reflection — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things the agent-foundations literature already knows, which parts have to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all? Two residues interest me especially. First, Lucas's key move is a first-person one: I see the truth of the Gödel sentence, from a standpoint no formal system occupies. My vocabulary has no such standpoint — an agent that "sees" something is just proving it in whatever system it runs — so is that inexpressibility a defect of my vocabulary, or is it precisely where the argument lives and dies? Second, and in the other direction: is there something the Löbian obstacle literature has settled — that a system's inability to establish its own consistency is a mundane engineering constraint on any self-modifying reasoner, not a mark of a non-algorithmic essence — that the philosophical debate, which mostly argues about minds, never quite gets on the table? I'd like to know whether the translation adjudicates anything, or whether each vocabulary quietly presupposes the conclusion about minds that it claims to be debating.
