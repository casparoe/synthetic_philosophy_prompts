I run a philosophy discussion group of ten adult non-specialists — sharp arguers, no formal training, comfortable with probabilities as they appear in gambling odds and weather forecasts, but not with the idea of graded belief as something one could measure. At our next meeting I have two hours, a whiteboard, and one job: to get them to genuinely understand the principle that a rational agent's degrees of belief should defer to whatever they take the objective chances to be. Plan the session for me; don't explain the principle to me, I understand it — what I need is the pedagogy.

Some constraints and requests:

- The dependency structure matters most to me. The principle is standardly stated as a constraint on conditional degrees of belief — how much of the machinery of conditional probability must I build first, and what is the cheapest scaffolding that will hold it up without the session collapsing into a tutorial on the probability calculus? Where you think the natural order (define chance, define credence, state the principle) gets something backwards, say so and take a side.
- Pick one carrying example and defend the choice. I had been imagining a coin of unknown bias drawn from a bag, but I want you to decide: tell me what the example must display, what it must avoid suggesting (that chance just is long-run frequency; that the principle is merely about learning from data), and whether you would replace or supplement it with something like a casino die or radioactive decay.
- The misconceptions map. I expect, roughly: that chance and credence are the same thing wearing different hats; that the principle just says trust the physicists; that if the underlying physics is deterministic, all chances collapse to zero and one and the topic evaporates; and that one's degree of belief in a future event should equal its chance even after one has learned things the chance itself doesn't encode. Tell me where each belongs in the sequence, and what should beat it.
- A check for the end: one question whose answer would show me they have grasped deference to chance as opposed to having merely acquired the vocabulary.
- What to refuse to explain. I know there are recognized difficulties about how the principle interacts with hypotheses about where the chances themselves come from, and about which background facts a rational agent may permissibly condition on alongside the chances. I would rather name each in a sentence as a live research problem than mangle it — unless you think one of them is actually teachable at this level, in which case say so, take the position that it is, and fit it in.

Please do not give me a menu of options at any point. Choose the order, the example, and the cuts, and give your reasons. If a choice is genuinely close, tell me what the runner-up buys and why it is not worth the cost.
