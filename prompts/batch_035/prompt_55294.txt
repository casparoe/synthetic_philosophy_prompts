I've recently been co-opted onto my city council's transport and environment committee, and the first genuinely contested item to land in my in-tray is one I'd like your help thinking through. Our engineering department wants to replace fixed-time traffic signals with an adaptive control system that optimises overall network throughput. Their modelling shows the largest gains on the radial routes into the city centre, with knock-on effects elsewhere: pedestrian crossing waits lengthen at several junctions in the eastern districts — where, as it happens, the least car-owning wards in the city sit, including the junction where I now walk my daughter to school each morning. When a resident wrote in asking why the change had not gone out to public consultation, the department replied that signal timing is an operational engineering matter, not a policy decision, and therefore falls outside the committee's remit. Several colleagues find that answer perfectly reasonable; a few of us suspect it smuggles a substantive view about the relationship between technical choices and public values through the back door.

Please act as a philosopher of technology with experience advising public bodies, and think carefully before you write. I'd like two things. First, a short preliminary section setting out the distinct positions one could take on whether and how artifacts and technical configurations can be political — roughly the terrain opened up by Langdon Winner's 1980 essay asking whether artifacts have politics — locating the question within the broader debate between technological determinism and the social shaping of technology, and distinguishing, for example, technical arrangements that genuinely require a particular social order from those merely compatible with one. Say which of these positions are near-platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are false. Second, the main deliverable: a draft decision memo for the committee recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame — for instance, whether to pull the retiming into committee business and require consultation, on what terms, and with what safeguards for the department's legitimate operational discretion.

A few requirements. The memo must give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the other side: that treating every tunable parameter as politics would swamp the committee and second-guess professional expertise the engineers genuinely possess. It should engage that worry rather than assume the retiming is obviously political. On the epistemic side, please keep what is settled clearly separated from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on technology and politics, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; that includes the famous example about Robert Moses's allegedly low bridges, which I gather has been seriously questioned, so please don't lean on it as though it were established fact, and don't simply assume either strong technological determinism or its denial. Where the empirical traffic predictions are uncertain, say so rather than papering over it. Please write in British English throughout, and keep the whole thing to something a councillor could read on the train — a few pages at most.
