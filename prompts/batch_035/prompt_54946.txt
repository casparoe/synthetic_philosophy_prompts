While I was hospitalized for three weeks this spring, my neighbor Rosa agreed to give my cat Minnie, who is diabetic, her morning insulin; I demonstrated the injection for her twice before I was admitted. Rosa forgot on at least four mornings, Minnie went into ketoacidosis, and she spent five days in the veterinary ICU. She is recovering, but at a family dinner last week my sister Anya and my friend Theo fell into an argument, in front of me, about what exactly Rosa's forgetting amounts to. It went, roughly, as follows. (1) Anya held that Rosa caused the near-death: had Rosa given the injection, Minnie would not have developed ketoacidosis, and that counterfactual dependence is the core of what we mean when we say one thing caused another. (2) Theo replied that the same counterfactual holds for everyone else who failed to inject Minnie — the vet two towns over, Anya herself, any stranger on the street — so Anya's test yields billions of causes of a single harm, which is absurd; on his view the cause of the ketoacidosis is the untreated diabetes, and Rosa's forgetting is at most a background condition, like the fact that no one else happened to check on the cat. (3) Anya answered that this is precisely where omissions differ from everything else: no one but Rosa had taken on the job, and the line between a cause and a mere background condition is drawn by exactly such facts about who was supposed to do what — if salience is norm-relative in this way, then Rosa's omission is a cause if anything is. (4) Theo said this smuggles responsibility into causation under another name: he was happy to grant that Rosa is culpably negligent and broke a promise, but he denied that her not-doing was an event that produced anything, since the chain that produced ketoacidosis ran through Minnie's own metabolism, and Rosa never entered it — the harm was the houseplant's withering while the gardener stands elsewhere, he said, not anything the gardener did.

They eventually turned to me and asked whether Rosa had caused it, and I found I couldn't say, though I understood each of them as they spoke. I'd like a verdict on the exchange itself, not on my household arrangements. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no overall verdict. Items one through four should rate moves (1) through (4) respectively, each in a sentence or two: say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken on that specific point, and why. Item five, also a sentence or two, should state what, if anything, both of them are missing.
