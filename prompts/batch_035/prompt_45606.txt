Next month I'm presenting to my department's joint seminar in philosophy of probability, which is run together with the statistics group, and I'm told the audience will be hostile in two different directions at once. My thesis is that Bertrand's paradox does not refute the principle of indifference but only exposes an ambiguity in how it has been formulated. On my view, indifference never says "put a uniform distribution over whatever parameter happens to be in front of you"; it says "put a uniform distribution over the orbits of the symmetry group the problem statement actually fixes." Bertrand's chord problem has three classical answers because its statement fixes three different symmetry claims, and the moral of the paradox is that the problem is underdetermined, not that the principle is broken. In the talk I apply this to a couple of the standard paradoxes of indifference — the wine/water case and the cube factory — arguing that each dissolves once you state which group of transformations the description of the setup is really meant to leave invariant, and I close by claiming that indifference, properly formulated as invariance, survives as a rational constraint on priors rather than a generator of them.

The audience, though, is the problem. There will be two committed subjective Bayesians who know the de Finetti representation theorem cold and think priors need no justification beyond coherence plus eventual calibration, so they'll want to know why indifference is doing any work at all that their machinery doesn't already do. There will also be a statistician who is a devotee of Jaynes and is convinced that transformation-group invariance uniquely solves Bertrand's problem outright — he'll think my "the problem is underdetermined" line is a retreat from the stronger position he already holds, and I expect him to press me on whether my formulation ever selects a unique answer or just relabels my ignorance. Scattered among them are a handful of agnostic graduate students who mostly want to know what's at stake, and at least one will have read about the notorious case where demanding invariance under both translations and rescalings of an instrument forces a prior that can't be normalized — the sort of thing that makes symmetry constraints look like a method for generating contradictions rather than resolving them.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I should actually expect from this particular room — not generic textbook worries, but the ones these people, with these commitments, will raise — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased roughly the way its likely questioner would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Distinguish clearly which objections come from the de Finetti camp and which from the Jaynesian, since they require different handling. And include the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — the place where my own position is genuinely weaker than I make it sound in the abstract — with some advice on whether to preempt it or pray.
