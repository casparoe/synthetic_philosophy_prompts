My behavioral-econ friend explains procrastination by saying that present you and future you are basically different people: present you is like a borrower taking out loans in the future's name, future you a stranger who inherits the bill. His evidence is preference reversal — people pick the smaller-sooner reward when it's right here in front of them, but the larger-later one when both are far off — which he reads as the two "selves" having genuinely different preferences, so willpower failure is really a coordination failure between agents, and commitment devices are just rational contracting (Odysseus tying himself to the mast). He even gets policy out of it: default enrollment in pensions is justified as one party protecting another from a predatory borrower. Something about this feels off to me but I cant put my finger on it. Audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence — who the agents are, what the bargaining game is, what plays the contract and what enforces it — then tell me where it breaks. The obvious candidates: future me can't refuse terms or retaliate the way a second person can, and there's an identity and memory continuity between the "selves" that no real bargaining pair has. Also, doesnt a single hyperbolic discount curve generate the reversals all by itself, with no second agent needed? So is the two-selves talk an inference to the best explanation, or a metaphor riding along on the math? And be straight with me about whether the paternalism conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Last thing: if this picture is right, classic akrasia seems to dissolve — nothing is ever done knowingly against one's better judgment, it's just the near-term agent winning the negotiation — and I want to know whether that counts as solving akrasia or explaining it away.
