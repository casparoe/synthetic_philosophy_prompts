I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and a recurring beat of mine has become the numbers that governments attach to things most people would call priceless. When American agencies weigh a clean-air rule, deaths avoided enter the ledger as value-of-a-statistical-life figures — several million dollars each, derived from studies of the extra wages workers demand for riskier jobs. When Britain's health service decides which drugs to fund, the health a drug delivers is converted into quality-adjusted life years, with the adjustment elicited from surveys asking members of the public, in effect, how many years of healthy life they would trade for ten years living with some condition. And in 2003 the EPA briefly valued the lives of people over seventy at $2.3 million against $3.7 million for everyone else — the senior death discount, abandoned after public outcry.

What I have not been able to get from the analysts I interview, or from the philosophers I have quoted, is a reconstruction of the theory of value these practices presuppose. That is what I want from you. The question I most want sharpened: does cost-benefit analysis require that health, life-years, and dollars be commensurable — measurable on a common cardinal scale — or only that they be comparable in the weaker sense of rankable as better, worse, or equal? The distinction matters because critics usually accuse these agencies of assuming that everything has a price, but I suspect the practice is stranger than the accusation. Agencies routinely decline to monetize some goods at all, listing them as unquantified qualitative effects alongside the numeric ledger; and Britain's health assessment body bends its own cost-per-QALY threshold for drugs that extend life by even a few months for people with less than two years to live — a carve-out that seems to concede, mid-analysis, that life-years are not all fungible, even though the method's whole architecture says they are.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory, and be precise about its structure. What exactly does the practice need to be true about value for the arithmetic to be coherent — and is that a claim about commensurability, or only about comparability, or possibly only about the legitimacy of a procedure that need not track any true ordering of better and worse? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose which claims, and which parts merely fit with several rival theories? Where is the practice underdetermined: could an official who believed that health and money are incommensurable, or that choices between them can be hard in the sense that neither option is better and yet one is still the one to make, nonetheless run the same spreadsheets in good conscience? And what would actually have to change in regulatory technique if incommensurability were taken seriously — would the thresholds and the statistical-life figures have to go, or would only the pretense that they measure something, with the numbers surviving as an accepted convention, the way the meter survives? Finally, give me your honest assessment of the senior death discount episode: was it, as most commentary assumed, a moral error by the agency, or was it the method's own internal logic — older people have fewer life-years to save — colliding with a public that regards the arithmetic itself as profane? And what does that collision suggest about the theory of value the voters, as opposed to the agencies, are actually working with?
