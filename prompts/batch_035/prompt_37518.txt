I am a novelist, and I am currently at work on a book whose narrator claims total recall — a woman who insists that everything she has ever experienced is preserved in her memory exactly as it happened, without later distortion. At a recent dinner party, in an effort to explain my character's predicament, I described the colour phi phenomenon: when two small spots of light, one red and one green, are flashed in succession at different positions on a screen, one typically experiences a single dot moving continuously between them, changing colour about halfway across — even though there is no dot in the middle and no colour-change to be seen. I naively remarked that my narrator, with her perfect memory, would be a wonderful test subject for the puzzle, and this ignited a furious argument between two of my guests, both of whom had read their Dennett. My friend Tom took what I gather is called the Stalinesque position: consciousness lags behind events by a substantial fraction of a second, so the brain has time to hold the incoming material in a buffer, edit it, and only then present the finished version to the conscious mind — the colour-change is inserted before the show is ever broadcast, and my narrator would simply remember the edited broadcast. My friend Irina took the Orwellian position: the broadcast goes out live and unedited, but within milliseconds the brain rewrites the record, rather like a totalitarian regime tidying the archives, so that what one sincerely reports afterwards differs from what one actually experienced in the moment — and my narrator's perfect memory, being a memory, would still preserve only the rewritten archive.

They fought through several rounds. Tom pressed what he took to be the decisive difference: on Irina's story there was a genuine, momentary experience of a colourless or greenless movement that is now forever inaccessible, whereas on his story nothing was ever experienced at all — and surely, he said, those are different situations, not merely different descriptions. A patient who briefly suffers during an operation that is then expunged from recall has been harmed in a way she has not been if the suffering was never broadcast in the first place; my fictional narrator would either retain the pre-edit experience (Orwell) or have nothing to retain (Stalin), and that difference should show up somewhere. Irina answered that Tom was sneaking in a privileged present moment — a place in the brain where the stream of experience is finally assembled and available to be either delayed or revised — and that no such place exists. Given the timescales involved, she argued, there simply is no fact about what one experienced, as opposed to what one remembers reporting; the two rival stories are indistinguishable not merely in practice but in principle, and the felt urgency of choosing between them is an artefact of imagining a little theatre inside the head where it all comes together. Tom's reply was that declaring the question empty is itself a substantive claim with teeth: it entails, for instance, that the distinction between actually suffering and merely having one's memory of suffering erased comes to nothing, and he found that consequence hard to swallow.

I should confess that, as a novelist, I am professionally drawn to the idea that memory and experience come apart, so I have tried hard not to let my sympathy show, but I confess I could not follow them into the deeper water, and they eventually appealed to me as the only neutral party left standing. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, at which steps does either of them go wrong, and what, if anything, are they both missing? I would also be grateful if you could tell me, on whatever view you find correct, whether my premise — a character whose memory is genuinely incorruptible — is even coherent, or whether I have built my novel on an incoherent conceit. Please write your reply as continuous prose only, in the manner of a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
