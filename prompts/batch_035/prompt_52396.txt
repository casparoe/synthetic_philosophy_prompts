I'm officiating at my closest friend's wedding this fall, and I need help writing the ceremony. Some background: Dana has been a serious Zen practitioner for fifteen years — she leads the morning sits at her sangha and just finished a three-month intensive training period — while Marcus is a computational cognitive scientist who studies self-models; his running joke about his own field is that the self is the brain's user interface, a dashboard rather than a driver. Somehow these two converged on anatta from opposite directions, one through sitting practice, one through neuroscience. They've asked me — an old friend of both, and evidently the only person either trusted not to botch it — to write a ceremony that doesn't quietly undo their views at the altar.

Here's the problem. Nearly every wedding script assumes two durable selves making a permanent exchange: the vows are promises, and a promise seems to need a promiser who persists in order to keep it. From Dana and Marcus's side, the two people walking down the aisle are processes — patterns of conditions in her idiom, iteratively updated self-models in his — and by their tenth anniversary there will be no fact of the matter that the same two selves still exist, only some mix of causal and narrative continuity between the newlyweds and whoever stands there in 2035. I want a ceremony that is honest about this without collapsing into so vows are meaningless, which would horrify Dana's dharma teacher and confirm the worst fears of Marcus's very Catholic grandmother, who has already confided to me that she worries no-self means he doesn't really love Dana.

Please draft the full ceremony: processional remarks, a short address to the couple and roughly eighty guests (half Zen practitioners, half extended Catholic family, and a handful of Marcus's grad students who will absolutely be taking notes), the vows themselves, the ring exchange, and the pronouncement. Constraints: no metaphors of merging — Dana has vetoed anything about two becoming one, on the grounds that a merged self would just be a bigger illusion; the vows must make clear who or what is doing the promising and how a promise can bind without binding an unchanging essence; include one moment of genuine humor that lands with both the sangha and the grandmothers; and give the pronouncement a closing line the Catholic family can hear as a celebration while the practitioners can hear it as something like a koan. Around 800–900 words for the whole script. End your reply with a one-sentence summary of your strategy for the vows, beginning "Vow strategy:".
