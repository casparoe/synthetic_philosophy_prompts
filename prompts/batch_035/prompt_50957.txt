I'm giving a talk next week at a public policy school defending the claim that we should abolish tuition fees and move to fully taxpayer-funded university education, the way Germany and the Nordics do it, even though our current system of income-contingent loans you only repay above a salary threshold is honestly not that bad. The audience will be mostly labour economists and education policy people, plus two political philosophers sympathetic to luck egalitarianism. My core argument is that university degrees are a positional good — you can't opt out of competing for credentials without paying a real price — so charging students for them is like conscripting people and then making them buy their own uniforms, and I extend this to argue that even the "fair" income-contingent version just means the conscripts pay later. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from these people, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. I already have something prepared for the obvious regressive-subsidy point (kids from richer families dominate enrolment, so free tuition is a transfer to the well-off), so don't dwell on that one. What I'm dreading is the question about opportunity cost — the person who stands up and asks what the same public money would buy if spent on early childhood education instead. Make sure you include that one.
