I spent most of my working life as an experimental geochemist, and the latter part of it on the kind of government advisory committees that produce consensus reports — panels of a dozen or so experts, convened to recommend exposure thresholds, monitoring standards, and the like. Now that I am retired I have begun reading philosophy and formal epistemology, and something I took for granted for years now strikes me as philosophically loaded. I would like your help reconstructing what it presupposes.

Here is the practice. The committees I served on were never permitted to issue findings while substantive disagreement remained among the members. Dissent was to be negotiated away behind closed doors, or, failing that, relegated to footnotes; deliberations were confidential; the published report spoke with one voice. Meanwhile, all of us had read the same literature, and the disagreements were rarely about access to data. What is more, in my own science, when different models give different forecasts, we publish the spread among them as the honest statement of what we know — nobody demands the models agree before the ensemble is released. So the committee practice puzzles me doubly: it demands a convergence my own field treats as neither available nor desirable, and it treats persistent expert disagreement as something to be hidden rather than reported.

Since retiring I have encountered Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want the practice analyzed through it. As I understand it — and please correct me if I am loose with the terms — the theorem says that two rational agents with the same priors cannot agree to disagree once their posterior probabilities are common knowledge between them. So my questions are these. What view of disagreement does the consensus-committee practice presuppose? Does it implicitly assume that something like the theorem's conditions hold — that a group of honest, well-informed experts, given enough closed-door discussion, ought to converge — so that failure to converge indicates a fixable defect (bad faith, miscalculation, politics) rather than genuine information? Or does it presuppose the opposite, and function as a machine for concealing disagreement that the theorem's conditions would never eliminate? Which of the theorem's assumptions — common priors, common knowledge, honesty, a shared partition of the evidence — does the practice pretend are satisfied and which does it silently give up? I would also like to know how the practice relates to the broader epistemology of disagreement I have read about under the names conciliationism and steadfastness: is a unanimity rule a kind of institutionalized conciliationism, a demand that committee members defer to one another until opinions merge, or is it something those categories do not capture? And tell me what a report would have to look like if we took the disagreement itself as the finding — my instinct, imported from ensemble forecasting, is that publishing the distribution of the panel's opinions would be more honest, but I would like to know whether that intuition rests on a defensible view of expertise or a naive one.

I have three requests about the answer itself. First, please keep it to at most 800 words; I would rather have your considered structure than an exhaustive survey, and I am content to sacrifice detail for that. Second, I ask you to be scrupulous about separating what is settled from what is contested: I assume the theorem itself and the precise content of its assumptions are not in dispute among people who know this literature, but I suspect nearly everything about whether the assumptions describe real experts, and whether consensus procedures track truth, is argued over — if so, say so plainly rather than presenting one side as the received view. Third, where I have misused a term of art above (and at my age and stage of philosophical education, I likely have), flag it without ceremony and proceed with the corrected usage; I am a newcomer to this vocabulary and would rather be corrected than indulged.
