I'm in an epistemology seminar this semester and we just finished three weeks on the surprise exam paradox, and honestly I came out of it less confused about the paradox than about why the debate around it never moves. Quick setup so we're on the same page: the teacher announces there'll be an exam next week, Monday through Friday, and that you won't be able to know the night before which day it's on. The classic backwards induction kills Friday (if you haven't had it by Thursday night you'd know it's Friday), then Thursday, and so on until there can't be an exam at all. But then the exam lands on Wednesday and you really are surprised, which feels like it refutes the induction — except nobody can point to the step that's actually wrong, and every "solution" we read seemed to just relocate the weirdness.

Here's the thing. The literature goes back to the 1940s and as far as I can tell it's completely stalled. You've got the Quine-style camp saying the students can never really know the announcement, so the induction never gets started, and the whole thing is an artifact of pretending they know something they can't. You've got the blindspot people (Sorensen and others) who treat the announcement like a Moore-paradox sentence — true, but unknowable by the very people it's about — and say the paradox is really about self-knowledge, not induction. You've got a camp that says the announcement, fully spelled out, is just flat inconsistent, and a camp that says it's perfectly consistent and the induction simply fails at the first step, though they can't agree on why. And lately there's a line of argument that the students can't know the exam will be a surprise, so the announcement couldn't truthfully be known in the first place. Everyone's read everyone else, nobody has budged in decades, and when we argued it in class we reproduced the whole stalemate in two hours, which was kind of depressing.

So what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right. My working hypothesis, and tell me if it's dumb, is that the camps aren't actually disagreeing about a proposition — they're modeling different objects. The "it's inconsistent" people treat the announcement as a static proposition to be evaluated once, the "induction fails" people model the students as agents updating over five days, and the blindspot people mostly care about the reflexive structure (a claim about what you won't know). If that's right, a lot of the fight is two groups answering different questions under one name. But maybe it's something else: maybe they disagree about what counts as data — the real-world cases where students are in fact surprised, do those count against Quine-style solutions or are they just sloppy versions of the setup where the announcement wasn't fully believed? Or maybe it's bedrock intuitions about whether "you can't be surprised" is even a coherent demand, and neither side can argue for theirs.

Also, if you have thoughts on whether the connection people draw to Moore's paradox and knowability results (Fitch-style stuff) is doing real work or is just a vibe, I'd take those too. And most importantly: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a fully formal treatment in some epistemic logic, a new distinction (e.g., between the exam being unsurprising and the announcement being unknowable), or is this one of those cases where the impasse tells us the question is ill-posed and we should just move on?
