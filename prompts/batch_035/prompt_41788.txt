I am a novelist, and my current book follows two sisters in a farming county: one a humane-law officer who investigates cruelty complaints, the other a manager at a large dairy operation. To write their arguments credibly I need to understand the moral grammar of the actual law they each live under, and the more I read the stranger it looks. In our jurisdiction, deliberately torturing a dog for amusement is a felony, and judges at sentencing say things about the offender's heartlessness — the crime seems to be, in part, about what cruelty reveals of a person's character. Yet the same code exempts customary agricultural practices that inflict suffering on animals on a scale no private sadist could match, and the exemption is justified by appeal to convention and economic necessity rather than to anything about the animals. Meanwhile, a farmer may lawfully have a vet euthanize a healthy animal simply because it is inconvenient, while a person who stages dogfights is treated as among the worst of offenders even though the dogs are, in some sense, doing what they are bred for. My sisters keep circling this without a vocabulary for it.

I have been reading Kant, whose view I want to put on trial here: that we owe animals no duties directly, since they are not ends in themselves, but that cruelty to them is still wrong because it damages the agent — it dulls the sympathy on which our duties to other humans depend, so that someone who is cruel to animals becomes hard in his dealings with people. What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit moral theory of this body of law. Does the whole pattern — felony cruelty keyed to the offender's depravity, the customary-practice exemption, the permission of convenience euthanasia alongside the ferocity of dogfighting prosecutions — presuppose something like Kant's indirect-duty view, on which the animal's suffering has no standing of its own and what is really being protected is human character and human sensibilities? Or could a direct-duty view, on which the suffering itself counts, still license most of it once tractability and line-drawing are factored in? I'd like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and to identify where it is genuinely underdetermined — places where a Kantian and a suffering-focused utilitarian would defend the same statute for different reasons. Then tell me which of these features of the law could not survive the replacement of the Kantian background with a direct-duty view: I suspect the sentencing language about character is the load-bearing wall, but I may be wrong. Finally, as a novelist I am interested in what a prosecutor or defense attorney in my book would say to a jury if the law's implicit theory were made explicit — would the jury accept it, or is there something in ordinary moral psychology that the law's philosophy quietly diverges from? That divergence, if it exists, is where my sisters will finally have their falling-out.
