I'm part of a small reading group that has been working through classic short texts on the justification of deduction, and this week we're discussing Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (published in Mind). I can follow the surface of the dialogue well enough, but I'd like help with the precise mechanics of the regress and a couple of points that seem to be doing more work than they appear to.

Here is the central portion of the text:

[A] Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other.
[B] The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same.
[Z] The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other.

Readers of Euclid will grant, I suppose, that Z follows logically from A and B, so that any one who accepts A and B as true, must accept Z as true?

[... the Tortoise then insists he accepts A and B as true but does not accept the Hypothetical:]

(C)  If A and B are true, Z must be true.

After some back-and-forth in which Achilles extracts this further concession and writes it down, the Tortoise repeats the maneuver:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it
(D)  If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.

[When the Tortoise still refuses Z, Achilles protests that Logic would take him by the throat and force him, and the Tortoise replies that whatever Logic is good enough to tell him is worth writing down, dictating:]

(E)  If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true.
Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z.

My questions:

1. Exactly which step generates the regress? It looks like the Tortoise treats the conditional statement C as just one more premise sitting alongside A and B in the notebook, rather than as a statement of the rule of inference (modus ponens) that would license the move to Z. Is he entitled to that treatment, or is the dialogue best read as exposing a confusion between propositions and rules? And if it is a genuine confusion rather than a genuine puzzle, what has the dialogue actually shown about the justification of deduction?

2. There seems to be a difference between accepting a conditional as true and being prepared to perform an inference on its basis. Is the Tortoise's position coherent because that gap is real, and if so, does any formulation of deductive justification that proceeds by adding further propositions (including conditional ones) fall to the same regress? What kinds of non-propositional answers are even available — for example, appeals to the meanings of the logical constants, or to the normativity of rules themselves — and do any of them avoid simply restating the problem?

3. At the end of the dialogue, the Tortoise finally does accept Z, once the discussion has moved on and the pair are sitting on the seashore, and Achilles is still filling the notebook. Carroll seems to be gesturing at a contrast between what logic compels in theory and what a reasoner can actually go on refusing in practice. How much of the dialogue's force rests on this closing contrast, and how should we read the difference between the Tortoise's merely possible stubbornness and any actual refusal to infer?

4. The dialogue opens with a joke about the race-course of Zeno's paradox, and the Tortoise proposes a new race-course whose distances constantly increase rather than diminish, which he claims most people think can be crossed in two or three steps. How exactly is this framing supposed to map onto the logical regress, and does the analogy carry any argumentative weight on its own, or is it purely ornamental?

Please explain as concretely as you can, using the letters A through E as the text does, and with particular attention to question 1 — I want to be able to state precisely, for our discussion, at which point the Tortoise makes a request that no legitimate deductive system could satisfy, and why.
