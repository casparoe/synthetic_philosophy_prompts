For a seminar on classical American philosophy I've been asked to write a short position paper stating where I stand on truth, so that my classmates can push back on it. Rather than an argued essay, it's meant as a bare profile — five or six numbered commitments with minimal defense, on the model of the one-page statements we circulated earlier in the term for the units on Wittgenstein and Quine. Before I submit mine, I'd like an audit of whether the package hangs together, since a couple of people in the group are far better read in Peirce than I am and I'd rather find the cracks myself than have them found for me in front of everyone.

Here is the draft: (1) I endorse the pragmatic maxim in something like its original form — the whole meaning of an intellectual concept is exhausted by its conceivable practical bearings. (2) I take truth to be the opinion on which the community of inquirers would converge under ideally continued inquiry — in principle reachable, never actually possessed by anyone at any finite stage. (3) I'm a thoroughgoing fallibilist: nothing I believe, including my belief that inquiry converges at all, is immune to future revision. (4) I'm firmly opposed to the Jamesian line on which a belief's being true has something essential to do with its being satisfying or good to live with — that strikes me as confusing the utility of believing with the truth of what is believed. (5) I nonetheless hold that there are unknowable truths: a claim about the exact number of leaves on a tree in a forest that burned before anyone counted, or about the outcome of an experiment no one will ever run, can be true even though ideally conducted inquiry by beings like us could never settle it. And (6) I think truth is, at bottom, fully captured by the disquotational schema — snow is white is true if and only if snow is white — so that truth is a transparent, non-substantive notion rather than a deep metaphysical property.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and how badly; for each real tension, what the cheapest repair is and what it would cost me elsewhere; and, at the end, which single revision would clean up the most. I'd also like the answer to separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested among people who know the topic — for instance, I've heard that even the question whether Peirce himself held a convergence theory of truth, as opposed to a more austere realism that merely appeals to the methods and habits of scientific inquiry, is disputed among Peirce scholars, so please flag wherever my package leans on a contested historical reading rather than on anything the arguments themselves require. Don't soften the verdict to spare me: if two of my commitments simply cannot both be kept, say which one has to go and why that one rather than the other.
