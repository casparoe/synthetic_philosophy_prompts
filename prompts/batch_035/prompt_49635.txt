At a strategy retreat for an animal-welfare organization, a colleague shut down a proposal to fund large-scale rewilding and habitat restoration — the proposal's rationale being that well-designed habitats would allow very large numbers of animals to flourish — with the remark that our task is to reduce suffering, not to create happiness. That line is repeated constantly in suffering-focused ethics circles, and it clearly did real work in the room: it converted a disagreement about a concrete grant into a matter of principle. I suspect, however, that the slogan packs together several claims of very different strength, and that not all of them would actually support the conclusion my colleague drew from it.

Please disentangle the slogan for me. Lay out, as a structured list, the distinct precise theses that "reduce suffering, not create happiness" could be expressing, ordered from the relatively innocuous to the maximally strong. For each reading, indicate: (a) whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; (b) what it costs to hold it, given known objections — for instance, that a lexical reading implies that preventing a single episode of mild discomfort could outweigh the creation of lives of overwhelmingly positive quality, or that the weighting reading can be eroded by enough happiness at the margin; and (c) whether accepting that reading would actually vindicate my colleague's use of it, namely the claim that bringing new flourishing lives into existence is of no, or merely lesser, moral importance in a way that settles the rewilding question.

In particular, I want the menu to distinguish at least the following possibilities, and to add any others you think are in play: a lexical priority (no amount of happiness can outweigh any amount of suffering, however slight); a mere differential weighting (happiness counts, but suffering counts for more); an asymmetry specifically about making happy beings exist, where the benefiting parties do not yet exist and so cannot be claimed to be benefited — a point on which the slogan may be trading, without saying so, on the difference between making people happy and making happy people; a scope restriction (the slogan applies only to beings that already exist); and a purely practical or epistemic reading (suffering is easier to alleviate than happiness is to create, or our evidence about future flourishing is too weak). Please also say, for each reading, whether it is one the people who repeat the slogan actually need for their conclusions, or one that merely sounds like it would deliver those conclusions.

I would like the list to be clear and self-contained enough that I can bring it to our next meeting.
