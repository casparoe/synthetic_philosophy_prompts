A chapter in a popular book on decision theory I've been reading summarizes the case for expected utility maximization roughly as follows: the coherence theorems show that if your preferences violate certain basic consistency requirements, you are guaranteed to lose money to anyone who can trade with you. For instance, if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a trader can cycle you around those trades until you have surrendered everything you have. The chapter then claims that von Neumann and Morgenstern proved the converse as well: any agent whose preferences cannot be exploited in this way behaves exactly as if it assigns numerical probabilities to outcomes and always chooses the option with the highest expected utility. From this it concludes that rationality literally forces you to be an expected utility maximizer — there is no coherent alternative — and that any artificial intelligence not built this way will inevitably be pumped of all its resources by a rational opponent. The chapter closes by inferring that probabilities must therefore be objective facts about the world, since even ideal rational agents cannot disagree about them.

I found this pretty persuasive, but I have a nagging feeling some steps are too fast. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a working decision theorist accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications that gloss over assumptions worth naming, and which are genuinely wrong or non sequiturs? In particular, I'd like to understand whether the conclusion that there is "no coherent alternative" to expected utility maximization actually follows from the accurate parts of the chapter's argument, and whether the objectivity-of-probability claim follows from anything in it at all. Finally, since I can't check this against the literature myself right now, please flag where your own audit is most likely to go wrong — which of your verdicts rests on a contested or minority reading of the theorems, and what a defender of the chapter's stronger claims could reasonably say that would make you revise your assessment.
