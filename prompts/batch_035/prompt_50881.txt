i write novels, which means I'm constantly making judgment calls about what caused what in the plot — was it the father's cruelty that ruined her, or was she headed for ruin anyway and the cruelty just set the date? I gather philosophers have two main tests for this kind of thing: the counterfactual one (would the outcome not have happened but for X?) and the interventionist one (surgically wiggle X, hold everything else fixed, and see if the outcome tracks). Please compare the two for me, but for a novelist's use. Two things especially: (1) overdetermined plots, where my character was headed for ruin down two independent roads and the visible trigger is just the one that arrived first — do the two tests disagree about whether the trigger caused anything, and does one of them give the answer a storyteller actually needs, namely that the trigger caused the timing of the ruin even if it didn't cause the ruin? (2) the awkward fact that in fiction I can literally run the counterfactual — rewrite the chapter without X and watch what happens — but I can never run a proper intervention, because in a story you can't hold everything else fixed; characters aren't wired that way. Does that asymmetry make the counterfactual test somehow native to narrative, and if so, could it be biasing me toward the wrong causal judgements when I plot? keep it practical — I'm not trying to read a philosphy paper here, I'm trying to figure out whose fault the ending is.
