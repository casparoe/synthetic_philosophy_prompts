I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of logic course, and this term we read Lewis Carroll's What the Tortoise Said to Achilles. In seminar we tried to turn the tortoise into a general argument that deduction cannot be justified, and I have been unable to find the flaw in it. The conclusion is not one I can live with. Here is the argument as we left it:

(1) A person is justified in accepting the conclusion of a deductive inference only if they are justified in accepting the premises and justified in accepting that the inference is valid.

(2) Carroll's regress shows that this justification cannot itself be one of the inference's premises. Achilles writes down A and B; the tortoise accepts both but refuses Z until Achilles writes down the hypothetical "If A and B, then Z"; the tortoise accepts that too and still refuses Z, demanding a license for the new, enlarged set of premises; whatever license is supplied is just another premise, and the demand recurs forever. So no finite set of accepted premises, however augmented, compels the conclusion.

(3) Therefore, if any deductive inference is justified, the justification must come from somewhere other than the premises from which the inference is drawn.

(4) The only candidates are the agent's recognition of the inference's logical form and the agent's acceptance of a rule of inference, such as modus ponens.

(5) But recognizing a form and accepting a rule are, like premises, states with content, and the tortoise applies to them too: to be justified in following modus ponens on a given occasion, the agent must be justified in taking the rule to apply on that occasion, and that step is either another premise (regress, by (2)) or another rule (whose application raises the same question, so regress again).

(6) Therefore no deductive inference is justified as such, and the tortoise can never be moved to accept Z by reason alone.

Two things make me suspicious. First, (6) seems to prove far too much: a structurally parallel argument appears to run for induction, for memory, and for perception, since for any source of belief we can ask for a license to trust it, and any such license either presupposes the source or regresses. If our regress and those regresses are the same shape, the argument shows not that deduction is special but that nothing is ever justified, which is the skeptic's blanket rather than a finding about logic. Second, a classmate pointed out that Quine pressed a related complaint against conventionalist accounts of logic, namely that deriving logic from conventions already presupposes the logic needed to carry out the derivation. If his complaint and our regress are the same phenomenon, I do not know what the moral is supposed to be.

Our seminar's leading diagnoses were these: reject (1), on the ground that it smuggles in an access requirement that a justification for inference need not satisfy; reject (4), because there is a third candidate, something like trained inferential competence, a matter of knowing how rather than knowing that; reject (5), on the ground that it treats every justification as inferential in exactly the sense at issue, which is the equivocation doing all the work; or reject the framing outright, on the ground that the tortoise concerns what licenses an inference rather than what justifies a belief, and that the argument slides between the two.

I would most like to keep (1), since it looks like the only thing standing between inference and guesswork. And whatever step you tell me to reject should, ideally, be a step whose rejection also blocks the parallel arguments for induction, memory, and perception — or, if it does not, you should say why logic is entitled to a treatment those are not. Please name the step you reject, explain why it looked fine when it was first stated, price the alternatives honestly (including the ones my seminar did not consider), and close with a final line in exactly this form: "Verdict: reject step (N) — <at most ten words on the cost>."
