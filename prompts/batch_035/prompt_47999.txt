I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. My background is in building reinforcement-learning systems, and for the past year I've been training pairs of agents to play long sequential bargaining games against each other. Next month I'm presenting to our working group on multi-agent systems, and I want to be ready for the question period. I'd like the prep sheet delivered as structured data rather than prose.

My thesis: backward induction is only sound within what I call the "experienced depth" of a game — the number of moves ahead that the players have actually encountered and learned from — and the classical centipede-game conclusion (take at the very first node) is an artifact of assuming the entire game tree is common knowledge with unbounded confidence. My argument, briefly: (1) classical backward induction requires common knowledge of the game structure and of rationality at every node, including nodes the theory itself declares unreachable; (2) real agents, whether human or trained, acquire their model of the game from experience, and the depth they have reliably experienced is finite and modest; (3) beyond that depth, the situation is better described as an open-ended repeated interaction, in which sustained cooperation is rational; (4) so in a long centipede game, rational agents should pass for most of the game and defect only near the end, and the familiar paradox of reasoning through nodes one's own theory calls unreachable dissolves — beyond experienced depth, those nodes are just ordinary decision points under a different and better-justified information state.

The audience: about fifteen people. Six are reinforcement-learning engineers like me, who mostly care whether self-play agents show late-game defection in practice. Four are game theorists and economists from our market-design group; at least two of them regard common knowledge of rationality as settled groundwork, view appeals to bounded rationality as a hand-wave, and will suspect an engineer of using "learning" to smuggle in whatever conclusion he wants. The rest are two philosophers of science who care about counterfactual reasoning at unreached nodes, one decision theorist, and two managers who will ask about product implications.
