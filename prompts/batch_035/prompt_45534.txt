I'm teaching a seminar segment on Ayn Rand's critique of altruism, and I want to stress-test her distinction between altruism (sacrifice of oneself to others as the standard of value) and mere benevolence (a minor goodwill she allows) against Dickens' A Christmas Carol. Please read the story through Rand's ethics, pursuing two threads in particular.

First, the conversion mechanism. On its face the story is an anti-Randian parable: the rich man is wrong to keep his money and right to give it away. But look at how Dickens actually persuades Scrooge. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows him his own unmourned grave and his bedcurtains stripped by scavengers; the earlier spirits show him his own forfeited past — his sister, his lost fiancée, Fezziwig's party. Nearly every persuasive move is prudential: this life is emptying you out and will leave you alone and unmourned. Is Dickens implicitly running on something like rational self-interest, properly understood, while rejecting Rand's conclusions — or does the ending undo that reading?

Second, the caricature problem. Pre-conversion Scrooge is no Roark: he doesn't create anything, he hoards; he's a lender who renounces every pleasure. On Rand's own terms that looks closer to the life-denying self-abnegation she condemns than to rational selfishness. So which is it: does the story give her the businessman caricature she says literature always gives, or does Scrooge's miserliness actually vindicate her complaint that selfishness gets equated with penny-pinching — the exact smear her ethics tries to break? Consider especially whether the reformed Scrooge is ever shown gaining anything a Randian would count as genuine flourishing (productivity, achievement, joy in one's own life), or whether his reward consists solely in others' warmth — and what that implies about whether Dickens' generosity is benevolence or full sacrifice.

Be fair to both sides, and say plainly where the story resists the Randian reading. Use no direct quotations from Dickens or from Rand — paraphrase scenes instead. Structure your response in these sections, in order: "What Rand means by altruism" (about 150 words); "The prudential reading of Scrooge's conversion"; "The miser as anti-Randian figure"; "Where the story resists Rand"; "Verdict for the seminar" — roughly 150–250 words per section after the first.
