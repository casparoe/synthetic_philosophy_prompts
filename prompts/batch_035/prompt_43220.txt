I've drafted the notes below for a short philosophical essay and would like critical feedback before I write it up properly. Please assess whether the central move works, and flag any place where the argument is doing something illicit.

Background for the essay: Daniel Dennett's "Where Am I?" tells the story of his brain (Yorick) being removed from his body (Hamlet), kept alive in a vat, and connected to the body by radio. Later, a duplicate brain (Hubert) is grown from a neural transcript, and the story ends with Dennett unable to say whether he is wherever Yorick is, wherever Hubert is, both, or neither.

My essay's idea: Dennett's puzzle, I want to argue, is an artifact of discreteness. Every stage of the story involves an abrupt switch — brain pulled out all at once, radio link established in one operation, Yorick and Hubert swapped like parts. I want to tell a modified version in which everything happens continuously: the brain's functions are replaced by neural prostheses one micro-circuit at a time, over months; the radio link is gradually replaced by a chain of relay stations extending further from the body; eventually the last biological neuron is superseded, with no moment at which anything is "transferred." My claims are: (1) in this smooth version, the question "where am I?" never becomes pressing, because there is no occasion on which anything jumps; (2) this reveals that Dennett's puzzle exploits our habit of tracking identity through discrete transitions, and that the felt paradox is a sorites effect — the "where" question has no fact of the matter because location-of-self is not a well-defined quantity, only a shorthand for various practical concerns (where my sensory inputs arrive from, where others direct their attention, where my body is); (3) the right conclusion is that Dennett's question is not hard but malformed.

My worries, which I'd like your comments on specifically: Is the continuous variant really coherent, or does gradual replacement just hide the same hard transition inside a lot of small ones — in which case the sorites pressure should make the question harder, not dissolve it? Is claim (2) fair to Dennett, given that part of his point seems to be exactly that our ordinary concept of a located self comes apart under pressure? And does claim (3) overreach — even if "where am I?" lacks a determinate answer, that might show something about personal identity rather than about the question's well-formedness? Please limit your response to 450 words.
