I'm an AI safety grad student and I've been getting pretty deep into mechanistic anomaly detection lately, and I want to stress-test the thing. Quick background in case it helps: the rough idea behind MAD is that you train a monitor only on "benign" activity — activations from the model while it does harmless stuff, or a reference model trained on benign data — and then flag anything mechanistically anomalous as potentially dangerous. The bet is that you don't need to enumerate the bad behaviors in advance; you just exploit the assumption that whatever a model is doing when it's, say, covertly planning a power grab, it's not doing the same *thing* it does when it chats about recipes, so the internals should look weird relative to the benign distribution. The load-bearing premise, as I'd put it, is something like "task dissimilarity implies mechanism dissimilarity": capabilities that are algorithmically very different won't be implemented by re-using exactly the benign machinery that the detector was calibrated on.

You're a veteran interpretability researcher who's also read way too much philosophy of science, so take this seriously. Here's my position: I think this is a sound research bet, i.e., I think that for realistic deceptive or dangerous capabilities, benign-only anomaly detectors have a decent shot at catching the dangerous stuff, because the mechanisms will differ enough. What would count against it? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, and I want you to cover all three of: (1) formal/conceptual results — e.g., anything suggesting that at scale almost everything routes through one general-purpose reasoning mechanism, so that "being goal-directed" or "deceptively planning" is more like a property of the input/goal than a distinct mechanism, meaning benign and dangerous capabilities differ in what they compute but not in *how*; (2) cheap empirical observations that could in principle be run on current models — like a specific pattern of results on held-out dangerous-capability evals that would make me update, and what result would instead be genuinely reassuring; and (3) the definitional worry my labmate keeps raising, which is that "same mechanism" is doing all the work and nobody has pinned it down — two computations can share subnetworks but differ in circuit structure, or share structure but differ in the role that structure plays, and depending on which notion you pick, the whole bet is either trivially true or trivially false.

Also, and this is the part I most want you to be honest about: which of these defeaters would a committed MAD defender concede as counting against the bet, and which would they swat away by moving the goalposts — say, by saying the detector only needs to add a few bits of evidence, or that "anomaly" never meant "distinct mechanism" in the first place? Has the position been quietly qualified into something unfalsifiable, and if so, is that fatal for a research agenda of this kind, or is it normal for a bet at this stage?
