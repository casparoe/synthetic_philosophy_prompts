The debate over whether 'knows' is context-sensitive has, on my reading, been running in place for a decade and a half. Contextualists hold that the truth conditions of a knowledge ascription vary with the attributor's conversational context; subject-sensitive invariantists hold that the standards are fixed by the subject's practical environment rather than the speaker's; assessment-relativists hold that the relevant context is neither the attributor's nor the subject's, but the context of assessment. Yet all three camps read the familiar bank cases in exactly the same way: in the low-stakes scenario, where a couple merely needs to deposit a cheque before Tuesday, we happily say they know the bank opens on Saturday, and in the high-stakes scenario, where the deposit cannot be made on any other day, we withhold the ascription — and nobody disputes the pattern. The divergence only surfaces in engineered corner cases: third-person ascriptions in which the attributor's stakes diverge from the subject's, retrospective assessments of past knowledge claims, ascriptions embedded under conditionals or in belief reports. These are precisely the cases about which ordinary speakers have the weakest and least stable intuitions. Meanwhile, experimental work suggesting that stakes effects on knowledge ascriptions are weaker and more heterogeneous than the case methodology assumed has somehow been absorbed by every camp as further confirmation of its own view, and papers in the area increasingly consist of re-running the same handful of cases with diminishing returns.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about the semantics of 'knows' at all, but about the division of labour between semantics and pragmatics — which is to say, about what a semantic theory is for — and that once this is granted, a good deal of the dispute turns out to be verbal. But I am not confident this diagnosis is right, because it does not obviously fit the invariantist who insists that interest-relativity is a fact about the state of knowledge itself rather than about the word: such a person can concede the whole pragmatic story about ascription patterns while retaining a substantive epistemological thesis that the contextualist need not deny but typically does. If that is right, the verbal and the substantive threads run tangled through the same debate.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'knows', or three camps answering different questions — one about a lexical item, one about an epistemic state, one about the truth of ascriptions — under a single heading? Please support your diagnosis with what each side actually claims about the cases, not with labels. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a sharper case, a psycholinguistic finding, a formal result about what assessment-relative truth can and cannot express, a principled redivision of semantics from pragmatics — or whether, instead, no progress should be expected, because the dispute rests on bedrock convictions about the point of semantic theorising that no side can argue for without circularity. I would also like you to address the possibility that the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed: that 'knows' simply is not the sort of term that has a single tidy semantics, so that demanding one is demanding a joint verdict where the phenomenon admits of none.
