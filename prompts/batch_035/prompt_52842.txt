I write novels, and a reviewer once complained that my characters treat promises wildly inconsistently. Rereading my own books I think she has a point, but now I can't tell whether its my characters or me. So here are my actual gut verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them. A promise made at gunpoint doesn't bind. A promise a con artist lied to get out of me doesn't bind either. But a deathbed promise to look after my friend's garden still binds after he dies, even tho there's no one left to be owed anything. A tipsy promise binds, but a falling-down-drunk one doesn't. A guy threatens to reveal true but damaging facts about a woman unless she promises to marry him — and I feel that promise binds a little, which bugs me, since it's still coercion. A promise to do something immoral never binds. And New Year's resolutions to myself don't bind in the promise sense at all, yet breaking them feels like a real wrong, just a seperate kind of one. Is there one principle behind all this, and if not, which of my verdicts would you throw out and why? Please also tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't decided: a character promises to repay what she thinks is a debt, then discovers the original "loan" was actually a gift the lender misremembered — does the repayment promise bind? Answer in plain prose, no headings, bullet points, or bold text, just continuous paragraphs like a letter to a fellow novelist.
