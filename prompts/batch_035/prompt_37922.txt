Next month I'm giving a talk at a interdisciplinary workshop on fiction and emotion. The audience will be mostly empirical emotion researchers — the kind who measure heart rate and skin conductance while people watch film clips — plus a couple of Waltonian aestheticians. My thesis: contra Walton's quasi-emotions idea, what we feel for Anna Karenina is a perfectly real emotion, just motivationally boxed in. It has the full physiological and appraisal profile of genuine fear or pity, but the action tendencies get quarantined because we're not located in the fictional world — we can't run from the slime, and we know we can't, so the motivation never issues in behaviour. Prep me for the question period: the objections the psychologists will actually raise, in their vocabulary (I suspect they'll say their data shows action tendencies are present, not absent — what do I say to that?), what the aestheticians will say about whether I'm refuting Walton or just restating him, ordered by how much damage each could do, with a short reply to each and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Answer in British English.
