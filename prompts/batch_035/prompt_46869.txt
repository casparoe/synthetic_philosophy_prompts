I do volunteer work at a tiny local history museum in an old mill town, and we've got a dilemma about our main exhibit. The story everyone in town grew up with is that the mill workers went out on strike in 1907 and, through sheer solidarity, forced the owner to grant the eight-hour day -- it's basically the town's founding myth, there's a mural of it, the high school teams are named after it. But I've been going through the company's actual records and the local newspaper archive, and it's pretty clear the strike failed. The owner brought in replacement workers, the strikers went back on his terms, and the eight-hour day came two years later because the owner wanted to switch to a new shift system and figured shorter shifts suited the machinery better. So the treasured memory is just wrong about the central facts.

Some of the other volunteers say the exhibit should tell the story the way the town remembers it, because that version is what actually made the town what it is -- people organizing around that memory for decades is arguably more historically real than what happened in 1907. Others say we'd basically be lying on a wall label if we don't present the archival record front and center. Is there some principled way through this, or even just a good compromise for the exhibit? Which should give, the community's story or the paper trail, and why? I keep going back and forth so any help untangling it is appreciated.
