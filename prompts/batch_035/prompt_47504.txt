I'm sketching out a blog post on how to think about heuristic arguments — like when someone estimates the probability that evolution would produce intelligence again, or multiplies a bunch of rough numbers to get a risk estimate — and specifically on the "presumption of independence" between such arguments. Would love comments, especially on accuracy and whether the core idea is actually doing any work. Here's what I have so far (notes, not polished prose):

1. When we reason with heuristics, each argument only "sees" the variables it explicitly models. So two heuristic estimates are independent relative to a given model, but the world may couple them through unmodeled variables. Independence between heuristics isn't a fact about the world, it's a fact about a model, and it silently breaks whenever two heuristics share hidden inputs.

2. My central claim: the presumption of independence should work like a legal default, i.e. innocent until proven guilty. Two quantities are presumed independent unless some other heuristic argument connects them, and the burden of proof sits on whoever alleges dependence. This is normatively different from just assuming independence because you're lazy — the presumption can be defeated by evidence.

3. I want to argue this is exactly how cryptography works in practice. Nobody can prove a hash function's outputs are pseudorandom, so the field just presumes the outputs behave independently of everything else, and treats any discovered structure as a defeater that resets the whole thing. The history of hash functions is basically the history of independance presumptions getting defeated one by one.

4. Formalization idea: treat a heuristic argument as licensing a partial model of the world, and treat independence claims as defaults in something like default logic / circumscription — so the whole enterprise of "heuristic estimation" becomes nonmonotonic reasoning about numbers instead of propositions. Adding a new heuristic argument can retract a previously licensed independence assumption, which is why estimates shouldn't just be naively multiplied and forgotten.

5. A worry I want to address head-on: the presumption is not neutral. Presuming independence seems to systematically underestimate tail risk, because in the real world lots of quantities share big common causes (think: "how well humans coordinate" affecting basically every risk estimate at once). Maybe I can reply that this just means the presumption is defeasible in exactly one direction — but I'm not sure that doesn't collapse the whole default into "assume the worst."

Specific asks:
- Is the legal analogy in (2) earning its keep or is it decoration? A reader I trust said analogies to burdens of proof are usually just vibes. Where would the legal framing and my actual proposal come apart?
- Is (4) formalizable, or is "default logic for numeric estimates" just a fancy name for "revise when you learn stuff"? If you think it's formalizable, what's the minimal toy version you'd want to see worked out?
- Is my claim in (5) right that independence presumptions skew estimates optimistically? I have an intuition that common causes are the main way heuristics get correlated, and that common causes usually make bad outcomes clump together, but I can't tell if that's a real observation or me pattern-matching.
- The piece obviously lives next to the principle of indifference and its paradoxes (the wine/water thing, etc.). Do I need to engage with that stuff for the piece to be intellectually honest, or is it a separate enough topic that I can just gesture at it?

Also feel free to push back on the whole framing — is "presumption of independence" even the right thing to be talking about, vs. just saying "heuristics have error bars and you can't combine error bars naively"?
