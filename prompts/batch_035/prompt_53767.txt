I am a recently retired neurophysiologist — thirty-six years studying sensory transduction and peripheral coding, first in molluscan mechanoreceptors and later in mammalian systems — and since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of mind course at the local university, which has dragged me into animal ethics and its strange intersection with my old field. The question I cannot resolve, and would like an essay on, is whether the nociception-versus-pain distinction can do the regulatory work the aquaculture industry wants it to do. On one side sits the argument, associated with researchers such as James Rose, that fish and decapod crustaceans lack the cortical machinery required for the affective component of pain, so that their nociceptive reflexes warrant no more moral concern than a thermostat's response to heat. On the other sits the accumulating behavioral evidence — opioid receptor distribution, motivational trade-offs in shocked hermit crabs, the self-administration studies that persuaded the United Kingdom's 2022 sentience legislation to extend formal recognition to decapod crustaceans, despite their being exempt from most welfare-at-slaughter rules. Having spent my career watching "simple" sensory systems do things the textbook said they could not, I confess I find the cortical-requirement argument less decisive than my philosophical classmates do, but I also distrust how easily precaution slides into sentience inflation.

Here is the specific framework I want adjudicated. I have been toying with something I call the asymmetry wager, by analogy with familiar decision-theoretic structures: as phylogenetic and cognitive distance from humans increases, the cost of falsely attributing pain appears to fall (mainly economic — slower slaughter lines, capital costs of stunning equipment, some interference with research), while the cost of falsely denying it rises without any obvious ceiling. If the wager holds, it seems to license a graded precautionary policy that regulates practices in proportion to the animal's demonstrated behavioral and neurochemical evidence, rather than an all-or-nothing gate at "proven phenomenal consciousness" — a standard we cannot even meet for each other. But I can see the obvious objection: this asymmetry seems to prove too much, licensing protection for insects, plants, and any system with irritability, unless some principled cutoff intervenes. Please evaluate the wager, say what cutoff (if any) it can support, and then defend definite verdicts — no balanced survey that leaves the reader to choose — on four specific practices: eyestalk ablation of female broodstock prawns without anesthesia; sustained high stocking densities in salmon aquaculture; slaughter of farmed shrimp and prawns by asphyxiation or chilling in ice slurry without stunning; and the live storage and boiling of crabs and lobsters. For each, say whether it should be prohibited, phased out, permitted under mandatory welfare conditions, or left unregulated, and say why, taking the economics of farming seriously rather than dismissing it.
