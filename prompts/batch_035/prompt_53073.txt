I direct a political-philosophy colloquium that has spent this term on the socialist calculation debate, and the discussion has by now settled into a ritual: the Mises–Hayek side intones that without prices for producer goods there is no rational way to choose among alternative production plans; the Lange–Lerner side retorts that this was answered in the 1930s by trial-and-error pricing, with managers instructed to minimize average cost and to equate marginal cost with the prices the center announces; and both then repeat themselves louder. Rather than let another meeting end in the same place, I want to impose the discipline of an adversarial collaboration and have the two parties draft the joint statement they could both sign.

The parties: a committed Austrian in the Mises–Hayek line, for whom the 1920 calculation argument and the later knowledge argument form one indivisible case against planning; and a committed market socialist in the Lange–Lerner line, for whom the trial-and-error mechanism answers the calculation problem as posed and was never intended to answer the others. The parties are these modern defenders, not the historical figures themselves; the document should not attribute to Mises or Lange claims they did not make, nor saddle either side with positions it does not hold.

I want the following, in this order.

First, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These must be substantive rather than anodyne — not merely that planning is hard. For instance: can they agree on what the Walrasian formal equivalence does and does not establish (that a system of equations has a solution is one thing; that any actual process finds it is another)? Can they agree on what a shortage or surplus relative to an announced price is a reliable signal of, and of what it is silent? Can they agree that Hayek's complaint about knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place was not made in 1920 and that the debate's object therefore changed between Mises's essay and Hayek's 1945 article?

Second, a disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should be stated once, in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view; where the two sides would use different words for the same bone of contention, the table should say so rather than average them into mush.

Third, the cruxes. My hypothesis, which the parties should confirm, revise, or reject, is that there are three: (a) whether the epistemic problem — knowledge that is tacit, dispersed, and not surveyable by any official — is separable from the computational problem of solving a large system once its data are given; (b) whether the motivational problem — incentives to reveal and act on dispersed knowledge — can be replicated without residual claimants, or whether Lange's managers will game the center however sincere the rules; and (c) whether the Austrian case, if sound, proves too much, indicting the modern multi-divisional firm and its internal transfer prices as severely as it indicts the ministry. For each crux, the parties should test it for verbalness — in particular, whether calculation means the same thing in Mises's mouth (choosing among production plans under scarcity) as in Lange's (converging on a solution of a system) — and state which parts of the dispute, if any, dissolve once the words are disambiguated.

Fourth, for each genuine crux, one case, argument, or thought experiment that both parties accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For this, use the scenario I will pose in seminar: a Ministry of Allocation equipped with modern computing, exhaustively fine-grained real-time data on quantities produced and consumed, and the authority to revise announced prices at any frequency — but no market in producer goods and no residual claimants anywhere in the economy. The parties must say what, specifically, this arrangement would fail at, and on what timescale: divergence from the least-cost pattern, failure to discover new ends or new methods, misallocation of the surpluses that price movements would have created, or something else. The two predictions must be distinct enough that an outcome could favor one side over the other; if they cannot be made so, that is itself a finding the document should record.

Finally, a closing section in which the parties state whether anything in the residual dispute would actually move either of them, or whether the collaboration has merely relocated the disagreement — from prices to tacit knowledge to motivation — without resolving it; and, if the latter, where they judge the discussion should be taken up next.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes each with the agreed test and the two recorded predictions, then the closing section. The register should be that of a document two academics would put their names to: exact, unsentimental, and free of the rhetorical capital each side is accustomed to spending.
