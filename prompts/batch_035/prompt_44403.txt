I've come to hold the Peircean convergence view of truth: roughly, that a belief is true if and only if it is the opinion that ideal inquiry would ultimately settle on — the belief that sufficiently prolonged, self-correcting investigation, carried out by a community of inquirers unimpeded and genuinely devoted to getting things right, would converge upon. I hold this specifically as a substantive, analysis-style account of truth, not merely as a methodological maxim about how to go on in the absence of certainty, and I hold it in deliberate opposition to the more Jamesian pragmatist line on which truth is keyed to what is satisfying, useful, or vindicated in the course of experience — a line I take to collapse the difference between being true and being verified, warranted, or good to believe. My attachment is partly temperamental: I like that the Peircean view keeps truth independent of what anyone now finds it convenient to think, while still tying its nature to inquiry rather than to some ghostly correspondence relation that seems to make truth unknowable in principle.

Lately, though, I've been pressed from several directions at once, and I'd like a map of my exposure rather than another round of advocacy. What would genuinely count against my view — not merely annoy it? Some worries I can already see the shape of: that inquiry might never converge even in the ideal limit (set theorists who take independence results as showing that mathematics itself tolerates permanently plural standards look to me like a live threat); that the view seems to presuppose truth in explaining convergence, since what the community fatedly agrees upon is presumably agreement that things are *so*; that deflationists will say my entire inquiry is a fuss over an insubstantial device; and that Peirce's own qualifications — the long run, the ideal limit, the indefinite community — may be doing all the work while risking nothing. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed Peircean would concede as real evidence against the view and which they would treat as a misunderstanding of it, with the reasoning for the sorting rather than a mere list.

Two further things. First, be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its idealizations, and — if it has — whether that is disqualifying or just what one should expect of a regulative, limit-concept of this kind; I'm open to the answer that the view was never meant to be an empirical hypothesis, but then I want it said plainly what content remains. Second, I'd personally be most moved by an argument that we could in principle have two mature, incommensurable but equally rational research traditions that both survive indefinitely — so if you think the actual history of science or mathematics provides any real pressure there, treat that as the centerpiece.
