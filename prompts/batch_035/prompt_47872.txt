I have a stack of verdicts about when a society "collapses" versus when it merely changes beyond recognition, and I would like to know whether any coherent principle stands behind them, or whether I should start overwriting my own judgments. Around 1200 BCE the palace economies of the Mycenaean world and the wider eastern Mediterranean disintegrated — the palaces burned or were abandoned, and writing disappeared from Greece for centuries — and I call that a collapse without hesitation, even though the villages and the people largely carried on. The Terminal Classic Maya, by contrast: the southern lowland dynastic cities emptied and monument-raising stopped, yet I call that a transformation, not a collapse — millions of Maya continued, other cities in the north carried the tradition forward, and something recognizably Maya persisted through the rupture. Cahokia: dispersed by around the fourteenth century, its population absorbed into other Mississippian communities with, so far as anyone can tell, no surviving memory of the city or its identity — that one I do call a collapse, even though here too the people went on living somewhere. Easter Island: the version I learned first was textbook ecological suicide; the version I now find more defensible is a population that got by on a degraded island for centuries and was then devastated by contact-borne disease and slave raids — and I've come to think "collapse" is the wrong frame for the islanders themselves, while still wanting to say that something collapsed there, namely the moai-raising ritual order. The western Roman Empire: tax revenues and command structures fragment, successor kingdoms pay lip service to Roman identity while doing their own thing, and I cannot make myself call that a full collapse — more a reorganization at a lower level of complexity. The Greenland Norse settlements: the whole thing simply ends sometime in the fifteenth century, and it feels like a collapse of yet another kind, closer to a community ceasing to exist than to a system failing. Two candidate principles I've been unable to commit to: the anthropological one that collapse is a society rationally shedding expensive complexity when the marginal returns on institutions go negative, which I find attractive but which does not predict several of my calls; and the uglier suspicion that my notion is mostly about monumentality — that I reach for "collapse" whenever the big durable things stop being built and inscribed, pretty much regardless of what happened to the people. Is there a single principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough to decide new cases? If not, which of my calls would you overturn, and why those? What does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — the winding down of the Indus cities, where the urban order dissolved, the script passed into permanent unreadability, and the population dispersed into villages whose descendants do not obviously preserve the memory of a rupture? Finally, if your best principle ends up rendering "collapse" as something like "change that looks bad from the outside" or "change that destroyed the record-keepers," I want to hear that conclusion stated plainly, along with what work, if any, you think the concept is still doing for archaeologists and for the rest of us.
