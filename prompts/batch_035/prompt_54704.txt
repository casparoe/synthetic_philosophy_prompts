As I understand it, an EEG signal called the readiness potential begins well before subjects report consciously deciding to move, and yet subjects apparently retain a last-moment ability to veto the movement. My friend takes the early signal to mean the brain decided before "I" did. Here is my half-formed thought, so you can work with it or against it. To a controls engineer, a ramping signal that precedes an action doesn't look like a "decision" at all — it looks like an integrator winding up toward a threshold, the sort of thing a well-designed system uses precisely so that noisy, low-confidence evidence doesn't trigger premature actuation. On that picture, asking whether the ramp "caused" the decision rather than the subject seems like a category error: the ramp is the mechanism by which the system defers commitment until evidence accumulates. Similarly, the "veto" looks like ordinary override logic — a supervisory controller that can inhibit a lower-level routine late in its execution — and the reported time of conscious intention looks like an unreliable timestamp from a subsystem that isn't the one keeping time. And the apparent gap between neural onset and reported intention looks like it could be pure measurement latency: asking a subject to watch a clock and report when they felt a movement is latency estimation over a noisy channel, which I'd never trust to a few hundred milliseconds.
