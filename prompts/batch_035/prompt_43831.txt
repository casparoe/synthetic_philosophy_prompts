My brother-in-law is a classicist who runs a standing game at family dinners: someone names a legend, and he rules on whether it's history. His record so far: the Trojan War, yes — he takes it to have been a real Late Bronze Age conflict over Wilusa involving the Ahhiyawa, and he doesn't care that the ten-year siege and most of the Iliad's plot are almost certainly invented; enough of the core checks out against Hittite documents and a destruction level at Hisarlik. Gilgamesh, yes — a real early king of Uruk, on the strength of the Sumerian King List, though he thinks essentially nothing else in the epic is history. King Arthur, no — nothing contemporary, and the figure grows with each retelling. The Exodus as narrated, no — no Egyptian record, no trace in the Sinai, and he bristles at the move of treating the expulsion of the Hyksos as the kernel, on the ground that then the tradition isn't remembering that event at all but reinventing something with a shared theme. Robin Hood, no. The fall of Jericho, no — the walls came down centuries too early for any plausible conquest.

Next month he's visiting, and three cases are queued up, none of which he's ruled on. First, the Amazons: Herodotus's nation of warrior women, now that excavations of Scythian burial mounds keep turning up women buried with weapons, some with healed battle wounds. Second, the Trojan Horse itself, as a question distinct from the war he already affirmed — is the horse history, story, or a malformed question? Third, Ragnar Lothbrok: the saga hero and father of more-or-less historical sons, plausibly anchored on a Viking leader whom the Frankish annals record being captured and hanged after the 845 siege of Paris.

I'd like a prediction for each case, but I'm even more interested in the rule he seems to be running, since I can see at least two that fit his record equally well — one under which a corroborated kernel suffices however much the tradition embellishes it, and one under which a tradition is only history when its load-bearing claims survive transmission. Please tell me what rule (or rules) best explains his verdicts, what each candidate rule predicts for each of the three new cases, where the rules agree and where they split, which of the three cases would best tell them apart, and whether there's a case nobody has asked him yet that would discriminate between the rules even more cleanly than any of mine.
