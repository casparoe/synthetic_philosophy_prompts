So I've landed on a view I'd call graded corticalism: an organism's moral status depends, roughly, on how much brain there is above and beyond what's needed to run the body, and it comes in degrees. More of the relevant integrating/planning machinery, more moral weight. It's not "only humans count," it's that moral weight scales with the size and complexity of the right kind of brain stuff. The view appeals to me mostly because it gives a principled middle ground between "only humans count" and "a fly counts as much as I do," and it explains why I wince more at a pig's death than a minnow's.

Tell me what would count against it. Not just the standard objections, though include those; I want a ranked map of the exposure. A few things I keep chewing on:

First, the metric problem. Absolute brain size, encephalization quotient, and cortical neuron counts rank animals differently — an elephant beats me on the first, loses badly on the second, and I honestly don't know about the third. Which statistic I pick changes the view's verdicts about actual cases. Is that a defect in the view, or just ordinary scientific underdetermination that will sort itself out as the neuroscience gets better?

Second, the awkward facts. Average male brains run somewhat larger than average female brains, and the view, if stated in terms of raw size, seems committed to saying men matter more — which everyone, including me, thinks is wrong. Is that a refutation, or just a case where the right response is "that's the wrong statistic, it's the integrating tissue that counts, not the whole skull-full"? And relatedly, does the fact that the view has to keep swapping its statistic (size → EQ → neurons → whatever's next) suggest it's a proxy chasing a real variable it hasn't found? If something like nociceptor density plus integration turned out to do the explanatory work, would that count against the view or merely refine it?

Third, the marginal cases. Infants, people with severe microcephaly, patients in persistent vegetative states — the view seems to want to say their moral status is reduced, and I want to know whether a defender can bite that bullet coherently or whether biting it detonates the whole thing. Related: are there actual empirical findings that would move this (say, if cortical neuron counts turned out to be lousy predictors of the capacities I actually care about — pain, planning, social bonding)?

So: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine trouble and which they'd wave off, and give me an honest verdict on whether the view is falsifiable in any useful sense — or whether every counterexample just triggers another round of "well, the relevant quantity isn't quite that quantity," and whether that's a fatal feature for a view of this kind or just what happens in any area where ethics waits on immature science.
