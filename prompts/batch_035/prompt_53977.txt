I teach the grad epistemology seminar, and every year around week nine we hit the knowability paradox (Fitch's 1963 paper, though I've since learned the proof was first sent to Fitch in an anonymous referee report back in 1945, and the referee turned out to be Alonzo Church, which is its own little lesson about the invisibility of refereeing), and every year I paper over what I actually think about it. So this year I'm writing my position down before I present, and I want someone to poke at it. Here's the package. I'm broadly Dummettian about meaning, at least for empirical discourse: to grasp a claim is to grasp what would warrant asserting it, and I think that makes the knowability principle — every truth is knowable by someone or other, at some time, in principle — pretty much constitutive of truth in that fragment, not some extra thesis bolted on afterwards. I'm a realist about mathematics: Fermat's Last Theorem was true before Wiles, and Goldbach is true or false right now regardless of whether anyone ever settles it. I'm also a realist about the unobserved — there are surely truths about patches of spacetime whose light will never reach any detector, let alone anyone who could read one. I hold that no possible knower is omniscient; knowing all the truths is not an epistemic state anything gets into. And I flatout refuse to deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction — if you know p-and-q, you know p — because treating that as negotiable just to keep a pretty thesis feels like cheating to me. Given all that, my official diagnosis of the paradox has always been that it's an aggregation artifact: it only works if you let "all the truths" behave like one giant proposition, whereas knowability attaches to claims one at a time — knowing each truth separately never adds up to knowing the monster conjunction with an "and this one isn't known" rider glued onto it.

But there are two things I keep circling and can't get straight. One: I've told students, and I half believe it myself, that the derivation is airtight and its conclusion — that some truths are unknowable — is something we can know a priori from premises everyone can accept. Two: a colleague in our logic group swears that my Dummett line for the empirical combined with platonism about arithmetic is incoherent, that either the assertibility-conditions story applies across the board or it's just a mood. So audit me. Which of these commitments are actually inconsistent with each other, which only look like they're, and what's the cheapest way to restore consistency, and what does each fix cost? Rank the problems by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad — I want to know what the real trouble is, not a scattershot. And specifically: does my aggregation story actually block anything given everything else I've said, or does the derivation never need "the totality of truths" in the first place — and if so, which of my other commitments is doing me in, which one would you tell me to give up, and why that one rather than the others?
