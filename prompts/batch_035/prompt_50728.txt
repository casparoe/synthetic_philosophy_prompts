I write for a magazine covering science and ideas, mostly longish features, and I've been kicking around a piece on how much of our moral thinking we actually outsource. The philosophers have a name for this — the problem of moral testimony, whether you can get moral knowledge the way you get the weather report — but the more I interview people the more I notice it's not one question, it's a bunch of cases that don't obviously hang together. So I want to run my gut verdicts past someone who thinks about this stuff for a living, and see if there's a principle underneath or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Assume you're a professional philosopher who works on testimony and trust, and think it through carefully rather than pattern-matching to the literature — I'd rather have your honest reconstruction than a book report.

Here's my sheet of verdicts. A friend whose judgment I really trust tells me our mutual friend is being quietly mistreated by her boss and that I should say something; I check in with her on the strength of that alone — feels totally fine. A juror who knows he's bad at reading people defers to another juror's read on whether a witness was lying — fine, even admirable self-knowledge. Someone decides to stop eating meat purely because a philosopher she admires said it's wrong, without ever working through the argument herself — this one bothers me, it feels like second-hand conviction, like she's wearing someone else's conscience. A woman asks her rabbi whether she should forgive her estranged brother, and forgives him basically on his word — this also feels hollow in a way the boss case doesn't, though I can't say why, since the rabbi presumably knows the brother and she doesn't. And then: a reader tells me he asks ChatGPT whether ghosting a friend was wrong, it says yes, he apologizes — and honestly I can't place whether that's the meat case or the rabbi case or neither.

So: is there one principle that saves all five verdicts, or close to it? The obvious move is something like "testimony is fine for facts about the situation but not for the moral verdict itself," but I don't think that cleanly works, since my rabbi forgiver seems to be taking a verdict and my meat-eater seems like she could've at least taken the facts. If you can't make one principle fit, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why, and please stress-test whatever you come up with against the ghosting case, since that's the one I keep flip-flopping on — plus give me your best guess on one further case I haven't listed, say, a politician who votes her conscience on bills she's read but defers to her party leader on bills she hasn't. Also flag where my verdicts are probably being distorted by the fact that I like some of these people more than others. No headings or bullet points or anything, just continuous prose like an email — I'm going to be reading this on my phone between interviews.
