The question, worth 25 points, read: "A lump of clay has sat on a sculptor's shelf since Monday. At noon on Tuesday he shapes it into a statue; call the lump Lumpl and the statue Goliath. At noon on Thursday he squashes it flat, and the lump of clay survives, though the statue does not. (i) Present the argument that Lumpl and Goliath are two objects rather than one. (ii) Explain why the conclusion of that argument is generally regarded as a problem rather than a straightforward discovery. (iii) Discuss one proposed solution and say whether you find it adequate."

Here is the student's answer, transcribed in full:

"Leibniz's Law says that if x is identical to y, then x and y share all their properties. Lumpl and Goliath sit in the same place and are made of the very same clay, but they differ in one crucial respect. Goliath could not survive being squashed — a statue that is squashed flat ceases to exist — whereas Lumpl could easily survive being squashed, since a lump of clay can be reshaped without being destroyed. So sensitivity to squashing is an essential property of Goliath and only an accidental property of Lumpl, and by Leibniz's Law the two cannot be identical.

The conclusion is a problem because of what happens between Tuesday noon and Thursday noon. During that window Lumpl and Goliath occupy exactly the same region of space and share every non-modal property: same mass, same shape, same color, same microstructure. But by Leibniz's Law, two objects that share all their properties must be one object. So the conclusion that there are two objects here seems to conflict with the very principle that was used to establish it. Intuitively, it is also hard to accept that two distinct material objects can be in exactly the same place at the same time.

The best-known solution is the four-dimensionalist one associated with David Lewis. On this view objects persist by having temporal parts, and Lumpl and Goliath can be distinct because they are sums of different temporal parts: both careers begin at noon on Tuesday, when the sculptor first imposes a statue-shape on the clay, and only Lumpl's continues past Thursday noon, so their temporal extents differ. This means the two objects share all their spatial parts throughout while remaining numerically distinct, and Lewis stresses that even if two objects shared every part, spatial and temporal alike, they could still be distinct provided they had different persistence conditions. The four-dimensionalist can therefore explain the modal asymmetry by pointing to the two different histories, and the puzzle is dissolved cleanly."
