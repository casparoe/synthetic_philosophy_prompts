Draft the joint statement that could emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have been arguing, in one form or another, for nearly a century: market socialists in the tradition of Oskar Lange's 1930s proposals, on which a planning board owns the capital goods, assigns them accounting prices, and revises those prices by trial and error in response to observed shortages and surpluses, while consumer goods and labor are traded in ordinary markets; and calculation critics in the tradition of Mises and Hayek, who hold that without private ownership of the means of production there can be no genuine exchange of producer goods, and hence no prices for them that can perform the comparison-and-sorting work that prices perform under capitalism. The two camps should be treated as equally competent: the market socialist is not a person who thinks a bureaucracy can just look up correct prices in a table, and the critic is not a person who thinks markets never misprice anything.

The document you produce should have the following structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: things both sides can sign. These should include claims about what each side's argument actually asserts (for example, that the critics' case comes in two separable parts — a Mises-style claim about the missing exchange that would give producer-goods prices their meaning, and a Hayek-style claim about eliciting dispersed, tacit, locally held knowledge — and that these can come apart); claims about what each side would accept as a counterexample to its own view; and claims about the historical record that neither side needs to dispute (for instance, that real planned economies solved some allocation problems while failing conspicuously at others, in ways both camps interpret differently).

Second, a disagreements table: the precise points of residual disagreement, each stated twice — once in wording the market socialist would accept as a fair statement of that side's position, and once in wording the critic would accept as a fair statement of the other side's — with the two formulations placed side by side so it is visible exactly where the wording diverges.

Third, the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect at least these three are live, and you should include them unless you find better ones:

1. The transition problem. Lange's procedure is usually described as converging toward equilibrium, but little is said about the path. When the board revises a price in response to observed excess demand, the revision itself changes the excess demands the board subsequently observes; during any stretch of adjustment, the board faces a signal-extraction problem — distinguishing a price that is currently wrong from a price that is on its way to a new equilibrium because earlier revisions are still working through the system. The question is whether this self-observation problem is a technical nuisance that a competent board with sensible rules can handle, or whether it compounds in a way that makes the procedure unstable except near equilibrium. Both sides should agree on a concrete episode that bears on this: a lasting supply shock to a widely used input (imagine a persistent crop failure raising the cost of an input that feeds into many industries). The market socialist predicts the board can pass the cost increases through under its revision rules within a few quarters and that the resulting pattern of prices and quantities will closely track what market economies did in comparable historical episodes; the critic predicts persistent mispricing in goods far downstream from the shocked input, with shortages and queuing concentrated in exactly those goods. Write both predictions down.

2. Whether administered prices for consumer goods can substitute for market prices there too. Lange's scheme left consumer goods to the market; later socialists have argued that consumer prices could also be administered and revised by rule, especially now that the original debate's computational limits no longer bind. The crux is whether the revision rules can do the discovery work that unsupervised retail experimentation does — particularly for a new product category with no trading history, where demand forms only as consumers gain experience with the good. Both sides should agree that the introduction of such a good is a fair test, and should record their predictions about how quickly and in what pattern its price and availability would settle, compared with the market baseline.

3. Whether part of the dispute over the word "price" is verbal. The critic may want to reserve the term for the terms of actual voluntary exchange between owners, while the market socialist counts any parametric number that the board sets and revises in response to excess demand as a price in every economically relevant sense. State, as precisely as you can, which portion of the disagreement survives once both sides stipulate their meanings, and which portion dissolves.

Fourth, a short closing section stating what, if anything, both sides agree would actually move the debate — an argument, a class of cases, or a kind of evidence — and naming any remaining disagreement that both sides acknowledge neither of them currently knows how to test.

Please write the entire statement in American English, and keep it formal: no dialogue, no dramatization, and no verdict on which side is right. The measure of success is that a committed market socialist and a committed calculation critic could each put their name to the agreed-claims section, recognize their own views fairly stated in the disagreements table, and accept the recorded predictions as ones they would actually have made in advance.
