So I've been chewing on Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" thing for like a decade now, and I've come to a conclusion that might be annoying: the standard moves in this debate are mostly exhausted. Wigner says it's a miracle, Hamming says it's partly selection and partly that math gets invented in response to the sciences, the anthropic people say we only notice the parts that work, the Tegmark-types say it's because the universe IS math. Fine. But the debate has been running in place, and meanwhile there are a bunch of questions that seem genuinely open and not just retellings of the same three essays.

Here's my situation: I've got a year of roughly 6 hrs a week (blog posts, maybe eventually a long-form essay series) and I want to spend it on questions where progress is actually possible, not on writing "yet another take on Wigner." Act as my consultant here: a philosopher of math who also reads ML papers for fun, has no patience for mysticism, and pushes back when a question is secretly not a question. Then give me your top 3-4 with a defended ordering. Some candidates off the top of my head so you know the flavor I want, but argue with these too, maybe some are overrated:

1. Why do extremely overparameterized statistical models generalize at all? Deep nets with way more parameters than data points still getting low test error looks like a live, empirical instance of the universe handing us more regularity than the counting arguments say we're entitled to. Does the double descent / benign overfitting literature actually import anything into the Wigner debate, or is it just a loose analogy people like me get excited about?

2. Selection effects, but done properly. Everyone hand-waves "we only notice the math that works." Has anyone actually tried to quantify that? Like, what fraction of math frameworks even COULD have described some physical domain in a usable way? If the space of viable formalisms is small, maybe the effectiveness isn't unreasonable after all. Is this tractable, or too vague to be a real question -- and if tractable, tractable by whom, a blogger with no lab or a computational person?

3. The declining-effectiveness question. My hot take: as physics moved from nice closed-form orbits to effective field theories and 500-page papers full of numerics, math went from unreasonably effective to laboriously effective. If that trend is real, does it bear differently on the different standard explanations -- like, does it hurt the "universe is math" story more than the selection-effect story? I've literally never seen anyone weigh the explanations against evidence rather than vibes.

4. Whether "unreasonable" is a well-defined predicate at all -- whether the framing smuggles in a prior over what math should be able to do. Feels like it might be upstream of everything, but also might be a deflationary cop-out that dissolves the debate without explaining anything. I genuinely can't tell wether this one is the most important or the least.
