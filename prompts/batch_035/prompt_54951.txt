I've just joined the clinical risk review board of a mid-sized regional hospital, and I've been asked to draft our recommendation on a case coming before us next month. Before drafting it, I want to be sure we stay consistent with the board's own precedents, and I'm struggling to see what those precedents commit us to. Our track record, as I understand it:

1. A ward nurse silenced a correctly firing, repeatedly alerting monitor because, based on the previous shift's pattern, she judged it noise; the patient then deteriorated unnoticed. The board placed the blame squarely on the nurse, noting that the system had worked exactly as designed.

2. An automated dispensing cabinet misfilled a dose because a vendor update introduced a software bug; the vendor's testing had followed industry-standard practice, and no clinician stood anywhere in the loop. The board declined to blame any individual, the hospital accepted institutional responsibility and paid compensation, and the file records that no personal fault was assigned to anyone.

3. A physician confirmed, on the confirmation screen, a system-generated dose recommendation that was wrong in a glaring way: the patient's documented allergy to the drug appeared on that same screen. The board held the physician responsible for most of the fault, with a lesser share assigned to the vendor.

4. A physician confirmed a wrong recommendation whose flaw turned on a subtle interaction between two lab values; a retrospective expert panel needed several days to identify it, and the physician had done everything the written procedure required of her. The board declined to blame the physician and, finding no one at the bedside at fault, recorded the incident as a system failure with no personal fault assigned.

The new case: our clinical decision-support system now operates with a physician nominally in the loop. For each flagged patient, the system issues a recommendation, and the duty physician must confirm or override it within ninety seconds, a window set by the vendor. On the night in question, one physician covered forty beds. She confirmed a wrong recommendation that a focused reviewer, given two or three uninterrupted minutes, could plausibly have caught, though no one plausibly could under the conditions the hospital actually ran: administration had set the staffing, and it had acknowledged a memo, co-signed by this same physician six months earlier, warning that the ninety-second window at current staffing made careful review impossible. The physician had overridden the system correctly twice earlier that night.

Three rulings seem open to us: blame the physician, assign fault upward to administration and the vendor, or file it as a faultless system failure in the manner of case 2. Please tell me which ruling our precedents most support, identify the closest analog among the four earlier cases, and — since I suspect our track record genuinely underdetermines this one — state precisely what principle we would be adopting, going forward, with each of the three rulings. Finally, describe the further incident that would most sharply expose the difference between whichever principles are really in play here, so the board knows what it is committing itself to before we sign anything.
