I do expected-value evaluations of charitable interventions, and I keep running into a configuration I can't find a name for. When an intervention has a tiny probability of a vast payoff, the sum of probability-times-payoff over all outcomes sometimes fails to converge; but in the cases that worry me it is worse than mere divergence — the total's sign depends on how you group the terms. If I tally all the gains first, the intervention looks clearly good; if I tally the losses first, clearly bad; and it seems that by reordering the tally I could make the average come out as any number I like. A colleague showed me a toy version with a coin-flip game where the payoff grows while its probability shrinks in such a way that the average comes out as something like one minus a half plus a third minus a quarter, and so on: taken in the natural order that sums to a definite number, but reshuffle the terms and you can get any total you want. I know about the St. Petersburg paradox, where the expectation is infinite, but that isn't quite this — there the verdict is at least stable ('worth more than any finite price'), whereas here the expected value seems to be no particular number at all. My colleagues brush this off with moves like capping the payoff at some multiple of the best we've seen, estimating from a finite sample of outcomes, or declaring that the order of tallying shouldn't matter for a rational agent, and I want to know whether any of those moves is principled rather than ad hoc. Does this situation have an established name? How exactly does it differ from St. Petersburg and from ordinary non-convergence, and is there a settled answer to what a decision-maker should do when the verdict depends on the grouping? Please distinguish what is actually agreed among decision theorists from what remains contested, and don't present any one proposed resolution as the consensus view.
