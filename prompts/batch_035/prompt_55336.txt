So this is a slightly odd request but here goes. I run facilities for a small college — one 150-year-old building, plus a couple of 1970s additions — and part of my job is figuring out why weird stuff happens. A stain spreads across a ceiling tile. Students complain of a smell in one stairwell on tuesdays. The lights on the third floor dim whenever it rains. My technicians all have theories, they argue, someone climbs into the ceiling, half the time we fix something that wasn't the problem. What I want is a diagnostic procedure rooted in inference to the best explanation — the idea that you go with the hypothesis that would best explain the observations, not just the first plausible one somebody shouts.

Concretely: a checklist my crew can run in order when a new symptom report comes in. Questions like what observations would distinguish candidate causes, whether we've compared explanations on all the evidence or just the evidence that suggested them (I've noticed we tend to fall in love with whichever cause we thought of first). I half remember some philosophy paper distinguishing explanations that are lovely from ones that are merely likely — if that distinction is real and usable, fold it in, but don't hang the whole thing on it.

It has to work under real constraints: a first pass in 15 minutes before anyone grabs a ladder, it should say when the answer is "observe for another week" instead of guessing, and it needs a rule for what to do when two causes explain everything equally well. Also tell me honestly which steps are still just judgment wearing a checklist costume — I'd rather know than pretend.

Then run it on three actual cases we've had: the tuesday stairwell smell, a cold classroom that only some students complain about, and a door closer on the east side that keeps failing. One of these, I suspect, the procedure will send us toward the boring explanation my senior tech keeps dismissing — include that one, I want to see if the procedure has teeth.
