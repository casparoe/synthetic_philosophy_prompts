I coordinate a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of probability, and after two terms of increasingly unproductive argument we have decided to try something more structured: a genuine adversarial collaboration between our two most committed factions. On one side sit the Humeans, who hold that objective chances are nothing over and above what earns a place in the best systematisation of the mosaic of particular facts — chance is a theoretical term fixed by simplicity, strength, and fit. On the other sit the propensity realists, who hold that chance is a genuine dynamical feature of the world, a disposition or power inherent in chance setups themselves, which would exist and operate exactly as it does even if no systematising description picked it out. Both sides accept the Principal Principle in some form — roughly, that a rational agent's credence in an event, conditional on the proposition that its objective chance is p and on suitable further admissible information, should itself be p — and both accept that this principle is the central datum any account of chance must respect. But they disagree about everything downstream: whether the Principle is an analytic constraint that partly *defines* chance or a substantive empirical regularity that could have failed, whether the Humean can vindicate the Principle without smuggling in the very dynamical structure she denies, whether the propensity theorist can explain why single-case propensities should constrain rational credence at all, and how each side handles the undermining problem — the awkward fact that information about the total distribution of events seems to bear on which chance theory is true, so that learning about outcomes can partially undermine the very chance assignments one is deferring to.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration conducted over the course of a year. The document should contain: (i) a numbered section of claims both parties accept — about the Principal Principle, about the role of fit between frequencies and chances, about what the mainstream interpretations of probability agree on regarding repeated trials, and about the failures of simple frequentism as a reductive analysis; (ii) a table of the precise remaining disagreements, with each formulation phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view — no slanted phrasings like "chance is merely a bookkeeping device" unless the Humean herself would sign it, and no "chances are occult powers" unless the propensity theorist would; (iii) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party — which I suspect include at least whether the Principal Principle is trivially true by definition or a non-trivial constraint, and whether admissible information can be characterised without circular appeal to chance itself; and (iv) for each crux, the argument, thought experiment, or class of cases that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance so that the dispute is in principle resolvable. Finally, if you conclude that some portion of their disagreement is merely verbal — for instance, a dispute about whether the word "chance" denotes a property that is genuinely modal in the propensity sense or merely useful in the way any theoretical term is useful, which dissolves once the term is disambiguated — say which portion and why, and separate it clearly from what remains substantive. Keep the tone of an official collaboration report: measured, precise, and jointly authored.
