I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a mid-sized daily, and I'm preparing a long-form column on the World Happiness Report and the annual ritual that surrounds it. I've drafted the argument below, and before I submit it to my editor I would like serious critical feedback on its accuracy and its reasoning. I am less worried about style than about whether I have the measurement science roughly right and whether my central argument actually holds together.

The draft, which currently carries the working title "The Race to Be Happiest," runs as follows. Every year the report crowns Finland, and every year the same ritual follows: government press releases, opposition jeering, and a wave of explainers about saunas and trust. But the survey at the heart of the ranking does not ask anyone how happy they feel. It asks respondents to imagine a ladder with steps numbered from zero at the bottom to ten at the top, with the top representing the best possible life for them, and to say where they stand. That is a life evaluation, a retrospective judgment about how one's life is going, not a report of mood — and this distinction does a lot of work in what follows. My first claim is that this kind of judgment is partly indexed to a comparison class: when a respondent places themselves on a rung, part of what they are doing is positioning themselves against the life they take to be typical or attainable, and that reference society varies in ways the ranking can't see. Second, I invoke the Easterlin paradox: I currently write that once a country passes a modest income threshold, further growth buys essentially nothing in self-reported wellbeing, so chasing GDP in hopes of climbing the ladder is a fool's errand. Third, and this is the column's centerpiece, I argue that a ranking converts a non-zero-sum quantity into a zero-sum contest: there is only one first place, so once governments care about position rather than level, we should expect Goodhart-style corrosion — ministries timing feel-good campaigns to survey season, public messaging encouraging citizens to count their blessings when a pollster calls, and eventually a measure that no longer tracks the thing it was meant to track. I illustrate this with a hypothetical: a small country whose tourism and wellbeing ministries jointly sponsor a national gratitude week in the month the fieldwork runs. I close by pairing this with a positional worry: if life evaluation is partly comparative, then one country's felt gain is partly another's felt loss, so the league table manufactures a rivalry in a domain where the underlying quantity is not actually rivalrous. My conclusion is that governments should keep using the underlying microdata to inform policy but should flatly refuse to compete on the league table itself.

I'd like your comments along three lines. First, accuracy: I have written that the ladder is a life-evaluation measure rather than an emotion report, and I'd want to know whether my characterization is defensible or oversimplified; I have stated the Easterlin paradox as if it were settled, and I suspect it isn't — I'd want to know what the live disputes are and how badly my formulation misleads a general reader; and I have asserted that comparison standards explain a substantial share of cross-country differences, which I should confess is a stronger claim than I can currently support. Second, the argument: the Goodhart concern in the piece currently rests on a hypothetical scheme no government has (to my knowledge) actually attempted, so I'd want to know whether it reads as a sober extrapolation of ordinary league-table politics or as paranoid fiction, and how I might make it more credible without softening it into triviality. Third, the positional claim, which I regard as the most original and the most fragile part of the column: can life evaluation really be comparative in a way that makes one country's improvement another country's loss, and if it can, does that cohere with the fact that all countries' average scores have generally drifted upward over the report's lifetime rather than summing to a constant? That last observation is one my editor raised, and I have not yet found a satisfactory answer to it in the draft. If the positional argument fails, I would want to know whether the column survives on the Goodhart and measurement points alone, or whether I should restructure. Please also flag any place where I overreach in ways a specialist in subjective wellbeing research would find dishonest, even if a general reader would never notice.
