I've been trying to work out where I stand on adaptationism by taking verdicts on whether particular traits are adaptations or byproducts, in the Gould–Lewontin sense, and I'd like to know whether my verdicts add up to a principle or just to my taste in stories. Fever: adaptation, costly as it is. The bright red color of oxygenated blood: byproduct — heme had to be some color, and nothing selects for the color itself. Blushing, though, I count as an adaptation, even though it is the same redness made visible in the face: the blood vessels of the cheeks had to be made to dilate on cue, and the signal would not otherwise exist. Male nipples: byproduct. The human chin: byproduct, a side effect of how the jaw grows in our lineage. The white sclera: adaptation — we are nearly the only primates to have it, and it looks built for gaze-following. Yet emotional tears I count as a byproduct, even though they seem as social, as species-specific, and as much in need of explanation as blushing. And the sweet tooth I keep going back and forth on: it was surely favored when sugar was scarce, so is it an adaptation that misfires in abundance, or does that fact not settle which category it belongs to now? Is there one rule that delivers all of these — something like: adaptation when the trait would not exist without selection for it, byproduct when it comes along for free with something else that was selected? If so, does it force me to give up the verdict on tears, on blushing, or on the sclera? If no single rule fits, tell me which verdict to overturn and why, and state the rule that survives, including what it predicts for a case I have not listed: contagious yawning, say. Finally, tell me whether the rule can be applied without knowing the actual evolutionary history, or whether it merely renames the complaint that byproduct explanations and adaptationist explanations are both always available.
