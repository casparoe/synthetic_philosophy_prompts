My closest friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about what the replication crisis actually shows, and we closed the argument with a deal: instead of another round of the same fight, we would try to write, together, the document a well-run adversarial collaboration between our two positions would produce. She is a committed frequentist — she thinks the crisis vindicates error control as a regulative ideal, and that its causes were quantifiable procedural failures: underpowered designs, optional stopping, forking-paths analysis, threshold-gaming. I am a committed Bayesian — I think the crisis is mostly about priors, that a low base rate of true hypotheses combined with weak evidence was always going to produce a literature dominated by noise, and that significance testing was at most an accelerant. Draft the joint statement we should both be able to sign.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims we both accept — about the scale and reality of the crisis, about which reforms are good regardless of framework (preregistration, larger samples, direct replication, publishing negative results), and about what the crisis does *not* show. Each side's suspicion that the crisis refutes the other's framework must not appear here; if neither of us can honestly sign a clause, leave it out.

2. A disagreements section, formatted as a table, in which each disagreement is stated twice — once in wording she would sign, once in wording I would sign — where it is clear that the two wordings are the same dispute seen from two sides, not two separate disputes.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. We think we identified three, though we may have mis-diagnosed them: (a) optional stopping — she holds that a trial that can be stopped at will, yet is analyzed as if its sample size were fixed, is invalid; I hold that the evidential import of the data in hand is the same however the sample size was determined; (b) the base rate of true hypotheses in, say, social psychology — she regards it as irrelevant to whether any given study was well conducted, I regard it as the whole story about why the literature went wrong; (c) whether "calibration" as she defines it and "calibration" as I define it are the same property. Reconstruct these as precisely as you can, and add or drop cruxes if our diagnosis is off.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both of us would agree in advance bears on it, together with each of our predictions about it, stated as a falsifiable commitment. Simulations count as cases: if there is a simulation we would both accept as a test, specify exactly what it would compute and what each of us predicts it will show.

5. A closing section identifying which of our remaining disagreements, if any, are merely verbal — disputes over the words "evidence" or "rational" rather than over substance — and which are genuinely mathematical or empirical.

Constraints: do not resolve the dispute in either direction; every claim in the agreed section must be one a thoughtful Bayesian and a thoughtful frequentist would each actually sign; attributing a position to a historical school by name is fine, but do not treat anyone's authority as an argument; and if you believe one of our cruxes is confused or badly framed, say so within the document rather than silently repairing it.
