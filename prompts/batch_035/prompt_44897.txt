Last month my nephew was automatically transferred to adult criminal court because his eighteenth birthday fell twelve days before the alleged offense; his co-defendant, by every account the more mature of the two and the one who planned it, is four months younger and remained in juvenile court. Watching the line do its work sent me back to reading about vagueness, and I now find I cannot tell what the institution of legal age cutoffs actually presupposes about it. The legislature draws a sharp line through what is plainly a gradual continuum — the slow onset of adult capacities — and treats the day before the birthday and the day after as categorically different. So the question I want answered: what view of vagueness does that practice only make sense under? The candidates I can see are an epistemicist reading, on which the statute merely locates, however arbitrarily, a boundary that in some sense already exists for the predicate adult; a supervaluationist reading, on which the statute stipulates one precisification in the penumbra, and the sharp line is true only according to that precisification; and a merely conventionalist reading, on which the line manufactures legal truth rather than tracking any boundary at all. Tell me which of these the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several of them would license it. Two further puzzles. First, stipulation seems to relocate vagueness rather than remove it — into midnight, into which time zone counts for a birth in flight across a border, and I have read that in some jurisdictions the age line runs to the first moment of the day before the anniversary of birth rather than the calendar birthday; what does the persistence of that residue tell us about whether law can sharpen a predicate at all? Second, the same legal system elsewhere refuses bright lines and lives with open texture — reasonableness, maturity, provocation, cruel and unusual punishment — and even convenes waiver hearings to ask whether a particular sixteen-year-old is adult enough. Are these two dispositions consistent, or do they quietly presuppose rival views of vagueness? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced whatever view it presupposes with its rival: on an epistemicist view, could the statutory line be criticized for being in the wrong place rather than merely arbitrary, and on a supervaluationist view, could it be criticized at all, or only on policy grounds? I want this argued from the theories themselves, not sourced to authorities. Please separate what is settled among people who work on vagueness from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restating of my question.
