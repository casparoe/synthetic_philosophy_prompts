I hold the scale argument for prioritizing insect welfare: even if my credence that insects are sentient is low — say one percent — the numbers (on the Smithsonian's rough estimate, about 10 quintillion alive at any moment, with short lifespans multiplying turnover) make expected insect suffering dominate nearly every other cause I could fund or vote for, provided welfare aggregates additively and choices are ranked by expected value. I'd like a map of what could count against this, organized by which of its three components the defeater strikes: the empirical premise (what would entomology or invertebrate neuroscience have to find — for instance, about nociception without centralized integration — for my credence to be doing the work the argument needs), the aggregation premise (whether additive aggregation over astronomical numbers of tiny welfare deltas is defensible, and whether the view is then committed to verdicts elsewhere that I should find unacceptable, as in the torture-versus-dust-specks comparison or micro-organisms), or the decision-theoretic premise (whether expected-value reasoning with very small probabilities and very large numbers amounts to fanaticism, and what a bounded alternative would do to my conclusion). For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as a genuine cost or instead absorb it by readjusting their credence — and I want an honest assessment of whether the position can be insulated into unfalsifiability precisely by that readjustment, since a lower credence seems always compensable by more insects. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of the three premises the view is actually most exposed on. What I would personally find most persuasive is a demonstration that the conclusion is being driven by one premise that I could abandon at little cost.
