my brother in law is one of those rationality podcast guys and at thanksgiving he finally got me. he says it's some famous thing called the allais paradox and apparently everyone falls for it. the setup: pick one option from each of two choices.

choice 1: A is $1,000,000 guaranteed. B is an 89% chance of $1M, a 10% chance of $2.5M, and a 1% chance of nothing.
choice 2: C is an 11% chance of $1M. D is a 10% chance of $2.5M and 90% nothing.

i picked A and then D, same as most people. he called that incoherent. his argument:

(1) if a scenario pays out exactly the same no matter which option you pick, that scenario can't be a reason to pick one over the other. a tie doesn't get a vote.
(2) in choice 1 the 89% scenario pays $1M either way, so cross it out — the real choice is "$1M for sure" vs "a 10-in-11 shot at $2.5M, else nothing."
(3) in choice 2 the 89% scenario pays nothing either way, cross it out — and whats left is the identical little choice as in (2).
(4) so i answered the same question two opposite ways, which he says makes me exploitable ("money pump," he kept saying, i think gleefully) and proves i need something he calls the independence axiom — gluing the same extra scenario onto both options can never flip my ranking.

i couldn't answer that at the table so i went home and built a defense, and honestly the defense scares me more than he does:

(5) i take A over B because B has a 1% chance of zero when a guaranteed million was sitting right there, and no amount of upside is worth that specific gut punch. the certainty itself is worth something.
(6) in choice 2 there's no certainty on offer anywhere — i'm probably walking away with nothing no matter what i pick — so i go for the bigger number.
(7) but if certainty is worth something, it's worth something everywhere. i'd take $1M sure over a 99% shot at $1.02M. i'm not risking a guaranteed million for a 2% bump. and $980k sure over a 99% shot at $1M. and so on down the ladder.
(8) chain those together (if x beats y and y beats z then x beats z — he's drilled even that into me) and my principle says: never take any gamble over the sure thing when the gamble carries any real chance of a real drop. which means don't drive to work, don't take the promotion that involves flying, don't get the surgery with the 99% success rate. it seems to prove that basically every normal life choice is irrational, and even my brother in law drives 75 on the highway.

so where does my defense go wrong — at (5) itself, or only at the slide into (7)? and if (5) somehow survives, does it actually answer his cross-out argument, or does he still win? if he wins, tell me which gut feeling to give up, because i'd rather keep my two answers if theres an honest way to. one more thing i can't shake: when he says the 89% "can't matter," part of me wants to say it obviously matters whether the background is "you're a millionaire either way" or "you're broke either way" — that's the whole difference between the two choices — but whenever i try to use that he just crosses the 89% out again and i go quiet. is that instinct worth anything, or is it just me not liking the answer?
