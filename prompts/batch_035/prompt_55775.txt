I've been reading a pop physics book on the nature of time, and it has a section on what it calls the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that I want to sanity check. Roughly what it says: special relativity shows that observers moving relative to each other disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with "now", and since no observer's frame is privileged, there is no objective fact about what is happening elsewhere in the universe at this moment. From this, Putnam argued in the 60s that the future already exists — any event in your future is "now" for some suitably fast-moving observer passing you at this very moment, so it must be just as real as your present. The book then draws the conclusion that relativity has basically proven determinism: your choices tomorrow are already laid out in the four-dimensional block, they only feel open from the inside. It finishes by noting that physicists consider the block universe settled and only philosophers still argue about it.

Is this a fair rundown? Which parts would a defender of the argument accept as stated, which parts quietly smuggle in something the physics doesn't actually give you (that determinism conclusion felt like a big leap to me but I can't put my finger on why), and does the bit about observers "passing you right now" really cover everything in your future the way the book needs it to?
