I'm a desk officer at a mid-sized foreign ministry, and every few months a crisis reaches the point where ministers ask whether we should support or join a military intervention, usually on humanitarian grounds and sometimes with regime change quietly attached. I have two days and a small team to produce the options assessment. The lawyers handle legality; what we have nothing for is operationalizing the just war tradition — just cause, last resort, proportionality, right intention, reasonable prospect of success — and the Responsibility to Protect doctrine that everyone cites and nobody applies. Please write a decision procedure we can actually run in two days: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure outputs a clear no, and where it stops and hands the decision back to ministers because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Flag the steps that look empirical but smuggle in values — I'm especially worried about 'reasonable prospect' and about 'last resort,' which seems to mean whatever the intervener wants it to mean. Then run the procedure on three cases: Kosovo in 1999, Libya in 2011, and Iraq in 2003. I expect it will handle the first two and struggle with the third, and I suspect there's a case it clears that I won't like — construct it if you have to, for instance a mission where the humanitarian case is strong but success depends on exactly the mandate creep that ruined Libya. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which failures of intervention it is structurally blind to.
