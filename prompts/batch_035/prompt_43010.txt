I'm taking a political philosophy course this semester and we just finished the liberal–communitarian debate, which I ended up liking way more then I expected. For my final portfolio I get to submit one creative piece instead of a third essay, and my idea is an advice column. The concept: a syndicated agony aunt who is a thoroughgoing communitarian, and every letter comes from a reader with thoroughly liberal-individualist assumptions. She never addresses anyone as a free-standing chooser — for her, every "should I quit my family / reinvent myself / opt out of this obligation" question is malformed, because the questioner is not the unencumbered self liberalism pretends they are. The humor has to come from the actual positions, not from making her a scold: she should be warm, a little smug, and always consistent — she can never fall back on "well, it's your choice" as a real answer, since that's precisely the liberal move she's rejecting. Someone who's never read a page of this debate should still find her coherent, if infuriating.

Could you write three letters and replies, around 700–800 words total? Seed it like this: (1) someone who moved across the country, "made themselves from scratch," and is now puzzled that they feel homesick for a place they deliberately left — she should point out, gently, that the self they made from scratch was assembled from materials none of us chose: the language, the norms, the very idea that a life is a project you author; (2) someone asking whether they owe anything to an elderly neighbor they never chose to be connected to by mere proximity — here she can go Walzer-ish, obligations of membership that aren't traceable to any contract or consent; (3) your pick, but it should target a different liberal commitment than the first two — maybe state neutrality between conceptions of the good, or the thought that choice is what makes a life valuable, or the right of exit as dissolving all duties.

One more ask: after the column, add a short note (a few sentences, clearly not part of the satire) on where the piece leans on contested ground — which of her replies a Rawlsian could actually answer, and which reply is the weakest point of the satire, the place a defender of liberalism could most plausibly push back. Don't bother citing anyone or giving me a reading list; if a reply only works because it borrows a specific author's move (MacIntyre on the narrative unity of a life, Sandel on the unencumbered self, Taylor on horizons of significance, Walzer on membership), just say so in plain words.
