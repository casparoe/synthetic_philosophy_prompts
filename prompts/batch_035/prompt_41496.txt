I am in my second year of theological training for ordained ministry, having converted in my late thirties after two decades of comfortable, secular moral realism. The Euthyphro problem, which I used to file away as a sophomore curiosity, has suddenly become a live vocational issue: when I eventually stand in a pulpit and tell a congregation that cruelty is wrong, am I reporting an independent fact about cruelty, or a fact about God's will — and does the difference matter for how I preach?

As I now understand the terrain: in Plato's Euthyphro, Socrates asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because the gods love it. Transposed into monotheism, the question becomes whether the moral status of an act is prior to God's command, or constituted by it. The voluntarist takes the second horn and accepts that morality is, at bottom, a matter of divine choice. The standard theological middle position — associated with thinkers in the tradition of Anselm and Aquinas and developed in its modern form by philosophers like Robert Adams — holds that God's commands flow necessarily from God's perfectly good nature, so that the good just is God's nature and morality is neither an external standard God happens to endorse nor an arbitrary fiat. The standard objection is that this middle position collapses onto one horn or the other: if God's nature is called good in a substantive sense, there is a standard of goodness independent of God; if the attribution is not substantive, saying God is good becomes a tautology and cannot do the explanatory work it was invoked to do. The literature on this has been running for centuries, the moves on all sides have been thoroughly mapped, and, so far as I can tell, nobody converts anyone.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the horns. I am asking for a diagnosis of the deadlock. Here are the candidate diagnoses I have been entertaining, offered as hypotheses rather than commitments. One: the disagreement is partly verbal, because the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading — one side is arguing about the metaphysical grounding of moral facts (what makes cruelty wrong), while the other is arguing about the semantics of moral terms (what we mean when we call cruelty wrong), and each side's stock objections only land on the question the other side is not answering. Two: the disagreement is substantive but not where it appears — it turns on a prior disagreement about whether moral obligations are necessarily relational in the way promissory obligations are, so that the theist's deepest intuition is that obligation requires an obliger, and the secularist's is that some duties hold without any transaction or command, and neither can argue for their starting point. Three: the question itself is ill-posed because it presupposes that there is a single direction-of-explanation fact to be discovered, when ordinary moral practice may not determine one.

Please assess these hypotheses and any others you find in the debate, using what the participants actually say rather than caricatures. In particular, I want to know whether the impasse is a sign that the question is badly framed or merely that it is hard, and whether anything — a sharper distinction, a test case, a reformulation of the question, or the admission that some component of the disagreement is bedrock — could genuinely count as progress here, as opposed to another century of restatement.
