I teach intro philosophy at a community college (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and every year the private language argument just slides right off them. They nod along with the beetle-in-a-box stuff, and then on the quiz half of them write that Wittgenstein proved we don't really have inner experiences, or that you literally can't talk about pain unless someone taught you the word first. I've just been assigned the unit again for the fall and want to try something completely different: instead of lecturing, I want them to actually try to build a private language and watch what it can and can't do.

The rough idea I had is Wittgenstein's sensation diary. For a few days before class, each student privately coins a name for a recurring sensation of their own choosing — something specific and inner that they think they get sometimes, not a standard pain or emotion with a public name — and keeps a diary using just their coinage, without ever describing the sensation to anyone. Then in class we run structured rounds where they have to use their private word in all the ways a real language has to work: re-identify the sensation later, in a different mood, and say confidently whether it's the same one; correct a past diary entry they now think was a misapplication; respond to a classmate's skeptical questions about it without gesturing or describing; and finally teach the word to a partner who then has to use it.

Can you design the whole thing for me? I need the diary protocol (with enough constraints that they can't just sneak in public descriptions), the in-class rounds with rules and what counts as success or failure, and a debrief that maps each round onto a specific step in the argument — private ostension, the diary check, the missing criterion, seems-right vs is-right. Also please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I expect some students will pick things that are actually publicly describable (like "the feeling when a song gets stuck in my head") and others will insist the whole thing obviously worked fine for them, and I don't quite know how to handle either. And be honest about which part of the argument no classroom activity can actually make them feel, so I know what I still have to argue for the old fashioned way.
