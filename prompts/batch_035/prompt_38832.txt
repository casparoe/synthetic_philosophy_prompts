I teach a 200-level seminar on time and modality, and we're ending the future contingents unit -- Aristotle's sea battle, bivalence, all that -- with a screening of Arrival. I want the students' writeup to actually track the debate, not just do vibes about time being a circle, so I've been trying out a reading myself and want to know if it holds up. My thought: once Louise has internalized Heptapod B, she effectively perceives the truth-value of future contingents, including that her daughter Hannah will die young. Conceiving Hannah anyway means acting on a future-tense proposition she knows to be true, which looks like the fatalist's worst case for bivalence -- the future is fixed by truth, so her choice does no work. The escape I want to teach is the Ockhamist one, where her foreknowledge is a soft fact: it doesn't cause the death, both trace back to a single fixed future -- and there's a nice parallel to Aquinas on divine foreknowledge, since the heptapods basically function as a timeless God and Aquinas gave exactly this compatibilist reply. But the film also leans on Sapir-Whorf, the idea that the language rewires how she experiences time, and I worry that trades on conflating epistemic openness (what's settled for her) with ontological openness (what's settled period) -- which is precisely the confusion I spend all semester trying to beat out of them. So: does the film dramatize a real position in the debate -- Ockhamism, Prior's Peircean open future, the three-valued Łukasiewicz line -- or does it equivocate between them, and does it know it's equivocating? Which scenes should I point the students at, and where does the film honestly resist the reading rather than being forced into it? Tell me straight if the Aquinas parallel is doing less work than I think.
