I'm a staff writer at a magazine that covers science and technology, and a growing share of my beat is automation and jobs — AI tools in radiology, warehouse robots, generative models and copywriting, the endless studies claiming some percentage of occupations is "exposed." My problem is that I have no economics training, my editor has less, and we have no framework for telling the serious claims from the noise, so coverage ends up driven by whatever press release arrives on a slow day. Rather than reach for the latest labor econ paper, I want to go back to the person who thought hardest about machinery and labor before it was a beat: Marx. Not for the politics, but for the conceptual apparatus — his distinction between tools that a worker uses and machines that use the worker, the idea that machinery doesn't simply abolish work but restructures the composition of labor, the reserve army of the unemployed as a structural feature rather than a policy failure, and the late notebooks where he speculates that automation might make labor-time cease to be the measure of wealth. What I'd like from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a pitch or a study in twenty minutes before a story meeting: the questions to ask in order, what would count as an answer to each, where the procedure stops with "don't write this, or write it differently," and — this matters — where it hands off to my judgment because the question is one of framing or emphasis rather than fact, so I know when I'm making a value-laden choice dressed up as a method.

Please then run the procedure on three cases, chosen so it has to work for its money. First, a pitch based on a consultancy report claiming that a new class of generative tools will "expose" 40% of tasks in a mid-skill occupation, with the consultancy selling retraining services. Second, the historical counterexample I keep getting thrown at me: the ATM, which was supposed to eliminate bank tellers and instead, on the standard story, coincided with more tellers doing different work — I want to know what the procedure does with a case where the doomsaying famously failed. Third, a hard one: a pitch arguing that this time is categorically different, because the machines are beginning to displace not just muscle and routine but the planning and judgment functions that always absorbed the displaced workers before — a claim that gestures, knowingly or not, at what Marx sketched about the general intellect and the end of labor-time as the measure of wealth. I'd like the procedure to give a verdict I won't like on at least one of the three, and I'd like you to say which verdicts it reaches only by leaning on a contested reading of Marx rather than the settled core.

Two honesty requirements. Marx scholarship is full of live disputes — whether the late notebooks are continuous with Capital or a detour, whether the reserve army mechanism says what it's often taken to say, how much of the automation story rests on the "Fragment on Machines" that enthusiasts quote and textualists distrust. Where a step in the procedure depends on one side of such a dispute, say so explicitly, and distinguish that from the parts where the texts and the secondary literature are roughly in agreement. And tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire — the kinds of automation stories where asking Marx's questions produces a worse article than not asking them — since a framework that can't name its own failure modes is exactly the kind of thing I'm supposed to be skeptical of for a living.
