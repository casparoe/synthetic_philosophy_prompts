I'm a hospital pharmacist, and a question that surfaced on the ward last month has refused to leave me alone. We had a patient on a medication that, at therapeutic levels, is genuinely dangerous — it can precipitate a serious arrhythmia — but our protocol pairs it with a second agent that blocks that arrhythmia if it begins to develop. When a colleague asked me whether the first drug "is" harmful for this patient, I realised I couldn't answer without first settling something much odder: when a substance is disposed to produce an effect, and something else stands ready to prevent that effect, what are we actually claiming? I've since noticed that my confusion splits into at least three distinct shapes, and I suspect they are not all the same thing, though I can't articulate why. In the first, the very trigger of the disposition seems to undo the disposition itself: consider a toxin that, the moment it is swallowed, provokes violent vomiting before any of it can be absorbed — we surely still want to call it toxic, yet "if swallowed, harm follows" is simply false, because swallowing removes the toxin from play. In the second, the disposition remains fully intact and something else intercepts the outcome further downstream — my arrhythmia case, or a poison given alongside a pre-loaded antidote that blocks the receptors. And then there is an inverse shape: a substance that is perfectly harmless, administered next to a chemotherapy drug, so that "if taken, vomiting follows" is true even though the substance isn't emetic at all — the conditional holds, but the disposition isn't there.

I gather that philosophers at some point analysed dispositions as straightforward if-then conditionals — whether that's still the received view I honestly don't know — and my three cases seem to break it from both directions: two of them satisfy the disposition while flunking the conditional, and the third satisfies the conditional while lacking the disposition. But what I mainly want is conceptual cartography rather than a history lesson. Does philosophy have established names for these configurations? In particular, are my first and second shapes recognised as genuinely different phenomena — it feels to me as though the first is a case where the trigger destroys the basis of the tendency itself, while in the second the tendency carries on doing its work and something else merely wins the race downstream — or are they standardly lumped together as one thing? And is the third, mimicking shape discussed anywhere? If established terms exist, please give them with careful definitions and tell me where my ward cases fit them cleanly and where they sit on the boundary; if what I'm describing doesn't match anything in the literature precisely, I'd rather you say so and coin terms that carve the cases correctly than stretch an existing label over them. Please don't just hand me a name — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
