So my reading group has spent the last couple of months on population ethics, and our most recent session was on the whole "humanity needs a backup planet" cluster of arguments, and honestly I've run out of patience for earnest essays about it — I want to process this one through mockery instead. The assignment I'd like: a satirical brochure-and-FAQ for a fictional private space colonization venture (name it something blandly corporate, your choice) selling one-way berths on the first permanent Mars settlement. The brochure's headline selling point is the existential insurance argument: Earth is fragile, engineered pandemics or nuclear war or an asteroid could wipe us out, and a self-sustaining colony is the species' backup drive. That's the thing being satirized — the argument and its rhetoric, not spaceflight itself — so the piece should exaggerate features the position actually has rather than inventing dumb ones.

The jokes need to come from getting the philosophy right, the way the fine print quietly takes positions on the live debates: whether creating the future colonists (from an onboard frozen gene bank, say) actually does anything *for* them — the non-identity problem buried in the reproductive-services clause; whether the marketing department is selling species survival as instrumentally valuable or intrinsically valuable, and what happens to the copy when nobody in the company can decide; the refund policy for the scenario where Earth turns out fine and never needs restoring (the "unused insurance" clause); whether a colony that culturally diverges from Earth over two or three centuries still counts as a *backup* of anything; and a testimonials section where customers disagree on population ethics grounds — one delighted total utilitarian glowing about the trillions of colonists to come, one sour average utilitarian who wants to know why the brochures count future people as a selling point when they don't exist yet. Deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, roughly 700 words, formatted as numbered clauses or FAQ entries — your call on the mix.

Last thing, and this matters to me: after the piece itself, append a short note (150-ish words, outside the fiction) flagging which bits exaggerate something pro-colonization writers actually maintain versus which bits lean on a reading that's genuinely contested — for instance, whether the backup argument really needs totalist population ethics to get off the ground or whether that's a caricature some of its defenders would reject. I want to be able to tell which jokes are fair and which are cheap shots, and I don't want the piece smuggling in a contested framing as though everyone in the debate accepted it.
