I am a high school senior in a philosophy elective, and I have been assigned to run our class's next seminar session. The topic I drew is the compatibilist idea that free will is not about whether our actions are caused, but about a certain structure among our desires — specifically the "hierarchy" view associated with the philosopher Harry Frankfurt, built on second-order desires (desires about which of our desires we act on). I have to present for about twenty-five minutes to a class of students who mostly arrived at the course thinking the free will debate is just "science says the brain is atoms, so free will is an illusion, end of story."

Please do not write the explanation itself. What I need is a teaching plan, and you should prepare it carefully as an experienced philosophy teacher who has taught this material many times and knows exactly where it goes wrong. Before you draft anything, take a moment to reason through the dependency structure of the ideas — what has to be established first for later steps to make sense — and brainstorm what could go off the rails, then organize that into the plan.

Here is the content I am supposed to cover, so we are on the same page about it. Frankfurt's basic apparatus distinguishes first-order desires (a desire to take some action) from second-order desires (a desire about one's own desires — for example, wishing I did not want to smoke, or wanting my desire to finish the paper to be the one that moves me). The special case he cares about is the second-order volition: wanting a particular first-order desire to be the one that effectively leads to action — to be one's "will." He illustrates with three characters. The unwilling addict takes the drug because his desire for it is strongest, but he wants that desire not to be effective — he wishes it were not the one that moved him. The willing addict also cannot resist, but he has no conflict: he endorses his desire, he wants it to be what drives him. And the wanton is a creature with no second-order desires at all — he just acts on whatever first-order desire is strongest, and it never occurs to him to have attitudes about which desire wins; importantly, a wanton is not necessarily a wild hedonist, just someone for whom the question of endorsement never arises. Frankfurt's line is roughly that the unwilling addict lacks freedom of the will, the willing addict has it but got it by luck, and the wanton isn't even a candidate for freedom of will — though all three may equally lack freedom of action, since none of them could have done otherwise at the moment of acting.

The plan should tell me: (1) the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why — in particular, whether second-order desires or the distinction between freedom of action and freedom of will should come first, and what breaks if I choose wrongly; (2) which single example should carry the whole explanation and what properties it must have and must lack to do that job — is the addiction case the right carrier, or does its messiness (physical dependence, relapse, moralized ideas about "real" addiction my classmates will have) get in the way of the structure Frankfurt is trying to display, and if so, what should replace or bracket it; (3) the misconceptions my classmates will predictably bring or form, in the order they will actually arise — I anticipate at least these candidates: that free will means you could have done otherwise, so the whole hierarchy is beside the point; that a second-order desire is just a stronger or more long-term version of a first-order desire; that the wanton is a strawman because everyone obviously has attitudes about their desires; and that Frankfurt has thereby defined away the problem rather than answered it — plus where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left to fester; (4) one check question I can ask at the end that would distinguish students who have actually grasped the structure from those who have merely memorized the vocabulary; and (5) what I should explicitly decline to cover in twenty-five minutes and openly say I am setting aside — I assume the deep question of whether endorsing a desire does any real work if the endorsement was itself caused, but tell me if there are other omissions I should announce rather than let students discover and use to derail the session.

Please be concrete about placement — e.g., "introduce X only after Y, because otherwise students will read X as Z" — rather than giving general teaching advice. The quality of the plan should live in the dependency ordering and the misconception map.
