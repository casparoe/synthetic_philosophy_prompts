I'm a PhD student in comp neuro and I've gotten sucked into this free will rabbit hole via robotics of all things, and I want to dump my case verdicts on you and see if there's an actual principle underneath them. The framing I keep coming back to is the old sphexishness thing — Hofstadter's coinage from the digger wasp, who drags prey to her burrow, goes in to check the nest, and if you nudge the prey an inch away while she's inside, she emerges, redrags it, and goes in to check *again*, on loop forever. Sphexishness is supposed to be the opposite pole of free agency: pure mechanical rigidity. But when I try to say what's on the other pole, "flexibility" alone doesn't work, and my own intuitions about cases are kind of a mess, so here they are.

The wasp is obviously not free, and neither is a thermostat, even one with a smooth continuous response — continuous isn't flexible in the right way. A chess engine that does deep tree search IS flexible in an intuitive sense (it handles any position you throw at it, never repeats the wasp's loop), and yet I don't want to say it's free — it's still sphexish, just sphexish over a bigger space, because the search procedure itself is frozen. But then here's the case that bothers me: a person rigidly following their GPS turn-by-turn down a obviously wrong route, in exactly the wasp's loop-like way, and I still feel like they're free *in that action* in a way the engine isn't — which seems totally inconsistent with what I just said about frozen procedures. Then there's the OCD case, someone who rechecks the stove in a perfect sphex loop and *knows* it's senseless while doing it — I feel like they're not free during the ritual but are free in the fighting-it, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or me being nice. Meanwhile pure randomness doesn't help either: a wasp with a quantum coin flip deciding whether to skip the second nest check is somehow still not free, just unpredictable — so it can't be flexibility-as-entropy either.

So what I want is: is there a principle that captures all these verdicts — something in the neighborhood of "the agent can evaluate and revise the procedure that generates the behavior, not just vary the behavior" — and does it actually save them, or does it force me to drop some? The GPS one is the one I'd most want saved and the one I most suspect can't be. Also please tell me what the principle says about two cases I haven't made up my mind on: (a) an addict who fully endorses their next fix in the moment and only regrets it later, and (b) a robot that monitors its own policy, predicts when it will fail, and rewrites that policy in real time — because my advisor insists the robot is "just sphexishness one level up" and that saying otherwise is special pleading for recursion, and I half think he's rigth and half think he's proving too much (wouldn't his argument apply to us too, and I'm not ready to concede that). If the answer is that "free will" fragments into two or three different things I'm running together, say that too — I'd honestly take a tidy dissolution over a forced principle.
