In a seminar I'm taking on moral disagreement and value pluralism, we've been discussing what agents who hold deeply different moral commitments owe each other when they must act together — say, co-legislators, or members of one organisation allocating a budget. A classmate has been pressing an analogy he finds decisive: moral compromise, he says, is just like trade between two economies. Two countries with different endowments both get richer by exchanging — each gives up something it values relatively less in return for something it values relatively more — and nobody thinks a country that imports grain has betrayed its farmers' interests. Likewise, he argues, two parties with different moral commitments can each end up with more of what they morally care about by trading concessions: pro-life and pro-choice voters trading restrictions in the third trimester for liberal access in the first, or a philanthropist who cares only about animals trading with one who cares only about far-future people, each getting the other to fund what they themselves would have funded last. He wants this to establish two conclusions: that compromise is not a regrettable dilution of principle but a genuine Pareto improvement over gridlock, and that we should accordingly regard moral compromisers the way economists regard traders — as creating value — rather than as weak-kneed or treacherous.

Our instructor thought the analogy was doing more work than it could carry, but the discussion stalled before we found out where. I'd like you to audit the analogy properly. First, set out the correspondence explicitly: in the trade model, what plays the role of the goods exchanged, of the preference ranking (is it my moral ranking of states of affairs, or my ranking weighted by agent-relative commitments?), of comparative advantage, of the contract and its enforcement, and of the "gains from trade" themselves — gains as judged by whom, and against which baseline? Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and flag which breaks matter for his conclusion and which don't. The worries that came up in seminar, at least vaguely, were these: economic analysis usually holds preferences fixed while trade proceeds, whereas conceding on something may change what I subsequently value; economic preferences are standardly commensurable and scalable, whereas some moral commitments seem to function as side constraints that cannot be partially surrendered or priced; and economists' "both parties better off" is assessed in each party's own terms, whereas a moral compromise might be endorsed by both parties' lights and still be something one of them is forbidden to make.

Finally, tell me what survives. Does his first conclusion — compromise as Pareto improvement rather than betrayal — stand on its own once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being carried by the analogy the whole time? And what about the stronger second conclusion, that compromisers deserve something like the positive status we give traders? If the trade model is fatally compromised, is there a better analogy available — for instance, treaty-making between states that each regard certain matters as non-negotiable, where the gains are real but bounded by what neither party may put on the table — that supports a more defensible version of his view? I'd appreciate a clear verdict at the end, since I suspect he'll raise the analogy again next week.
