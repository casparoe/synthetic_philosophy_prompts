My cousin, a palliative-care nurse of fifteen years, has firm convictions about when pain is bad for the person feeling it, and next week our family's long-running argument resumes over three new cases, so I want to predict her verdicts before she delivers them. Her record so far, on which she has been unwavering: a man with a badly broken ankle, in severe pain he hates — bad for him; a stroke patient with pain asymbolia who says "pain, pain" when pricked, locates and rates the pinpricks accurately, smiles throughout, and once let a hot cup scald her hand without pulling away, leaving a real burn — not bad for her; a postsurgical patient on morphine who reports that the pain is still there but that it doesn't bother him — not bad for him; a woman with a burning neuropathic pain in a leg that healed years ago, which she hates and constantly tries to escape — bad for her; and a faint ache that someone notices but genuinely doesn't mind — not bad for them. She is consistent, of that much I'm sure; what I'm not sure of is the rule she's running underneath. The three new cases are these. First, an ultramarathoner at kilometer seventy: her leg pain is intense, and in every single moment she wants it to stop and would press a button that removed the pain while leaving her legs fully functional, yet between races she insists the pain is part of what finishing means anything at all and would never sign up for pain-free versions of her races. Second, another asymbolia patient, who says the pain in her hand doesn't bother her in the slightest, but adds that she would rather it weren't there and would accept any treatment that removed it. Third, a man with a mild ache that he describes as almost weightless in itself — he would not mind the sensation at all, he says, if he didn't know what it might turn out to mean — and who wants it gone purely because of its diagnostic significance. Predict her verdict on each case, but what I really want is the analysis underneath: the different rules that fit her entire track record equally well (I can see at least four — something about how the pain feels in the moment, something about what she'd endorse on reflection, something about the felt urge to make it stop, something about whether the pain moves the person to protect or escape — but there may be others, including ones I haven't spotted), what each rule predicts for the three new cases and where they diverge, which verdict in her record is doing the most work in narrowing things down, your best guess at which rule she's actually running and why, and the single further case, described precisely enough to adjudicate, that would best separate the surviving rules. Lead with your bottom-line verdicts and your one-line bet on the rule in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
