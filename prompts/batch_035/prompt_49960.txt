I teach a third-year seminar on ethical theory — fourteen students, one two-hour session — and we've just finished a stretch on rule-based and principle-based ethics. For the following week I want to run an activity that sets up the particularism/generalism debate before I've lectured on it at all: the students shouldn't hear the word "particularism" until the debrief. My idea is something along the lines of a "codification game": small groups act as an ethics committee that issues verdicts on case files I hand them, and after each batch of cases they must commit to written rules that explain their verdicts — the rules then get applied, mechanically and in advance, to the next batch, and the groups are scored on how well their rulebook predicts their own later judgements. Crucially, I want the case sequence engineered so that features which counted in favour of a verdict early on come to count against it later: courage in an agent's character making things worse rather than better, or a promise being kept in a way that makes the action odious, or someone's wellbeing being promoted in a way that insults them. The point is for the students to run headlong into the thought that the same feature doesn't always carry the same weight in every context — that its contribution can invert or deflate depending on what else is present in the case — and then to discover that the natural response ("my rule just needs a ceteris paribus clause") quietly hollows out the rulebook.

What I'd like from you: a full session plan with a timeline, the case files themselves (at least eight, short but non-thin — the sort where a student can genuinely go either way), the exact rules for when and how the groups must codify, and a scoring scheme that makes the hedging move costly without my having to police it. One constraint I want built into the mechanics: at some point the groups should be forbidden from revising their rules mid-round, so that they feel the pull of the generalist's complaint that pure case-by-case judgement is unprincipled and unpredictable, and not only the particularist's complaint that the rules keep letting them down. If you can find a way to have different groups, each with a different accumulated rulebook but the identical final case, arrive confidently at divergent verdicts, that would be ideal.

Finally, the debrief: I want it to name Dancy's holism of reasons and Ross's prima facie duties as the two poles the class has just been oscillating between, and to map specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the literature — e.g., which case did the most damage to the invariant-reading of a rule, and which round made case-by-case judgement look least reproducible. Please also tell me frankly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I anticipate students writing rules so vague that nothing can defeat them; how do I handle that without simply legislating the conclusion?), and be honest about which feature of the actual debate no classroom game can reproduce — the particularist's positive claim that mature judgement can be trained and criticised without recourse to principles at all, which presumably can't be staged in ninety minutes and I'll have to supply myself at the board.
