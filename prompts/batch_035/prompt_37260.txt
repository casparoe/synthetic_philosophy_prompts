I've spent the past few years in and around a philosophy of mind reading group that keeps returning to the causal exclusion argument, and I've come to suspect that the debate about it is not going anywhere and, more importantly, that it isn't clear what kind of not-going-anywhere it is. Kim's argument, roughly: if a mental event M is realized by a physical event P, and P is by closure and completeness sufficient for the later physical effect P*, then M's causing P* is either causally overdetermined (which nobody accepts in the systematic, pervasive way the argument would require) or M is preempted, so either mental causation goes, the mental itself goes, or the mental is reducible after all. And Kim, as I understand him, took the last horn to be the live one — nonreductive physicalism comes out unstable, drifting toward reduction or elimination. What puzzles me is the shape of the response literature. Everyone concedes the argument is valid. The standard moves — Yablo's claim that mental properties are determinables of physical determinates and so don't compete for causal relevance; Shoemaker's subset account of realization, where the causal powers of the mental are a subset of the physical powers; List and Menzies's interventionist proviso, which on their counterfactual criterion can leave the mental property as a cause of the effect while the physical realizer is not a cause of it, blocking exclusion at the level of the causal relation itself — have all been on the table for decades. The rejoinders are also all on the table: that subset accounts just inherit the exclusion problem one level down, that the interventionist proviso works only because the counterfactual criterion is too weak, that a real causal power must be a producer and not merely a difference-maker, and so on. Nobody seems to move. Each side writes as though the other's position is a straightforward confusion, and neither side's papers from ten years later read differently from their papers ten years earlier.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the impasse is at bottom a verbal or quasi-verbal disagreement about causation itself: one camp is tacitly working with a productive, necessitating conception of causation on which exactly one sufficient physical chain gets to be the cause, and the other camp is working with a difference-making, interventionist or counterfactual-relative-to-a-description conception on which causal relevance is interest-relative and there is no fact of the matter about the cause of an effect simpliciter. If that's right, the exclusion argument is valid under both readings but unsound under each for different reasons — the production reading validates exclusion but at the price of abandoning the macrocausal talk that everyone, including hard-nosed reductionists, uses without compunction in every other part of science (elasticity, temperature, predator-prey dynamics); the difference-making reading secures mental causation but arguably only because it has lowered the bar to something Kim was never denying in the first place. But I'm not confident, and I can see a rival diagnosis: that the disagreement is substantive and formal, because the two conceptions of causation are not actually equivalent, and there are or could be principled reasons to choose between them that do not simply restate the intuitions that generated the impasse; or even that the debate is ill-posed because the question of what the cause of an effect is, as opposed to a cause of it, or a cause under a description, has no determinate answer until something else is fixed — the explanatory context — which the argument never supplies.

What I'd like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who wins. Engage what the sides actually say — Kim's exclusion principle and his own drift toward reduction, the determinable/determinate and realization-subset responses, the counterfactual-interventionist response — and tell me which of these disagreements is really doing the work: Is it the concept of causation, as I suspect? Is it a hidden factual question, for instance about whether there is a single, context-independent causal relation in nature, which developments in the general theory of causation outside philosophy of mind might in principle settle? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one about whether the mental is ontologically indispensable given what causes what, the other about whether mental-level explanations earn their keep? Or is it bedrock intuitions about cases (the pain causing the wince, the neuron firing causing the muscle contraction) that no argument can touch? And then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a settled account of causation imported from statistics or the sciences, a clearer statement of what nonreductive physicalism is even supposed to be for — or an argument that the debate is stable at an impasse because the choice between conceptions of causation is itself made on the basis of the very intuitions the exclusion argument mobilizes, making the whole affair circular in a way no theorem can undo. I'd like your reasoning to be concrete and to quote positions accurately by their content, not by reputation, and I'd like you to push back on my hypothesis if you think it misses something. Please close with a final line in the following machine-readable form: a label from the set {verbal, substantive-formal, substantive-empirical, ill-posed, mixed} after the word "Diagnosis:", followed by at most one short clause of justification — for instance, "Diagnosis: mixed — the causation concept carries most of the weight but a residue remains."
