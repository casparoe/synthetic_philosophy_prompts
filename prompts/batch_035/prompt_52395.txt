I have recently taken a research-assistant post on a wellbeing study that uses experience sampling: participants are pinged several times a day and asked to record what they are feeling, and our analyses treat those reports as measurements. Whether that is reasonable has become my problem in a concrete way, because I am due to write the section of our methods document that justifies the instrument. Two friends, canvassed over dinner last week, reached opposite verdicts via analogies, and I would like an audit rather than another dinner.

The first friend, a longtime insight-meditation practitioner, holds that introspection is a sixth sense. On her picture, attention plays the role of the sense organ; feelings are the stimuli; meditative training sharpens the organ rather as spectacles sharpen eyesight; and since perception is fallible yet basically reliable in ordinary conditions, trained introspective reports are data, subject to the usual caveats about illusion. She uses the analogy to license treating our participants' reports as measurements — at least for those who practise.

The second friend, who did his doctorate in cognitive psychology, counters with the press-secretary picture: introspection is a spokesperson who explains decisions after the fact, in ways designed to flatter rather than report. He reels off the stock confabulation results — people confidently justifying choices that were secretly swapped for their opposites; people explaining preferences by appeal to factors that could not have influenced them, position-on-the-table effects and the like — and concludes that a mood report is not a reading but a press release.

Please audit both analogies. First set out what maps onto what in each: for the inner-sense picture, a correspondence table from perception to introspection (organ, stimulus, distal object, attention, illusion, corrective procedures); for the press-secretary picture, whatever table it needs (who the spokesperson's public is, what the already-made decisions are, what would correspond to the transcript). Then say where each breaks. I can already see some candidate disanalogies — that attending to a feeling seems to change it, in a way that looking at a lamp does not change the lamp; that when asked whether I am anxious I find myself looking outward at the deadline rather than inward at anything; that there appears to be no external standard against which an inner reading could be checked, as a friend can confirm I misheard a sound — but I do not know which of these matter for my use and which are merely colourful. Please distinguish the disanalogies that are fatal to treating our reports as measurements from those that only complicate it, say whether the conclusion each friend drew survives once the broken parts of their analogy are on the table, and, if neither analogy will carry the weight, propose a better one — something that shows what participants are actually doing when they answer the ping, and what corrections we therefore owe the data.
