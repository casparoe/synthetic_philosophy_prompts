My reading group did Sen's liberal paradox a couple weeks ago (the 1970 impossibility of a Paretian liberal thing), and it turned into a running argument between two members, Priya and Dan, that never got resolved. The setup, in case it matters for understanding their fight: the paradox purports to show that if you want (a) a Pareto condition — if everyone prefers X over Y, the social choice mechanism shouldn't pick Y — and (b) a minimal liberalism condition, where each person is decisive over at least one pair of social states that differ only in their own "personal sphere," you can't always have both, because of cases like the prude and the lewd guy ranking the options over who reads a certain book. Anyway. Here's their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, and I'd honestly appreciate someone going through it carefully because I refereed it live and failed.

(1) Priya opened by saying the paradox is a real result about the world: there just is a structural tension between letting everyone have a protected private domain and doing what's best for everyone, and any society has to pick which value to betray. (2) Dan said no — the result only bites when people have what he called "nosy" preferences, preferences about what goes on inside other people's spheres. If everyone only cared about their own sphere, the two conditions never conflict, so the lesson isn't "liberalism is unstable," it's "the paradox is a tax on being a busybody, which is fine." (3) Priya pushed back that you can't build a theory that works only for people with the right preferences — social choice has to take preferences as it finds them, and a liberalism that comes with a pre-commitment about which preferences citizens are allowed to have is already illiberal at a deeper level. (4) Dan replied that there's an obvious difference between morally disapproving of what someone does in private and being concerned for their welfare — like wanting your housemate to quit smoking because you don't want him dead — and that the legitimate, welfare-concerned kind doesn't generate the trap. (5) Priya said she couldn't see a principled line between those two kinds at all, that most moralism dresses itself up as concern, and that in our own house (this is a real thing, see below) the "concerned" preferences are exactly the ones causing the standoff. (6) Dan's last move: fine, but the theorem also assumes a one-shot social choice mechanism — in real life people talk, negotiate, pay each other off. The trapped outcome is an artifact of modeling rights as a rigid ballot. (7) Priya closed by saying that if your liberal guarantee only works when people happen to be reasonable or rich enough to buy their way out, it's not a guarantee but a favor, and informal bargaining just replaces formal rights with whoever has more leverage.

The house situation, since it's the concrete seed of all this: we have a rule that your own room is your business. One housemate keeps his radiator blasting all winter (his room, his sphere), and another housemate genuinely thinks that's immoral because of emissions — she's not bothered by heat, she's bothered by the wrongness of it. And she in turn reads true-crime all night, which distresses him, though it affects nobody outside her room. Each of them thinks the other's preference is the illegitimate one. Classic Priya says our house proves her point; Dan says it proves his.

Can you adjudicate move by move? Like, rate each of the seven moves from clearly wrong to clearly right with a short justification, tell me who's more right overall, and also flag anything you think both of them missed in the whole framing — it feels like there's something neither of them ever brings up, maybe about how the theorem treats people's preferences as fixed rather than something that gets argued about, but I couldn't ever put my finger on it during the meetings.
