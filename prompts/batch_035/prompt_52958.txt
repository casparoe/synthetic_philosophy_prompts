You are a professional epistemologist with deep expertise both in Wittgenstein's later philosophy, including the posthumously published remarks gathered as *On Certainty*, and in the contemporary literature that has grown up around it under the label "hinge epistemology". Please think carefully about the question below, and begin with a short period of private brainstorming before settling on your structure.

I hold a fairly strong version of hinge epistemology. On my view, certain certainties — that I have two hands, that the world existed long before my birth, that other people have minds, that I have never been to the Moon — are not pieces of knowledge and not really beliefs either. They function as hinges on which our ordinary empirical judgements turn: they set the bounds of sense-making for a practice of giving reasons, and so sit outside the game of justification rather than at its bottom layer. In this I follow what I take to be the spirit of *On Certainty*: Moore was right to be certain and wrong to think he thereby *knew*; and the sceptic is wrong to treat the hinges as targets for doubt, because doubt only makes sense against a background of things held fast. I am inclined, further, towards the more radical corner of the literature: that hinge commitments are not arational episodes of believing but something closer to grammatical rules or animal certainties — patterns of acting rather than states of assenting.

Tell me what would count against this. I want a map of the view's exposure, not a defence of it. Specifically:

1. Which observations, cases, or arguments would tell against it — including ones that arguably already occur. For example: hinges that plainly changed their status over time (the fixity of the Earth, or the immutability of species, seem to have once operated as hinges and now not to); apparently rational disagreement about hinge-level claims between communities or epochs; apparent cases where a hinge is genuinely criticised from *within* a practice, not just displaced by external events. Which of these could a committed hinge epistemologist absorb as a friendly extension of the view, and which should they concede as genuine counterevidence?

2. Where the view risks having been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as a shift in grammar, what does the view still predict or rule out that its rivals do not? Is there any observation about how people actually deliberate, doubt, or change their minds that would make you revise the grammatical reading? And if the answer is no, say plainly whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or just what one should expect of a thesis about the framework rather than the moves within it.

3. A clear separation, throughout, between what is settled among people who know this material and what is genuinely contested — both across the field and within hinge epistemology itself. To be concrete about a few live disputes I am aware of: whether hinge commitments are arational, non-doxastic, or in fact beliefs of an ordinary kind with an unusual epistemic role; whether *On Certainty* is continuous with the rule-following considerations of the *Investigations* or marks a break; whether hinges are immune to epistemic evaluation or only to a specific kind of it; and whether the therapeutic, non-theoretical reading of Wittgenstein's remarks is correct or an over-reaction to it. Please do not present any of these as resolved, and where the leading interpreters disagree, flag that disagreement rather than adjudicating by majority.

4. A ranking of the defeaters you identify, from fatal (if any exist) through serious to merely awkward. In that ranking, distinguish what the view's defenders would themselves concede is a cost from what only its critics allege. If you think no defeater rises above "awkward", say so and say why — I would rather be told the view is well insulated than be handed a manufactured crisis.

Please answer in British English, and where you touch on specific remarks from *On Certainty* or specific interpreters (say, the grammatical or "non-epistemic" readings associated with commentators such as Moyal-Sharrock, or Pritchard's epistemologised version of hinge commitments), give them accurately but do not dress up contested interpretive claims as settled textual fact.
