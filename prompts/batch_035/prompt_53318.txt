Went down a rabbit hole on the no-free-lunch theorems after someone in the comments of a lesswrong-adjacent blog brought them up, and I've built an argument that seems to prove induction is a total scam, and I genuinely can't find the bad step. It goes:

1. NFL theorems (Wolpert, roughly mid-90s): if you average over all possible problems uniformly, every learning algorithm has the exact same expected performance as every other. Performance differences between learners completely cancel out. Fine, it's a real theorem, no dispute there.
2. Any learner -- a neural net, a bird, a human scientist -- is some algorithm or other. So averaged over all possible worlds, every learner does no better than chance at generalizing.
3. For any learner to actually do better than chance in *our* world, either our world is drawn from a non-uniform, structured distribution (simple regularities more likely than chaotic noise), or the learner got lucky.
4. But how do we know our world is structured like that? Only via induction: past generalizations kept working, so future ones will. That's literally the thing in question, so the justification is circular.
5. So we can't non-circularly justify the premise that makes learning possible, which puts us in the "lucky" branch of (3): our generalizations work, but for no reason we can give.
6. And if (5), then science has no epistemic authority over superstition -- both are just guessers that happened to get lucky so far, and there's nothing to say about why ours keeps winning.

I half believe a Humean version of (6) anyway, but the NFL version feels somehow *stronger* than plain Hume, because Hume at least lets you say "well, we can't justify induction but we can't help using it," while this seems to show the question of whether our world is the structured kind has no non-circular answer available even in principle. That seems to prove too much. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Also, does it change anything if instead of *us* inferring the structured prior, evolution baked an inductive bias into our brains -- is that a way out of the circle, or just the same circle one level down? I'd like to keep (3) if I can, and honestly (1) is not negotiable since it's just math, so I suspect the rot is somewhere in (3)-(5), but I can't tell exactly where and would rather hear the actual diagnosis than vibes.
