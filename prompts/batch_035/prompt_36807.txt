At a recent family dinner, my brother-in-law Dev recounted something from his two years working for a water-infrastructure NGO in a country I won't name. To get his project's permits processed, he gave the permits clerk a bottle of whisky at the new year and, on another occasion, an envelope of cash to the clerk's office as a collective thanks after a difficult application finally cleared. Two of my friends who were present — Amara, who studied political science, and Tom, an anthropologist — spent the rest of dinner arguing about whether Dev had behaved corruptly, and they each asked me afterward to say who had won. I couldn't, and I'd rather not signal a view. Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) Amara opened by saying the envelope of cash was a straightforward bribe: a payment by a private party to a public official in connection with the performance of duty is corrupt by definition, and dressing it as thanks or custom doesn't change what it is. She added that definitions like this aren't Western parochialism — impartial public office is a norm every modern state officially endorses, including this one.

(2) Tom answered that Amara's definition does the work only if you already know which payments count as "in connection with duty." The cash came after the application cleared; the whisky was a seasonal courtesy the clerk would have received from dozens of others; neither payment was conditional on anything. In that setting, he said, the gift is not an exchange at all but what anthropologists would call diffuse rather than specific reciprocity — it builds a relationship rather than purchases a service. Calling that corruption imports a Western wall between the market and the gift that this society never built.

(3) Amara replied that the timing distinction is illusory: a tip is just a bribe paid after the fact, and everyone who tips a waiter knows that the real currency is future service. What the payment actually purchases is standing in a queue that officially doesn't exist. And the clerk's "duty" is not a fixed output — processing speed, order, and attention are all discretionary — so a system in which gifts are expected quietly converts equal entitlement into something auctioned off in envelopes.

(4) Tom countered that on that reasoning every gift economy is corrupt, since all gifts create expectations; Amara had quietly reduced reciprocity to transaction and then indicted the transaction. He also noted that Amara had shifted from deviation-from-duty to outcomes-over-time: if corruption just means "any practice that generates unequal access to discretionary services," then legacy admissions at Amara's own university are corruption too — which she might accept, but then her charge no longer singles out Dev's whisky.

(5) Amara said she was happy to bite that bullet: her real criterion is not deviation from duty but the erosion of the ideal that public decisions should track the merits of the case, and institutions that auction access — whether via envelopes, legacy preferences, or campaign contributions — sit on a continuum of failing that ideal, with the envelope further along it. Corruption, she said, is best understood as a property of the system of expectations, and Dev made himself a consenter to that system.

(6) Tom closed by saying that a system-level standard gives Dev no action-guidance at all: an individual cannot unilaterally redesign a gift culture, refusing the whisky would have read as contempt, and Amara's position amounts to demanding that Dev absorb the moral costs of a badly designed institution so that she can keep her categories clean. Better to say the system is corrupting without saying Dev behaved corruptly.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six numbered moves, say whether the point is sound, defeated by something said earlier or later in the exchange, or sound only given a hidden assumption, and identify that assumption. Then say who is right about what, and — importantly — what, if anything, both of them are missing. In particular, I'd like your view on whether the distinction Tom draws in (2) between specific and diffuse reciprocity can bear the moral weight he puts on it, and whether Amara's shift in (5) to a system-level criterion is a principled refinement or a moving of goalposts.
