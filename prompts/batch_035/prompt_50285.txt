I'm a novelist preparing a manuscript, and I'd be grateful for a careful audit of a passage before it goes to my editor. The scene is a dinner party where the conversation turns to why there is anything at all. One character, a retired mathematician given to grand pronouncements, offers what he calls "the deepest of the old arguments for God" — his account is drawn, he says, from a popular history of philosophy he read years ago, and it goes roughly like this: Leibniz asked not what caused the universe, but why there is something rather than nothing; his principle of sufficient reason holds that everything must have an explanation, so the totality of contingent things — the universe as a whole — must have one too; since every member of that totality is itself contingent, the explanation has to lie in something that exists necessarily, and that necessary being is what the argument identifies with God; and because an infinite regress of contingent explanations would leave the whole still unexplained, the series of contingent things must have a first moment, a beginning in time — so, unlike the kalam argument, this one doesn't depend on astronomy and still establishes that the universe had a start. He then adds that Hume demolished the whole thing by pointing out that once you've explained every member of a collection, you've explained the collection itself, so demanding a further explanation of the totality is a mistake. He closes by telling his fellow guests they're free to deny the principle of sufficient reason, but that they must then accept that the universe is simply a brute fact, which he takes to be a reductio.

I don't know enough philosophy to tell whether my character is being fair to the argument or to its critics, and I'm inclined to believe the book he "read" garbled something, since his speech is meant to sound impressive but slightly secondhand. Please go claim by claim: tell me which parts a defender of the contingency argument would accept as accurate, which a scholar of Leibniz would wince at, and which are simply wrong; then say whether his closing move — deny the principle and accept brute facts — actually follows from whatever in his summary is true. I'd also like to know what the reader is left with if the errors are corrected, since the scene depends on whether his dinner companions have any good reply left. Kindly reply in American English, as the manuscript is for an American publisher.
