You're a philosopher who's spent the last few years hanging around conservation biologists and wild-animal-welfare people, so you know both literatures well. Think for a bit before writing — brainstorm what the hard cases actually are — and then give me the following.

I keep getting into arguments that go like this: someone says "habitat destruction is bad", someone else says "actually, most wild animals live short brutal lives, so fewer of them might mean less suffering, so maybe habitat loss is net positive", and then everyone throws population ethics at each other until the meeting ends. I don't want another take. I want the spec first: the list of things a theory would have to get right before it's allowed to issue verdicts on whether destroying or restoring a given habitat reduces wild animal suffering. Like, what any adequate theory of *net ecosystem welfare* has to deliver.

Some things I want the list to reckon with, at minimum — the theory has to handle r-selected species (destroying a wetland can mean preventing billions of short insect lives — does that count as preventing suffering?), the baseline problem (compare the destroyed habitat to what — pristine wilderness, current degraded state, the parking lot that replaces it?), whether it can even say destruction *wrongs* animals that will never exist because of it, the fact that evolution optimizes fitness and not welfare, and whether the theory says anything about predation (a theory on which more habitat is always good had better not be secretly assuming most lives are worth living). It should also respect the individualism/holism split — Aldo Leopold's land ethic treats the ecosystem as the unit of moral concern, most welfare people treat individual animals as the unit; a theory that stays silent on that is hiding the ball. And it has to be *usable* under massive empirical uncertainty, or admit it isn't and tell me what that admission costs.

Structure it like this, sections in this order:

1. **Why the obvious answers die fast** — ~150 words, the two or three moves everyone tries and why each begs a question.
2. **The desiderata** — the bulk of it, ~700 words. Each requirement stated, then the case or consideration behind it, briefly. Don't just list the repugnant conclusion by name and move on — tell me what applying it to animals specifically changes.
3. **Tensions** — ~250 words. Which pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and what it means for the whole enterprise if the list is unsatisfiable.
4. **Non-negotiables vs camp preferences** — ~150 words. Which requirements any serious theory must meet and which are just what utilitarians (or holists, or person-affecting people) happen to want.
5. **Scorecard** — ~200 words max, and keep it quick: total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, a person-affecting view, and the land ethic, against the list. I care about the criteria, not the scoreboard.

Don't pad, don't hedge everything into mush, and dont pretend there's a view that passes every requirement — if there isn't, say which requirement you'd drop and why.
