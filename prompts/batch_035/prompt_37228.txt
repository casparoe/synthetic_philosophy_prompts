I am a patent examiner in the mechanical engineering art unit, and a few times a year we receive applications for machines that purport to extract net useful work from a single heat bath — ambient air, a tank of water at uniform temperature, an isothermal environment of some kind. Our practice, like that of most offices, is to reject such claims as violating the second law and, given the history of these filings, to require a working model before granting anything. But the applicants have changed. The current wave does not deny the second law; they cite it. Their machines are Maxwell's demons in hardware: a sensor measures some microscopic fluctuation, an actuator gates or ratchets in response, and the specification asserts that sensing and actuation cost nothing or almost nothing, so that the cycle exports work. When I reject these with boilerplate about perpetual motion, the applicants amend the claims, insert a line item for the energy to run the sensor, and refile. I have a physics degree but no specialist training in statistical mechanics, I get about a day per case, any grant on one of these requires sign-off from a supervisory examiner, and my grounds have to be specific enough to survive appeal.

Please write a decision procedure my art unit could actually run on a specification. Ground it in what the demon literature actually establishes: Szilard's one-molecule engine and the kT ln 2 of work it appears to export; Landauer's principle that erasing one bit in a bath at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 as heat; and Bennett's observation that the measurement itself can in principle be made reversible, so the inescapable cost of the cycle is the resetting of the demon's memory. If you also know the later refinement in which the second law under feedback is restated with an information term that increases the extractable work, fold it into the appropriate step; if you don't know it, say so and proceed without it — I believe the Szilard–Landauer–Bennett triad is sufficient for the procedure. The deliverable should be a checklist with the questions in order: what to look for in a specification, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure outputs a rejection, where it outputs "not a second-law problem, examine on other grounds," and where it stops and hands the decision to me because the question is legal or factual rather than physical. It must say explicitly what to do when the specification is silent about the memory — applicants never mention it — and whether silence defaults to a rejection or to a request for information.

Then run the procedure on three applications, as they typically arrive:

(1) A membrane divides a gas chamber; a proposed nanoscale sensor predicts on which side a molecule will shortly arrive, a gate opens or closes accordingly, and the gas is claimed to sort itself into one half at constant temperature. The specification accounts for the sensor's electrical draw and claims the remainder as net output. It says nothing about what becomes of the sensor's readings after each cycle.

(2) An application that concedes the full demon analysis. It states the measurement cost, states that the memory is erased every cycle at the Landauer bound, and then claims that the erasure heat is dumped into the very bath the machine draws from — the machine plus bath is a closed system with no external input — so that the machine is a legitimate engine with information as its fuel. The claimed net output is positive, by exactly the margin between the sensor energy they charge on their books and the Landauer floor for the bits their sensor processes.

(3) A device with no sensor and no gate: a rotor in a fluid at uniform temperature, with asymmetric teeth, claimed to rectify Brownian motion into rotation. The standard ratchet-and-pawl analysis says this fails for a specific reason; I want to know whether the same step of my procedure catches it or whether it needs its own line.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the cases on which it will misfire, and the point at which the physics runs out and the rejection has to stand on legal doctrine — enablement, utility, the working-model requirement — rather than on thermodynamics.
