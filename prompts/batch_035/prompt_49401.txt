I hold a strong version of the common prior assumption: ideally rational agents cannot differ in priors, so any residual disagreement between them must be traceable to information asymmetry. My defense involves a move I haven't seen spelled out elsewhere: what looks like prior divergence is really disguised evidential disagreement. Each agent has credences about the reliability of other agents' credence-forming processes, including their own. So when I discount your confident take on a disputed matter, I'm not violating the common prior — I'm treating your confidence as a noisy signal from a poorly calibrated instrument.

What would count against this position? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically: (i) Does my move make the view unfalsifiable, since any stubborn disagreement can be redescribed as mutual distrust of reasoning rather than prior divergence — and does that redescription always work, or are there symmetrical cases, such as moral or aesthetic judgments where no one can plausibly claim superior calibration, where it becomes incredible? (ii) Does a regress bite: if my prior over your reliability is itself fixed prior to all evidence, hasn't the disagreement been smuggled back in one level up? (iii) What would a committed defender of the view concede as counting against it, as opposed to dismissing? I'd like an honest verdict on whether the position, once qualified in these ways, retains any real exposure or has been insulated — and whether that insulation, if present, is a defect in a principle of this kind.
