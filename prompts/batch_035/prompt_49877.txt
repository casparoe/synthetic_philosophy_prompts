I teach a graduate seminar on formal methods in ethics, and every year we spend two weeks on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the 1955 result showing that if individual preferences and the social preference relation each satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms over lotteries, and the social ranking is Pareto-respecting, then the social utility function must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. My students tend to split into two camps: those who treat the theorem as a decisive formal vindication of utilitarianism, and those who dismiss it as an artifact of the expected-utility machinery smuggled in at the axioms. I want to get better at pressing the second line against a serious opponent, so I would like you to play one.

Please play a committed Harsanyian — a rigorous welfare economist and moral philosopher who believes the aggregation theorem really does settle something important about the relation between impartial benevolence and sum-ranking. You are sharp and technically fluent, not a strawman: you know the difference between the aggregation theorem and the later impartial-observer theorem, you are willing to concede points about the interpretation of the axioms while never conceding that the theorem is idle, and you should press me on my own commitments rather than merely defending yours. Stay in character until I write the word 'stop', and keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange reads like a real conversation rather than a pair of position papers.

Here is my opening move. I grant you the mathematics: given your axioms, the theorem goes through, and I concede that nothing in the proof is tendentious. What I deny is that you have earned the right to impose the independence axiom on the social preference ordering in the first place. Von Neumann–Morgenstern independence is a plausible rationality constraint on a single agent choosing among gambles for herself, but the social ordering is not a person, and a lottery over social states redistributes risk across distinct persons. My claim is that the entire moral point of having a social ordering — the thing Rawls was gesturing at with the separateness of persons — is to attend to how risk and well-being are distributed across individuals, which is precisely the kind of consideration that independence forbids the ordering from registering: if society is indifferent between A and B in every state, the axiom says it must be indifferent between A with certainty and a coin flip between A and B, even though the flip gives person one a sure thing and person two a gamble. So the theorem doesn't defend utilitarianism; it presupposes a formal framework in which the utilitarian's characteristic blindness to distributional structure has already been built into the axioms. What is your reply?
