I'm the student representative on the allocation board of my university's Effective Altruism chapter, which each year distributes roughly £15,000 of fundraising revenue to a small portfolio of animal welfare organisations. This year one proposal has split the board down the middle: a grant to an organisation working on welfare standards in insect farming, specifically black soldier fly larvae reared as feed and crickets reared for protein, where the core case is what our course on moral uncertainty called the scale argument — the number of individual insects involved annually is in the trillions, so even a very low credence in insect sentience, multiplied across that many lives and the severity of plausible conditions on farms (crowding, heat stress, being ground or boiled alive at harvest), is alleged to dominate the expected-value calculation over our usual mammal-focused portfolio. The sceptical half of the board counters that this proves too much: they say the same arithmetic obliges us to care about nematodes and dust mites, that nociception — which insects clearly have — is being quietly conflated with valenced experience, and that a chapter shouldn't put donor money behind interventions premised on a probability its own members can't put above the low single digits.

Could you draft the decision memo I'll circulate before our vote? It should recommend a concrete allocation for the £15,000 — whether the insect organisation gets nothing, a token exploratory amount, a substantial share, or a majority share — with reasons the board could actually act on, not just a survey of positions. Please engage the strongest consideration on whichever side loses: if you recommend against the grant, deal seriously with the scale arithmetic itself rather than changing the subject to tractability; if you recommend for it, deal seriously with the "this proves too much" reductio and with the worry that credence in sentience isn't the kind of number that can responsibly be multiplied, given how underdetermined the evidence is. Where it helps, you can bring in frameworks we've touched on in the course, like handling decision-making under moral uncertainty and expected-value maximisation versus risk-averse or threshold-based alternatives, but the memo should stand on its own reasoning.

One request on how you write it: please mark clearly, in the text of the memo or in a short note appended to it, which premises are reasonably settled among people who know this literature (for instance, the basic facts about nociception and learning in insects) and which are genuinely contested (for instance, the probability of phenomenal consciousness in insects, or whether expected-value reasoning is even the right decision procedure when the probabilities are this soft). Don't present a contested view as though it were the consensus — the board will notice, and I'd rather the memo be honest about where the numbers come from than persuasive at the cost of the distinctions we've spent the term learning to respect.
