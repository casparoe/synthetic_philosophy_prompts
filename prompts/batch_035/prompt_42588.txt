I need your help refereeing a dispute that broke out at my company last week, because I've been going in circles ever since. Some context: I work as a junior analyst at a mid-sized logistics firm, and last year HR ran a voluntary "leadership bootcamp" pilot. About 250 employees enrolled, and the question on the table at our meeting was whether to expand it. The headline numbers looked good: 48.8% of participants were promoted within 18 months, versus 43.6% of the roughly 550 eligible employees who didn't enroll. Daniel, who runs the program, opened by declaring it a clear success and recommending expansion.

Maya, a colleague in analytics, pushed back with a breakdown by seniority level. The participants weren't a representative sample: 200 of the 250 were senior analysts, who get promoted at high rates no matter what, while the non-participant pool was dominated by junior associates. Within each band, the participants actually did *worse* — senior participants were promoted at 55% versus 60% for senior non-participants, and junior participants at 24% versus 30%. So, Maya argued, the aggregate advantage is a compositional artifact: the program didn't make anyone more promotable, it just attracted people who were already likely to be promoted, and Daniel's number is essentially measuring the participants, not the program. Daniel's counter was to repartition the data himself: broken down by department instead (we have four), participants outperformed non-participants in every single one. Since the choice of partition flips the conclusion either way, he accused Maya of cherry-picking her breakdown — if there's no principled way to say which grouping is "the real one," the honest summary is the overall number, and hers is no less arbitrary than his. Maya answered that there is a principled difference: seniority plausibly affects both who enrolls and who gets promoted, which is exactly what makes it the variable you should control for, whereas department is largely a reflection of seniority mix and doesn't drive enrollment — so her partition tracks a genuine confounder and his does not. Daniel's final word was that this reply concedes his point: she's now appealing to assumptions about how enrollment worked, assumptions that live outside the data, so the numbers alone don't settle anything — which means her confident claim that the program *hurt* everyone who took it was just as overreaching as his original claim that it worked.

They asked me to write up a recommendation and I genuinely can't tell who's right about what. Please go through the exchange move by move — Daniel's opening aggregate claim, Maya's seniority breakdown, Daniel's department counter, Maya's confounder reply, and Daniel's final underdetermination point — and rate each as clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification for each. Then tell me who is more right overall, and, importantly, whether there's a question both of them seem to be missing — including, if you can, what evidence would actually settle whether we should expand the program.
