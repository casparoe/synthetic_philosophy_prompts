I'm TA-ing a second-year normative ethics course and grading take-home finals this week. one of the exam questions was:

"Explain lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and contrast it with classical utilitarianism and with plain negative utilitarianism. Does the view succeed in limiting the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and at what cost, if any?"

One student answered:

"Classical utilitarianism puts pleasure and pain on a single scale and says the right act maximizes the net balance. Plain negative utilitarianism counts only suffering: pleasure has no weight at all, so the best world is the one with the least suffering — which famously seems to imply that painlessly ending all life would be optimal, since that eliminates suffering for good. Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which is basically Popper's view in The Open Society, instead sets a threshold of severity. Suffering above the threshold gets lexical priority: no amount of pleasure, however large, can justify pushing anyone past it. So unlike plain NU it avoids the kill-everyone conclusion, because below the threshold mild suffering still trades off against pleasure like normal. Does it limit commensurability? Yes, completely. By definition a negative utilitarianism can't let pleasure count against suffering anywhere, so pleasure and pain are never on a common scale at any point — below the threshold as much as above. The cost is that it inherits plain NU's problem in the everyday range. And it follows that we'd have to abolish any activity that risks pushing someone over the threshold, like mountaineering or boxing, since a chance of threshold-level suffering can never be bought back with any amount of fun."
