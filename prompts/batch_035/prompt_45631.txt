My friend and I spent a long drive home from a conference arguing about the replication crisis, and we have ended up stuck at an impasse that I would like you to help me decompose. The proposition under dispute: if experimental psychology had switched wholesale to mainstream Bayesian practice around 1990 — estimation with posterior intervals, Bayes factors in place of threshold significance tests, hierarchical modeling — the replication crisis would have been substantially smaller than it turned out to be. He puts this at roughly 0.6; I put it at 0.15. Please give me your own credence, and then show your work by decomposing it into at least the following terms, each with a probability and a one-line justification: the probability that the crisis was substantially a statistical-framework problem at all, as opposed to a publication-incentive and career-incentive problem that would have found an outlet in any inferential machinery; the probability that the familiar questionable research practices — optional stopping, flexible specification, selective reporting — would simply have migrated into the Bayesian toolkit, via choosable priors and the analogous forking paths of model choice and analysis plan, rather than being prevented by the switch; the probability that the deepest cause is low prior odds, so that with most tested hypotheses false from the start, most positive findings would have been spurious even under ideal inference, a problem Bayesian reporting would have made visible without fixing; and the probability that the frequentist machinery was failing even in its own terms, given that its guarantees concern the procedure as run rather than the procedure as selected and reported — a conditioning problem that Bayesian inference arguably inherits just as fully. Add any further terms you think belong, combine them into a number, and then tell me which single term does the most work in separating my 0.15 from his 0.6, and what evidence — a subfield, a natural experiment, a line of argument — would move that term the most.
