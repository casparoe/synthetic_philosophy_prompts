before a poli-sci seminar thing I have to submit my "theoretical commitments" on collective action so the others can pick at them, and I want to check myself that it's not incoherent before I embarass myself. Here's roughly where I stand:

1. Hardin was basically right about the tragedy of the commons: if a resource is open to all and each user pockets the full benefit of use while the costs of overuse are spread across everyone, rational users will grind it down, and the only real fixes are top-down regulation or privatization.
2. Ostrom's fieldwork (irrigation cooperatives in Spain, alpine pastures in Switzerland, inshore fisheries, that kind of thing) shows communities managing commons sustainably for centuries with neither the state nor private property, so the "privatize it or regulate it" dichotomy is empirically false.
3. The logic of collective action is airtight: with public goods, rational individuals free ride, so voluntary provision fails. Not "tends to be a bit low" — fails.
4. Wikipedia and open source software disprove this in practice: enormous public goods produced entirely by voluntary contributions.
5. The second-order free rider problem: punishing defectors is itself a cost with no private return, so rational agents won't enforce norms, which means norms can never be a self-sustaining solution to a collective action problem. Anyone who "solves" a commons with norms is just relying on people being irrational or having moral preferences, which concedes the whole framework.
6. Climate change is the paradigm case of all this: the atmosphere's capacity to absorb emissions is a global commons, so we should expect exactly the collective failure we're seeing, and I don't buy that some emergent global norm will save us.

I have a suspicion 2 and 5 can't both be true, but I honestly can't tell wether the real problem is there or somewhere else — maybe it's 1 vs 2, or 3 vs 4, or something I haven't even noticed.
