I teach a philosophy of law / collective responsibility seminar every second spring, and we always spend three weeks on the debate about whether a nation as such can be guilty — Jaspers' little book from 1946, the four types of guilt he carves out (criminal, political, moral, metaphysical), and then the whole cascade of twentieth century cases: reparations, state apologies, truth commissions, the works. And every year the class splits into two camps that cannot move each other, and honestly neither can the literature. The individualists dig in on the point that guilt requires a guilty mind — intention, knowledge, the possibility of remorse — and only individual human beings have those, so talk of national guilt is at best a metaphor and at worst a rhetorical club. The collectivists point out that nations genuinely do things — declare wars, run camps, sign treaties — and that if the nation can be an agent, it's strange to say agency is fine for praise and for legal personality but mysteriously unavailable for guilt. Then somebody brings up the intergenerational part and things get worse: if the perpetrators themselves are dead, what exactly is being inherited by the grandchildren, and is it guilt at all or something else wearing its clothes? At that point the discussion basically just... stops, and I hand out the next reading.

I've got a working hypothesis I'd like you to take seriously but not just confirm. I suspect a good chunk of this is a verbal dispute where 'guilt' is doing double duty: one side means a juridical status (the thing a court assigns, or that God assigns if you're Jaspers), and the other means something more like a ledger relation between a persisting corporate entity and a wrong it did, a relation that can survive changes of membership the way a debt survives the death of the borrower. If that's right the two camps are answering different questions under one word, and the real fight is being had by proxy. But I also suspect there's a genuinely substantive question hiding underneath, about what makes a nation numerically the same entity across two or three generations — which is maybe where the intergenerational intuition gets its bite, since nothing obvious (not genes, not property, not the state apparatus) seems to be the bearer of whatever gets passed down. And there might be a third element I keep going back and forth on: the participants' differing starting intuitions about whether blame-talk between groups is even intelligible without a blamer with a mind, which nobody on either side ever argues for.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, two different questions run together, or bedrock intuitions that argument can't touch — and support it with what each side actually says, not with a survey of the literature. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: is there a distinction to be drawn, a thought experiment that would isolate the disagreement, a reformulation of the question that would let the two camps see they're not fighting? Or is this one of those cases where the impasse is itself informative — where the fact that smart people have circled this for seventy years without convergence is evidence the question is ill-posed as stated? My seminar would thank you, though they don't know it.
