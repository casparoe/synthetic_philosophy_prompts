I'm a cognitive science PhD student who came into the symbol grounding debate late, and I'm struck by something odd about its history. Harnad's 1990 formulation seemed to define a crisp research program: purely symbolic systems manipulate uninterpreted tokens, meaning can't be bootstrapped from symbol-to-symbol mappings alone (his analogy was learning Chinese from a Chinese–Chinese dictionary), and so symbols must be grounded bottom-up in nonsensory, non-symbolic representations — iconic sensory analogs and learned categorical feature-detectors — with connectionism as the natural bridge. There was a burst of engagement around that and the adjacent Chinese Room argument, and then, as far as I can tell, the debate just went quiet rather than being resolved. Now large language models have dragged it back with a vengeance, but in a strange form: one side says these systems are exactly what Harnad warned about, ungrounded symbol manipulation at industrial scale, a tower of tokens referring only to other tokens; the other side says the grounding problem as posed is already dissolved, because the training text is not an arbitrary formal system but a sedimented trace of a world — millions of people using words to point at, act on, and structure things — so the models inherit a connection to the world through the statistics of its descriptions, and besides, multimodal extensions are now grounding tokens in images and audio directly. Both sides have been saying roughly this for three years and neither has moved an inch.

In our lab reading group I've developed a working hypothesis I'd like pressure-tested: that the dispute is substantially verbal, and that "grounding" is being used to mean at least four different things across the literature — a causal-historical connection between internal states and referents, sensorimotor embodiment of the kind Harnad's iconic representations require, a functional or inferential role that makes a system's internal states matter to what it does, and a social or communal anchoring in a language community whose use fixes meaning (which is why the text-as-trace defenders keep saying the models are "parasitic" on human grounding without seeming to think that's disqualifying). If that's right, the two camps might both be correct under their respective readings, and the standoff is a bookkeeping failure rather than a substantive one. But I can also see it going another way: maybe the disagreement is empirical in disguise, about whether a system that has never had perceptual or motor contact with the world can nevertheless acquire whatever internal structure does the work of understanding, and we simply lack the experimental techniques to tell. Or maybe it's methodological at bottom — the sides disagree about what would even count as evidence of groundedness, which would make it self-sealing.

What I want is a genuine diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, empirical, methodological, or a case of two research traditions answering different questions under one heading — and support the diagnosis with what the parties to it actually claim, including whether the LLM era changed the terms of the debate or merely scaled it up. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: whether some conceptual distinction, behavioral test, comparison between multimodal and text-only systems, or roboticist's intervention could in principle settle it, or whether the honest answer is that no experiment or argument should be expected to move either side, and why. If you think my four-reading taxonomy is wrong or incomplete, say where it breaks and what a better one would look like.
