You are an expert on the foundations of heuristic reasoning — the informal arguments (first-moment bounds, counting heuristics, and above all the presumption that pseudo-random-looking quantities behave like independent random ones) that mathematicians and cryptographers lean on when proof is out of reach. Your specialty is reverse-engineering the tacit rules people run when they decide which of these arguments to trust. Think carefully and brainstorm before committing to anything.

I'm in a reading group on these topics, and one member — call him D. — has accumulated a firm track record of verdicts about when the presumption of independence is trustworthy:

1. He will call a uniformly random 1024-bit odd number prime after a handful of Fermat-test passes, happily treating the witnesses as independent draws; but he refuses to run the same test as evidence about a number handed to him by an adversary, since Carmichael numbers show exactly how the independence assumption fails on adversarially chosen inputs.
2. He believes the counting heuristic that random graphs just past the 3-colorability threshold have no proper colorings, and that such graphs are genuinely intractable to color, though nothing like a proof is known.
3. He rejects estimating SHA-256's preimage resistance by treating the compression function's output as random: the design was iterated against decades of cryptanalysis, so its random-lookingness was selected for, not sampled.
4. He thinks the random-oracle model is fine as a heuristic for natural schemes, but expects it to fail for schemes deliberately engineered to be secure only in the model — and he holds that such contrived failures don't impugn the heuristic's use elsewhere.

Next week's case: a colleague has generated a candidate hash function by literally sampling its entire structure (wiring, tables, constants) from a seeded random generator, and has had it cryptanalyzed by no one. He proposes to rely on it wherever a random function is assumed, arguing it is strictly more "generic" than SHA-256 and so a better target for independence heuristics.

Predict D.'s verdict. Before predicting: lay out at least two candidate rules that each rationalize his whole track record (for instance, rules about what process generated the object versus rules about how simply a failure could be described versus rules keyed to the domain's track record), state what each predicts about the coin-flip hash, and flag exactly where they diverge. Give your best guess with your reasons, an honest statement of your confidence, and the single further case that would best discriminate between your candidate rules — and say whether any pair of them is verdict-identical on every case you can construct.
