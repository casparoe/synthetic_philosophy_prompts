I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have a decent grounding in mathematics and a growing interest in formal results about ethics and collective decision-making — something about the idea of a theorem governing how a society "ought" to aggregate its members' preferences appeals to the part of me that likes well-specified problems. A popular economics book I've been reading devotes a chapter to John Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and it summarises the result roughly as follows:

"In the 1950s, John Harsanyi proved that if every individual's preferences are rational in the sense of expected utility theory, and society's preferences are rational in the same sense, and society always follows its members whenever they are unanimous, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities; add the requirement that citizens count equally, and the weights must all be the same, yielding the classical utilitarian prescription to maximise the total. The result extends Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance argument, in which you choose society's arrangements without knowing which position you will occupy, with an equal chance of being anyone. Because expected utility theory is the theory of rational choice under risk, the theorem amounts to a mathematical proof that rational social choice is utilitarian. It also settles the old worry about interpersonal comparisons: since the utilities are being added together, they must be measurable on a common scale, so the theorem establishes that utilities are interpersonally comparable. Critics have objected that the approach is blind to the distribution of wellbeing — that it rates a society with a blissful majority and a miserable minority the same as one in which contentment is spread evenly — but Harsanyi answered this: any social rule that sacrifices total utility for the sake of equality can be recast as a gamble that every rational citizen would reject, so preferring equality is formally incoherent."

Something about the closing stretch troubles me, though I can't pin down why. My own instinct, perhaps naively, is that the distribution of wellbeing across people is a genuine concern that a formal framework cannot simply dissolve — but the book is by a professional economist, and the chapter reads very confidently. Would you assess whether this is a fair summary? Finally, please tell me whether the book's conclusion — that rational social choice must be utilitarian and that preferring equality is incoherent — follows from whichever parts of the summary survive scrutiny, or whether it depends on the parts that don't.
