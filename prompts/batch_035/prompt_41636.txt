For the last few weeks I have been recording my honest verdicts on a series of thought experiments about whether word meaning lives in the head or in the world, and I have reached the point where I want someone to tell me what, if anything, my verdicts add up to. I would like a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — stated precisely enough to be tested, together with its verdicts on cases I have not yet considered, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are my cases and my verdicts.

First, the classic twin scenario: in 1750, a speaker here on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate on a distant planet are intrinsically identical, equally ignorant of chemistry, and each surrounded by a clear, tasteless liquid filling the lakes and seas. The liquid there is not composed of what ours is — call it a different compound with all the same everyday properties. My verdict: the two speakers mean different things by their word for the liquid. They are talking about the stuff in their respective environments, not about the description either of them could give.

Second, the medical misuser: a man tells his doctor that his arthritis has spread to his thigh. He is wrong — the disease in question, as the community and its physicians use the term, afflicts joints only. My verdict: he still means arthritis by his word; he has not coined a private term for thigh ailments. The community's usage fixes what his word means, and his false belief is merely that.

Third, deference: I cannot distinguish gold from pyrite, but when I say of a ring that it is gold, I take myself to have said something about the metal, not about pyrite-lookalikes. My verdict: yes — what my word picks out is fixed partly by experts I have never met and could not identify.

Fourth, the artifact twin: on that same distant planet, the objects my duplicate sits on are shaped and used exactly as our chairs are, but they are made of a resin secreted by something that is not a tree. My verdict: my duplicate and I mean the same by our word for these objects. The difference in material, decisive in the water case, seems irrelevant here. I confess I do not know why I draw the line where I do.

Fifth, the stipulator: an eccentric announces, in so many words, that whenever he says the word for the liquid, he shall mean the stuff in the pond behind his house, whatever its chemistry turns out to be. The pond, it later emerges, contains a compound different from the one in the town reservoir, indistinguishable in every ordinary respect. My verdict: his stipulation succeeded; his word means the pond stuff, full stop. But this sits very badly with my verdict in the medical case. The misuser and the stipulator are both out of step with their communities, yet I let the stipulator succeed where the misuser fails, and I cannot articulate what separates them beyond the fact that one said what he was doing out loud.

Sixth, the slow switch: a person lives here for thirty years, then is relocated, unknowingly and without incident, to the twin planet, and lives there another thirty. My verdict: by the end, their word for the liquid refers to the twin compound; before the move, it referred to ours; and nothing in their inner life marked the change of meaning. I accept this, but uneasily — the thought that a person can mean one thing on Monday and another on Tuesday while everything between their ears is, by hypothesis, as similar as you like, seems to leave something out.

So: is there one account of how reference is fixed that delivers all six verdicts? My own crude attempt goes something like this — a word's reference is fixed by a causal-historical chain terminating in the samples the speaker's community interacted with, where the relevant kind is whichever accounts for the preponderance of those samples; but an individual can override the community by explicit metalinguistic stipulation; and for artifacts, functional role rather than material constitution is what counts. I can already see that this is less a principle than a patchwork, and that clauses two and three do real work only in cases five and four respectively, which is a bad sign.

Please test my patchwork against my six verdicts, repair or replace it as needed, and then tell me what the resulting account predicts for cases I have not decided: a community whose samples of the local yellow heavy metal turn out, on closer study, to consist of two distinct elements that behave identically in every everyday respect, never separated in practice; a word whose every sample in the community's history was, unknown to anyone, an elaborate fake of the kind the word was meant to track; and a community that uses a word for a kind of animal that has since gone extinct in its original habitat and been replaced, without anyone noticing, by a behaviorally identical descendant. Finally, be blunt about which of my verdicts has to go. I suspect I cannot keep both the asymmetry in the artifact case and a uniform story about natural kinds, and that I cannot keep both the misuser and the stipulator without paying for it somewhere — but I would rather hear where you think the cost is lowest, and what my surviving principle commits me to that I have not yet noticed.
