My grandfather passed away a few weeks ago, and at the reception after the funeral my uncle Dale and my cousin Sabrina spent over an hour arguing about whether death is actually bad for the person who dies. I mostly listened, and now I can't stop turning it over. Dale opened by saying it can't be: a bad thing has to be bad for someone, and there has to be a someone around for it to happen to, but once death arrives you're no longer there to be the victim — and before it arrives, nothing bad is happening yet. He said he'd read that some ancient Greek made this argument ages ago and that nobody has ever really answered it. Sabrina's first reply was that plenty of bad things happen to you without your ever feeling them — if a supposed friend spends a year trashing your reputation in another city where you'll never hear about it, that's still bad for you. Dale answered that all of her examples are still events within your life, entries on the ledger of things that happen to you, whereas death isn't an entry on the ledger — it's the ledger's running out, and you can't be harmed by what is written nowhere.

Sabrina then shifted ground: the harm of death isn't an event, it's a hole, all the good things — the books, the birthdays, the people — you'd otherwise have had. To make the point vivid she invented a case: suppose you book a two-week dream cruise, but a stranger secretly doses you with a drug that keeps you in dreamless unconsciousness the whole time, plus a second drug that erases your memory of being dosed, so you wake up at home convinced you simply slept one night. Nothing bad is ever felt by anyone, no one is aware of losing anything, yet almost everyone would say something bad was done to you: the vacation was taken. Dale came back with what he called his best card: by her logic, the eternity before your birth is also a hole, an infinite deprivation of everything you'd have had if you'd been born earlier, yet nobody walks around mourning their prenatal nonexistence, so it can't be the hole that does the work. Sabrina said the cases aren't symmetric: death takes away a future that was actually coming to you, while there was no person hanging around before conception waiting to be born, so nothing was taken from anyone. Dale's last stand was about measurement: to say grandfather was deprived of goods, you have to say which ones and how many — if he hadn't gone at 83, would he have had two more good years? five? ten, some of them sick ones? — and since no answer to that is anything but a guess, the "harm" has no size, and it's odd to say someone was robbed when we can't even say of what, or how much.

I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, and they finally asked me to referee, which I was useless at. Could you go through the exchange move by move and evaluate each of the five or six moves on its merits — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons? Then tell me who, if either, is right about the central question, and whether there's something both of them are missing.
