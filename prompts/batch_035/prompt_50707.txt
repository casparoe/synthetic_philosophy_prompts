Question (10 marks):

A recent research program, motivated partly by AI safety, aims to formalize the "heuristic arguments" that mathematicians and physicists use when exact proof is out of reach. A central role in that program is played by the "presumption of independence." Answer all four parts.

(i) State the presumption of independence in your own words.

(ii) Illustrate with the standard primality example. What probability does the prime-density heuristic assign to the claim that a uniformly random 1000-digit number is prime? What happens to that assignment when the number is instead one written on a scrap of paper by an adversary who wants the heuristic to be wrong? Say precisely what the presumption is doing in that case.

(iii) Explain why a heuristic argument that is fine on randomly chosen cases can fail systematically when the cases are instead chosen by a search process, and why this matters for trusting safety arguments about AI systems.

(iv) What is a formalization of heuristic arguments supposed to add over informal ones? Be explicit about what it could and could not deliver.

---

Student's answer:

(i) When we support a mathematical claim by a heuristic argument, we treat the quantities we cannot compute exactly as if they were independent of one another, unless we can point to a specific reason why they would be correlated. In practice: if we cannot find any argument that two quantities are related, we presume that they are not.

(ii) The prime number theorem puts the density of primes among 1000-digit numbers at about 1/ln(10^1000), i.e. about 1 in 2300, so the heuristic assigns a uniformly random 1000-digit number a 1-in-2300 chance of being prime. Since the prime number theorem is a theorem, the 1-in-2300 figure is proven rather than heuristic, so this estimate is on firmer ground than the presumption of independence: it is not an assumption we could turn out to be wrong about. Now suppose the number is not random but was written on a scrap of paper by an adversary who wants the heuristic to be wrong. The adversary simply searches for a 1000-digit prime and writes it down — primality testing is easy, so this costs the adversary almost nothing — and the heuristic then assigns a 1-in-2300 chance to a number that is prime with certainty. The presumption of independence is what excludes this in ordinary cases: we presume the number carries no hidden structure, no special provenance connecting it to the primes, unless we can exhibit an argument for such a connection.

(iii) Under random sampling, each way a heuristic argument can fail is rare — that is what makes the heuristic look safe. But a search process can concentrate its effort on exactly the rare cases where the argument fails, and searching is often cheap even when stumbling on such a case by chance is astronomically unlikely, as with finding a 1000-digit prime. This matters for AI safety because our safety arguments for a system are heuristic: in the absence of a visible connection, they treat the situations the system might face, or the components of its behavior, as independent. But an AI system — or the optimization process that trained it — is itself a search process, and can find the rare situations in which the heuristic argument for safety fails, even though a randomly chosen situation almost never exhibits the failure. Heuristic arguments that merely hold on typical cases therefore cannot establish that a system is safe.

(iv) A formalization of heuristic arguments would give a precise language in which such arguments can be written down and mechanically checked, as proofs are. Once we have it, the presumption of independence is no longer an assumption: for any two quantities, the formal system can search through every argument in the language that could establish a dependence between them, so if it turns up none, their independence is a theorem. A formalized heuristic argument is therefore as reliable as a proof; the only difference is that its conclusion may be a probability rather than a certainty. Moreover, since the system can always find some argument or other for any given input, an adversary cannot produce an input about which the formal system has no view. So once heuristic arguments are formalized, they can be trusted even on adversarially selected cases — which is what the program was after.
