I've spent about fifteen years in video engineering and post-production, first in live broadcast and now on the delivery side, and I think almost entirely in the vocabulary of that trade: timecode, PTS and DTS, decode order versus presentation order, B-frames that are demuxed out of order and reassembled for display, frame buffers, compositing and render pipelines, latency budgets, motion interpolation, and the edit logs and archives that record what a render actually did and when. When I recently read Dennett's discussion of the color phi phenomenon in Consciousness Explained, I had the uncomfortable feeling that I already knew this debate — but knew it in a language Dennett doesn't speak.

As I understand it, the phenomenon is this: when two stationary spots of different colors are flashed in alternation at different positions, with the right timing, subjects report not two flashes but one spot in continuous motion that changes color midway along its path — a path and a color the subject's retina never received. Dennett then presses a dilemma about the timing of this experience. On one solution, the brain holds the raw material in a buffer, composes the interpolated trajectory, and only then "publishes" it to consciousness — so the experience itself, as reported, never happened in that form; Dennett likens this to Soviet show trials and calls it the Stalinesque option, a rewrite before the moment of broadcast. On the other solution, the subject really did have a plain experience of two flashes, and the interpolated story is a later fabrication that overwrites the record — his Orwellian option, a post-broadcast edit of the archive. Dennett's punchline, via his Multiple Drafts model, is that there may be no fact of the matter about which story is true: the question "when exactly did it become conscious?" may be ill-posed, rather than merely hard to answer.

Here is what I'd like you to do. Restate the whole exchange — the phenomenon, the Stalinesque option, the Orwellian option, and Dennett's no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. To me, the Stalinesque story sounds like a pipeline that adds latency on purpose: hold the frames in a buffer, run interpolation or compositing, and render to the display only after the fake intermediate frames have been generated. The Orwellian story sounds like the broadcast going out clean while the archive is silently rewritten afterward — and motion interpolation in modern TVs, which synthesizes frames the source never contained, seems like a cousin of the effect itself. But I want you to do this honestly rather than conveniently: don't smuggle in assumptions of my framework and present them as translation. In particular, my vocabulary takes for granted a master clock, a canonical presentation timestamp for every frame, a single privileged output (the display), and logs that settle disputes — and it may be exactly these assumptions that can't be carried over. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophers' debate map cleanly, which had to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all — above all, whether Dennett's claim that there is no fact of the matter translates into my terms or simply cannot be expressed in them. My engineer's reflex is that "no fact of the matter" is always an artifact of missing logs: if you can't tell when the frame went out, you haven't checked the PTS. I want to know whether that reflex is a defect in my vocabulary that makes Dennett's conclusion inexpressible, or whether it is a genuine objection that his argument has to answer — and I'd rather hear the honest version, even if it leaves my side looking worse.
