**The Lateral Progress of Philosophy**

People say philosophy never settles anything: the same debates run for centuries and nobody wins. I want to argue this is the wrong way to measure what philosophy does. Philosophy progresses, but it progresses *laterally* — by handing questions off, not by closing them.

Think of the sciences as spin-offs. For most of history, "philosophy" included what we now call physics, psychology, economics, and linguistics. Each of those fields began as a philosophical tangle and became a science once someone found a way to make the questions tractable — Newton, Wundt, the rest. When a question emigrates, that's not philosophy failing; that's philosophy succeeding. The discipline's output isn't answers, it's *successfully formulated questions*, and the progress is invisible if you only count answers.

But some questions never emigrate, and I'd group them under one heading: the ones where we can't agree what would even count as an answer. Take the mind-body problem. Chalmers can define the hard problem as crisply as you like, and twenty years of neuroscientists have produced stunning maps of the brain, and yet the gap between firing neurons and the taste of coffee is exactly as wide as it was for Descartes. No lab will ever close it, because it's not an empirical gap — it's a gap in what we mean by "explaining." The same, I suspect, goes for free will and for the sorites.

So my picture is this: philosophy is a sorting machine. Questions go in, and one of three things happens: they get shipped to a science, they get dissolved (Wittgenstein's old saw about showing the fly the way out), or they stay and circle forever. On this view Gettier's little paper is a model of progress — not because it solved knowledge, but because it showed the analysis was broken in a precise, permanent way. Philosophy doesn't climb a mountain. It redraws the map so we can see which parts are mountable at all.
