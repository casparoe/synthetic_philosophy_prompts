I am a colour scientist by training: my day-to-day work involves cone fundamentals, metamerism, opponent-process encoding, appearance models of the CIELAB and CIECAM variety, device characterisation, and gamut mapping. I have a layman's familiarity with philosophy of mind and none beyond that. I want you to restate Frank Jackson's knowledge argument in the vocabulary I actually use, and then tell me honestly what does not survive the translation.

To be clear about the target vocabulary: when I talk about colour, I talk about reflectance spectra and illuminants; about LMS cone excitations and how the visual system's adaptation state transforms them; about opponent-channel encodings and their non-linearities; about the difference between a metamer pair (two spectra that produce identical cone responses under a given illuminant) and a pair that are genuinely distinct in the data; and about the difference between a specification (say, the tristimulus values of a patch) and the state of an observer who is actually looking at it. I am fluent in all of this; I am not fluent in philosophy.

Now the translation task. Take Mary, the scientist who knows every physical fact about colour but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room, and render her situation using only the concepts above. In particular, I want you to address what "knows all the physical facts" could mean when spelled out as data Mary could actually possess, and what "learning something new" could mean when she first sees a red rose: acquiring new data, instantiating a new encoding of data she already had in description form, or gaining a practical ability such as recognising, imagining, or matching red things. Then translate the standard replies to the argument into the same vocabulary: the ability hypothesis, according to which she gains know-how rather than know-that; the phenomenal concept strategy, on which she acquires a new way of thinking about an old fact rather than a new fact; and Jackson's own later recantation of the argument. I do not need the history or the citations; I need the positions themselves stated in terms a colour scientist can check against what she already knows about observers and encodings.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which parts of the debate map cleanly onto things I can express (for instance, the distinction between a spectral description and an opponent-channel state, or between possessing a lookup table and running the transform); which parts you had to distort to make them fit my vocabulary, and in which direction the distortion went; and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all. After that, apply the translation to two test cases. First, tetrachromatic Mary: a trichromat who possesses every physical fact about tetrachromatic colour experience without being tetrachromat herself; does anything in the argument, once translated, distinguish her case from the ordinary one? Second, the metamer worry: Mary's data distinguish spectra that her own visual system cannot distinguish, so her room apparently contains strictly more information than her experience does; does the knowledge argument, in my vocabulary, turn on anything beyond the distinction between a description and an instantiated encoding, and if so, what exactly?

Finally, give me your judgement on the residue itself: does it indicate that my framework is missing something the philosophers need, or that the philosophers' claim was never as clear as it looked? I would rather be told my vocabulary is inadequate than be flattered.

Please write your reply as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write it as you would a long letter to a technically minded colleague.
