Before my city council takes up another fight over a contested Confederate-era monument, I want the terms of evaluation settled in advance, not improvised hearing by hearing. So: write the desiderata a theory of commemoration ethics must meet before I'll take it seriously -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the facts about how statues, street names, and holidays actually work that it can't contradict -- with the motivating consideration behind each requirement, and an indication of which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are camp prejudices dressed up as constraints.

The purpose the theory must be good enough for: giving a principled decision procedure to a municipal body that must render verdicts on contested commemorations -- keep, remove, relocate, recontextualize, or replace -- under real political constraints, not adjudicating a seminar dispute. So the requirements should be ones that a mayor could actually act on, and a requirement that can't be operationalized at all deserves a flag saying so.

At minimum, I want the theory to confront the following, though you should add, subtract, or sharpen as the list itself demands. First, the distinction between honoring a person and merely recording that they existed: a statue in a public square plausibly performs a commendatory speech act on behalf of the current community, while a plaque or a museum exhibit records without commending -- and the theory must say whether this distinction can do the work it is usually asked to do, given that defenders of contested monuments routinely claim the commemorative function is really documentary. Second, the temporal problem: what a monument says is indexed to the values of whoever currently maintains it, so a statue erected under one moral regime makes an assertion under another -- the theory must say whether the original intention of the honorees' contemporaries has any standing at all, and if not, what becomes of the charge that we are "rewriting history." Third, the no-clean-hands threshold: if no historical figure clears modern scrutiny, the theory needs a principled cutoff between the Lincoln-with-flaws case and the dedicated-white-supremacist case, not just an intuition. Fourth, the heterogeneity of commemorative media: a street name is embedded in wayfinding infrastructure, contracts, and people's home addresses; a statue in a park is not -- removal costs and side effects differ in kind, and a theory that treats all commemoration alike should explain why we tolerate it. Fifth, the countermonument possibility: Hamburg's disappearing column against fascism and similar works suggest commemoration can be performative and self-undermining rather than laudatory -- the theory must accommodate or rule out honoring through negation. Sixth, the standing question: whose objection counts, and does it matter whether the wronged group's descendants are the ones being asked to live under the honorific? And seventh, the act of removal itself: dismantling is also a public act with meanings, including the meanings it will be given by bad actors -- should a theory of adequate commemoration ethics weigh a monument's afterlife as a rallying point, or is that a prudential consideration that has no place in the theory proper?

Where I most want your judgment is on joint satisfiability. I suspect, for instance, that a requirement the theory not honor the dishonorable, a requirement that it preserve public historical memory in situ, and a requirement that it not distort the monument's original meaning through recontextualizing plaques may not be satisfiable together -- and if so, that is a result about the list, not a failure of it. Say which pairs you think are in tension, and which single requirement, if enforced, does the most to decide the familiar debates before any scoring happens. Then, briefly, hold the standard up against the three positions that dominate public argument -- keep-and-contextualize, remove-and-relocate-to-museum, and remove-and-replace-with a countercommemoration -- and tell me which of them survives your own list, and where it survives only by quietly downgrading a requirement you marked non-negotiable.
