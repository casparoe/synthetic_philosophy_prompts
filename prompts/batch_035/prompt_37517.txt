A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the axiom of choice, and I came away suspecting the argument isn't really about the axiom at all — but I can't pin down what it is about. I'd like a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on the axiom.

First, the argument itself. My friend works in set theory and treats choice as settled. On his view, ZFC is simply the background theory mathematics runs on, the equivalences with the well-ordering theorem and with Zorn's lemma are mathematically unimpeachable, and an axiom of this kind is justified by its consequences: measure theory, comparability of cardinals, every vector space having a basis. The fruit is so extensive that the occasional embarrassment — the Banach–Tarski decomposition of the sphere, say — is the price of a powerful theory, like accepting a false-positive rate in a good diagnostic test. I'm more sympathetic to the constructivist side. My line all evening was that no justification of choice is non-circular: the intuitive argument, that given a family of nonempty sets one may simply pick one element from each, only feels innocent because we're tacitly imagining finitely many sets, or countably many, or sets that come with a distinguishing rule; for an arbitrary family with nothing to guide the selection, the picking is a metaphor, and the axiom converts that metaphor into an existence claim. His reply was that the same complaint applies to every set-existence principle, including the power set axiom: if I'm allowed to collect all subsets of an infinite set, why can't I collect one element from each member of a family? My answer was that the power set of a given set is a determinate totality, while a choice set is a selection with nothing determining which object gets selected from each set.

What struck me afterward is that we agreed on every formal fact in the vicinity — the independence of choice from ZF, the equivalences, which weak fragments of choice suffice for which theorems, that Banach–Tarski depends on choice — and the argument still went nowhere. So here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not about the axiom of choice but about the background conception of the set-theoretic universe. He is talking about what is true in a fixed universe of sets, one in which the iterative hierarchy is given all at once, while I am implicitly demanding that an existence claim come with a procedure or at least a determinate description. On that reading we are answering different questions under one name — roughly, whether there is such a set versus whether it could ever be exhibited — and the stall is only to be expected. But I'm not confident this survives scrutiny. He doesn't literally believe we can exhibit power sets either, and I don't reject all non-constructive mathematics; the people who reject it all the way down strike me as coherent, whereas my own position looks like an arbitrary stopping point, and I'd like to know whether it is.

So, the questions I want answered: What kind of disagreement is this, actually — substantive, and if so about what: the objects, the standards for asserting existence, or what the justification of an axiom can consist in? Is the intrinsic-versus-extrinsic distinction doing the work here (self-evidence about the universe of sets versus justification by consequences), and can the two sides even state their disagreement to each other without begging the question against the other? Does the equivalence of choice with well-ordering and Zorn's lemma matter to the diagnosis — is there a reading on which the equivalence itself is the interesting datum, since people's intuitions come apart across the equivalents, some finding well-ordering absurd while accepting choice without a blink? And what would count as progress? A new independence result clearly won't do it, since the independence of choice from ZF never moved anyone on either side. Would a conservativity result of the right kind, or a natural theory that recovers most of the fruit of choice while avoiding its pathologies, or a sharper distinction in the neighborhood of my hypothesis change anyone's mind? Or is this a debate permanently insulated from mathematical fact, so that the honest diagnosis is that my friend and I hold different conceptions of what mathematics is — less a disagreement than a choice about which game to play — and if that's the answer, say so plainly.
