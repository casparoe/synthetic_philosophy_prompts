I spent most of last evening in a long argument about Newcomb's problem with a friend, and I want to have her central move examined properly rather than left where the argument ran out.

To fix terms: a predictor that has been almost always right has already put $1,000,000 in an opaque box if and only if it predicted that I would take that box alone; a transparent box in front of it holds $1,000 regardless. I now choose either the opaque box or both. My friend takes one box, and her case for this is an analogy. Facing the predictor, she says, is just playing a prisoner's dilemma against a duplicate of yourself. Imagine that you and a psychological copy of you are placed in separate rooms and must each independently write "cooperate" or "defect": if both cooperate, each receives $1,000,000; if both defect, each receives nothing; if exactly one defects, the defector receives $1,001,000 and the cooperator nothing. She holds that in that room anyone can see at a glance what to do — you and your copy will reason identically, so defecting is just choosing the low-payoff cell for both — and that one-boxing in Newcomb's problem is precisely the same act of cooperation. Two-boxing is defecting against your own duplicate, which, on her view, is not merely the wrong choice but a conceptual confusion: no one who understood the room he was in could defect in the hope that his copy wouldn't notice.

Please give me a proper audit of this analogy rather than another stand-off. Specifically: (1) set out the correspondence table — what in Newcomb's problem plays the role of the duplicate, the duplicate's choice, the simultaneity of the moves, the payoff matrix, and the other player's rationality; (2) identify where the mapping breaks — I can gesture at some candidates (the predictor decided before I did, it is not itself deliberating, its accuracy is stipulated rather than flowing from shared reasoning, and it can in principle be wrong about me where a copy of me, by hypothesis, cannot differ from me), but I do not trust myself to rank them; (3) sort the disanalogies into those that are fatal for her conclusion and those that are harmless idealizations a defender of one-boxing could grant without loss; and (4) say whether her conclusion survives.

Two features of the conclusion make me suspicious, and I would like both addressed. First, the claim that in the duplicate case everyone obviously cooperates seems too quick: the man who defects against his copy for $1,001,000 appears to be running exactly the causal-dominance argument that two-boxers run, so I suspect the analogy does not settle the disagreement so much as relocate it. Second, late in the evening she extended the analogy to the predictor's fallibility: even if the predictor were right only four times out of five, she said, you would in effect be cooperating with a copy who matched you four times out of five, and cooperation would still come out ahead — and I could not tell whether that extension strengthens her case or quietly concedes that the stipulated near-infallibility had been doing the work all along.

If your audit finds that the duplicate analogy cannot carry her conclusion, please say what part of it, if any, it can carry, and whether there is a better analogy available to the one-boxer. For background only: my friend and I gathered that the identification of the two cases goes back to a paper by David Lewis from the late 1970s, but I do not need the history confirmed or cited, and the audit should not turn on it.
