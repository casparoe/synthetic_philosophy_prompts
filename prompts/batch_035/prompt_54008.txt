I am a novelist drafting a scene in which two sisters argue at a family wedding reception. The elder sister, who opposes same-sex marriage, reaches for an analogy I have heard used in earnest in such debates: that a marriage is like a lock and a key. The two are shaped for one another; only their fit opens anything; and two keys, or two locks, however well they go together, open nothing. In the scene I mean the analogy to do real work — it should give her the better of the argument for a while, so that her younger sister's eventual reply lands harder. Before I commit it to the page, I would like to know how much work the analogy can actually do outside the novel. Please set out what maps onto what — the lock, the key, the opening, the fit, the polishing — and where the mapping breaks down; a table of correspondences would be welcome. I am especially interested in which features of a designed artifact the analogy quietly imports into marriage (locks have makers and a purpose; do couples?), in how it classifies the infertile married couple — as a broken lock, a lock without a door, or something the analogy cannot place at all — and in whether the conclusion it is recruited to support, that marriage is by nature a procreative union and that same-sex unions therefore cannot be marriages, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it turns out the analogy is carrying the conclusion rather than the argument, I would like to know that too, along with what a better analogy for marriage would be, if there is one, and which sister it would favor.
