been seeing this argument over and over in abolition-of-the-monarchy threads and i want someone to actually stress-test it instead of just upvoting it: the claim is that a constitutional monarch is basically a circuit breaker. the government is the current, flowing day to day, and the crown just sits there doing nothing for decades. but if something shorts out -- a PM who refuses to leave after losing a vote, tries to cancel elections, that kind of thing -- the breaker trips and blocks it. people always bring up norway in 1940 (the king refusing to appoint quisling as PM, which is like the one time anyone's seen this thing actually fire) and treat the uk's 2019 prorogation mess as a near-miss where the court did the tripping instead.

the argument concludes that the monarchy should be kept *precisely because* it does nothing, and that republicans sneering about a ribbon-cutting pensioner are missing that it's rated for maybe one fault per century, and that's the whole spec. my friend takes it further: replacing the crown with an elected president would be like swapping the breaker out for a smart switch that whichever party is in power can just log into.

audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table (breaker : current : fault : trip : reset : what, the electorate?), then the places it breaks. the ones that jump out at me: breakers trip on physics and get tested monthly, while the crown trips on one person's judgment, in the exact kind of crisis they've spent 40 years being trained to have no opinions about. there's no rated interrupting capacity for coups. breakers in a panel are in series with other protection, whereas the crown is in series with... courts? elections? a grumpy army? and the "does nothing for decades" feature is doing something weird -- breakers can be trusted to do nothing becuase they're dumb, and the crown is a person, so the property that makes the source work (no judgment, no ambition) is exactly what the target lacks in the middle.

then tell me whether the "keep the monarchy" conclusion survives once that's on the table, or whether the analogy only carries the weaker claim that you want SOME crisis mechanism, with a hereditary crown being one implementation among several (a constitutional court with teeth, supermajority thresholds, security services refusing unlawful orders). also genuinely curious whether scandinavia and japan are even the same device -- a monarch with actual folklore of resistance (Haakon in the snow) vs a monarch who is constitutionally told to be a symbol and not have powers (japan, article 4 iirc?) seem to have totally different failure modes, and i want to know if the breaker picture fits one and not the other. and if the analogy is bad, what's the better one -- national fire extinguisher? off-site backup generator? or is the honest version just "expensive insurance with an untested actuarial table"?
