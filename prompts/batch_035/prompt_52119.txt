Longtime lurker of the rationality blogosphere here (lesswrong, SSC-adjacent stuff, etc), and last week I finally got to watch two of my meetup's regulars go at each other over free will, and I could not for the life of me keep score. Setting: someone had demoed an AI agent that plans out its actions, and someone else joked "see, even the robot has free will now", and it all spiraled from there. I'll number the moves so the adjudication is easier.

(1) Dan said look, physics is deterministic or close enough — and where it's not, quantum randomness doesn't help you anyway, because a random twitch isn't any more "up to you" than a determined one. So free will in the strong sense is just out, full stop.

(2) Maya said the "strong sense" was never a coherent target in the first place. Even if libertarian free will existed, what is it supposed to be — your choice popping out of nowhere, caused by nothing? That's not you authoring anything, that's a miracle. The compatibilist move is just saying: the thing we ever actually cared about — acting from your own reasons and values, uncoerced — is fully compatible with determinism.

(3) Dan said that's a definitional retreat, and he's read enough of this blog culture to know one when he sees it. Everyone feels the pull of "I could have done otherwise, in exactly those circumstances," and when that gets threatened, compatibilists switch to the safe counterfactual version — "you could have done otherwise if you had wanted to" — which is true but weaker, and then act like the original worry was answered. Changing the subject and calling it a solution.

(4) Maya said fine, then spell out the strong version. If "could have done otherwise in exactly identical circumstances" can't be cashed out as anything except the same input producing a different output with no difference in you, then what you're mourning is the loss of noise. And the deliberation point: your deliberating isn't bypassed by the causal chain, it's a link in it. It's not like determinism deliberates instead of you.

(5) Dan said he can't fully cash out the strong version, but he knows it matters, because of the predictor feeling. The thing where some model can predict your choice better than you can while you're still mid-deliberation, and suddenly your "reasons" feel like weather — stuff that happens to you. Sure, his deliberation is "part of the causal chain," but so is a hurricane's pressure gradient. Causal involvement isn't the same as being in charge.

(6) Maya said that last bit actually cuts against him. The predictor is good precisely because it models his deliberation — the prediction tracks his reasoning, doesn't skip it. If anything, being predictable-by-simulation-of-your-reasoning is the strongest evidence that the reasoning is load-bearing. And being "in charge" was never about being outside the causal order; it's about which parts of the causal order are the parts that constitute you.

They went around in circles after that and eventually someone changed the subject to the buffet.

Please go through moves (1) through (6) one at a time: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification, and tell me where either of them is talking past the other. Then, if you can, say who comes out ahead overall, and whether there's some move that neither of them made that would have actually moved the dispute somewhere productive — I have a feeling there is, something about what happens when you stop asking whether the will is free and start asking what the concept is for, but I can't get it into focus myself.
