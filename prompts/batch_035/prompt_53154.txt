Novelist here, fifteen years of crime fiction, and I've talked myself into a philosophical hole I can't write my way out of. It's Camus's fault, sort of — I read his stuff on the death penalty ages ago and what stuck was the bit about his father going to watch a public execution in Algiers as a young man and coming home physically sick, never the same again. And the general shape of the argument, which as I remember it goes something like: you can maybe kill someone who is fighting you, but once he's beaten and chained up and waiting in a cell, killing him is a different thing entirely, and no punishment can justify it. Don't quote me on any of that, the book's in a box somewhere.

Anyway, I always assumed I was an abolitionist like him. Then I started testing my gut on cases for the novel I'm working on and it turns out my gut is a mess. Here's what it says:

A resistance tribunal in an occupied country executes an informer whose tips got four people shot. Dark basement, twenty minutes, no appeal. Gut says: wrong, but not horrifying — closer to the okay side than I'd like to admit. The same informer, but captured and held two years, then executed by the legitimate new state after a full trial with layers of appeals. Gut says: this is somehow WORSE, even though they took more care. A serial killer of children, convicted beyond doubt, painless execution. Gut says: I don't feel the horror I'm supposed to feel, and honestly I sort of shrug. The victim's father shooting the killer on the courthouse steps: wrong, but I'd want the judge to go easy on him. A sniper killing an enemy officer mid-war: fine, obviously. A botched, painful execution feels much worse than a clean one — but if the clean one is already wrong, why does the botched one feel worse than an ordinary painful murder, which is what it literally is?

Is there a principle that captures all this? I keep circling something like Camus's supposed line — combat okay, executing the helpless never — but my serial killer verdict strains it, and my resistance-tribunal verdict strains it more. So either the principle needs repair or some of my verdicts have to go, and I can't tell which. Also tell me what the repaired principle says about a case I haven't listed: a drone operator killing a target who could have been arrested that afternoon. And be honest about which of my verdicts you'd overturn, because I suspect the shrugging one is doing a lot of damage.
