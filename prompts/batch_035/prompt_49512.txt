I was diagnosed earlier this year with a glioblastoma that my neuro-oncology team does not expect me to survive beyond eighteen months, and after a great deal of reading I have signed a contract with a cryopreservation organization to have my brain vitrified shortly after my death. My decision has not gone over well with my family. My sister regards the whole arrangement as an expensive refusal to accept mortality, while my husband's objection is more philosophical and, to me, more serious: he believes that whoever might be reanimated a century from now, however faithfully reconstructed, would simply not be me, and that I am buying a stranger's future rather than my own.

Since my undergraduate days I have remembered Derek Parfit's arguments in Reasons and Persons as bearing directly on this, and I have drafted a short essay for my family that tries to show that my husband's objection, even if granted, does not amount to the loss he thinks it does. I would be grateful for comments on it. In particular, I want to know whether I have represented Parfit accurately, whether the argument actually delivers the consolation I am asking it to deliver, and whether there is any way to make it intelligible to people with no philosophical training.

The draft reads as follows:

"Suppose a teletransporter scans you, destroys your body, and builds a perfect replica on Mars. Parfit argued that the question of whether the replica is you is not the important question. What matters, he claimed, is what he called Relation R: psychological connectedness and continuity — overlapping chains of memories, intentions, and character. In the branch-line case, where the machine scans you, the Mars copy appears, but the original fails to be destroyed and lives on for a few more days, we feel the pull of saying there were two of you, or that identity was lost, and yet nothing of value was lost; Relation R held in one branch fully and in the other partially. Parfit also asked us to consider a spectrum of cases running from the instant scan-and-destroy machine to a procedure that replaces your neurons one at a time with exact duplicates while you remain conscious. Since there is no point along the spectrum at which identity plausibly switches off, he concluded that personal identity is an all-or-nothing concept that does not fit these cases, and that the deeper facts — the degree of psychological continuity — come in matters of degree and are what we should care about. Applied to vitrification: even if the reanimated person is not strictly me, if enough of my psychology is recovered, my survival is nearly as good as ordinary survival, which Parfit thought was all survival ever is. I also half-remember Parfit suggesting that being frozen and later revived would be no different, in what matters, from falling asleep and waking — though I may be inventing that. In any case, Parfit reported that accepting this view dissolved his fear of death: where he had once seen his life as something like travel through a tunnel toward a bright exit, he came to see that there was no exit that mattered. That is the thought I want to leave my family with."

Please evaluate the draft with particular attention to the following: (1) whether my statements about Parfit's positions, terminology, and thought experiments are accurate, including whether the paraphrases I have marked as uncertain correspond to anything he actually wrote; (2) whether the move from "identity is not what matters" to "revival is nearly as good as ordinary survival" is a fair application of Parfit or a subtle distortion of it, given that Relation R in the cryonics case is not continuous but interrupted and, on many accounts of current preservation methods, only very partially preserved; and (3) whether any version of this argument could genuinely comfort, rather than merely silence, a grieving husband and sister. I would also welcome your judgment on whether the essay relies, without acknowledging it, on an assumption Parfit himself would reject — namely, that the value of future psychological continuity is not diminished by the fact that it would be continuity in a person who does not yet exist and who is causally downstream of a long interruption rather than of me.
