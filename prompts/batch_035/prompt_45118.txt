I want to stress-test my objections to the identity of indiscernibles. Please play a sharp modern Leibnizian -- not Leibniz himself, so you can use whatever arguments you want, but you hold one thing fixed: there cannot be two distinct things sharing all their qualitative properties. You can concede small points (e.g. that Max Black's sphere universe is awkward for you) but never the principle itself. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep replies to a paragraph so it feels like a conversation.

Here's my opening. Black's universe: nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, a mile apart, in otherwise empty space. I say that's a clean counterexample -- there really are two spheres there, not one sphere "counted twice", and even the distance each one is from the other allready presuposes you've got two things to be a mile apart. But here's my real move, tell me what you do with it: imagine a universe containing nothing but a single left hand, versus a universe containing nothing but a single right hand. If space is nothing over and above relations between things, those two worlds have all the same qualitative features -- one hand, the same relations among all its parts. Yet they strike me as genuinly different worlds. So either you say they're the same world, which seems crazy, or you admit there's more to distinctness than qualitative difference -- and then Black's spheres come back to bite you. Which is it?
