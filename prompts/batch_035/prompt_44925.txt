Over dinner recently, two friends of mine — Dana, a criminal defense attorney, and Marcus, an emergency-room physician — spent an hour arguing about something my cousin did, and I want an outside adjudication because the later stages of the argument outran my ability to referee.

The facts, as both accepted them: my cousin, running late for a flight, overtook another car on a blind curve at well above a safe speed. Nothing happened. He made the flight, and no one would ever have known about the risk had he not told the story himself.

(1) Dana opened by saying that whatever my cousin did wrong was done entirely in the moment of the overtaking: his culpability was fixed then and there, and the outcome could add harm but not guilt. Nobody becomes more blameworthy by events outside their control; a gust of wind and a child on that curve would have changed the tragedy, not the wrongness.

(2) Marcus replied that our entire practice of holding people responsible contradicts her: we punish completed harms more than near misses, we treat the driver who kills a child not merely as someone who caused a catastrophe but as someone worse than an identical lucky driver, and Dana's position implies that our considered judgments are all one big error. Dana answered that this institutional fact proves nothing about desert — sentences track realized harm partly because harm is provable in court, partly because it needs deterring, and the practice may be an administratively necessary distortion rather than a window into true blameworthiness.

(3) Marcus then argued that Dana's own control principle, pressed hard, destroys itself: even the choice to overtake had roots — temperament, impatience, a lifetime of habits — that my cousin never chose, so if control is the measure of blameworthiness, nothing at all is blameworthy, and the principle cannot mark the line she wants it to mark. Dana replied that she never claimed responsibility requires control over one's whole character; it requires only that the immediate choice flow from his own values and responsiveness to reasons. Where those values came from is a separate and luckier story, and blame can fairly stop there.

(4) Marcus said this is where her position quietly collapses: if the values themselves are lucky, then the difference between my cousin and the actual killer is still nothing but luck — merely relocated from the outcome to the character. Dana has not restored control; she has moved the luck somewhere less visible. Dana insisted that the relocation matters: the unlucky driver's harm flows through his choice in a way that answers to him, and a person's values are the proper seat of responsibility in a way the position of a pedestrian on a curve plainly is not.

(5) Finally, Marcus conceded that blame might not fit the lucky driver, but proposed that my cousin still owes something — that his escape calls for a moral remainder: an obligation of gratitude expressed as lasting change, perhaps a self-imposed debt to compensate the world for the risk he imposed on it. Dana called this an invented obligation, a commendable but strictly optional virtue, and accused Marcus of manufacturing a duty to rescue his intuitions from the earlier round.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say who is right and why; identify what, if anything, both parties are missing; and say who is more right overall.
