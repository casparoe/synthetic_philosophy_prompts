Here's what I want the analysis to do, and what I mean by "machinery." Arendt's famous distinction, roughly, is between the old-fashioned liar -- someone who knows the truth and hides it, like a counterfeiter who knows the real currency -- and the modern organized image-maker, whose real product is a substitute reality. Her unsettling claim is that the second kind is more dangerous, and not for the reason people assume. The danger isn't that the public gets fooled; it's what repeated substitution of image for fact does to our capacity to orient ourselves at all. In "Truth and Politics" she puts it like this: "It has frequently been noticed that the surest long-term result of brainwashing is a peculiar kind of cynicism—an absolute refusal to believe in the truth of anything, no matter how well this truth may be established."

So here are the pieces I actually care about, and I'll warn you upfront that I'm less interested in the standard "Arendt predicted post-truth" take, which I've read a hundred times and think is mostly cheap.

First, the two-kinds question. Iraq looks on its face like old-fashioned lying -- specific false claims, aluminum tubes and the rest, sold to Congress and the UN. But a lot of it also had the texture of image-making: the constant "mushroom cloud" imagery, the overall picture of a looming threat that survived the collapse of any particular claim. Which of Arendt's two liars is this, really? Or is the interesting answer that it's a hybrid, and if so, does her framework handle hybrids or does it start creaking?

Second -- and this is the part I keep chewing on -- the self-deception wrinkle. What came out later suggested that many of the people pushing the claims had, to a degree, talked themselves into them; the intelligence was politicized but the polticians were also in a strange way consumers of it. Arendt's problem-solvers in the Pentagon Papers essay were like that, weren't they? They believed their own numbers because they'd built a world out of them. Does that change the moral picture she's drawing? Is it even "lying" anymore, by her lights?

Third, the aftermath as a test of the brainwashing passage I quoted above. The WMD story collapsed pretty quickly and very publicly -- inspectors went in, nothing was there, everyone knew it. By a simple model the lie failed. But notice what people actually took from the failure: not "that was a lie" so much as a generalized rot, a permanent shrug about whatever the government or the intelligence community or the press says next. That looks almost eerily like what she predicted. But then again, no one important suffered any consequences, and the machinery kept running. Where does that fit? Her picture relies on facts being stubborn, recalcitrant things that eventually push back -- what does it mean that they pushed back and it didn't seem to matter?
