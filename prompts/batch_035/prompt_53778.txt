My supervisor sent back the chapter on individual consumers and factory farming with a single comment in the margin: this argument proves far too much. I have gone through it a dozen times and cannot find the fault, and what makes it worse is that the argument is not really about meat at all — it is about thresholds, and it seems to generalize to everything. Let me lay out the steps and ask you to find where it breaks.

(1) If my buying a chicken at the supermarket is wrong, its wrongness lies in its effects: either the purchase makes some difference to how many chickens are raised and killed, or nothing wrong-making occurs at all. I am, or was, a thoroughgoing consequentialist about this, so I regard (1) as non-negotiable in spirit, though I suppose I could pay to weaken it.

(2) Production does not respond smoothly to demand. Birds are raised, transported, and processed in batches; farms restock on a months-long cycle; retailers order in discrete units and hold inventory to absorb ordinary day-to-day fluctuation. A purchase that lands anywhere in the slack between these steps changes nothing about how many birds are raised and killed. Only the single purchase that lands exactly on a reorder threshold triggers a change in production.

(3) Given the volume moving through any large retailer, the probability that my particular purchase is the one that lands on a threshold is vanishingly small — the same order of smallness as the chance of casting the decisive vote in a large national election. Call it effectively zero.

(4) An act that would make a difference only with effectively-zero probability makes no difference, full stop. A difference I bring about with probability one in a million is not, for the purposes of what I have done, a difference I bring about.

(5) So my purchase makes no difference to how many chickens are raised and killed, and by (1) it is not wrong.

(6) The reasoning applies identically to every individual purchase, since no consumer is relevantly special.

(7) So not one of the billions of purchases composing industrial animal agriculture makes any difference to how many animals are raised and killed.

(8) If no individual contribution to a practice makes any difference, then the wrongness of the practice, however real, cannot be decomposed into wrong-making contributions by individuals — there is nothing there to decompose.

(9) Therefore no individual consumer has ever acted wrongly, even pro tanto, by contributing to industrial animal agriculture; and by parity of reasoning, no one has ever done anything wrong by contributing to any large-scale collective harm — every flight, every vote in a mass election, every purchase from every objectionable supply chain. The harms are real, but they are harms that no one's act helps bring about.

I accept that (9) is unbearable, and I can see that the conclusion is what my supervisor meant by proving too much: it does not just excuse my chicken, it dissolves the entire category of individual contribution to collective harms. What I cannot see is which step gives way. Each of (2) through (6) still looks fine to me in isolation, and the slide from (5) to (7) looks like nothing more than the generality I already granted in (6). For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (2), which seems like plain empirical common sense about how production works, and the falsity of (9). Tell me exactly which step or transition to give up, what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the argument, and — the part I need for the revision — why the fault was invisible to me: what is it about the move, wherever you locate it, that the eye slides over so easily?
