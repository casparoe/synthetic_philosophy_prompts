My city council has just announced that it will convene a citizens' assembly on housing policy: roughly a hundred residents will be chosen by lottery rather than election, hear from experts, deliberate over several weekends, and issue recommendations that the council has promised to debate. I was initially enthusiastic, but reading the recruitment documents I noticed two design choices that have been puzzling me ever since, and before I decide whether to respond to the invitation letter, I want to understand what the whole scheme actually assumes. First, the draw is not a pure lottery: after randomly selecting names from the civic register, the organizers will adjust the pool so that it mirrors the city on gender, age band, income, education level, and district — they call this a stratified sample. Second, everyone drawn is free to decline, and a substantial share apparently do, so the final membership consists of lottery winners who answered a letter and agreed to give up their weekends, with the strata refilled by redrawing until the quotas are met.

My confusion is this: the two choices seem to presuppose different and possibly incompatible pictures of what the assembly is for. The stratification suggests a descriptive, statistical notion of representation — the group is legitimate because it is a miniature of the population. But the lottery itself is usually defended on other grounds: that it gives each citizen an equal chance at political power, or that it insulates the body from the self-selection of career politicians. And the opt-in layer seems to undercut both rationales at once: a self-selected sample of lottery winners is neither a faithful microcosm (the people who decline are surely not randomly distributed — think of the busiest, the least trusting, those who move often) nor an equal-chance draw in any meaningful sense. Meanwhile, the organizers choose which experts brief the assembly, frame the question it answers, and decide how recommendations are worded.

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory under which the whole package — stratification plus lottery plus voluntariness plus expert facilitation — makes sense? Is it descriptive representation, lottery-based fairness, an epistemic story about the value of diverse perspectives, or some claim about legitimacy flowing from an impartial procedure? In your answer, I'd like you to distinguish which parts of the practice are strictly required by that theory, which merely fit comfortably with it, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of representation would license it equally well. And finally: if a city took the descriptive-representation rationale fully seriously — microcosm above all — what concretely would have to change? Compulsory service on the model of jury duty? Pay levels that make refusal costlier? Or does the voluntariness protect a value that a pure microcosm would sacrifice?
