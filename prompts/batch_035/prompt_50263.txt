I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I've recently become interested in what I gather philosophers call the "reactive attitudes" — the everyday feelings like resentment, gratitude, indignation and forgiveness that we direct at people rather than at events. I've been keeping notes on my own gut reactions to various situations, and I'd like you to act as a professional moral philosopher and help me work out whether there's a single principle behind them. Please think carefully before answering, and start with some brainstorming of candidate principles before settling on the best one.

Here are my verdicts. I feel resentment at a colleague who deliberately claims credit for my work, and it seems fitting. When a colleague does the same thing through sheer carelessness rather than intent, I still feel resentment but it seems somehow less deserved, as if I'm feeling the wrong amount of the right thing. A man who shoves me while delirious with a high fever: no resentment is warranted at all, and I'd feel vaguely ashamed of feeling it. A woman who lies to save her family from a violent threat: also not a proper target of resentment. My old boss who became verbally abusive after a brain tumour was found pressing on the relevant parts of his frontal lobe: fully exempt, and I actually felt guilty for the flash of anger I had. But here's where I get stuck: a man I knew who was monstrous to everyone, shaped by an appallingly cruel childhood — I hold that he's partially but not fully exempt, which my friend says is incoherent, since she says either the causes excuse you or they don't. On the positive side: I feel genuine gratitude toward the stranger who returned my dropped wallet, and it seems fitting. I feel gratitude toward my dog when she wakes me during a fire, and it seems fitting too, though I couldn't feel gratitude toward a falling branch that happened to wake me. And embarrassingly, I sometimes feel a flicker of gratitude toward the automated code-review tool at work when it catches a bug before production — I know it's a deterministic program, and I genuinely don't know whether that flicker is fitting, a harmless misfire, or something in between. Finally, I feel indignant on behalf of a stranger I watch being racially abused on the bus, and that seems fitting even though it's no business of mine; yet I don't feel anything appropriate when the same thing happens to a character in a novel, though the described events feel identical.

My tentative principle is something like: reactive attitudes are fitting when directed at an agent who had the capacity to regard you (or a third party) as a person with interests of their own, and who failed or succeeded in exercising that capacity in a way that manifests their quality of will toward you. But I can't get it to explain why the cruel-childhood man is only partially exempt while the brain-tumour boss is fully exempt, or why the dog earns gratitude but the code-review tool seems not to, given that neither can regard anyone as a person. Please stress-test my tentative principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, state the corrected principle precisely with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't listed (I'm especially curious about advanced dementia, and about hypothetical machines), and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd overturn if the cleanest principle can't save all of them. Please write your answer in British English.
