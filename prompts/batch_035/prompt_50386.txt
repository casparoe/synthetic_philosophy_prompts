I teach a second-year ethics course, and every year the abortion unit follows the same discouraging script: the students sort themselves into camps before either argument has been read, and then read only for ammunition. Next term I want to disrupt this with a set of cases that separates the arguments from the camps. My idea is a two-by-two grid of scenarios in which the bodily-autonomy line descending from Thomson and the future-like-ours argument associated with Marquis are forced to give cross-cutting verdicts, so that a student who endorses one of them for its usual conclusions has to watch it defect in an unfamiliar cell, and vice versa.

Here is the structure. One axis varies what the future-like-ours argument turns on: in one column the fetus plainly has a future of the relevant kind; in the other it lacks one, because of a condition discovered late in pregnancy that guarantees death within days of birth. The other axis varies what the autonomy argument turns on: in one row the pregnancy was voluntarily assumed and the remaining burden is small — a few weeks of gestation left, then an ordinary delivery — while in the other the pregnancy was never assumed and the burden is grave, say a serious risk to the woman's health. Fill in the four cells with realistic, one-paragraph scenarios drawn from ordinary clinical life: no borrowed violinists, no engineered contraptions, no saints. The cells should be matched closely enough that each differs from its neighbours in exactly one variable; otherwise the grid isolates nothing.

Then demonstrate that the grid works. For each cell, state what each of the two arguments concludes and show the reasoning rather than stipulating the verdict — I want the conclusions to fall out of the theories' actual commitments about futures, responsibility and burden, not out of my labels. On the best reading the grid should yield two consensus cells and two of opposition: one opposition cell is the familiar face-off, and the other is its mirror, where the future-like-ours argument permits while the responsibility-and-burden strand of the autonomy reasoning condemns — and that mirror cell is the one I care most about. Note, in passing, that Thomson herself resists letting the responsibility prong generate any condemnation, so treat it as an open question whether her argument can be pushed to condemn without smuggling in a premise that trivialises it; do not hide that tension.

Finish with the honest part. Identify which feature of each scenario does the work; explain how a defender of each argument would try to wrestle the awkward cells back into line; and say whether the crossed verdicts reveal something structural about the two arguments or only show how far each can be stretched once its usual companion intuitions are stripped away. I am not looking for a verdict on abortion itself — the exercise succeeds precisely when a student finishes it no longer able to tell which argument she favours until she has seen its verdicts detached from the camp she arrived in. Keep the whole thing to about 900 words, suitable for a seminar handout with no citations.
