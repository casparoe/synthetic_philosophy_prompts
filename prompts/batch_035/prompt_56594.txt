So we covered the absent-minded driver problem in class this week — the one where the driver goes through two identical intersections and can't tell which one he's at, exiting at the first gives him 0, exiting at the second gives 1, and continuing through both gets him home for 4. The answer is supposed to be: continue at each intersection with probability 2/3, expected payoff 8/3.

Here's what bugs me though. Everyone talks about this thing like it's some deep paradox that's supposed to threaten the foundations of decision theory, and I honestly don't see it. To me the lesson is just: when you don't know which situation you're in, committing to the same action every time is bad, so mix it up — basically the same reason you randomize in matching pennies. The driver is confused about where he is, randomizing hedges against the confusion, done. Why do people treat it like an attack on rational choice itself instead of just one more case where a mixed strategy happens to be optimal? Like is there actually a version of this where a rational agent can't pick a strategy at all, or is the "paradox" just homework dressing?

Also a smaller thing I keep wondering about: if randomizing is the answer, why does everyone picture him flipping a coin *at* the intersection? Couldn't he just flip once before he starts driving, at home, and precommit to "if heads, I go straight through everything"? That seems way more realistic than somehow drawing a fresh independent random number at an intersection he can't even recognize. Does setting it up that way change the answer at all, or is it just the same thing written differently?
