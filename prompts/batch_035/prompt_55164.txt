I am a novelist, and for eleven years I have watched two of my oldest friends fail to settle a question that touches, though they pretend otherwise, on everything I do for a living. One of them, a retired museum curator turned philosopher, holds that what makes something a work of art is the standing it acquires within the institutions and practices of the art world — the conferral of status, not the possession of intrinsic features. The other, an aesthetician of an older temperament, holds that art is essentially whatever is capable of affording a certain kind of valued experience to a suitably prepared audience, and that the institutions merely track, however fallibly, this experiential fact. Each thinks the other's position abolishes something essential: one accuses the other of making art depend on the arbitrary breath of gatekeepers, and is accused in return of making it depend on a mysterious reverie no two readers share. Last month, over a dinner that ended more coolly than it began, they agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and — because they trust me to be partial to neither of them, and because I can be relied upon to write things down accurately — they have asked me to serve as scribe for the joint statement.

My qualification is not neutrality of opinion, which I lack, but a certain division of sympathies. My trade supplies me with material for both camps, and I confess that I do not know which side my own practice supports. Consider three cases I intend to put before them; I want the joint statement to confront all three.

First, my drawer novel: in my late twenties I wrote a novel that I have never shown to anyone, and my will instructs my executor to burn it unread. I believe it is good — I believed it when I wrote it — but no audience will ever verify this, and no institution will ever touch it. Is it a work of art now? Was it ever? I notice I cannot answer without feeling that I am betraying one friend or the other.

Second, the unread manuscript: suppose a prestigious publishing house, as a provocation, announces that it will print, unedited and unread by any human being, whatever emerges from a stochastic text generator operated under a famous name, and suppose that when sincere readers eventually encounter the printed object, some of them are genuinely moved by it. Was the thing art at the typescript stage, before a single experience had been had? Did it become art only in the reading? Each of my friends, I suspect, will want to date the artwork's existence differently, and I want their reasoning exposed, not papered over.

Third, my early failure: suppose my publisher reissues a novel of mine that died on publication, unchanged except for a new title and a preface declaring it to have been, all along, a deliberate satire of its own genre. Critics now receive it as satire and praise it. Has the artwork changed when the text has not? And note the trap: the preface itself becomes part of the presented work, so the experience on offer changes too. I want to know whether my two friends can jointly acknowledge that trap, or whether it will divide them.

So here is my request. Take the role of a professional philosopher of art, senior enough to have drafted consensus documents for working groups, and to know exactly how hard it is to find wording that committed opponents will both sign. Before writing anything, think carefully and brainstorm privately: generate the candidate formulations of agreement and disagreement, and discard the ones that are vacuous (so hedged that neither party is committed to anything) or slanted (so phrased that one side is quietly made to concede). Then draft the joint statement itself, structured as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims of substance that both parties can sign, phrased so that neither is merely being polite.
2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the institutionalist would accept for his own view, once in wording the functionalist would accept for hers — together with a note on what, concretely, turns on the disagreement.
3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and, for each crux, the case or argument both parties agree would bear on it, with each party's prediction written down in advance.
4. A final section applying all of the above to my three cases — the drawer novel, the unread manuscript, the reissued satire — stating, for each, what each party concludes and whether they consider the question determinate, and, if any part of their dispute strikes the drafter as merely verbal, saying which part and why.

Two constraints on your craft. You may gesture toward the general traditions here — the institutional line of thought associated with George Dickie, the experiential line with Monroe Beardsley, the old problem of perceptually indistinguishable twins in the gallery made famous by Arthur Danto — but do not attribute specific claims, page numbers, or quotations to specific texts unless you are certain of them; a fabricated citation would discredit the document with both signatories. And resist the temptation to adjudicate: your job is to make the disagreement exact, not to win it. If, however, one of my three cases strikes you as one on which both parties should in honesty be able to converge, say so — but earn the convergence rather than assuming it, and show the reasoning that would get each of them there. I will be reading the document aloud to them both, and I will know if any sentence was written for the file rather than for the room.
