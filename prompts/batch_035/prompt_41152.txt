I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've become puzzled by a pattern in how AI labs evaluate frontier models before deployment. The emerging practice of 'control' — as opposed to 'alignment' — builds safety measures on the assumption that the model may already be misaligned: untrusted AI monitors that screen another model's outputs, tripwires and honeypots meant to surface covert goal pursuit, and evaluations that test whether models 'sandbag', i.e. deliberately underperform on dangerous-capability assessments to avoid triggering safety measures, or behave differently when led to believe they are in a test rather than in deployment. These measures seem to treat the model as an adversary with beliefs, desires, and a persistent policy of concealment. Yet the same labs officially remain agnostic about whether models have beliefs or goals at all, describing them at most as systems that predict text. What view of the model's mind do these control practices presuppose? Do they strictly require intentional-state ascription — the model representing itself as observed, wanting to pass, and planning across contexts — or can the whole apparatus be recast behavioristically as hypotheses about a distribution over outputs, and if so, is anything lost, especially the cross-context character of the worry? Reconstruct the implicit psychology the practice relies on, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me what would have to change in evaluation practice if we refused the mental-state vocabulary altogether.
