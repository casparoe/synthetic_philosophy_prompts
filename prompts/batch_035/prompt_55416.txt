I'm a science journalist who has spent the last year reporting on origin-of-life chemistry and on the recurring fight over whether viruses count as living, and I've come out of it with a set of positions I'd like stress-tested before I bake them into a long piece I'm drafting. (1) Life is a real natural kind, not a human filing convenience, and any adequate definition must be non-parochial: a shadow biosphere running on unfamiliar biochemistry, or a genuinely alien organism, would be as much alive as we are. (2) The best working definition going is the one long used in NASA's astrobiology program — a self-sustaining system capable of Darwinian evolution — and I think it is on the right track. (3) Viruses are not alive, and I take this to be a discovery about viruses rather than a bookkeeping decision. (4) Sterile animals such as mules and worker bees are unambiguously alive, and so are deep-frozen spores that have sat metabolically inert for tens of thousands of years. (5) The origin of life on Earth was a gradual chemical process with no first organism, and any correct definition will leave a grey zone of borderline prebiotic systems — which I am at peace with. Please audit this package: which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe is each real tension? For each one, tell me what the cheapest fix is and what it would cost me, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most.
