Write a joke about Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that a rational agent who maximizes expected utility can never be worse off, in expectation, from observing the outcome of a cost-free experiment before acting. The humor should turn on the theorem's actual content rather than on a generic quip about information being power; someone familiar with the theorem should be able to see why the joke is specifically about Good's theorem and not about information in general. You are encouraged to exploit the theorem's fine print — for instance, that the information must be free, that probabilities are updated by conditioning on what is learned, and that the agent must actually be prepared to change course in light of the news — or to set the joke in a concrete decision problem, such as a physician deciding whether to treat before or after a diagnostic result comes back. The joke should make sense, be recognizably about the theorem, and at least attempt to be genuinely funny to someone who knows decision theory.
