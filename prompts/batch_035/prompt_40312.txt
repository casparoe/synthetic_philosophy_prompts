My friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about Mencius and Xunzi, and somewhere in the fourth hour she started issuing verdicts faster than I could object to them, so I began taking them down. The question before her each time was whether a moral response is inborn or the product of training, and her track record so far reads as follows: a toddler who has never been told to, patting a crying playmate and looking distressed — inborn; a young child insisting that a promise be kept even when keeping it costs the child something — inborn; a child feeling wretched over a lie that nobody discovered — inborn; formal bowing and table manners — trained; modesty about the body — trained; queuing — trained. She left the argument claiming the Xunzian side, while I pressed the Mencian line, but I have begun to suspect her verdicts are running on some rule of their own, and I would like to know which.

Three new cases are coming up. First, a child who has never been taught anything about dirt or disease, who is revolted by a dead bird, refuses to go near it, and whose revulsion persists although no adult ever says a word about it. Second, two siblings who close ranks against a neighbouring child who has done nothing to them, covering for each other's mischief, though nobody has ever instructed them in loyalty. Third, an adult moved to tears by a stranger's grief who then walks past without helping. For each case, please predict whether she will rule inborn or trained. Then tell me which rules fit her track record equally well, which of the three cases would best tell those rules apart, and if the three together leave the matter open, what further case would settle it. I am less interested in the predictions themselves than in the rules she seems implicitly to be applying, and in where those rules quietly part company from the schools she claims to favour.

Please answer in American English.
