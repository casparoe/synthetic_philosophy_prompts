I'm taking a philosophy of history seminar this term, and our current unit is on whether historical explanation can ever be genuinely scientific. In office hours my professor brought up cliodynamics and Peter Turchin's work on secular cycles, so I went and listened to a podcast episode about him afterward. The host summarized the whole program roughly like this:

"Turchin was a physicist who got bored of physics and decided that history just needed the right equations. His breakthrough book, War and Peace and War, came out of his mining a huge global database of past societies, and it proved that civilizations run on clockwork: every agrarian state rises and falls on an exact fifty-year cycle, driven by resource depletion — the land and food run out, the peasants starve, and the whole thing collapses. He predicted the unrest of 2020 a decade in advance, which showed the method works, and now that the Seshat databank is up and running his equations can forecast specific future events — which wars will break out, who will win elections, even the year of the next civil war — the way meteorology forecasts the weather. Critics have pointed out that you can always fit cycles onto the past after the fact, so the theory has essentially been refuted."

I'm inclined to think there's something to the cycles idea, but the refutation at the end sounded too quick for something my professor seems to take seriously. How much of this summary is right? Please go claim by claim and tell me what a cliodynamics researcher would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. In particular, I'd like to know whether the fifty-year figure is actually the claim, since my professor spoke as though Turchin works with timescales of quite different lengths and seemed to distinguish cycles of instability from something longer and slower. And separately, does the host's conclusion — that we could soon time the next civil war to the year — follow from any of the parts of the summary that survive correction? If it doesn't, I want to understand exactly where the leap is made, since that leap is the version of cliodynamics most of my classmates now believe in.
