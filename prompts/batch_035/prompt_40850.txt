I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital, where the distinction between what a physician does and what a physician allows does constant work: our policy permits withdrawing a ventilator at a patient's request while forbidding the administration of a lethal dose on the same request. A philosophically ambitious resident has been pressing an argument that this distinction, and the policy resting on it, cannot survive scrutiny, and I have been unable to locate the weak step. The argument leans on the pair of cases James Rachels made famous — Smith drowns his young cousin in the bathtub to secure an inheritance, while Jones, wanting the same inheritance and planning the same act, watches the child slip under on his own and lets him drown — and proceeds as follows:

(1) The deontic status of a piece of conduct is fixed by three things and nothing else: its consequences, the agent's motives, and the pattern of counterfactual dependence between the conduct and those consequences — that is, which outcomes depend on which stretches of the agent's behavior.
(2) In the bathtub pair these are fully matched: the same motive, the same outcome, and in each case the child's life depends on the man's conduct — had Smith not pushed him under, the child would have lived; had Jones pulled him out, the child would have lived.
(3) We judge the two men equally culpable, so nothing is lost by conceding that the difference between killing and letting die carries no intrinsic moral weight.
(4) In the committee's cases the three factors are likewise matched whenever consent, motive, and prognosis are: withdrawal and injection at the same patient's request differ in nothing that (1) counts.
(5) The same parity holds between diverting a runaway trolley from five workers onto a side track where it kills one and pushing a bystander off a footbridge into its path to stop it: the same motive, the same net outcome, and in each case each of the five lives depends on the agent's act, as does the bystander's death.
(6) So withdrawal and injection are on a par, and diverting and pushing are on a par, and anyone who feels a residual difference is feeling bias, since nothing among the surviving features distinguishes the pairs.

When I objected that there is surely a difference between initiating a fatal process and declining to interrupt one, the resident replied that this is just the now-inert doing/allowing distinction relabeled, and that the bathtub pair has already shown that this difference does no work once everything real is held fixed.

Here is my predicament. I am confident of the bathtub verdict in (3): those two men are equally culpable, and I will not give that up. I am equally confident, against (6), that diverting and pushing are not on a par — that contrast feels like knowledge rather than bias. I am, candidly, unsure about the committee's policy: the resident may be right that withdrawal and injection at a patient's request differ in nothing I can defend. What I cannot do is find the false step, because each premise looks right to me when checked one at a time. Name the step I should reject, explain why it looked fine, and tell me what rejecting it costs — including, if you think the argument sound all the way through, which of my two certainties I should surrender and why that one is the cheaper loss.
