i'm writing up my views on disagreement for a little paper section and it occured to me they might not all fit together, so I want an audit before I send it anywhere. here's the package:

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem is right: if two agents start with the same prior and their posteriors on some question become common knowledge — each knows the other's credence, knows the other knows, and so on up — then they can't end up with different credences on that question.

(2) the common-prior assumption is basically harmless in real life. smart honest people raised on the same planet start from roughly the same ignorance, and anyway once they've pooled enough evidence the priors wash out and any leftover differences shrink to nothing.

(3) two well-informed honest people can rationally disagree forever. I know two philosophers who've read all the same books and argued with each other for thirty years, and they're further apart on free will now than when they started. neither is making an epistemic mistake as far as I can tell — they just wieght the considerations differently.

(4) a layperson should defer to expert consensus, but an expert should not defer to the average of the other experts — if you've done the work, you're entitled to your considered view even against the whole field.

(5) my brother keeps saying that if I really believed (1), I'd be happy to bet with him on any question where we've pooled everything we know, and that my refusing to bet (which I do) shows I don't really believe it. I think he's wrong about that but can't quite put my finger on why.

so: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair in each case? rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing them. I especially want to know whether (3) can survive once you've granted (1) and (2) — and if it can't, which of the three has to give. also go easy on (5), I'm fairly sure there's nothing wrong with it, I just want it double-checked.
