I'm putting together a seminar session on self-reference for a philosophy and film course, and I want to pair Douglas Hofstadter's ideas about tangled hierarchies with Charlie Kaufman's Synecdoche, New York (2008). To fix the vocabulary: in Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), and later in I Am a Strange Loop (2007), Hofstadter is fascinated by tangled hierarchies — systems in which each level seems to create or govern a level that is supposedly beyond it, as in Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands, where each hand is drawing the other. His claim is that every real tangled hierarchy is anchored in an inviolate level: something outside the loop that the loop cannot reach back and redraw. Escher's hands, considered as a print, were drawn by Escher, at a level the picture cannot touch; a formal system that talks about itself owes its meaning to mathematicians it cannot formalize. In the later book he goes further and argues that the human "I" is itself a strange loop — a self-referential pattern the brain spins about itself. What I want to know is whether Kaufman's film is a demonstration of that thesis or a quietly devastating refutation of it.

For anyone who needs the premise: Caden Cotard (Philip Seymour Hoffman) is a theatre director in Schenectady — the title is a pun on the city — who, after his marriage to the painter Adele falls apart (her celebrated canvases are tiny, yet uncannily detailed) and a MacArthur grant lands in his lap, moves to Manhattan and spends the rest of his life building his masterwork: inside a vast warehouse he constructs a life-size replica of New York, populated by actors hired to play himself and everyone he has ever known. A replica of the warehouse itself eventually appears within it. Sammy, the actor assigned to Caden, studies him for decades and comes to understand him better than Caden understands himself. The production, decades in the making, never opens, and the film ends with an aged Caden wandering the decaying set while a voice in his earpiece directs his final moments — the last direction being, in effect, that he die.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind who is also a serious film critic, and think carefully before writing; in particular, begin by sorting out what the inviolate level of Caden's project could be, before applying the concept. My core questions: Does the film depict a genuine tangled hierarchy, and does anything in it remain inviolate? The obvious candidates — Caden's body (his name can't be an accident: it evokes Cotard's delusion, the belief that one is already dead), his death, the audience — all seem to get absorbed into the production; even dying is directed like a scene. Is that the film's horror: a strange loop with no anchoring level, so that what results is not a self, as Hofstadter would have it, but a regress? Relatedly, is Sammy the self-model that outruns the self, and what does that suggest about the later Hofstadter's claim? Finally, be honest about where the film resists the reading: a replica is a copy, not a representation, and a director casting actors is a hierarchy but arguably not self-reference — Hofstadter's loops are loops of representation and compression, and it is fair to ask whether Kaufman's warehouse is a strange loop or merely an infinite regress with better production values. Do not retell the plot at length, do not quote dialogue from memory, and do not invent scenes; the premise above is the film for our purposes. I'd like the reading to end by saying what the film gives us that the argument alone cannot — what it feels like, from inside, to be a loop that cannot find its outside.
