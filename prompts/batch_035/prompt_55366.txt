ok so i'm writing this thing for my school's philosophy club, sort of a mini essay, and i want to figure out the following. basically the whole college app system makes you commit to a life direction at 17: you declare a major, you write essays about your "future plans," the career counselor gives you aptitude tests and tells you to picture where you see yourself in ten years, everyone says follow your passion, etc. but i keep coming back to this idea i read about (i think it was a philosophy book my cousin had) that you can't actually know what being a parent is like until you are one, because the experience changes your preferences so much that the pre-parent version of you can't simulate the post-parent version. and if thats true for parenthood it seems like it would be true for a ton of jobs too? like i can shadow a nurse for a day, but that's not the same as actually being one for years, because the job itself would change what i want and who i even am. so the thing i want you to work out is: what does the whole system (school, college apps, career counseling) actually presuppose about decision making? does it presuppose you can somehow know in advance what a job will be like for you, or just that its acceptable to gamble on big life choices with basically no information? and can you tell whether the practice strictly needs that presupposition, or whether several different theories of choice would license the same practice anyway. here's the part that confuses me most: the same adults who make us commit early all reassure us with "don't worry, you can always switch majors later," but switching later costs a bunch of money and time, so the friendly advice and the actual incentives point in different directions. also i want you to address whether the standard fix actually works — like the "just try stuff before you commit" advice, gap years and internships and all that. does a two-week internship solve the problem, or is it just the same problem one level down, since you still can't know what the full experience is like until you've lived through the whole thing? and finally, what would the system have to look like if the can't-simulate-your-future-self idea were taken totally seriously? like would colleges have to be built completly differently, or would we just have to admit the whole thing is a lottery and stop pretending otherwise. i'd like an actual answer to the presupposition question, not just vibes, but keep it in language a high schooler can follow.
