I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and this is about my tech lead, Marcus, whom I've worked beside for six years. Over the years I've collected his opinions about trade-offs between now and later, because they don't seem to add up to any single picture I can name. Next month, three decisions land on him at once, and I want a prediction before I watch it play out.

His track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. At our previous employer, the deferred-compensation election let you take your bonus now or 15% more twelve months later, with the deferred portion held in escrow at a bank. He took it now, and his stated reason was essentially that he can see it now.

2. Yet last year he moved $20,000 of personal savings into a government-backed, inflation-indexed instrument that pays out roughly $50,000 twenty years from now, and he described it as obviously smart. The same man, near-total patience at a twenty-year horizon.

3. At work he championed a migration costing about $800k of loaded team time across two quarters to eliminate a drag he estimated at $250k per year starting in year two. When finance proposed discounting the future savings at 10% a year, he pushed back — his line was that money in four years is still money — and the team ended up running undiscounted numbers.

4. But when the proposal was a $1M platform bet now for, on the team's own honest estimate, a coin-flip chance of $8M in savings spread over years six through ten and nothing otherwise, he voted no, on the grounds that the 50% was a vibe and one-shot bets like that deserve a big haircut.

5. In a long argument about climate policy he maintained that a person born in 2150 matters exactly as much as a person born next year — counting someone less purely because of their date struck him as monstrous — while also saying governments are right to use a positive discount rate, because the future may be much richer than we are and we can't be sure the benefits will arrive at all.

The three cases landing next month:

(i) The bank holding his twenty-year instrument offers a mini version: move $100 today, receive a guaranteed $450 in twenty years, same terms. Does he buy it?

(ii) His late aunt's will directs $50,000 either to famine relief spent this year, or into a legally ring-fenced, inflation-indexed fund that releases $600,000 to the same century-old charity's relief work in forty years, with the terms locked so the money can only ever fund that mission. Which does he choose?

(iii) He must schedule the worst project of our lives, two months of pain he will personally lead: start Monday, or start in two years. By his own estimate, starting now prevents about $300k of incident cost in the interval. When does he schedule it?

Here is what I want back, in this order and rough length. First, **Verdicts** (about 100 words): your single best guess on each of the three, bottom line first. Second, **Candidate Rules** (400–500 words): at least three general policies that each fit all five items in the track record, stated precisely enough to generate predictions, along with what each rule predicts for each of the three cases — I expect the rules to disagree somewhere, and I want to see exactly where. Third, **Best Guess** (about 150 words): which rule you'd bet on and why, plus one sentence on whether it amounts to a principled position about time or just an inconsistency he would be embarrassed to have pointed out. I've heard it said that preferring a benefit merely because it arrives sooner is like preferring a benefit merely because it happens to fall on you rather than a stranger, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real distinction or word games, so feel free to use it or not. Fourth, **The Deciding Case** (about 100 words): one further concrete case — a situation I could actually put in front of Marcus, not a question I could ask him — that would best split the rules you found, with a note on what each possible answer would reveal.
