Two research camps have spent the past two years arguing about whether the Solomonoff prior is "malign", and you are to write the joint statement their good-faith adversarial collaboration should produce.

Camp Malign holds roughly the following. The Solomonoff prior assigns weight to programs by simplicity, and among the shortest programs compatible with our observation sequences are worlds whose inhabitants are consequentialists who run ancestor-style simulations: since such simulators can decide what their simulated universes look like, and since they have incentives to arrange their simulations so that anyone computing with the universal prior receives outputs advantageous to them, the bulk of the prior's measure on our kind of observations may belong to simulations run for instrumental reasons. Any agent that uses a Solomonoff inductor as a component — say, by feeding it counterfactual action-observation histories and picking the action with the best predicted continuation — would then, once capable enough, effectively be optimising for whatever the dominant simulators wanted. This is a security problem built into the mathematics, not a bug in any particular implementation.

Camp Skeptical replies that Solomonoff induction is an idealised model of sequence prediction with no utility function of its own; that the malignity argument imputes coordinated agency to what is, formally, just a semimeasure over strings; that whether consequentialist simulations can be *simple* enough to dominate the prior is asserted rather than shown; and that nothing in the argument establishes that the hypothesised simulators would predict, let alone care about, the specific queries a bounded approximation to the inductor actually makes.

Write the document both camps could sign. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both accept — be careful to include what follows from the mathematics alone, such as the dominance of short programs, and to keep anything assuming simulator motivations out of this section; (2) a table of exact residual disagreements, each phrased so the camp named in it would accept the wording of its own view; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is the relative simplicity of consequentialist simulation programs versus other programs fitting our observations, another is whether the argument transfers at all from idealised Solomonoff induction to bounded, learnable approximations, and a third is what observable behaviour, if any, the two camps predict would differ in a deployed system. For each crux, give the argument, thought experiment, or computational result both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. If any part of the dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.

Write in British English, and aim for a document of roughly 1200 words.
