So everyone knows the Popper "paradox of tolerance" line — unlimited tolerance destroys tolerance, so tolerance can't extend to the intolerant. Fewer people remember what Popper actually said in The Open Society: he wanted intolerance to be countered with rational argument first, and only said suppression becomes justified when the intolerant start using fists, i.e. when they stop playing the game of persuading by words. Meanwhile Herbert Marcuse, in his essay on what he called repressive tolerance, went the opposite way: he thought tolerating every viewpoint equally in practice just entrenches the dominant view, so genuine tolerance might require being actively intolerant of the status quo's ideas. Those two positions basically bracket the space.

Now take the Skokie affair in 1977-78: a small Nazi group announced it wanted to march in the Village of Skokie, Illinois, a suburb where a large number of Holocaust survivors lived. The village threw every legal obstacle it could at them — permit rules, an injunction, even an ordinance requiring the marchers to carry insurance they could never get. The ACLU, to its members' horror, took the Nazis' side on free speech grounds, lost something like a quarter or a third of its membership over it, and won in court — the Illinois courts, following Brandenburg-style precedent, said the march couldn't be banned in advance. In the end the Nazis mostly fizzled: the big Skokie march never really happened, they held a small rally elsewhere, counterprotesters outnumbered them, and the whole thing arguably did more to educate people about the Holocaust than anything else that year. (I'm telling you the facts since you can't look them up — you can treat these as given and don't need to add historical color you're unsure of.)

Analyze Skokie through both Popper's paradox (the real version, not the meme) and Marcuse's repressive tolerance. Who counts as the intolerant here, and were they "playing the game" by Popper's standard or already breaking it? The ACLU behaved like the Popperian case doesn't even arise yet — argument meets argument — while the Village behaved like the paradox was already triggered. Which side does the episode actually vindicate, or does it maybe show that Popper's threshold (fists, direct incitement) is set at the wrong place for symbolic speech aimed at a community of survivors? And then run Marcuse through it: from his standpoint the whole "neutral tolerance" framework the ACLU defended was itself the rigged game. Does Skokie support him, or does the fizzled march and the public education effect cut the other way?
