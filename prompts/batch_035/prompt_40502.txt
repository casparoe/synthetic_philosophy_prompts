Two friends in my logic reading group got into a dispute last week over the two-envelope paradox, and I've been asked to write up who won — but I genuinely can't tell. The setup as they told it: one envelope contains twice as much as the other; you may open yours, see some amount X, and then choose to keep it or swap. (1) Ravi said: whatever X you see, the other envelope holds either X/2 or 2X, each with probability one half, so the expected value of swapping is 1.25X — a quarter of your amount in pure profit. Swap. (2) Marta said that can't be right, because the same reasoning would tell you to swap back, and swap again, forever; by symmetry neither envelope can be preferable, so somewhere the argument cheats. (3) Ravi answered that the arithmetic is beyond reproach and that "symmetry" is doing no work: there's no principle guaranteeing that conditional on what you see, the two envelopes are equally good, and symmetry of the original setup doesn't survive conditioning on the observed amount. (4) Marta replied that the equal half-and-half probabilities are exactly the cheat: if the amounts in the envelopes were drawn from some proper distribution over sums of money, then seeing a large amount would be evidence you hold the bigger envelope, the conditional probabilities would tilt away from one half, and when you average over everything correctly, the apparent 25% gain vanishes. (5) Ravi said this imports a distribution the problem never supplied — and, worse, proves too much: he sketched a case where the amounts are powers of two chosen with probabilities shrinking fast enough that, whatever amount you open, the other envelope genuinely has an even chance of holding double; there the swap really is favourable at every turn, so the "paradox" isn't an error at all but a true fact about setups with no finite average. (6) Marta answered that in that setup the expected amounts are infinite, so comparing them by saying one is "larger" is meaningless — decision theory isn't breaking, the quantities have simply stopped being comparable — and that Ravi's example is a curiosity, not a vindication of his original argument about ordinary pairs of envelopes.
