I've gotten pretty deep into bargaining theory lately, mostly for fun, and I've landed on the view that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the right one for the two-person bargaining problem, not Nash's. Quick setup so we're on the same page: you've got a feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and Nash says pick the point maximizing the product of each player's utility gain over the disagreement outcome. KS instead says: find each player's utopia payoff (the best they could get in the whole feasible set), and pick the Pareto-optimal point where both players get the same *fraction* of their utopia gain. The thing that sold me on KS is individual monotonicity: if one player's bargaining position improves (their utopia payoff goes up, feasible set expands in their favor), they shouldn't end up *worse* off — and Nash can actually do that to them, because of independence of irrelevant alternatives. That always struck me as a flat-out unfairness of the Nash solution, and I've come to think IIA is the axiom that deserves to go.

But I want to stress-test this rather than just enjoy it. What would count against the KS view? I know the obvious one — KS gives up IIA, so it can recommend a point that isn't even feasible anymore once you remove alternatives, or make both players worse off when the feasible set shrinks in a way that should be irrelevant — but I want more than the textbook objection. Which arguments should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they are? For instance I've heard people say the Nash solution has better strategic foundations, that it pops out as the outcome of an actual alternating-offers bargaining procedure, while KS is more of a purely axiomatic thing with no story about why rational bargainers would reach it — how bad is that for my view, if bad at all? I also vaguely remember there being some impossibility result about extending KS-style monotonicity to three or more players, which if real would suggest the axioms behind my favorite solution only cohere in the two-person case — does that undercut the position or is it just a technicality?

I'd also like you to poke at the equity rationale itself, since that's the heart of why I prefer KS: if utilities are only defined up to independent rescaling, in what sense are two players getting "the same fraction" of their hopes really being treated equally? Is the fairness intuition doing work that the formalism can't actually cash out? Please distinguish what a committed KS defender would concede as a real cost from what they'd wave away, tell me if there's a version of my view that's been qualified into unfalsifiability, and be honest if you think Nash's solution just wins on the merits — I'd rather find out now than keep believing something because of one nice axiom.
