The question (Question 4, worth 25 points) read as follows: Ian Hacking has argued that classifications of human beings differ in kind from classifications of anything else. (a) State his distinction between interactive and indifferent kinds, with an example of each. (b) Set out what has to happen, and in what order, for a classification of people to exhibit a looping effect — as opposed to merely changing over time — and illustrate with a psychiatric example. (c) Hacking is often read as holding that looping effects show psychiatric classifications are not real. State what you take his considered position on the reality of such classifications to be, and defend your reading.

The student's answer, verbatim:

(a) Hacking divides kinds into interactive kinds and indifferent kinds. An indifferent kind is a classification whose instances are in no way changed by being classified; the natural sciences are full of them — quarks and chemical elements do not react to our sorting of them. An interactive kind is a classification of people which, when applied, alters the people it picks out. His central example is multiple personality: once the DSM-III gave the condition official criteria in 1980, diagnoses multiplied, and patients increasingly presented in the shape of the published criteria, so that the category and the people classified kept remaking each other.

(b) A looping effect has a definite order. First, the classification is applied to a population and becomes part of the institutions surrounding them — clinics, records, schools, courts. Second, the classified come to know the label and organize their self-understanding around it; this is what the loop runs on, since without that awareness there is nothing for the classification to act on. Third, the people so classified behave differently, and the new behavior is recorded by the institutions. Fourth, the classification is revised in light of what has been recorded, and the revised category acts on a new cohort, restarting the loop. It follows that a looping effect is present whenever a human classification and the people it names change together over time. The classic illustration beyond multiple personality is the deletion of homosexuality from the DSM in 1973: the classification and the classified population shifted together until the category vanished from the manual.

(c) Here Hacking is a social constructionist. Since the classification keeps altering the classified, there is never a stable object for it to refer to, so interactive kinds cannot be real kinds; psychiatric categories are inventions rather than discoveries, and the looping effect makes psychiatric classification in principle self-undermining — each category tending to destroy itself as soon as it is applied. This makes interactive kinds moving targets rather than fixed objects of inquiry, and it is also why the DSM is better read as an instrument of social control than as a nosology.
