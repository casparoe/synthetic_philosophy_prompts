"Ryle argued that knowing-how is not a species of knowing-that. (a) Reconstruct his regress argument against what he called the intellectualist legend. (b) Explain the view of Stanley and Williamson, on which knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that, including the role of the knowing-wh constructions and the notion of a practical way of thinking. And here is the student's answer:

"(a) The intellectualist legend is the doctrine that intelligent action must be guided by an inner act of contemplating propositions. If I act intelligently, the intellectualist says, this is because I first consider a regulative proposition telling me how to act, and then apply it. But applying a maxim is itself an intelligent performance, so by the intellectualist's own lights it requires another prior consideration of a proposition, and each application of that proposition requires another belief, and so on forever. Since the regress is infinite, the intellectualist legend is false, and therefore knowing-how is not propositional at all. Ryle's own positive view is that knowledge-how just is an ability: knowing how to swim is nothing over and above being able to swim, a matter of dispositions rather than of facts.

(b) Stanley and Williamson defend the opposite view, that knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that. They note that 'knows how to F' behaves grammatically like other embedded question constructions — 'knows where the party is', 'knows who came to dinner' — and since those are all analyzed as knowing a proposition (e.g. knowing a location, under a way of thinking of it), knowledge-how should be treated the same way. On their view, 'Hannah knows how to ride a bicycle' is true when Hannah knows a way w that is a way for her to ride a bicycle, and her way of thinking of w is a practical one — the same kind of action-oriented way of thinking that connects to her intentions.

(c) I think Stanley and Williamson's view is just the intellectualist legend in modern dress. If knowledge-how is propositional, then intelligent action requires knowledge of propositions, and Ryle's regress argument shows that you can't get intelligent action out of prior propositional knowledge without an infinite regress. So either they've revived the legend Ryle refuted, or their 'practical way of thinking' is doing all the real work and the propositional surface is idle. Since Ryle showed the legend is false, Stanley and Williamson's view must be wrong too."
