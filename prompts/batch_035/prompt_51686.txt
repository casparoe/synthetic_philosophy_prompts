I sit on the panel that signs off on new writing at a theatre company that does a lot of work based on real lives — docudramas, one-woman shows about dead singers, that kind of thing. Every time a script plays fast and loose with a real person we have the same fight, and I've realised we don't have a shared standard at all, just pet cases. So before the next meeting I want the full specification: what would an adequate standard for permissible invention about real people have to deliver? Not abstract principles — it has to be usable, i.e. good enough that the panel could reach a verdict on a particular script by applying it. Please write the requirements list for me, in British English (it'll be circulated as a document to a UK-based panel).

Cases I think the list has to handle, at least — a good answer should add ones I've missed, and say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one faction on the panel:

- composite characters: the writer merges three real workmates into one so the story has a single antagonist. Nothing invented is demeaning, but one family now sees their father portrayed as the man who blocked the hero at every turn, when in life he said one sceptical thing at one meeting.
- invented interiority: a diary scene or a 2am monologue we have no record of, put into a real person's mouth.
- motive substitution: the documented reason for a decision was boring (money) and the script swaps it for a flattering one — or a damning one — because it plays better.
- selection that lies without stating a falsehood: every scene happened roughly as shown, but the choice of scenes adds up to a picture anyone who knew the person would call false. I want the standard to be able to catch this, and I suspect it's exactly here that the "it's all fiction" people and the "only the facts matter" people both wriggle off the hook.
- recency and who's still around: a monarch from 500 years ago versus someone who died in 2009 whose kids are alive and will get asked about the show at the school gate.
- the frame: does it change things if the production is openly a fantasia (singing, dream sequences, the dead person narrating their own funeral) versus sold with "based on true events" in the marketing?
- private material the person kept secret in life — an affair, a diagnosis — where the biographer has solid evidence. Does the standard treat "true but private" differently from "false but public"? It should, I think, but I cant yet say how.

Also, the standard can't just be "did it harm anyone" — some harmless distortions still seem wrong, and some harmful ones seem clearly fine (a satire of a living politician is unfair by design). And it can't collapse the difference between a distortion that misleads the audience and one the audience is in on. If any pair of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, say so — that's the bit I most want to see. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the obvious candidate standards: the facts-only view, the "emotional truth" view (get the essence right and the details are free), and the genre-contract view (all that matters is what the audience was led to expect). But dont spend long on that last bit — the criteria are where the thinking is, I'll do the scoring at the panel myself.
