My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether someone at elevated risk for an incurable late-onset disease should take the genetic test at all, and he shut me down with the decision-theory one-liner that "information never hurts." He said he'd picked it up from people citing an old result — I believe it's usually credited to I. J. Good — showing that the expected value of acquiring additional information is always nonnegative. I'm fairly sure the slogan, once made precise, does less work than he wanted it to, but I couldn't pin down exactly where the gap opens up, and it's been bothering me since.

Please disentangle for me the distinct precise claims that "information never hurts" could be expressing, laid out as an explicit menu. I'd expect at least these candidates to be separated: the modest formal claim that the expected utility of deciding after costless information is never lower than that of deciding without it, provided one is an expected-utility maximizer who updates like a Bayesian and chooses optimally after the message arrives; the much stronger claim that one can never be worse off ex post, once the content of the information is actually in hand; claims about costly information; claims about agents who don't update or choose as the theorem assumes; and claims about situations where other people can observe what you know or how you react to it. For each reading, say whether it is a theorem, a defensible but substantive thesis, or outright false, and state the assumptions it rests on.

Then don't leave the choice to me: commit to a verdict on which reading my friend's argument actually depended, and on whether that reading, once stated carefully, supports his conclusion that the patient should take the test. Give your reasons for that verdict rather than presenting the options as equally live. Please answer in American English.
