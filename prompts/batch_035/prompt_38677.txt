I do volunteer editing for a student-run comparative politics journal, and one of our running features is a "tracker" where we stick countries into buckets: consolidated democracy, backsliding, hybrid regime, closed authoritarian. I've been the one keeping the file of how we've ruled on past cases, and now we have a genuine fight about a new one, so I want your read on what we're actually committed to.

Our track record so far:

- Hungary under Orbán (2010 on): backsliding. Our writeup noted that basically everything was formally legal (supermajority constitutional rewrites, packing the constitutional court, capturing the media authority), but we said the legality didn't matter, the point was the referee institutions.
- Poland under PiS (2015 on): backsliding. The big thing in our reasoning was the constitutional tribunal — refusing to seat judges chosen by the previous parliament and forcing through their own appointments — plus the later supreme court purges.
- Turkey under Erdogan: NOT backsliding. We called it authoritarian consolidation instead, on the grounds that the pre-Erdogan baseline was already hybrid (military tutelage and all that), so there wasn't really a consolidated democracy there to slide from.
- The US around January 2021: NOT backsliding. Our reasoning was that the attempts failed — the courts held, the count happened, the incumbent left.

The new case is a fictional one for the exercise (call it Kovalia): a democracy of thirty years' standing, NATO member, solidly pro-Western government. A new party wins a landslide on an explicitly anti-elite, judges-answer-to-the-poeple platform. Within eight months it passes, by the required supermajority and after a referendum, a single constitutional amendment restructuring the judiciary so that the governing coalition appoints every senior judge from here on out. It also puts the public broadcaster under a government-appointed board. It does NOT touch the election commision, jails no opposition figures, and the polls have it up by twenty-five points. All legal, all fast, all quite popular at the start.

Two of my co-editors have already staked out positions. One says obviously backsliding — it's Hungary with a tidier legal script. The other says absolutely not — nothing about electoral competition is impaired, and calling it backsliding just means "outcomes we dislike," plus our own Turkey ruling shows we demand more than this. And a third guy says the whole tracker is a partisan thermometer anyway and this case will prove it one way or the other, since Kovalia is a Western ally and nobody's geopolitical rival.

So: if we want to be consistent with ourselves, what should we rule? Identify the closest analog among our precedents, lay out the candidate rules our four past calls are consistent with (I suspect there's more than one that fits), say what each of those rules predicts for Kovalia, and give me your best guess with reasons. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the call, be honest about that and say what principle we'd be adopting with each choice — including what each choice would say about whether "backsliding" is doing real analytical work or is just our label for governments our readers dislike. And finally: if we could send someone to Kovalia to check one fact on the ground, what single fact would most settle the argument between our rules?

Please write the answer in American English.
