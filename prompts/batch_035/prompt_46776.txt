I've ended up holding a pretty hard-line position on this and I want to stress-test it: when a community's collective memory and the historical record conflict, the record should win -- and not just in the archive but everywhere the conflict shows up, in textbooks, speeches, museum placards, anniversary rituals. Memory can keep whatever the record doesn't contradict, but wherever it contradicts, it loses. I don't believe in a special "commemorative truth" that gets an exemption just because a memory is load-bearing.

Here's the kind of case I have in mind (made up, but realistic). A mill town has commemorated "the bridge dead" for a century: local memory says militia fired unprovoked into a crowd of strikers crossing a bridge in 1907, and the annual ritual, the mural, the town's whole labor politics are built on that story. Then the coroner's inquests get digitized and they suggest something messier -- a panicked crush, a handful of shots but mostly from private guards, two militia dead too, and a death toll maybe a third the size of the remembered one. My instinct is that the town should redo its mural and shrink its number, eat the awkwardness, keep the commemoration but tether it to what the documents support. If the falsity is what holds the community together, that's a defect of the community, not a virtue of the falsehood.

What would count against this view? I can see the obvious objections already -- that archives are themselves the sediment of who held power, that memory sometimes preserves a pattern (say, decades of violence against strikers) that no single document captures, that "the record" is rarely as unified and settled as my framing assumes -- but I want more than the obvious, and I want it sorted. Which of these would a thoughtful defender of my position concede as real pressure, and which would they parry, and how? Are there realistic cases where correcting the memory does damage that outweighs the epistemic gain -- not bruised feelings but something like the commemoration collapsing entirely and nothing taking its place? Is there a defensible middle version where the record wins in the archive but only "wins on appeal" in public life, after the community has had time to metabolize it -- and is that a genuine qualification of the view or a capitulation dressed up as nuance? And, honestly: has my view been quietly insulated by defining "the record" as whatever survives scholarly scrutiny, so that by construction nothing could ever beat it? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what rigor looks like?

One request: keep straight what's actually settled versus what's live among people who know this area -- e.g., how much of collective memory studies is Halbwachs-descended social theory rather than empirical history, and whether the standard story about societies "working through" their pasts is itself contested rather than received wisdom. If something is one scholar's contested take rather than the field's consensus, flag it that way instead of presenting it as settled fact.
