I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar in moral psychology and ethics — eighteen students, one 75-minute session — and next week we take up the question of whether there is a real moral difference between lying and merely misleading: saying something false, versus saying something true that you arrange to be understood falsely. Most of them arrive already skeptical that the distinction could matter ("the intent is the same, so the blame is the same"), and I want them to feel the pull of the other side before we read the Kant material on truthfulness and the murderer at the door. A lecture won't do it; I need them to run into the distinction from the inside.

Design a structured role-play for this session. The scenario should be one in which participants are under genuine pressure to deceive, with real (if small) stakes in the form of points or privileges, and with a Kantian constraint baked into the rules: some participants are absolutely forbidden to assert anything they believe false, on pain of a severe penalty, but are otherwise free to answer as cleverly as they like — through truthful-but-engineered answers, implication, silence, redirection, exploiting what their questioner already assumes. The opposing side should have incentives and limited "interrogation moves" that force the truthful side into a live decision each round between flat honesty, refusal, and artful misdirection. I want the rules simple enough to teach in five minutes but tight enough that students can't escape the dilemma by stonewalling or changing the subject every round. The design should also include a second phase or variant in which the lying ban is lifted, so students can compare what deception feels like when it must be done with truths versus when it may be done outright — whether the constraint felt like a genuine moral safeguard, or like a legalism that made them feel dirtier precisely because of the ingenuity it demanded.

Please give me the full package: premise and roles, the exact rules and penalty structure, a round-by-round timeline for the 75 minutes, materials I'd need, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical distinction — assertion versus implicature, the wrongness of lying versus the wrongness of deception more broadly — and that raises but doesn't settle the question of whether the truthful schemers in the room did anything better than the liars would have. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect stonewalling, rules-lawyering, and students cracking and lying anyway in round two), and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical debate the game cannot reproduce — what I will have to supply myself in the debrief. Keep the whole thing under 750 words, and write it in American English.
