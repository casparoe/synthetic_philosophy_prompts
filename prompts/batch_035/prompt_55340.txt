For most of graduate school I have kept quiet about the interpretation of quantum mechanics, but last month I agreed to defend Everett's view — many-worlds — in our department's workshop against a colleague who defends objective collapse, and preparing for it has forced me to write down what I actually believe about probability in a branching universe. Before I stand up in front of people who will be looking for weaknesses, I would like an audit of whether my position hangs together. Here is the package:

1. The universal wavefunction is the whole of reality; there is no collapse, and every outcome of a well-run measurement occurs in some branch.

2. I take the decision-theoretic line on the Born rule: given plausible axioms of rational preference for an agent embedded in the branching structure, the agent must weight future outcomes by squared amplitude, and this is the heart of the solution to the probability problem in Everettian quantum mechanics.

3. I also think that pre-measurement uncertainty is genuinely self-locating: before the apparatus fires, I am really uncertain which future branch contains me, and the correct prior over candidate locations is the amplitude measure.

4. Branch counting is hopeless: the number of branches is vague, hostage to how finely one individuates decohered histories, and nothing rational could correspond to spreading credence equally over them in any case.

5. Still, I insist that the Born weights delivered by the derivation in (2) are objective chances in the ordinary philosophical sense — the sort a rational agent's credences should defer to when known, so that they satisfy the Principal Principle, and the sort that explain recorded long-run frequencies.

6. And on a concrete case: suppose a spin measurement has spin-up amplitude 9/10 and spin-down amplitude 1/10, but the spin-down branch subsequently decoheres into vastly more fine-grained branches than spin-up does (many more distinct states of the recording device and the environment). My considered verdict is that the correct credence in spin-down is exactly 1/10, and I would bet at the corresponding odds.

My opponent's opening salvo, in our preliminary exchange, was that (1) and (6) are flatly inconsistent — that if I am certain spin-down occurs somewhere, a credence of 1/10 in it is theater. I suspect this is a confusion, but I cannot quite see through it. More troubling to me are the seams I can see but cannot diagnose: whether the decision-theoretic story in (2) can really cohabit with self-locating uncertainty in (3), or with objective chances in (5), and whether the vagueness of branch structure conceded in (4) is compatible with chances as precise as (5) and (6) demand. Please go through the pairs: which are in genuine tension and how badly, which only look to be, and for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
