The exam question was:

"Kant holds that a maxim can fail the universalizability test in two distinct ways. (a) Explain the difference between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will, making clear why they are different tests and not just two labels for the same failure. (b) Apply both tests to the maxim of promising to repay a loan when you need money, with no intention of repaying it. (c) Then do the same for the maxim of refusing to help others in need while expecting that you can get by on your own and that others will leave you alone too. In each case, say which test the maxim fails, why, and why it does not fail the other one."

Here's the student's answer, transcribed as written:

(a) A contradiction in conception arises when the universalised maxim makes the very practice or institution the maxim relies on impossible. If everyone acted on the maxim, the background conditions for acting on it successfully would disappear, so the maxim destroys itself. A contradiction in will is when you can perfectly well imagine a world where everyone acts on the maxim -- nothing self-destructs -- but no rational agent could reasonably want to live in such a world, because it conflicts with things they want. So the first test is about conceivability and the second is about what a rational person would choose if given a choice. The first kind of maxim is wrong in a stricter sense, and the second kind is wrong in a weaker sense, like it's irrational rather than impossible.

(b) The maxim is: when I need money and believe I won't be able to repay it, I will promise to repay anyway. If this were universal law, everyone would know that people make false promises whenever they need money, so people would stop taking promises of repayment as any evidence of actual repayment. Banks would stop lending on the basis of a promise. So no one could any longer use a false promise to get money, which means the maxim, universalised, defeats its own purpose. This is a contradiction in conception: the maxim can't be conceived as a universal law without collapsing. It would also fail a contradiction in will test, since nobody would want to live in a society where promises meant nothing. So it fails both tests.

(c) The maxim is: never help others in need, while counting on being left alone in return. If everyone adopted this, it's still possible to imagine it -- nobody helps anybody, but that world doesn't logically contradict itself, it's just harsh. However, a world like that couldn't really hold together: eventually everyone hits a situation they can't handle alone, illness, disaster, etc., and if no one ever helped anyone the human race might not even survive. So the maxim undermines the conditions everyone depends on, which makes it self-defeating in the same way as the false promise, and it also fails the will test because you couldn't want a world where you're on your own when things go wrong.
