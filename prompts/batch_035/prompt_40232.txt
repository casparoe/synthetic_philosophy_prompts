I teach an upper-level seminar on democratic theory and collective decision-making — 18 students, one 80-minute session — and next week we reach the judgment aggregation literature and the discursive dilemma. The students have already had the Condorcet jury theorem and Arrow-style results, so they're comfortable with the idea that voting rules have structural pathologies; what they haven't felt is the specific thing the discursive dilemma does, namely that a group can hold a majority view on every relevant premise while a majority rejects the conclusion those premises entail, so that "just vote on the reasons" doesn't rescue majority rule the way they keep suggesting it will.

I'd like you to design the activity itself. My instinct is a mock hiring committee: three or so candidates for a fictional position, and committee members holding private judgment cards specifying their sincere yes/no positions on, say, research quality, teaching fit, and whether a disqualifying circumstance applies, with the bylaws requiring all criteria to be met for a hire. You'd need to construct the cards so that the majorities on the premises and the majority on the bottom-line conclusion come apart in the alarming way, and the class shouldn't see that coming. But I'm open to a different staging if you think one lands harder — an appellate panel on a two-element tort claim, a grant review board, whatever — as long as participants make real decisions with some stakes attached (points, small privileges, anything but real money) rather than watching me work the paradox at the board. Ideally there should be a second phase where someone controls the order in which questions go to a vote, so the class discovers that agenda-setting flips the outcome for identical preferences.

Beyond the rules, materials, card contents, and a minute-by-minute timeline, I want a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation and shows where each coalition came from. Please also include the practical honesty: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (probably students comparing cards early, or one person recognizing the structure from a reading and announcing it), and how I should handle that. Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — which features of the real problem (deliberation before voting, the systematic impossibility results, the incentive to misreport one's premises strategically) have to be supplied by me at the board — and tell me what would make you abandon or redesign this activity: where, specifically, the payoff scheme or the card construction is most likely to teach students the wrong lesson, and what evidence in how the room behaves would tell you the design failed.
