I'm preparing a session for my department's decision-theory reading group on a pair of cases that circulate in the rationalist-adjacent literature under the names "counterfactual mugging" and "XOR blackmail," and I've been asked to adjudicate them rather than merely survey them. So that the discussion doesn't depend on anyone having read the original sources, I'll state both cases in the form I intend to use, and the essay should treat my stipulations as fixed. The first case: a predictor with a long, unbroken record of accuracy announces a protocol — it will flip a fair coin. If heads, it will place $10,000 in your bank account and never contact you, but only conditional on its forecast that a tails-version of you would hand over $100 when asked. If tails, it will do nothing — until today, when it appears, tells you the whole story, and asks for the $100. You can be all but certain the coin landed tails; that is why the predictor is in your kitchen and the $10,000 is not in your account. The money either exists or doesn't, regardless of what you do now, and paying helps no one you will ever meet — unless you count the counterfactual heads-version of yourself as a beneficiary. The second case: a blackmailer writes to say, truthfully, that it operates under a fixed policy — a letter goes to a person exactly when either that person will pay the demanded $5,000 or the blackmailer holds a genuine, career-ending dossier on them, but not both. If you refuse and a dossier exists, it is released tomorrow; if you pay, the matter is closed. Work through the logic and you find that if you are the sort who refuses, the letter on your doormat is evidence that a real dossier exists and will be released whatever you do; whereas if you are the sort who pays, the letter is evidence that there is no dossier at all, and the threat you are paying to avert is empty — you would simply be paying because you are the sort of person who pays. In both cases a seductive mode of reasoning treats your present choice as information about the world: in the mugging, it says your policy now reaches across worlds and made the heads-you rich or poor; in the blackmail, it says your refusal would reveal a dossier about to detonate, so you pay, and — sure enough — nothing is ever released.

Please write an essay of roughly 1,400 words for the reading group. It should (i) reconstruct in plain terms what a causal-decision-style agent, an evidential-decision-style agent, and an updateless or "policy-first" agent would each do in each case, applying the principles to the stipulated setups rather than name-dropping them; (ii) diagnose precisely where the two cases come apart — many people's untutored verdict is "pay" in the mugging but "refuse" in the blackmail (or the reverse), and I want to know whether any single coherent decision rule yields exactly that pair of verdicts, and at what cost in other cases; and (iii) reach and defend a definite conclusion for each case, engaging the strongest argument on the losing side rather than ending on a note of "further work is needed."

Two constraints on the essay's epistemics. First, it must explicitly separate what is settled among people who actually work on decision theory — for instance, the dependency structure of each case: who observes what, and which facts are fixed independently of your act — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether an agent should treat its own choice as correlated with its counterfactual counterparts, or whether updatelessness is a rational ideal or an artifact of one particular formalism. Second, it must not present any one school's verdict as the consensus of decision theory, because there isn't one. Assume the reader has no internet access and no prior acquaintance with the literature: argue everything from the cases as I've stipulated them, and cite nothing — the group wants the reasoning, not a bibliography.
