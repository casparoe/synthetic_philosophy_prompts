I'm a PhD student in philosophy of the social sciences, and as the TA for our graduate seminar on conventions and norms I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the evolutionary critique of David Lewis's analysis of convention. The worry, roughly: Lewis analyzed conventions as regularities solving coordination problems, sustained by agents' conditional preferences and their common knowledge of one another's conformity and expectations. But Brian Skyrms-style evolutionary models seem to produce conventional outcomes — the side of the road a population drives on, bargaining norms approaching the Nash solution in ultimatum games — in populations with no beliefs, no salience, and no common knowledge at all; and Lewis himself, in his later reflections on Convention, already conceded that the common knowledge requirement was too strong. So the question should force students to say whether the evolutionary result refutes Lewis's analysis, or instead exposes a fork in the concept: either conventions don't require any intentional/epistemic apparatus, or else evolutionary game theory and Lewis are tracking two different phenomena that both get called 'convention.' It should not be answerable by reciting Lewis's definition followed by the word 'Skyrms,' and a strong answer should say something about whether fairness norms in mixed-motive bargaining games even count as Lewisian conventions, given that Lewis's paradigm cases are pure coordination problems where the alternatives are arbitrary. Please give me (1) the question, suitable for a 72-hour take-home with a 2,500-word cap; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points built around five must-hit moves, each with its point value and a one-line note on how students typically fumble it.
