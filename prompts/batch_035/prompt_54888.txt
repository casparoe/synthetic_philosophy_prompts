Two of my grad students, Priya and Marcus, got into it after our screening night last week and I still genuinely can't decide who came out ahead, so I'm outsourcing the referee job. The film was a weepy — doesn't matter which one, a slow tragic one that had half the room in tears and everyone saying afterward how great it was. That's the phenomenon: the paradox of tragedy, why we seek out art that makes us miserable and count that as a good night.

Priya went with catharsis first: the crying itself is purgative, we discharge pity and fear, and the enjoyment is the aftermath of the purge, the way a good cry leaves you lighter. Marcus said that can't be right, for two reasons — the relief should be available from any sad stimulus (a newspaper story, a documentary about a famine), yet we specifically want the fiction, and more awkwardly, relief-then-pleasure makes tragedy like a dentist appointment: nobody books the dentist for the enjoyableness of it. Then he offered what he called the meta-response view: we don't enjoy the sadness at all, we enjoy appreciating how well it's rendered — the sadness is the ticket price, and the actual pleasure is this second-order delight in the craft of producing it.

Priya's comeback was the wallowing point: if the pleasure is all meta, why do I deliberately pick the saddest option on the shelf, rewatch it knowing full well what's coming, and skip the technically accomplished but emotionally flat stuff? Sometimes the sadness itself seems to be the thing we're after. Marcus said that's what the safety buys you: fiction is a sandbox where the emotion runs at full intensity with no consequences, which is why nobody walls in a documentary — the wallowing is savoring real-grade sadness at zero cost. Priya pounced: then why does "based on a true story" on the poster make people want to see it more, and why did she actually grieve a documentary subject for days? The safety theory predicts we should prefer the fake-est sad things and get turned off by real ones.

They went around one more time — Marcus accusing Priya of treating "sadness" as one thing when fictional sadness and real sadness might not even be the same emotion, Priya accusing Marcus of a theory that quietly redefines the enjoyment until it's not about the sadness anymore, at which point the paradox is solved by removing it rather than answering it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — who's right at each step, where each of them is smuggling something in, and whether either of them is actually responding to the paradox or just relocating it. Also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing entirely. Be honest if one side is just clearly winning; don't split it evenly to be nice.
