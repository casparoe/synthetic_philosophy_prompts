I am a teaching assistant grading a take-home exam for an intermediate philosophy course on death and the meaning of life. Below is the exam question, followed by a student's answer. Please grade it. Your response must be a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Items one through four should each take one specific claim or move in the student's answer and say whether it is correct, incorrect, or subtly misleading, and why; item five should state the letter grade (A through F) you are assigning, with a one-sentence justification. Assess only the accuracy and quality of the student's reasoning — do not signal your own view about which side of the debate should win.

Exam question:
(a) Reconstruct the core Epicurean argument that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, being careful to distinguish the state of being dead from the process of dying. (b) Explain the deprivation account as the standard objection to the Epicurean, and one supporting consideration offered on its behalf. (c) Explain the symmetry argument concerning prenatal and posthumous nonexistence and the best reply available to the deprivationist. (d) Say which side you think wins on the arguments given, and why.

Student's answer:
Epicurus's argument rests on the premise that whatever is bad for a person must be experienced as bad by that person: when death is, we are not, and when we are, death is not. It follows that the state of being dead cannot be bad for the one who dies, though the process of dying can involve pain, and death can of course be bad for the survivors who grieve, which Epicurus would not deny. The argument is aimed at the state of being dead, not at dying, so pain during dying is no counterexample.

The main objection is the deprivation account: death is bad for you not because it feels bad but because it deprives you of the goods your continued life would have contained. Nagel supports this with cases of unexperienced misfortune, such as being betrayed by trusted friends without ever finding out, which are bad for a person even though the person never experiences them. Once such unexperienced harms are admitted, the timing problem dissolves as well: there is no remaining puzzle about when death is bad for you, since we have already granted that harms need not be located in experience. It also follows that death is worse the earlier it comes, and since the account makes death worse in proportion to the goods it deprives one of, immortality would have to be the greatest possible good.

Finally, Epicurus himself offered the symmetry argument: our prenatal nonexistence mirrors our posthumous nonexistence, and nobody regards the time before birth as a misfortune, so we should not regard the time after death as one either. The deprivationist's standard reply is that posthumous nonexistence deprives a person of goods, whereas prenatal nonexistence deprives no one, since there was no person there yet to be deprived. So the symmetry consideration favors the Epicurean, and given that the deprivation account also seems to entail that immortality is best, neither side has a decisive case.
