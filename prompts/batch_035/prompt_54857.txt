Last friday a few of us from the department (two of my grad students, actually — Dana, who works on functionalism, and Marcus, who's more of a consciousness guy) stayed late after our philosophy of mind reading group and ended up playing this open-world game on the department TV, and afterwards we got into the kind of argument that only grad students can have at 10pm. Dana had spent a chunk of the session going on a rampage through a town of NPCs, and afterwards she said, half joking, that she felt a bit weird about it — the villagers screamed, begged, tried to crawl away while injured, mourned their dead. Marcus said feeling weird is fine but it's just projection, there's no one in there. Dana said hold on, our evidence that animals and human infants feel pain is basically behavioral, plus analogy to ourselves — and the NPCs behave exactly like suffering beings, so on our own epistemic standards we should at least be uncertain. Marcus said no — with animals we know the underlying machinery is the same kind as ours, neurons and nociceptors and so on, whereas with the game we can look at the code and see there's nothing remotely like a persistent experience being implemented, just hitbox checks triggering scream animations. Dana called that substrate chauvinism: a good functionalist should say that if the right causal organization is there, pain is there, and you can't just peek behind the curtain and stipulate otherwise.

Marcus came back with what I thought was his best point: the "organization" in a game like that is absurdly thin. Each villager is a handful of variables, they have no memory from one scene to the next, and when you reload a save the whole event never happened to anyone — there's no continuing subject across time to be the bearer of the harm, so even if some momentary state were painful, there'd be no one who it was painful *for*. Dana said that proves too much — it would seem to rule out momentary pain being bad for an animal killed instantly, and plenty of philosophers (she mentioned person-stage stuff, I forget whose) are happy with harm to momentary subjects. Marcus replied that if she really bites that bullet, then every scripted scream in every game ever played is a moral atrocity, and a view with implications that unhinged needs to go back to the drawing board; Dana shot back that "your view has weird implications" is not an argument, people said the same thing about basically every moral expansion in history. At that point they both looked at me and I confess I punted and said I had a train to catch.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I'm genuinely torn about a couple of the middle moves (the epistemic parity one especially, since it's basically the other-minds problem wearing a costume), and I'd also like to hear whether there's something both of them are missing that would reframe the whole thing. They've asked me to referee at our next session, so I'd rather show up with actual positions rather than the train excuse again.
