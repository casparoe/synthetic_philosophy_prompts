Last week my son came home from school and, after some pressing, told me that a classmate had spent the lunch hour directing a slur at his ethnic background. I raised it with the deputy head, who was sympathetic enough but kept returning to the old line that words will never hurt him the way sticks and stones would, using it as if it were a settled truth that settled the matter: no bruise, no real harm, and therefore nothing called for beyond a quiet word with the other boy. I have a follow-up meeting next week and want to be precise there rather than merely angry. My sense is that the proverb is several quite different claims wearing one sentence, only some of which are even relevant to what happened, and I would like them separated. Please set out the distinct things the line might be asserting, from the trivially true to the substantive to the outright false — for instance: that physical and verbal injury differ in kind; that verbal harm, unlike a blow, depends on the hearer's interpretation or cooperation and so can be refused; that words can express a wrong but never constitute one; that the right response to insults is to train oneself into indifference; that institutions should treat only physical harm as actionable — and for each reading, say whether it is plausible, and whether it would actually support the deputy head's use of it, since some readings that are true as generalities clearly do not license inaction in this case. A separate question I would like folded in: I understand linguists disagree over whether slurs do their damage in their literal meaning or in some surrounding pragmatic effect, and I would like to know whether the proverb comes out differently on those two pictures, in particular whether the reading on which harm requires the victim's uptake survives if the slur's sting is carried by its semantic content. Please lay the readings out as a list I can take into that meeting, with a verdict attached to each.
