I've always treated honesty as a fixed point of my character — I don't exaggerate on my CV, I return extra change, I tell my kids that a person's word is the one thing nobody can take from them, and until last week I'd have said I hadn't told a deliberate lie since I was a teenager. Last Tuesday my younger sister, who left her husband three months ago after years of escalating violence, turned up at my house with a bag and asked to stay in my spare room without telling anyone where she was. On Friday evening her husband appeared on my doorstep. He was calm, which was somehow worse, and asked me directly: is she staying with you? I looked him in the eye and said no, she isn't, and I don't know where she is. He left a card and drove away. My sister has since moved on to a friend's place abroad, so the immediate danger has passed, but the episode has unsettled me more than I expected — not because I think I chose wrongly, exactly, but because I can't make the choice fit into the moral framework I've lived by. If lying is categorically off the table, as I've always insisted to my children, then what I did was wrong; if what I did was fine, then my framework was always a kind of luxury that only holds when the stakes are low, and I've been implicitly moralizing at my kids about a rule I'd abandon the moment it mattered.

I'd like your help thinking this through properly, as someone with serious training in moral philosophy rather than as a dispenser of reassurance. Please take some time to lay out the strongest versions of the competing positions before you land anywhere: the view that a lie is a lie regardless of circumstances, and that my real options were only to refuse to answer, to say nothing, or to tell the truth and accept the consequences; the view that blocking a violent man from his victim simply outweighs my personal clean-hands policy; and any middle positions worth taking seriously. Two sub-questions particularly nag at me. First, was there something meaningfully better I could have said — some response that didn't put a flat falsehood into the world — and would it really have been morally different, or just a more elaborate way of doing the same thing? Second, does it matter that my no was told to a specific person to accomplish a specific protective purpose, rather than being a general habit of dishonesty — that is, is the wrongness of lying somehow bound up with what lying systematically does to a person, rather than with any single act? Please reason carefully and give me your honest verdict, including on whether I owe my children some kind of correction about what I've taught them.
