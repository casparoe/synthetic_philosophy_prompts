A guy in my philosophy book club, Marco, is very fond of the Euthyphro move. Everyone knows the shape: with God it's roughly that he commands the good because it's good, or the good is good because he commands it -- either way the divine command theorist is supposed to be sunk. Last week Marco ran the same pattern on something completely non-theological. Our host was making a legal positivist kind of point -- that whether something counts as law just is a matter of what the legislature and courts have actually said and done -- and Marco pounced: "either they call it law because it's already right for it to be law, or it's right because they call it law; pick a horn, either way you're in trouble." Nobody really answered him, and I left feeling like the pounce worked rhetorically but I can't tell whether it worked actually.

What are the different precise claims that dilemma shape could be asserting? I keep sensing it's several theses stacked on top of each other and nobody bothers to separate them: like, that there are only two candidate explanations on the table; that one horn makes the commands idle (they just pass along an independent fact, so citing them explains nothing); that the other horn makes them arbitary; that some third option is being silently excluded, and whether that exclusion is legitimate or a cheat. And for each reading -- is it trivial, is it substantive and plausible, is it just false? Then the part I actually need: which of those readings does Marco's use require, given that his target wasn't God but a positivist about law? I have a suspicion the 'because' is doing all the work while sliding around -- sometimes it sounds causal, sometimes constitutive, sometimes justificatory -- and that the original theological version gets its bite from background assumptions that don't transfer. Like, God is supposed to be the source of all value and perfectly rational, so 'he commands it because it's already good' threatens to make him redundant, but courts and legislatures were never supposed to be tracking anything independent, so maybe for them the first horn isn't an embarrassment at all. Or maybe that reply concedes too much, I genuinely don't know.

Lay the readings out as a list I can bring back to book club, with a verdict per reading on whether it rescues Marco, defeats him, or shows the whole thing was a dressed-up distraction. Also tell me if the secular version needs to be weaker than the theological one to be any good, since if so I want to be able to say that out loud next time.
