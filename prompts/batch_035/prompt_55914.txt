French laïcité is usually presented as a single principle: strict separation of religion and state, under which the state neither recognizes nor funds any religion, and citizens enter public institutions as abstract citizens rather than as members of religious communities. The actual body of French law and administrative practice, however, is a patchwork, and the pieces appear to pull in opposite directions. Since 2004, students in public schools have been barred from wearing conspicuous religious symbols, a rule justified by the claim that the school is a neutral public space in which citizens must appear without religious marks. Yet the very same state: (1) applies no separation at all in Alsace-Moselle, where the 1905 law on the separation of churches and state has never applied, so that the state pays the salaries of Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, and Jewish clergy, and the President of the Republic nominates bishops on the Pope's proposal; (2) owns and maintains at public expense all religious buildings that were in use for worship before 1905, on the understanding that they are heritage; and (3) heavily subsidizes private religious schools, most of them Catholic, under contract, paying their teachers and requiring only that they follow the national curriculum.

What theory of religion, and of neutrality, does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view. Consider, in particular, the following hypothesis, which you should assess, refine, or reject: the practice treats religion in two distinct registers. As an object of maintenance and funding, religion is handled like a language or an inherited culture — something a population simply has, which the state may steward as patrimony. As an object worn or displayed by an individual within a public institution, religion is handled like an opinion — a chosen, assertive act that stakes a claim on the public space, and is therefore subject to the same discipline as partisan political speech. On this hypothesis, the same state can pay a priest's salary in Strasbourg and forbid a schoolgirl's headscarf in Strasbourg without sensing any contradiction, because the salary is heritage and the scarf is a claim.

Is that reconstruction correct, and is it coherent? Does the practice quietly presuppose a conception of religion as inner belief rather than as embodied or communal practice — a conception critics have called Protestant even where it is applied to everyone — such that a small piece of cloth can be classified as an assertion while a cathedral is classified as furniture? Which rival views does the pattern thereby rule out: for instance, a view on which neutrality requires symmetry of funding across religions, or one on which the state should be neutral toward identities rather than toward expressions? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that an Anglo-American multicultural state — which funds and accommodates religious identity on the theory that religions are like languages, but regulates them where they become claims on others — could license some of the very same legal pieces for the opposite reason? And if the implicit theory is incoherent rather than merely underdetermined, say which piece would have to give: would consistency require extending the concordat regime, ending separation everywhere; or extending separation, stripping Alsace-Moselle of its recognized cults and transferring the pre-1905 buildings; or something else besides? Finally, test whether the heritage/assertion distinction can be stated in a way that does not collapse into a rule favoring old, majority religions over new, minority ones, and draw from your analysis a verdict on whether laïcité as practiced is, as advertised, neutral among religions.
