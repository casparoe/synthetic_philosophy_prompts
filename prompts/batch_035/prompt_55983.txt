Aaron said that gets things backwards. Entropy increase can't explain temporal direction because describing it as *increase* already presupposes you know which way the future is: to say entropy goes up over time, you need to have identified the future direction beforehand, so thermodynamics is piggybacking on our temporal orientation rather than grounding it. His own view was that what actually does the work is the low-entropy initial condition of the universe: once you posit that the early universe was in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, ordinary statistics makes it overwhelmingly likely that entropy grows away from that end, and that gradient fixes a direction without ever consulting our sense of before and after.

Nadia's comeback was that positing a special low-entropy past isn't an explanation at all, it's the thing that demanded one — by any reasonable measure such a state is staggeringly improbable, so appealing to it is like explaining a coin that landed heads a billion times in a row by saying it just started out heads. Aaron answered that improbability is a poor test for explanatory posits: an explanatory posit doesn't need to be probable, it needs to make the rest of the phenomena expected, and the low-entropy start does exactly that — given it, ice melting, cream mixing into coffee, our memories being of the past rather than the future, all become statistical near-certainties.
