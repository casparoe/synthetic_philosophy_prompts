Last weekend I had a long argument with a close friend of mine, a criminal lawyer by training, about whether practical stakes can affect what a person knows. He kept insisting that when a court asks whether a witness "really knew" the defendant was at the scene, it isn't using some different, watered-down concept of knowledge — it's demanding more of the same thing. I found myself agreeing with that, but when I tried to spell out the position I realized I fumbled it badly: he slid without noticing from "the standard for knowledge goes up when a lot hangs on it" to "so the word 'knows' must mean something different in the courtroom than at a dinner party," and I could not find a clean way to show him that these are two quite different theses — one about the subject's practical situation, one about the meaning of the word. The argument ended with both of us dissatisfied. It turns out this matters practically, because in two weeks I have to teach a fifty-minute first-year section in intro epistemology on precisely this material, and I would rather not reproduce my dinner-table performance in front of forty students.

What I want from you is a plan for the section, not the content itself. In particular: what has to be established before either thesis is even statable — my instinct is that the students first need a very firm grip on what is being held fixed ("same agent, same proposition, same moment, same evidence") and I'd like you to confirm or correct that and tell me what comes after; then what single example should carry the whole session. Please not the bank cases — my students will half-remember them from the textbook, and I suspect those cases smuggle too much in at once. Tell me what the replacement example must display and what it must be deliberately silent about. Then the misconceptions: I anticipate (1) students reading pragmatic encroachment as a psychological claim, as though high stakes make you believe harder rather than making knowledge harder; (2) students collapsing pragmatic encroachment and contextualism together into a single thought they'd phrase as "context changes knowledge" — this is precisely my friend's slide and the confusion I most want to kill, so I want to know exactly at which point in the sequence it should be confronted; and (3) a student offering what they take to be a reductio — that on the interest-relative view a person giving testimony under threat knows less than the same person, with the same evidence, gives the same testimony at ease — and where in the plan that objection is best absorbed rather than suppressed. Finally, give me one question I could put to the section at the end that would separate students who have actually grasped the distinction between the two views from students who have merely become comfortable with the jargon, and tell me candidly what I should decline to cover at this level and say so out loud.
