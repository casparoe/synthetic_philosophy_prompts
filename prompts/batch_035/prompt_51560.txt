So at work we got into a two hour argument about whether you could build a smarter version of democracy, and I want you to referee it. Context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and the two people arguing are engineers too, so assume none of us have read the actual literature on this.

My coworker Dev's proposal: stop giving everyone exactly one vote. Instead, at election time you also answer a bunch of prediction questions — will unemployment be above X% next year, will there be a government shutdown, will inflation fall in a given band, that kind of thing. Later, when reality resolves the questions, a fixed public scoring rule awards points based on how calibrated you were (confident + wrong should hurt more than unsure + wrong). Your weight in the *next* election is your running score, normalized so everyone keeps at least a small baseline weight no matter what. He says it's like the difference between letting anyone push straight to prod versus requiring CI to pass first — nobody thinks the CI check is authoritarian.

My other coworker Mara's objections, roughly in the order they came out:

(1) She said the whole "informed voters are better voters" idea assumes everyone is answering the same question, but prediction questions and value questions are different things. Being good at predicting whether a tariff will raise prices says nothing about whether raising prices is worth it, and elections are mostly about the second thing.

(2) Dev said fine, values are out of reach, but at least the scheme filters out people who are confidently wrong about basic facts, and confident wrongness is most of the actual problem. No system gets at values directly anyway, so why hold this one to that standard.

(3) She said the score would just track who has time to follow the news obsessively, which means it proxies for class and education, so you'd be handing even more political power to the already-advantaged — and that this has an ugly history, since poll taxes and literacy tests were also "neutral on their face."

(4) He said literacy tests were administered by officials with discretion to fail whoever they disliked, whereas a fixed public scoring rule has no discretion at all, so the analogy fails. And we already accept competence gates for pilots, surgeons, etc. — nobody calls that discrimination.

(5) She said pilots are a bad analogy because flying a plane has an objective success criterion and governing doesn't — if governing had one, we wouldn't need elections at all, we'd just measure outcomes and appoint whoever scores best, and his scoring rule is quietly smuggling in exactly that assumption.

They basically went in circles after that and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through the five moves one at a time and say who's right at each step, then say who's more right overall?
