I keep going in circles on Benacerraf's dilemma and I want a definite verdict out of you, not a survey that ends with "more work is needed." Quick setup so were on the same page: the dilemma is that math truths seem to be about abstract objects (so their semantics should work like ordinary truth-conditional semantics), but then our knowledge of them looks causally impossible; and if we instead make the semantics non-standard, mathematical truth gets mysterious in a fresh way.

Rate these escape routes on a 1-10 scale for how much you'd trust them to actually dissolve the dilemma rather than relabel it, and put them in order best to worst:

(a) structuralism — "the number 2" is really a position in a pattern, so we were never talking about objects in the first place
(b) hermeneutic fictionalism — math is a useful pretense, and "2+2=4" is true in the way things are true inside a story
(c) the easy-road platonist line — knowing a math fact just is being able to prove it, so no causal contact with numbers was ever needed
(d) the necessity move — mathematical truths are necessary, so if it's provable it's true, and necessity itself does the epistemic work
(e) the hybrid I've been toying with: keep the semantics uniform, but replace the epistemic story with something like inference-to-the-best-practice — the justification flows from what the practice of proving does for us, not from access to any objects

One sentence per item on why it lands where it does. Then name your winner and the single thing most likely to make you downgrade it. Don't hedge by saying they're all partly right — pick.
