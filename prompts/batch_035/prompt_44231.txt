I lead the platform team at a mid-sized software company, and I recently said two things that a colleague tells me cannot both be true. At an internal demo, presenting our new database migration to the engineering staff, I said we know it can handle full production load — and I'd stand by that: the load tests, the canary rollout, and the weeks of monitoring gave us everything we'd ordinarily ask for before shipping. Two days later, on a call with our largest customer, whose contract lets them walk if we breach a single uptime clause, they asked whether we know the migration is safe for their data, and I said that we have strong evidence but not the kind of certainty they should require before relying on it, and I recommended they keep a staged rollout on their side. A colleague who was on both calls told me afterward that I had contradicted myself, and that the only honest reading is that I didn't really know in the first case either — I was just talking loosely to keep morale up. My director attacked my hedge from the other direction: if I could genuinely know in one room and not the other, then we could simply have bought the extra downtime insurance we were quoted, made the stakes trivial, and thereby known all along — which he takes to be a reductio of whatever principle I'm implicitly relying on. I have the sense that the difference between the two conversations is doing real work — who is asking, what rides on the answer, how much evidence someone needs before it's reasonable for them to act on it — but every time I try to spell that out, it collapses into "I felt more pressure on the customer call," which is not a principle. So my questions: Is there a principled defense of having said both things, or was one of the two statements straightforwardly false? If there is such a defense, does it survive my director's insurance counterexample, or does it have to be revised — or abandoned — in light of it? And if I do have the defense, should I stop telling my own team that we know things, since the eventual customers of that work will always face higher stakes than the engineers in the room?
