I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social choice theory (18 students, one 90-minute session), and the next unit brings us to the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. I don't want to state the theorem and watch it wash over them. I want them to feel, first, the temptation to misreport a preference when it pays, and then the accumulating exhaustion of trying to design a collective decision rule that removes the temptation. Design a multi-stage in-class activity for that session with this arc: an initial sincere vote over three or more alternatives that produces an outcome many of them dislike, a second stage in which pre-ballot communication is allowed and strategic voting visibly pays, and a third stage in which small groups act as constitutional committees — each designing an aggregation rule for three or more alternatives that its members believe is immune to strategic misreporting, while rival groups act as adversaries hunting for a preference profile under which some voter gains by lying. Include concrete rules, a materials list, a payoff scheme requiring no real money (a genuine choice the class must live with for the rest of the term works best — the weighting of assignments, the ordering of seminar topics, or something similar, so the stakes are real but small), and a minute-by-minute timeline.

Then give me the closing debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the theorem — the unrestricted-domain assumption the committees were silently granted, the three-or-more-alternatives condition, and the two escape hatches their committees will in fact have discovered, namely installing a dictator and collapsing the set of outcomes the rule can ever select. Keep the theorem's name out of the room until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — adversaries finding only manipulations requiring implausible coordination, a committee confusing nobody-found-a-manipulation with there-is-no-manipulation, participants sulking over the payoff — and how I should handle each. Be explicit about what the activity cannot show: the committees will have tested their rules on a handful of profiles at most, so the universal claim that every non-dictatorial rule over three or more alternatives with unrestricted domain admits some manipulable profile will have to be asserted by me in the debrief rather than experienced by them. Note also in the debrief plan the distinction the activity tends to blur — that a rule has some manipulable profile is not that every profile is manipulable, and that failing to find a manipulation is not evidence of strategy-proofness.
