A feature of the modern welfare state has always puzzled me. We maintain elaborate public insurance schemes for misfortunes that strike during a life — illness, workplace accidents, involuntary unemployment — and we finance them through premiums that barely track individual risk, so that the lucky subsidize the unlucky. But we do almost nothing for people who are disadvantaged from the very start: the congenitally unattractive, those with low innate cognitive ability, those born into families and neighborhoods that our schools fail to offset. Both kinds of disadvantage are, from the individual's point of view, matters of luck; yet the accident victim receives a replacement income while the merely unlucky-at-birth receive, at most, a basic safety net. To complicate the picture, unemployment benefits typically come with conditions — an obligation to search for work, and loss of benefits if you quit — while disability benefits come with none. What theory of fairness does this pattern presuppose? Is it some form of luck egalitarianism, on which we compensate bad brute luck but hold people responsible for their choices — and if so, why does the operative line fall between accident victims and the congenitally disadvantaged rather than between chosen and unchosen disadvantage more generally? Or is the better reconstruction something less responsibility-sensitive, like a mutual insurance arrangement among people uncertain of their own risks, under which the accident/birth distinction is not principled at all? Please say which parts of the practice fit which theory, whether the various parts presuppose something consistent or pull in different directions, and what a welfare state built consistently on the strongest reconstruction would have to change.
