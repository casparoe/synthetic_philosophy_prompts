For a term paper in a political philosophy seminar on Ulysses contracts and constitutional self-binding, I've been asked to write a conceptual chapter, and my supervisor's recurring complaint about the literature is that people argue about isolated cases without ever stating what a theory of legitimate precommitment is supposed to deliver. So, before I touch the cases, I want the specification written out. Please take the role of a political philosopher who has worked on both the rational-choice treatment of self-binding (the Elster tradition, from Ulysses and the Sirens onward) and on constitutional theory, and begin with some quiet brainstorming about the logical space before committing to any list. Then write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of legitimate precommitment has to get right, where the purpose to keep in view is classifying cases — given a concrete episode in which a person or a polity arranges things so that a later self cannot do what it would then choose to do, the theory should tell us whether that binding is legitimate, and if not, whether it is voidable or void.

I have some candidate requirements I'd like you to consider, prune, or add to. The theory must get the paradigm cases right: Ulysses asking to be tied to the mast and ordering the crew to ignore whatever he later shouts — noting the awkward feature that the binding is at its strongest exactly when the bound party protests; the recovering alcoholic who hands her stash to a friend with instructions not to return it, and who shows up at midnight; Germany's Basic Law making certain principles unamendable via Article 79(3); and the ugly cases, like a constitution entrenching slavery or an incumbent majority entrenching its own incumbency, which had better come out illegitimate and not merely resemble Ulysses with different content. It must also respect distinctions people are prone to collapse: binding oneself versus binding others who never consented (the dead-hand problem — why may one generation of a demos govern the decisions of its successors?); binding against foreseen akratic relapse versus binding against the future's considered judgment, the second looking more like domination the longer it lasts; precommitment versus mere prediction; and reversible versus irreversible self-binding, including the bootstrapping problem of a clause that can be repealed only through the very procedure it forbids. I'd also like you to decide which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp — for instance, whether a consent requirement is a genuine desideratum or a liberal reflex that the eternity-clause case makes look fatal — and to flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied (I half-expect that strong protection against temporary passions and responsiveness to genuinely changed circumstances will conflict, and that saying so explicitly is itself a result, so don't paper over it). Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories.

Close with a brief scorecard — Elster's account of constitutional precommitment, a Buchanan-style constitutional contractarian view, and Waldron's well-known critique of precommitment in Law and Disagreement — but keep it short, a paragraph at most, since the interesting work is in the criteria and not in the scoring, and I'll be doing the real comparison myself later. A table for the desiderata is fine, as long as each entry carries the motivating case or consideration rather than just a label.
