I run community safety for a mid-sized virtual world platform — a few hundred thousand monthly users, real-cash economy where people buy clothes, pets and land for their avatars, and a moderation team of about 12 handling roughly 300 escalations a week. Most are routine (slurs, spam) but a steady stream are these weird in-between cases and my team handles them totally inconsistently. Some real examples from the last month:

- a user tricked another into "lending" a rare item worth about $200 in real money, then just vanished
- a group of users repeatedly surrounded one user's avatar and performed simulated sexual acts on it every single time she logged in, until she quit the platform entirely
- someone spent six months building a huge cathedral in-world and a griefer crew destroyed it overnight using an exploit that breaks our TOS but isn't illegal
- a user complains he was "scammed" in a card game where lying and bluffing is, honestly, part of the game

The philosophical fight my team keeps having without knowing that's what it is: are virtual things and virtual harms real? Half the team thinks it's all pixels and nobody's actually hurt, the other half wants to treat in-world theft like actual theft. I want a decision procedure we can actually run — a checklist in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and where it lands on "no action" vs "warn" vs "refund/compensate" vs "this is a real-world matter (police, payment dispute)".

One thing I insist on: actually take a position on the metaphysics. Don't give me "some philosophers say X, others say Y" — commit. If you think the harassment victim's distress is a real harm to her even though nothing physical happened, say so and build the procedure on that. Same for whether the $200 item counts as property and whether months of someone's work being wiped counts as a loss. If two of my cases should come out the same way because of the principle you picked, make that explicit even if it sounds weird. And be upfront about which steps can't be settled by checklist and have to punt to a human judgment call — mark those clearly so my moderators know when they're deciding, not applying.

Then run the procedure on the four cases above. At least one should come out somewhere I won't like — that's fine, I'd rather have a consistant answer I disagree with than team-by-team flip-flopping.

Constraints: we have to resolve each escalation within 48 hours, my moderators are not philosophers, and they can only work from chat logs and transaction records. So the procedure needs to fit on one page and be answerable from that evidence alone. No steps that require interviewing the victim about their inner life beyond what they've already said in the complaint.
