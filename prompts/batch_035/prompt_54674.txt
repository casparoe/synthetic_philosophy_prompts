For the last few weeks my local rationalist meetup has been stuck on the question of whether AI-generated images are art, and I've come to suspect the disagreement isn't about AI at all: people are quietly using different definitions of "art" and fighting over the label. I want to build a diagnostic instrument instead of having the argument again.

Please write a decision tree that takes someone from object-level judgments about cases to a named position on the definition of art. The tree should be built from the logical space first — the questions cases force on us, then the positions located in the answer space — not from a recited history of aesthetics. The internal nodes must each be a concrete case with a question a person can answer without knowing which position it commits them to, and the leaves should be the actual positions in the debate: formalism or aesthetic functionalism, the procedural/institutional view, historical/intentional accounts, cluster accounts, and the anti-essentialist claim that "art" is an open concept with no definition at all. If some answer combinations correspond to no defended position, mark the leaf "unnamed" and say in one sentence whether the view is incoherent or just unexplored.

Cases I want on the tree, because I think they do real diagnostic work (feel free to add one or two of your own if a branch needs it):

1. A physically perfect forgery: a canvas visually and chemically indistinguishable from a famous painting, but produced last year by a skilled copyist. Art or not? If not, what exactly is missing?
2. A chimpanzee that smears paint in a way a gallery would hang. Same question.
3. A hermit who spends forty years producing stunning paintings no other human ever sees; she dies, and the paintings are discovered. Did they become art only when discovered, or were they art in the cave?
4. A signed urinal — Duchamp's Fountain and its descendants down to the banana taped to a wall.
5. A work that exists only as a certificate: the "piece" is a signed instruction sheet entitling the owner to fabricate an unremarkable concrete block. The block is identical to the one at a hardware store.
6. A medieval altarpiece produced purely as liturgical equipment, with the craftsmen contractually forbidden from adding anything that wasn't specified by the patron — no aesthetic intent anywhere in the causal chain.
7. An image-generation system that was deliberately trained on no human art (only weather radar and electron micrographs, say) and yet produces images people find inexhaustibly beautiful, with no human choosing anything about them.
8. The provenance flip: a piece everyone treats as profound conceptual art turns out to have been an actual janitor's discarded mop display, mislabeled by accident twenty years ago and never questioned since.

For each leaf, give the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst. Also flag, explicitly, any pair of positions your tree cannot actually separate: I suspect the institutional and historical-intentional branches give the same verdicts on all eight cases, and if so I'd rather be told that than have it papered over with a fifth question that only works by giving away the labels.

Audience is smart laypeople who know no aesthetics but will follow careful case descriptions. Format: a plain-text tree with indent levels, node questions before leaf names, then a short scoring key mapping persistent answer patterns to positions for readers who fall between leaves. Keep the whole thing under 700 words — cut case descriptions to the bone rather than cutting the awkward-commitment notes, which are the part that does the work.
