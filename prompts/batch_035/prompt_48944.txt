I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last spring I was reporting a feature on why careers remain so gendered even in societies that have spent decades trying to un-gender them. I set up what I intended as a routine joint background call with two sources: a developmental psychologist who studies vocational interests across countries, and a sociologist of gender who studies labor markets. It turned into a two-hour argument, and both of them have since followed up with long emails pressing their cases. I need both as sources going forward, I don't want to burn either, and honestly I keep losing track of who has established what. Here is the exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, in order.

1. The psychologist opened with what everyone calls the gender-equality paradox: across countries, the more egalitarian the society — by the standard indices of legal rights, educational access, and labor-market participation — the *wider* the gap between men and women in who earns degrees in science, technology, engineering, and math. The Nordic countries show larger relative gaps than several poorer, less egalitarian countries. He said this is precisely the opposite of what a simple social-constraint story predicts, and that at minimum it means science journalists like me have to stop implying that gendered career choices shrink as equality grows.

2. The sociologist said the inference was far too fast. Equality on those indices means equal rights and equal formal opportunities, not the absence of gendered culture or the absence of economic pressure. Richer, more egalitarian countries are, first of all, rich: a woman there can afford to follow her interests toward people-oriented, often lower-paid work, whereas in a poorer country a medical or engineering degree may be the only route out of precarity, whatever one's interests. So the pattern could be prosperity plus breathing room, not the unveiling of a biological default.

3. The psychologist granted that prosperity matters, but pressed the point anyway: if culture is the residual explanation, then the countries with the most aggressive equality policies and the least overt gender stereotyping somehow coexist with the widest outcome gaps, which is an odd kind of cultural causation. He added the comparative-advantage observation: girls on average outperform boys in reading by more than they lag in math, so wherever students are free to specialize, those who are relatively stronger in reading — more often girls — rationally exit math-intensive tracks, even though girls are perfectly good at math in absolute terms.

4. The sociologist replied that the whole measurement is doing smuggled work. The paradox is defined as a ratio: women's share of STEM degrees relative to their share of all degrees. In absolute numbers, richer and more egalitarian countries graduate more women in STEM than less egalitarian ones — often many more. Whether that counts as "more gendered" depends entirely on which quantity you think tracks the thing that matters, and that choice is a normative one that his colleague was quietly making for the reader.

5. The psychologist said that whatever the metric, the trend line runs the wrong way for the standard narrative, and the policy implication stands: if the goal is parity in every field, then the societies closest to the formal ideal of equality are the furthest from parity in outcomes. That has to complicate remedies premised on the idea that removing obstacles automatically converges on parity.

6. The sociologist closed by saying this only follows if you think choice settles it. No serious person denies that the choices are uncoerced; the question is what shaped the preferences feeding into them. By the logic of move five, a society could never have any reason to examine why its free choices sort so neatly by gender — and that has never been considered a knockdown move in any comparable debate we cover, from consumer protection to voting behavior.

My editor wants me to write a piece that comes down somewhere rather than retreating to "scholars disagree," and I'd rather earn the landing than fake it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether the point as made is right, mistaken, or right-but-not-carrying-the-weight-claimed, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing that a careful piece would need to take a position on.
