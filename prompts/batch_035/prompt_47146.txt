I teach a fairly standard graduate seminar on rational choice, and last week we did Allais. One of my students — the annoying kind who's always one reading ahead — raised her hand and said fine, she accepts she has the Allais preferences (she'd take the sure thing over the gamble in one choice and flip in the other, like most people), and she simply doesn't care, because nobody has ever pumped a cent out of her and nobody ever will. I told her the coherence arguments have her dead to rights: any agent whose preferences aren't representable as expected utility maximization is exploitable, some bookie can run a money pump on her and take her whole bankroll, and her intuitions about a sure thing are a liability that the market will eventually collect on. She just shrugged and said "show me the sequence of trades, then."

Here's my problem: when I sat down to actually write the pump on the board, I couldn't get it to run. The classic pumps I know — the ones with the coffee, the radio, and the vacation day, the ones I can actually draw — all turn on preference cycles, taking A over B, B over C, and C over A, and then you just walk around the cycle charging a small fee at each step. But my student's preferences are transitive; she violates independence, not transitivity, and every attempt I made to build a pump out of the two Allais choices needed the bookie to re-offer her a choice she'd already declined, or needed her to be indifferent enough to accept a tiny fee for switching when she clearly wouldn't. It also bugged me that all my constructions assumed she'd evaluate each trade on its own, in the moment, rather than just deciding once, upfront, "I'm not trading past this point" — which she says she'd obviously do, and which seems rational enough to me when I think about how actual people handle predatory salesmen.

So that's what I want from you. First, walk me through, concretely, how (or whether) you can money-pump an agent with transitive Allais-type preferences: what exactly does the bookie offer, in what order, and what assumption about the agent does each step require her to satisfy? And second, help me figure out what I should actually claim in next week's seminar: if the pump only works when you load it up with extra assumptions — the agent is myopic, she treats each decision as a fresh choice, there's a competitive market of bookies with no transaction costs, whatever it is — then what is it, precisely, that the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization DO establish, and how much of the usual "rational agents must be EU maximizers" line survives? I don't want to overcorrect into "the theorems are junk," but I also don't want to stand up there and tell thirty students a story I can't cash out at the blackboard.
