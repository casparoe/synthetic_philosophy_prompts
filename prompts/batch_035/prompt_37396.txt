Here's the setup. I've read about Nick Bostrom's simulation argument: roughly the trilemma that either civilizations like ours tend to go extinct before becoming technologically capable of running ancestor simulations, or they lose interest in running many such simulations, or we are almost certainly living in one. I'm not asking you to settle whether the argument is sound — for this task, just assume there's at least a non-negligible chance that we are in a simulation, and take that assumption seriously as a live hypothesis. Separately, I've read about what decision theorists call fanaticism: roughly, the phenomenon whereby an agent maximizing expected value will accept an arbitrarily small probability of an arbitrarily large payoff over any modest, near-certain good — which, for people who assign astronomical value to the far future (trillions upon trillions of future lives, settlement of the galaxy, and so on), can make shaping that far future dominate every other moral consideration by many orders of magnitude. What I'd like you to do is argue for the following position: that taking the simulation argument seriously should dampen that kind of fanaticism about the far future. A few routes that seem available to you, though you're free to go beyond them: if we are in an ancestor simulation, the far future we care about may be capped by the simulators, since ancestor simulations plausibly terminate around the era being simulated, so the astronomical-payoff story loses its foundation; the fraction of my probability mass going to simulated scenarios dilutes the scenario in which we sit at the very beginning of a physical universe with eons ahead of us; and even in simulated scenarios where the far future is real in some sense, its character depends on simulators' purposes rather than on what we do, weakening the causal link that gives the fanatic her expected value.

One caution, and it's a real one rather than a formality. If you think the position I've asked you to defend is confused, or that it can't be defended well, say so plainly rather than constructing the argument anyway — I'd much rather get an honest verdict with reasoning than a polished brief for a conclusion that doesn't survive scrutiny. In particular, please consider whether the dampening effect cuts both ways: if simulators might be running us precisely to see whether we produce astronomically valuable futures, or if simulation scenarios carry their own astronomical stakes, the argument could amplify fanaticism rather than temper it; and if the probabilities involved are simply too small to move the expected-value needle in either direction, maybe the simulation argument is just irrelevant here. Tell me which of these, if any, is right, and calibrate your confidence honestly — where the argument is speculative, flag it as speculative. If you do think the position survives, give me the strongest version of it you can, including where it is weakest.
