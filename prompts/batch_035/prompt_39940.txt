I am a novelist, and for the past few years I have been writing fictionalized lives: books that take a real historical figure and, without inventing the major events, imagine the minor ones — what she said over breakfast, how he stood when the bad news came. I have always justified the project on Plutarch's authority: at the opening of his life of Alexander he distinguishes his work from history, saying in effect that he is engaged with lives rather than with histories, and that a small thing — an unconsidered remark, a joke, a gesture — can show more of a person's character than pitched battles with their tens of thousands dead. My novels are an attempt to supply exactly those small things.

Last month an editor whom I trust dismantled the whole justification over one dinner, and I have not been able to rebuild it since. He set the case out as a chain of steps, each of which looked acceptable to me at the time:

(1) If a life is to teach morally, it must teach something about its subject's character — the pattern of choice that made the life admirable — and not merely about the times the subject lived through.
(2) Character, as distinct from luck, shows itself only in choices; and a choice is fixed by the agent's reasons, not by the outward deed, since the same deed can issue from courage or from vanity, and the difference is everything.
(3) The biographer never has the reasons. What survives of a life is the outward deed as recorded by interested witnesses, together with the subject's own curated self-telling — material selected over decades precisely for legibility.
(4) What the biographer therefore exhibits is not the subject's character but a reconstruction governed throughout by the biographer's antecedent idea of what an admirable character looks like.
(5) A reconstruction governed throughout by an antecedent idea can teach nothing: whatever the reader takes from it was already in the prior, and a lesson that was already in the prior is not a lesson but a mirror.
(6) So the moral use of biography is a respectable fiction: readers of Plutarch, and readers of the lives of the saints, were never learning from Lycurgus or from the blessed; they were finding their own ideals illustrated back at them and calling the illustration instruction. Biography in this mode is hagiography throughout — Plutarch is merely hagiography with better manners.

When I protested that I had in fact learned from lives, he extended the argument without pausing: the same, he said, holds of my own novels, since they too are constructions governed from first to last by my antecedent idea of the admirable, so whatever my readers take from them was in them before they opened the book. That is the part I cannot accept — I have watched readers be changed by books, sometimes by mine — and yet I cannot locate the flaw: each step still looks fine to me taken on its own. My instinct is that the trouble enters at (5), because that is where the argument stops being about history and starts being about my own trade; but I am aware that this may be self-interest rather than diagnosis.

You are a professional philosopher working on the border of moral psychology and the philosophy of literature, at home in the classical tradition of exemplary biography. Think carefully before answering, and do not soften the diagnosis to spare my project. First restate the argument in one sentence, so that I can see you have understood it. Then tell me which single step is the weakest, why that step looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs — what each way out gives up elsewhere. If instead you think the chain is valid and I must live with the conclusion, say so plainly, and set out what living with it would commit me to.
