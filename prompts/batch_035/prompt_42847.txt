I am a novelist, and for the past several weeks I have been waiting on the announcement of a literary prize. To pass the time, and because I have a novelist's habit of taking notes on my own mind, I have kept a record of how my confidences move when people give me information in conditional form — remarks of the shape "if such-and-such, then so-and-so." I now have five such remarks, each with my verdict on how it ought to move my credence that my novel will win, and I have come to suspect that these verdicts do not all answer to one principle. I would like to know whether they do, and if not, which of them I must surrender.

First, the cast and the numbers. Three novels contend in effect for two places on the shortlist: mine, a debut, and a veteran's. When the remarks began I put my chance of being shortlisted at about three in ten, and my chance of winning the prize outright at about one in ten. The remarks came at different moments, so my numbers were not the same for each; I give them as they stood.

First remark, from my agent, who has seen the judges' working notes: "If your book makes the shortlist, the judges will prefer it to the other finalist, two to one." My verdict: this roughly doubled my credence in winning, from a tenth to about a fifth, while leaving my three-in-ten for the shortlist exactly where it was. The remark told me what happens in the event; it could not be evidence about whether the event occurs.

Second remark, the same words from the same agent, but a day later, after a leak of the longlist put my book on it beyond serious doubt, so that my credence in being shortlisted stood at nineteen in twenty. My verdict: this time the conditional was nearly invisible. I moved almost as much as if she had told me outright that the judges prefer my book two to one. The same content, under a near-certain antecedent, behaved like an unconditional assertion.

Third remark, from a friend consoling me in advance: "If your book doesn't make the shortlist, the debut will take the prize rather than the veteran, two to one." My verdict, or rather my difficulty: this remark was expressly about the world in which I fail, so I feel it cannot touch my credence in failing — not by an inch. Yet whenever I sit down and try to write out a set of odds that feels internally consistent, I find my shortlist credence written a little higher than before, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine constraint or an artifact of my bookkeeping. I record it as a verdict with a trembling hand: the shortlist credence should not move, though something in me keeps moving it.

Fourth remark, from my editor, over wine: "If your publisher had held the book for the autumn season, the judges would have taken to the ending far better." My verdict: no update at all. The remark is about a world that did not happen; it flatters me, but it is not evidence about this one.

Fifth remark, from the same consoling friend, earlier in the year: "If anyone from your cohort wins, it will be you." My verdict: this raised my credence in winning a little, but it also lowered my credence that anyone from my cohort wins at all — because a friend who believed in the cohort would have said "you can win," and the choice to put it conditionally seemed to carry news of its own, news about the antecedent.

So there is the difficulty in plain view. My first verdict says the content of a conditional leaves its antecedent's probability alone; my fifth says the mere form of a conditional can move an antecedent's probability down. My second verdict says a conditional fades into an assertion as its antecedent becomes certain; my fourth says a conditional with an impossible antecedent is inert. And my third sits between gut and bookkeeping, unable to decide whether a remark about my failure can raise my credence in failing. I am told there is a textbook rule for updating on evidence that arrives uncertain, and that it is known to misbehave on remarks shaped like my friend's consolation; I do not know the mathematics, though I can follow arithmetic, and I would be grateful for whatever can be said plainly.

What I would like from you is this. Is there a single principle — or failing that, a short list of candidates — that stands behind my five verdicts? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which clause earns its keep from which remark. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and what it costs to save it: I want to know whether I should give up the verdict or accept a more complicated principle. And finally, apply the surviving principle to a remark I have not yet rendered a verdict on, my agent's last message before the announcement: "If the shortlist leaks before Thursday, your book is on it." I put the chance of a leak at about one in twenty. Does that message move my credence in being shortlisted at all — and if so, in which direction and by roughly how much? Does it move my credence in winning, or does it leave that untouched? I ask because I have gone back and forth on it for three days, and I would like to stop.
