Before I wade into the whole species-concepts literature I want the spec for what would even count as winning. Write the desiderata any adequate species concept has to meet: the cases it has to get right (ring species, asexual lineages, hybridizing populations that exchange some genes but stay distinct, chronospecies along an unbroken fossil sequence, organisms with rampant horizontal gene transfer), the practical jobs it can't botch (it has to be usable by a working taxonomist with finite data, conservation law needs countable units, and species boundaries can't depend on knowing the lineage's whole evolutionary future), the distinctions it can't collapse (being a species vs. being a diagnosable population; and treat the view that species are individuals rather than kinds as a live contender, not a given), and the empirical results it can't contradict (e.g. it can't merge sympatric sister taxa that are fully reproductively isolated). For each requirement give the case behind it and mark which are non-negotiable and which are basically preferences of one camp — applicability to asexuals looks like it matters mainly to the microbiology crowd, but you tell me wether that's fair. If any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable (I suspect universality across all life plus treating species as real countable units might be), say so explicitly — that's the part I actually want, since then the list itself is my thesis. Skip the scorecard of the ~twenty named concepts, just maybe gesture at how the biological and phylogenetic concepts do against your list. And when you're done, seperate out what's genuinely settled among people who work on this from what's still contested — I don't want to write the paper as if one side already quietly won.
