For the last three decades of my working life I ran numerical weather prediction models, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science to work out what my career actually demonstrated. I have settled on a definite view and want to test it against a capable opponent: chaos is a fact about our knowledge, not about the world; the underlying dynamics is deterministic, and sensitive dependence on initial conditions means only that our measurements have finite precision. Please play a philosopher of science who rejects precisely this, and let her take her strongest line: she may concede that the equations on my screen are deterministic, but she will argue that a deterministic-but-chaotic world and a genuinely stochastic world could agree on every possible observation, so that determinism, once transferred from model to world, becomes metaphysical decoration rather than a scientific claim. She should be rigorous, willing to grant small points but never the main one, and should press hardest on why I believe the model's determinism carries over to the atmosphere at all. Stay in character until I write 'stop', and keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose, with no headings, lists, or other apparatus. My opening move: I have watched a deterministic model, started twice from initial conditions differing only in the sixteenth decimal place, produce two utterly different forecasts within three simulated weeks, and I never for a moment believed anything chancy had intruded — the divergence was in the arithmetic of the equations and the initial conditions together, nothing else. A theory that tells me exactly how and why my forecast will fail is earning its keep; that makes determinism more empirical, not less.
