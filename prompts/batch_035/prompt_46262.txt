I'm a machine learning engineer who spends most of my time on the preference-modeling side of RLHF: training reward models on pairwise human judgments, fitting Bradley-Terry scores, and then having to turn a pile of per-labeler preference data into one scalar reward that a policy can be optimized against. Lately the aggregation step has started to feel philosophically loaded. We pick a way to combine labelers' preferences — majority vote, averaging imputed utilities, z-scoring everyone's outputs before summing — and each choice silently seems to take a stance on whether the numbers we're adding together mean the same thing across different people, which I suspect is not something any of us actually believes we've established.

When I mentioned this to a friend who studied philosophy, she told me I had reinvented, in miniature, the classic debate over interpersonal comparisons of utility: Lionel Robbins's ordinalist challenge that one person's utility is introspective and inaccessible to observation, so there is no scientific basis for putting it on a common scale with another's; the way utilitarian aggregation and old-school welfare economics simply assumed the comparability Robbins doubted; Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and his impartial-observer construction as an attempted justification; Arrow's impossibility theorem as the formal price of refusing comparability; and the various rescue attempts since — extended preferences, veil-of-ignorance devices, and the thought that sufficiently structured choices reveal enough to make comparisons meaningful. So here is my ask: restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use — pairwise comparisons, Bradley-Terry likelihoods, calibration, affine invariance of utility, reward normalization, scalarization weights, distribution shift — as faithfully as you can manage, saying for each philosophical position where it shows up in my pipeline, or where the closest analog lives.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which come out distorted by the translation, and which have no counterpart at all in what we do? I especially want your judgment on whether my pipeline smuggles in interpersonal comparability without noticing — for instance, whether z-scoring labeler outputs before averaging amounts to a substantive comparability assumption masquerading as a mundane preprocessing step — and, in the other direction, whether anything my field worries about (miscalibrated or adversarial annotators, trying to model a single canonical "human" as if that were a coherent target) is genuinely new or just the old debate re-run on GPUs. Finally, tell me where you think the residue lies: does it point to something my engineering vocabulary can't currently say but should be able to, or does it suggest that some of the philosophers' worry was never as substantive as it sounded?
