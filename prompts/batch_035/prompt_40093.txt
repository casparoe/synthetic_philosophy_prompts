I teach an introductory philosophy of science course, and every year the unit on interpretations of probability goes the same way: students memorize the labels — frequentism, subjectivism, propensity theory — and a few slogans attached to them, then use whichever label sounds most sophisticated without noticing that their answers to concrete cases commit them to something else entirely. Someone who says they're a frequentist because "probability is just long-run frequency" will cheerfully assign a probability to a coin that has already been flipped and is sitting under a cup, which no actual frequency is available for. Someone who says probability is "just degree of belief" will refuse to say the die on my desk has any probability of coming up six before I've decided to throw it. So I want to sort them by what they do, not by what they say.

Please write a diagnostic quiz for my students: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a student can answer without ever seeing the names of the positions. The cases should be ones where the interpretations genuinely come apart, and I'd like at least these to appear among them: the probability that a fair coin already flipped and hidden under a cup shows heads; the probability that the ten-billionth digit of pi is a 7, before anyone has computed it; the probability that this particular uranium atom decays in the next hour, given that the decay is genuinely indeterministic; whether a die that will in fact never be rolled has any probability of landing six; and whether a well-calibrated forecaster's credence that a one-off election goes a certain way counts as a probability or merely as a useful stand-in for one. Beyond those, choose cases you think do the best diagnostic work.

Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions: actual frequentism (finite and hypothetical-infinite variants, if the answers separate them), subjectivism (with the betting/calibration reading flagged), single-case and long-run propensity views, and a deflationary "probability is just the measure-theoretic formalism, and interpreting it is somebody else's problem" option as a leaf. The key should explain, for each position, which pattern of answers lands a student there and which single answer most embarrasses them. Please also flag two things explicitly: any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate because all my questions give them the same answers (my guess is the two propensity variants will be a problem — if so, either fix it with a question that splits them or say honestly that it can't be fixed at this level), and any coherent pattern of answers that leads to an unoccupied leaf — a combination nobody in the literature actually defends — with a sentence on whether that's because the view is untenable or merely unpopular. Please keep the whole thing usable as a one-page class handout; the students are first-years with no measure theory, so no question should require them to compute anything.
