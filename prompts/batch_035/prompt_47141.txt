For a seminar on causation in historiography, I would like a case analysis that runs the outbreak of the First World War in July–August 1914 through the metaphysical apparatus of overdetermination, and then runs the apparatus back through July 1914. The metaphysical concept is the strict one from the literature on redundant causation: two or more independent conditions obtain, each individually sufficient for the effect, and neither preempts the other — the structure of the textbook case in which two assassins independently fire fatal shots at the same moment. The question is whether the war is a real instance of that structure, and what the answer does to both the metaphysics and the historiography.

Please organize the analysis as follows.

1. Classification, not cataloguing. Take the familiar candidate causes of the war: the Austro-Hungarian decision to exploit Sarajevo to settle accounts with Serbia; the German encouragement of that decision in early July 1914 (the so-called blank check); the Russian decision to mobilize in support of Serbia; the rigid mobilization schedules, above all the German plan that made a war with Russia appear to require attacking France first and thus almost guaranteed British entry; the British cabinet's prolonged ambiguity about whether it would fight; and the Serbian nationalist milieu that produced the assassination itself. For each, determine whether it is (a) an individually sufficient cause that would have brought a general European war even in the absence of the others — making the war genuinely overdetermined; (b) merely one necessary element in a sufficient set, in the sense of the NESS test familiar from legal causation, so that the war is conjunctively multi-causal rather than overdetermined; or (c) a preempted or preempting cause, on the model of the two-assassin cases where one shot kills and the other would have. Defend each classification with the corresponding counterfactual: hold everything else fixed, remove this condition alone, and ask whether a general war still arrives by August 1914.

2. Where the counterfactual test strains. In overdetermination cases the counterfactual test is famously uninformative: remove either assassin and the victim still dies, so each cause appears to make no difference to the outcome. Show where exactly this un-informativeness surfaces in the historiography of 1914. Note how it licenses opposite readings: the Fischer controversy over German war aims (Germany's Aims in the First World War, 1961) treated the non-difference of the other powers' contributions as showing that German decision-making was the differentiating, sufficient cause; Clark's sleepwalkers reading of 2012 treats the same non-difference as showing that responsibility was distributed across all the capitals because no single power's choice made the difference. Say whether each reading is an honest application of the redundancy structure or an exploitation of the counterfactual test's silence, and where you think the balance lies.

3. Levels and silences. Metaphysical overdetermination is synchronic and applies to precise events; historians' causes operate at several levels at once — trigger, decision, background condition, structural cause. Address directly whether the apparatus has anything principled to say about the distinction between the assassination as trigger and the alliance blocs, the arms races, and the domestic-political fragilities of the empires as structure. If the answer is that the metaphysics is simply silent at the structural level, say so rather than forcing the mapping, and consider whether the episode reveals a causal formation the metaphysical vocabulary has not yet named — for example, a case in which each candidate cause is individually sufficient only given the others, so that the sufficiency relations are circular rather than independent, which is neither the conjunctive case nor the two-assassin case.

4. The verdict in both directions. Conclude by saying what overdetermination, properly handled, illuminates about July 1914, and what July 1914 does to overdetermination: whether the historiographic practice confirms the concept's usefulness outside the thought experiments, exposes it as an anomaly it cannot absorb, or presents a case it never anticipated. Where the fit is partial, mark the boundary explicitly rather than presenting a tidier picture than the evidence allows.

I am interested in the disciplined mapping, not in a retelling of the July crisis; the standard facts of the episode can be assumed as known and should be referred to rather than narrated at length.
