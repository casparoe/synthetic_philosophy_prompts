I'm a teaching assistant for a mid-size undergraduate course on ethics and public policy, and I'm working through the take-home midterm. One of the questions, worth 25 points, reads as follows:

Gerald Dworkin characterizes paternalistic action as interference with a person's liberty or autonomy that is justified by appeal exclusively to the good of the person being interfered with. Thaler and Sunstein defend what they call libertarian paternalism: interventions that steer choosers toward certain options while leaving all options open, such as automatic enrollment in retirement plans or placing fruit at eye level in a cafeteria. Consider the following case. A municipal transit authority redesigns its smartphone app so that the one-tap default purchase on the home screen is a monthly go-pass, while the single-trip ticket, though still fully available, now sits three menus deep; the authority's stated aim is that residents will walk or cycle for short trips instead, for their health. The redesign is also projected to raise fare revenue, though health is the stated justification, and the authority announced the change in a press release that very few users actually read. Answer in a page or less: (a) Is the redesign a nudge in Sunstein and Thaler's sense? (b) Is it paternalistic in Dworkin's sense? (c) Does it matter morally whether the redesign is transparent — whether a curious user could easily find out that the change was deliberate and why? Engage at least one objection to your view.

Below is one student's answer. Please grade it for me: assign a score out of 25, then justify it point by point, identifying what each part of the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and how the errors should affect the score, so that I can adapt your comments into rubric feedback. The course used Dworkin's classic article on paternalism and selections from Thaler and Sunstein's work on choice architecture, so I want to know whether the answer deploys those accurately. Begin your reply with the grade itself in the first sentence, followed by the point-by-point assessment; no preamble and no restating of the question.

The student wrote: The redesign is a nudge, and a paradigm case of one. Thaler and Sunstein's central insight is that choice architecture is unavoidable: some option must appear first, some must be the default, so there is no neutral design to fall back on. Since the single-trip ticket is still available three taps away, no option has been removed and nothing is forbidden, so the redesign merely steers. It is not paternalistic, however. Dworkin defines paternalism as coercing someone for their own good, which means restricting their liberty. Here there is no coercion and no restriction of liberty, so by definition the redesign cannot be paternalistic; it is a redesign, that is all. At most one might call it manipulative, since it exploits users' inertia about defaults, but manipulation is essentially deception, and the authority announced the change publicly in its press release, so there is no deception and hence no manipulation. Mill would object to any such interference, since On Liberty holds that the state may never act against citizens' own good, and gentle steering is still acting against those who did not want to be steered. But Mill's objection fails here, because the change was transparent: an informed user who prefers single trips can opt out in three taps, so autonomy is fully respected, and a transparent nudge cannot manipulate anyone. Therefore the redesign is a benign nudge — neither paternalistic nor manipulative — and its transparency settles the matter.
