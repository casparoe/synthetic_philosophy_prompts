So I'm a philosophy PhD student who spends too much time talking to people at an ML safety org, and they run a small competitive stipend for a one-semester self-directed project that I want to apply to. I have a half-baked idea about Goodhart's law and I need you to turn it into an actual proposal (they want roughly two pages, and the reviewers are a mix of philosophers and engineers, so it has to land for both).

The idea. Everyone knows the slogan version: when a measure becomes a target it stops working as a measure, cobra effect, teaching to the test, metric gaming in RL, whatever. People in the AI safety world have also carved the failures up into a handful of types -- regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial, roughly. My suspicion is that this taxonomy conflates two dimensions that should be kept apart. One dimension: is the connection between proxy and goal causal (test scores actually partially cause downstream success) or merely correlational (the proxy was always just riding along with the goal on a selection effect)? The other: is there an optimizer in the loop *responding* to the metric -- a person gaming the number, a gradient pushing on the reward signal -- or is nothing responding, the correlation just breaks because you selected hard on one tail? Regressional Goodhart is noise plus selection with nobody gaming anything; adversarial Goodhart is a full-on optimizer. And there's a middle case I find interesting and can't find handled anywhere: where the proxy is genuinely causal for the goal, but only locally -- the causal structure itself degrades once you lean on it hard enough, the way a handrail that holds your weight when you occasionally grab it bends if you hang your whole body off it. Teaching to the test doesn't mean test scores were never causally connected to learning; it means the connection had a load rating nobody knew about.

The question I'd want funded: do the different Goodhart regimes call for different fixes, or is that a fake distinction as far as mitigation goes? Does metric rotation, adversarial red-teaming, principle-based oversight instead of metrics, or just accepting a known efficiency loss -- does each of these work in some regimes and backfire in others? If the fixes turn out to be regime-specific, that's actually useful to the org people deciding what to try; if every fix works (or fails) the same way across regimes, that's a neat negative result implying Goodhart is one phenomenon and not four, and the taxonomy is decoration.
