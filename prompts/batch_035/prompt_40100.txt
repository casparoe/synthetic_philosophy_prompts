Write a dialogue between two members of a hospital ethics committee: a convinced utilitarian and a convinced Scanlonian contractualist. The committee has just finished reviewing a case from a mass-casualty event last spring, in which a rescue crew with time and fuel for one trip chose to evacuate five injured workers from one site rather than a single worker from another. The utilitarian had signed off on the choice as obvious; the contractualist had dissented — not because five lives count for less than one, but because she could not find, within the contractualist apparatus, any single person's complaint that the choice of the five answers better than the one survivor's complaint, and she suspects the committee smuggled in arithmetic without a license.

The two should argue it out in the empty conference room afterwards. Both must be at full strength and neither may be a strawman: the utilitarian should press the claim that refusing to count lives is a kind of moral negligence, and should at some point turn the tables by asking what her view requires when the single patient has far more life-years at stake than the five combined; the contractualist should explain what reasonable rejection does in rescue cases, why Scanlon's individualist restriction blocks the mere summing of complaints, what she makes of flipping a coin Taurek-style between the two sites, and how she handles the transmitter-room scenario — millions of mildly inconvenienced television viewers versus one technician kept from unbearable pain — which the utilitarian should deploy as his hardest case. Give each character at least eight substantive turns, make them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past each other, and have each concede at least one real point to the other along the way.

Then append two titled sections. "Where the exchange turned" (roughly 150 words) should identify the single move in the dialogue that did the most work for either side. "My verdict" (roughly 300 words) should take a definite position on whether contractualism can account for the moral significance of the numbers without smuggling aggregation back in, and defend that position rather than merely cataloguing the options.
