A guy on my team is basically a selection-maximalist about AI safety, and his argument is tighter than I want it to be. It goes like this:

(1) Alignment work only matters if it ends up in deployed systems, which means it has to survive inside a firm that stays alive.
(2) Firms in a competitive market are under something like selection: the ones that ship capabilities faster get the users, the revenue, the funding, the talent; the ones that fall behind shrink or get absorbed.
(3) Any safety measure that carries an alignment tax — anything that adds compute, delays a release, caps a capability, slows an eval loop — is by definition a competitiveness cost. That's what "tax" means here.
(4) In a winner-take-most race, small taxes compound: you lose the round, which loses you the users, which loses you the data, which loses you the next round.
(5) So firms carrying any nonzero alignment tax get selected out over time, and the only measures that can persist in the population of surviving systems are the ones that are free on capabilities or actively speed them up.
(6) Therefore the whole alignment agenda reduces to making safety a byproduct of capabilities work. Everything else is temporary — it'll live in a lab or a blog post but never in a deployed system.

The reason I can't sleep is that (6) seems to prove too much. Run the same argument on security audits, HR compliance, employee safety, product testing — those are taxes in exactly the same sense, they slow shipping and cost money — yet plenty of companies carry them and survive, some for a century. So either those aren't really taxes (and then what is he even talking about), or something in the chain is wrong, and I can't tell which.

He's pretty sure I'm going to attack (4), the compounding step, and he thinks that's the weakest move since winner-take-most dynamics in tech are real and the empirics there are genuinely ugly. If you do reject (4), tell me what rejecting it costs, because "small taxes don't always compound" needs some story about when they do and don't. But honestly I'd also like to hear about (3) and (5) — something about the way he slides from "this firm gets selected out" to "the measure can never persist anywhere" smells like it's assuming the selection environment is fixed, and I can't put my finger on whether that's the equivocation or whether that's just true.

Also, one more thing: he keeps saying the fix can't be "firms will just voluntarily carry the tax because they're responsible," since that's exactly what (5) rules out. If the repair is something like "the environment isn't fixed — liability, trust, regulation change what gets selected for" — then say that plainly, and tell me what it implies strategically. Does it mean the actual move is to change the selection environment rather than hope any single firm eats the tax? Where does the argument go wrong?
