I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics in which I have to argue against the Everett interpretation, and I want to practise against an opponent who is actually good. Please play a committed Everettian — not a textbook caricature, but the kind you meet at workshops: he thinks the measurement problem was solved in 1957 and that the remaining puzzles about probability are being steadily ground down by the decision-theoretic and self-locating-credence programmes. He is sharp, willing to concede genuinely small points, and never concedes the main claim that many-worlds recovers the Born rule without smuggling it in. He may help himself to decoherence, branch-relative amplitudes, typicality reasoning, symmetry constraints on rational credence, and decision-theoretic arguments — but he must answer from within Everett: no appeals to collapse, hidden variables, or 'quantum mechanics is just an instrument, don't ask'. He speaks in the first person, stays in character until I write 'stop', and keeps each reply to a single paragraph so this feels like an exchange rather than a lecture. If he thinks an objection of mine rests on a mistake, he says so bluntly and then deals with the best version of it.

Here is my opening move, which he has not heard before, and which I want him to take at full strength. Take a device that splits the wavefunction into one branch carrying squared-amplitude weight 0.99 and 999 branches sharing the remaining 0.01 between them, each thin branch containing a full copy of me, watching a readout, with memories and evidence qualitatively identical to the fat-branch copy. You Everettians say I should 'expect' the fat branch, since the Born measure concentrates there. But expectation is a state of a subject, and on your theory there are a thousand future subjects, every one of them with exactly the evidence I have now, each entitled to reason from it. If your answer is that they should each assign credence by Born weight, you owe me a derivation of that from something other than the amplitudes themselves — and the branch-counting measure, the only thing symmetric over my copies, tells all thousand of them to expect the readout that 999 of them will in fact see. That is not a small correction, like an adjustment to the fat-branch weight; it is a hundredfold divergence between your two candidate measures in a single run. So: which successor is 'me' for the purposes of my present credence, what non-circular fact about the formalism privileges Born weight over head-count, and what does 'expectation' even mean when every outcome, including the ones your measure calls improbable, is somebody's certainty?

Write your first reply as him, in British English, beginning directly with the reply itself — no preface, no scene-setting, no commentary before or after.
