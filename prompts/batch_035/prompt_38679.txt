I'm a PhD student in political theory writing a chapter on scholastic theories of resistance, and I keep circling a problem in Aquinas's De Regno (On Kingship, addressed to the king of Cyprus) that I'd like your help thinking through. The treatise runs on an analogy: the political community is like a living body. Aquinas argues that just as a body would disintegrate unless some general ruling force within it watched over the common good of all the members, so a multitude of people, each pursuing his own interest, would scatter unless something impelled them toward the common good; the king, correspondingly, is identified with the heart, the first principle of life and motion, from which the members receive what they need to function, and the bees with their single ruler are trotted out as nature's own testimony that one head is best. The analogy is doing serious work: it establishes monarchy as the natural regime, and it installs the premise that each part exists for the good of the whole rather than conversely.

The trouble arises in the later discussion of whether one may proceed against tyrants. There Aquinas gestures at the opposite analogy, inherited from Augustine's City of God, according to which a kingdom without justice differs from a robber-band mainly in scale — and indeed he notes that the Hebrews who killed their oppressors, Ehud dispatching Eglon and Jael driving the tent-peg through Sisera, are praised in Scripture. On the robber-band picture, tyrannicide looks like self-defense against a highwayman writ large. Yet Aquinas's verdict is cautious: private persons should not presume to kill the tyrant, because proceeding against his cruelty belongs to public authority, because the killing of tyrants has historically been followed by many worse ones, and because where no human remedy avails one should have recourse to God. In the Summa's treatment of sedition, meanwhile, he says that disturbing a tyrannical government is not seditious at all, since such government is not directed to the common good — apparently the tyrant himself is the seditious one. My advisor thinks the body analogy is quietly carrying all of these conclusions, and that once you notice where it breaks, the caution about private tyrannicide loses its support. I suspect the analogy is doing less work than she claims, but I can't yet show it.

So: audit the analogy for me. First, give the mapping explicitly — kingdom to body, king to heart, citizens and artisans to members, civic unity and peace to health, tyranny to disease, deposition to the removal of a diseased part — and then say where the mapping breaks. Which disanalogies actually matter for the tyrannicide question (body parts can't judge, deliberate, or consent; they have no claim against the whole; a body cannot misdiagnose itself, has no succession problem, and no one else can perform surgery on it) and which are idle? Second, tell me whether Aquinas's practical verdict — that public deposition can be licit while private tyrannicide is not — is generated by the body analogy, or by prudential considerations (scandal, usurpation of public authority, the empirical record of worse successors) that would survive the analogy's collapse. Third, explain how the robber-band comparison is supposed to fit alongside the body comparison: is the tyrant a diseased organ of the body politic or an external predator on it, and does Aquinas need one picture for the question of legitimacy and the other for the question of remedy? And finally, if neither analogy can carry the weight, propose a better one for his considered position.
