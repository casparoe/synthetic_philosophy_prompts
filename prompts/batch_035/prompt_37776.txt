Question (25 points): Inverse reinforcement learning treats an observed agent as approximately rational and attempts to recover the reward function that the agent appears to be maximizing. (a) Explain why the IRL problem is in general ill-posed: why can many distinct reward functions explain the very same demonstrations? (b) Describe assumptions or regularizers commonly used to make the inference well-posed, and state precisely what each one buys in terms of identifiability. (c) Suppose we want to use IRL-style methods to infer a particular person's preferences, for instance to build an assistant that acts on their behalf. Discuss at least one fundamental difficulty this raises and one way it might be partially addressed.

Student's answer: (a) IRL is ill-posed because reward functions are only a device for generating behavior: what we observe is a policy, and the mapping from rewards to policies is many-to-one. Any reward that makes the demonstrated behavior optimal is consistent with the data, so demonstrations alone cannot narrow things down. In the worst case the demonstrations are nearly uninformative — if the demonstrator's optimal policy is to stay put, or only visits a small part of the state space, the reward on unvisited states is completely unconstrained. Two symmetries are worth noting: positively rescaling a reward leaves the induced optimal policy unchanged, and potential-based shaping transformations also leave the optimal policy invariant. In fact these are essentially the only transformations with that property — whenever two reward functions induce the same optimal policies, they differ by a positive rescaling composed with potential-based shaping. Ng and Russell, who introduced IRL in 2000, resolved the degeneracy by modeling the demonstrator as Boltzmann-rational rather than strictly optimal, which breaks the ties. (b) The main regularizer used today is the maximum-entropy principle: among all policies consistent with the demonstrations, prefer the one with the highest entropy, and then find the reward under which the demonstrated behavior is most probable. If the reward is linear in the same features whose empirical counts we match, the reward is uniquely determined — once the demonstrator's expected feature counts are matched, the weights follow. A second common assumption is a fixed, known discount factor, which removes the confound between how much the demonstrator values future states and how patient they are. In practice one also assumes the demonstrator is noisily optimal, so that even suboptimal demonstrations remain evidence about the reward rather than being uninterpretable. (c) The worry people raise is that humans aren't rational, so IRL will infer the wrong preferences. But this overstates the problem. Under the revealed-preference view, a person's preferences simply are whatever rationalizes their choices, so the reward recovered from behavior is by definition the person's true preferences; irrationality is a fact about our model of the person, not a defect in the inference. Hyperbolic discounting, for example, is just evidence that the human's discount factor drifts over time, and a sufficiently flexible IRL system can fit a time-varying discount factor and still recover the underlying reward. The real remaining worry is noise, which makes the methods data-hungry. Overall, then, IRL is on firm ground as an approach to value learning, provided the demonstrator model is made flexible enough.
