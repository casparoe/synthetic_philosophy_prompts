I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on confirmation theory, and I want to build the paper around the classic case study: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion and Einstein's general theory of relativity. The episode, in brief: by the late nineteenth century, astronomers knew that Mercury's perihelion precesses by roughly 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian mechanics plus the perturbations from the other planets can account for. Various ad hoc fixes were proposed, most famously the hypothetical planet Vulcan, and none panned out. In November 1915, Einstein derived the perihelion advance from his field equations and found that general relativity yields almost exactly 43 arcseconds per century without any adjustable parameter fitted to do so — reportedly one of the great emotional moments of his life. Crucially, the anomaly was old evidence: everyone, including Einstein, already knew the number with certainty before the theory was ever on the table.

The lens I want applied is Bayesian confirmation theory, and specifically the problem of old evidence. In the standard framework, if an agent assigns E probability 1, then P(E|H) = P(E) = 1, so no Bayesian measure of confirmation — difference, ratio, likelihood-based, anything — assigns general relativity any confirmation whatsoever from the perihelion fit. That is plainly the wrong verdict, and the episode is the canonical motivation for the problem. I'd like a careful, disciplined mapping rather than a survey. Work through what plays the role of the hypothesis, the evidence, and the background: note, for instance, that the "43 arcseconds" residual isn't a raw observation at all but a derived quantity that only exists relative to Newtonian perturbation calculations of the other planets' effects — so the evidence itself is theory-laden in a way the standard formalism has no place for. Consider too the Vulcan episode: for decades the community had a live alternative explanation on the table, which complicates any story on which E was simply common knowledge with probability 1.

Then I want an honest account of where the theory breaks and what the episode shows about it, running in both directions. Evaluate the main repair strategies as they apply to this concrete case: Glymour's bootstrapping, on which the perihelion derivation confirms the theory because the theory itself computes the evidence from its own resources; the Howson-style move of replacing the agent's actual epistemic state with a rational reconstruction in which E is not yet known, and asking how the reconstruction should be chosen when, as here, the agent (Einstein) knew the target number in advance and was, by some accounts, aware that getting Mercury right was a constraint his theory would be judged against; and the Garber-style approach on which what's really learned is the theory, or the fact that the theory entails E, so the update happens on a partition the simple formalism doesn't represent. Distinguish throughout between Einstein's personal epistemic state and the community's — the perihelion fit plausibly did confirm the theory for astronomers who learned the theory by way of it, even if it's awkward to say it confirmed it for Einstein. Finally, give a verdict: does this episode show that Bayesian confirmation theory simply cannot capture scientific confirmation, or only that the standard formalism needs machinery it currently lacks? And say explicitly where you think the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak — I'd rather read an admission that some part of the episode resists formalization than a cleverness that papers over it.
