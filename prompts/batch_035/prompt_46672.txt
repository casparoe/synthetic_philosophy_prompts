I'm the engineering representative on my company's architecture review board, and I need a decision memo for our next meeting. The case: a proposal to retire the reconciliation module of our core billing system — roughly 90,000 lines written over twelve years by people who have all left the company — and rebuild it as a clean service. The old module is full of things nobody can explain: a forced sequential lock on all write operations, an overnight job that re-flags transactions that were already settled, a comment-free special case for one enterprise customer that may or may not still exist. One board member opposes the rewrite outright on Chesterton's-fence grounds — the principle that you shouldn't remove a fence until you know why it was put there — and wants to require full documentation of the module's behavior before any approval. The CTO counters that this requirement is a de facto veto, since full understanding of twelve years of accreted behavior may be unattainable at any acceptable cost, and that we'd be letting dead code govern the living system. Draft the memo the board will circulate: approve the rewrite, reject it, or approve with conditions, stating any conditions concretely, giving a realistic time frame, and giving an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. Along the way, take a position on what the Chesterton's-fence principle actually requires in a codebase: must it mean an individual explanation for each fence, or can something weaker — behavioral tests that pin down current outputs, shadow-running old and new implementations side by side, a staged cutover with instant rollback — legitimately satisfy it? And say where you think the analogy between inherited institutions and inherited code is doing real work versus where it quietly breaks down.
