My department runs a small reading group on formal models of conflict, and this term we've been working through the notion of a safe Pareto improvement. The setup, for orientation: two parties face a game — call it the default game — whose outcomes range from mutually tolerable to mutually disastrous, with Chicken as the canonical illustration: two drivers headed toward each other, each most preferring that the other swerve, both worst off if neither does. A safe Pareto improvement (SPI) is a deal the parties can sign before playing, which remaps each possible outcome of the default game onto one that is at least as good for both and strictly better for at least one, with the remapping enforced not by trust or goodwill but by the deal's own structure — typically a third party, an escrow arrangement, or a public randomization device. The canonical move in Chicken: instead of playing the game at all, flip a fair coin and let it decide who swerves. No party can end up worse off than it might have in the default game, both expect to do better, and the guarantee survives even if each party is convinced the other is entirely self-interested. The concept comes out of the literature on bargaining between automated agents, where the worry is that negotiating algorithms might clash in ways their owners would both regret — but our group is interested in whether the machinery does any real work in ordinary human affairs.

For next session I have to bring case studies, and I want them built to order rather than found lying around. Construct two scenarios. Scenario A: a realistic setting — a business dispute, a neighborhood conflict, an international standoff, your choice — in which an SPI is available and does its advertised job. Constraints: no science fiction, no perfect predictors or exotic enforcement technologies; enforcement must be limited to what a contract, a court, an escrow agent, or a genuinely public randomization device can deliver; and the safety of the deal must fall out of its structure, not from the parties happening to like each other. Scenario B: the same setting, minimally modified, in which the analogous deal fails — either it is no longer safe (one party can come out worse off than in the default game) or it is no longer an improvement (neither party expects to gain). The failure must be generated by a genuine structural feature of the situation, not by stipulating that someone behaves irrationally, misjudges the odds, or turns out to have a hidden agenda. Some directions you might consider: payoffs that are not commensurable or transferable across outcomes; the very availability of the deal changing incentives upstream, for instance making it profitable to cultivate a reputation as someone who would never sign such a thing; or the remapped outcome being unverifiable by whoever is supposed to enforce the deal.

For each scenario, lay out the default game explicitly — a small payoff matrix with numbers, or a clear ordinal ranking, is fine — then present the deal, then verify the safety condition outcome by outcome: for every way the modified situation could unfold, check each party against what it could have gotten in the default game. Afterward, identify which feature of Scenario B is doing the work, say what happens to your case if you delete just that one feature and leave everything else fixed, and give the strongest objection a skeptic of SPI-style mechanisms would press against Scenario A. I have my own suspicion to lodge here: that the safety guarantee quietly assumes the default game's payoffs are fixed and known before the deal is signed, so that anything that changes what the default game even is — reputation-building, preparation, deterrence posturing — slips outside the guarantee. I would like to see whether you can make that suspicion precise within your own case, or whether you think it ultimately misses the mark.
