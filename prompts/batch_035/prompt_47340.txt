I've been getting into ethics podcasts lately and one I quite like did an episode on moral luck, which I'd somehow never heard of despite it apparently being a big deal. The host summed the whole thing up roughly like this: "Back in the seventies Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel wrote a famous pair of papers showing that moral luck is everywhere, and that this basically destroys the old idea that you can only be judged for what's under your control. Nagel broke luck down into four kinds: luck in how your actions turn out, luck in the situations you happen to find yourself in, luck in the person you turn out to be, and luck in the causes that shaped you. The classic case is two drivers who are equally careless, but only one of them happens to hit a child, and we blame that one far more even though the difference between them was pure luck. And here's the kicker: if you trace everything back, luck goes all the way down — even your choices flow from your character, which you never chose. So Nagel's conclusion was that moral judgement has to be abandoned entirely, and that blame and punishment as we practice them are never actually justified."

I found the episode pretty gripping but something about the last part felt too tidy, like the host turned Nagel into someone who just thinks no one should ever be blamed for anything. Is this a fair summary? Go through it bit by bit and tell me what a philosopher working on free will and responsibility would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. In particular I want to know wether he's flattened Williams and Nagel into one position when they were actually up to different things (Im pretty sure Williams wasn't just co-signing Nagel's paper but I don't know what the difference is), and whether the "blame is never justified" conclusion actually follows from the parts of the summary that are right.
