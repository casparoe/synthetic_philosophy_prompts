At a post-election dinner, a friend who had voted lesser-of-two-evils for a candidate she disliked cut off my objections to that candidate's record with the old proverb: "If you don't vote, you can't complain." I had abstained — deliberately, in a first-past-the-post race in a jurisdiction my side was going to lose by a wide margin, and in part because I couldn't bring myself to endorse either option. I suspect the proverb is bundling several distinct claims, and I doubt the one she needs is the one that is actually true. Please lay out, as a numbered list, the different precise principles the one-liner could be expressing — a purely conventional norm of fair play in political conversation; a causal-responsibility principle under which complaint requires having helped produce (or having had a chance to help produce) the outcome; a free-rider principle on which voting is a burden in a cooperative scheme and abstainers are shirking while enjoying the scheme's benefits; an expressive or solidarity principle on which voting is worthwhile independently of any effect on the result; and a quasi-consent principle on which casting a ballot is how one accepts the authority of the outcome. For each reading, give three verdicts: whether it is plausible at all; whether it survives the fact that a single vote is essentially never decisive, which on the strictest causal reading would seem to bar voters and abstainers alike from complaining; and whether it actually supports dismissing the complaint of someone who abstained on considered lesser-evil grounds, as opposed to someone who abstained from apathy or ignorance — the two cases seem to stand differently under any fairness-based reading, and if there is an epistemic variant of the principle floating loose in the proverb, note where it would send us (some writers have argued there can be a duty to stay home). Keep the whole answer under 450 words; I want the menu, not an essay on any one dish.
