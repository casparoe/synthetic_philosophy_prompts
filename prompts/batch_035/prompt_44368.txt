I run performance measurement for a mid-sized regional hospital network, and my small team of analysts reviews roughly thirty metrics a quarter — ER door-to-doctor time, thirty-day readmission rates, hand-hygiene compliance, discharge-before-noon, and so on — each of which feeds executive dashboards and, increasingly, compensation decisions. The trouble we keep hitting is Goodhart's law in all its guises: metrics getting gamed (clock tricks, upcoding, patients parked in hallways so a timer never starts), metrics drifting away from the care outcomes they were chosen to track, and departments selected or funded partly for scoring well on numbers that no longer mean anything. I want a decision procedure the team can actually run, in about forty minutes per metric per quarter, with no background in economics or machine learning: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers terminate in a specific recommendation (keep, revise, pair with a counter-metric, retire) and which terminate in a flagged handoff to the executive committee because the question is one of values rather than facts. Ground it in what the actual theory of Goodhart-style failure distinguishes — gaming versus selection effects versus plain regression to the mean versus the proxy simply being pushed off the distribution it was validated on — because I want the procedure to inherit those distinctions rather than lump everything into "the number looks suspicious." Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the ways the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three metrics: door-to-doctor time in the ER, where we suspect the improvement is partly an artifact of registration games; hand-hygiene compliance measured by secret observers, where staff may be performing compliance rather than practicing it; and discharge-before-noon, which the floor managers loathe and the executive committee loves. I am particularly interested in whether the procedure says to retire or heavily discount the hand-hygiene metric despite regulatory pressure to keep reporting it, since that is a conclusion I am braced to dislike. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after, with no preamble and no restating of this request. And if you think the enterprise is confused at bottom — if, for instance, no checklist applied to the numbers themselves can reliably separate gaming from genuine improvement, so the procedure can only ever find symptoms and never causes — say that plainly at the top instead of building me something I should not trust.
