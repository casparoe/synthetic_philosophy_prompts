I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the original Sequences were still being posted, and I've watched the idea of indirect normativity go from a niche proposal for Friendly AI to something people invoke in every third argument about AI values. But a recurring dispute with a friend has never been settled to my satisfaction, and I'd like a proper essay on it.

The core question: does indirect normativity actually solve the problem it's meant to solve, or does it just relocate it? The idea, in its paradigm form, is something like Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition — don't hand the AI a fixed list of values we can't get right; instead have it do what we would want if we thought faster, knew more, and were more the people we wish we were. My friend's objection is the bootstrapping worry: every one of those stipulations — "faster," "knew more," "more the way we wish we were" — encodes a substantive normative choice by the designers. Choosing which cognitive changes count as improvements rather than mere drift is itself a value-laden judgment about what counts as flourishing or corruption. So on his view, indirect normativity doesn't defer to our idealized values; it launders the designers' current values through an extra step and calls the result ours. My reply has been that this proves too much — it would indict reflective equilibrium and every other method of reasoning from where we stand, and we don't refuse to revise our beliefs just because the rules of revision aren't themselves derived from nowhere.

The essay should weigh both sides seriously, and I'd especially like it to address three specific pressure points. First, the epistemic/alethic ambiguity: is the extrapolated agent supposed to be discovering moral facts, in which case indirect normativity quietly presupposes moral realism and collapses into a moral-realist decision procedure, or merely coherifying and enriching whatever values we happen to start with, in which case it seems to bake in a relativism many of its proponents would reject? Note that the original CEV document reportedly floated the trick of *stipulating* convergence with moral truth as a forcing assumption — I'd like the essay to consider whether that stipulation is legitimate or whether it's precisely where the smuggled content enters. Second, the losers problem: extrapolate two people whose idealized values still conflict, and the procedure now needs an aggregation theory — whose is never explained by the idealization itself. Third, whether the moral-uncertainty program — decision-theoretic approaches that treat competing moral theories the way you'd treat competing empirical hypotheses, and maximize expected choiceworthiness across them — is a genuine rival that handles the same problem better, or just indirect normativity with the idealization left implicit.

Please reach and defend a real conclusion, not a taxonomy with a shrug at the end. A conditional or qualified verdict is fine — e.g., that indirect normativity is legitimate as an engineering answer but not a philosophical one, or that it stands or falls with a version of moral realism its proponents haven't owned up to — but commit to something and argue for it against the best counterargument. Assume a reader who knows what CEV, reflective equilibrium, and moral uncertainty are, but do define anything more obscure. No reading lists or citations needed; this is for my own thinking, and I can't check references anyway.
