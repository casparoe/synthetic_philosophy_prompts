Write a dialogue, in the form of a scene with continuous prose interspersed with exchanges of speech, between two philosophers spending an afternoon at a field station where a researcher studies solitary digger wasps. They have just watched the researcher demonstrate the classic finding about *Sphex*: the female wasp, before dragging her paralysed cricket into the burrow, leaves it at the threshold and inspects the burrow first; if the researcher nudges the cricket a few centimetres away while she is inside, she emerges, retrieves the cricket, and — instead of carrying it straight in — performs the entire inspection again. She will do this every time, apparently indefinitely, with no sign that anything is amiss. The behaviour looks intelligent from the outside, yet it is exposed as a fixed subroutine that cannot register its own failure. Hofstadter coined the term "sphexishness" for this sort of mechanical rigidity; the two philosophers have taken to using it.

Give the philosophers opposing views, and let both argue at full strength:

The first, whom you might call the Unmasker, holds that the wasp is a revelation about us rather than an exception to us. Her line: the wasp's competence and its total inflexibility are two faces of the same thing — goal-directed behaviour produced by mechanisms that are blind to their own operation — and there is no principled reason to exempt human beings. Our procedures are more elaborate, but elaborateness is a matter of degree; the wasp is simply the thin end of a wedge that reaches all the way to the deliberating human, and any line we draw between her loop and our freedom will be arbitrary. On this view, the kind of free will that could ground, say, ultimate desert is absent even in us; the wasp merely makes it vivid.

The second, the Gradualist, holds that the sphex case marks a genuine border, not the thin end of a wedge. The relevant contrast is not mechanism versus non-mechanism but sphexishness versus flexibility: the capacity to notice that one's routine is misfiring, to represent counterfactuals, to interrupt a procedure and redesign it mid-course, and to act for reasons one can cite. The wasp is unfree not because she is mechanical but because she cannot so much as notice she is looping; a creature that can notice, even if the noticing is itself implemented mechanically, is free in the only sense worth wanting.

Make the exchange a real argument, not two monologues. In particular, have the Unmasker press her strongest objection — that the Gradualist's loop-detector is itself just another subroutine, so that flexibility merely relocates the sphexishness one level up; whatever watches the watcher is watched in turn, and the Gradualist's "noticing" is as stimulus-bound as the wasp's inspection, only described at a coarser grain — and have the Gradualist answer it rather than concede it, for instance by arguing that the objection trades on an equivocation between being caused and being compelled, or that self-monitoring need not be unbounded to matter. Let the Unmasker reply to that reply, and let the argument continue until it reaches some genuine crux: a point where the two disagree about what would even count as settling the matter, or about whether "could have done otherwise" was ever the right question. The dialogue may end unresolved, but if so it should end with the residual disagreement stated sharply, not vaguely.

Requirements: neither character may be a strawman; each should score real points against the other; both should respond to what the other actually says. Please write in British English throughout, and aim for roughly 1,500–2,500 words.
