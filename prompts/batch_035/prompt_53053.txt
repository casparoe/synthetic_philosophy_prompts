I've become fairly convinced of the functional-decision-theoretic picture: the rational agent treats her decision as the output of a fixed decision procedure and asks which output of that very procedure yields the best results, which means paying the $100 in the counterfactual mugging (the coin was fair, you know Omega already predicted your response before flipping, and the $1,000,000 is there iff the procedure outputs pay in the tails world), while refusing to pay in XOR blackmail (the blackmailer's letter is sent iff she predicted you will not pay, so paying is bad news about your own decision function and buying silence only rewards a policy of exploitable predictability). I find the package attractive precisely because it gets both cases right without ad hoc machinery, where causal decision theory pays the blackmailer and evidential decision theory balks at one-boxing's cousins. What would actually count against this view? I want exactly five candidate defeaters, presented as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion, each item a sentence or two: these might be a decision problem where the functional prescription seems clearly wrong, a way the notion of "the same decision function" fails to be well-defined across agents and predictors of differing accuracy, a result about the view collapsing into or being extensionally equivalent to a rival, or a general worry about whether the theory is tested against intuitions it has already been tuned to fit and so risks nothing. For each defeater, say whether a committed functional decision theorist should concede it as counting against the view, and rank the five from most to least threatening.
