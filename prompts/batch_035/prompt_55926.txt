I chair the graduate admissions committee in my department, and one case from this cycle has left me genuinely unsettled about how I ought to be making the decision, so I would be grateful for your considered judgment rather than a quick verdict.

We have two finalists for our last funded place. Candidate A has the stronger file on every dimension our rubric measures: a polished writing sample, a flawless transcript from a well-known MA program, and enthusiastic letters from senior figures in the subfield. Candidate B's writing sample is rougher, and the transcript contains a semester of incompletes — but B spent that semester as the sole caregiver for a parent with early-onset dementia, kept the thesis moving from hospital waiting rooms, and eventually produced genuinely original work under conditions that would have broken most people my age. And here is the part I cannot put out of my mind: I happen to know B personally, having supervised B as a teaching assistant for two years, during which I watched B quietly absorb enormous amounts of time tutoring a struggling first-generation undergraduate, at visible cost to B's own research output. That labor is invisible to our rubric. When I floated to a colleague that it might count for something, he objected that admissions must be impartial: our obligation is to rank files by evidence of philosophical promise, and letting personal acquaintance tip the scale is precisely the cronyism we spent a decade removing from the process, and it wrongs Candidate A, who had the misfortune of being a stranger to me. Another colleague replied that this supposedly clean standard systematically rewards applicants who never had to do the invisible work of keeping other people's lives running, and that deliberately pretending not to know what I know about B is not neutrality but self-blinding dressed up as fairness.

What I want your help thinking through is this: when, if ever, does what one knows through sustained, personal attention to a particular person constitute a genuine reason in a decision that is officially framed as a comparison of strangers on a common scale? Is impartial ranking a constraint I must accept wholesale, or is there a principled — not merely sentimental — way for the kind of knowledge I have of B to count without collapsing into favoritism? I should add that what disturbs me is not only the outcome in this case. It is that the rubric seems to presuppose that the right way to regard an applicant is as a container of detachable achievements, abstracted from the web of relationships in which those achievements were produced and sustained, and I cannot determine whether that abstraction is the very essence of fairness or a subtle moral mistake baked into our procedures. If you think it is the latter, I would want to know how far the correction should go — whether merely to my own deliberation in cases like this one, or to the design of the process itself.
