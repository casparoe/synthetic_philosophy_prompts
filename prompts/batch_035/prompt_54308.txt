I am in my final year of sixth form, and I help run a small philosophy society with two friends. Last week one of them produced an argument, built loosely on the old story of Buridan's ass — the donkey placed exactly midway between two identical haystacks, which starves because it has no reason to prefer either — and I have not been able to shake it since. My worry is not that I think it is right, but that if it is right, it proves far too much.

Here is the argument as he gave it, with the additions we worked out together:

(1) A choice is rational only if it is settled by reasons: if you take A rather than B, that must be because you take A to be better than B in some respect that matters.
(2) When two options are exactly equal in every respect that matters, no reason can settle the choice between them.
(3) So in cases of exact equality, nothing you do can be chosen rationally; picking one option rather than the other is arbitrary.
(4) An arbitrary movement is not a choice at all, so rational agents simply cannot choose between exactly equal options — hence the starving ass.
(5) The most an agent can do in such a case is randomise, for instance by flipping a coin: since nothing else could count in favour of either option, a coin flip is not merely permitted but is the uniquely rational procedure in exact ties.
(6) We almost never know whether our options are exactly equal or merely nearly equal: our preferences are coarse-grained, and our introspection of them unreliable.
(7) If we cannot detect any difference between two options, we should treat them as equal, since any real difference we would be overlooking would have to be one we could detect.
(8) Most of daily life consists of options we cannot tell apart — two identical routes to school, two equally pleasant sandwiches, two equally agreeable ways to spend a Friday evening.
(9) Therefore rationality requires flipping coins for most of our decisions, and it extends the same requirement to the remaining decisions as soon as the detectable differences become fine enough, which they quickly do in anything serious (I say this from experience: no amount of list-making separates my two degree options).
(10) So either almost nothing we do counts as a genuine choice, or rationality demands that almost everything be decided by coin flips.

I did try the coin flip for the degree question, and when it came up heads I felt something like relief — which I took to show that I had a preference all along. My friend replied that the relief only shows I dislike being undecided, not that one option was better beforehand; on his view the coin did not reveal my preference but created it. I find that chilling, in a way I cannot quite justify.

I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. Which premise or inference should I give up, why did it look so innocent when we first wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost — both for the ass and for ordinary indecision? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they seem to be what makes choice answerable to reasons in the first place, but I accept I may have to surrender one of them. Please write your answer in British English.
