International law treats captured enemy soldiers oddly, and I want to know what theory of their moral status the treatment rests on. Under the Geneva Conventions, a prisoner of war cannot be prosecuted merely for having fought: detention is supposed to be non-punitive, and he is released when the war ends, even if the war his side waged was flagrantly aggressive. States accept this for their own enemies knowingly. The aftermath of wars follows the same pattern: leaders of the losing side may be tried for the crime of aggression, individual soldiers may be tried for atrocities, but the ordinary infantryman who fought and killed in an unjust cause is released and typically pensioned at home — Germany was paying war pensions to veterans of the Wehrmacht within a few years of 1945. Yet the very same legal order insists that soldiers are personally responsible for how they fight: superior orders is no defense to a war crime, and a low-ranking conscript could be executed for shooting a surrendered prisoner.

What view of combatants does this package presuppose? On its face it looks like the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants: a soldier's permission to kill derives from his status as a combatant rather than from the justice of his cause, so soldiers on both sides are equally entitled to fight and become liable to punishment only through violations of the rules of war. But I can see at least two other readings. Perhaps the law presupposes only that unjust combatants are excused rather than justified — conscripts are coerced, volunteers misled — so their detention is preventive rather than deserved punishment. Or perhaps it presupposes nothing about desert at all and is simply a reciprocal bargain: we do not prosecute their soldiers so they will not prosecute ours, and the practice would survive even if unjust combatants act wrongly every time they fight.

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, say whether the moral-equality reading, the excuse reading, and the reciprocal-bargain reading really do license the same institutions, or whether some detail of the practice (the non-punitive character of POW detention, the pension, the amnesty for ordinary soldiers) discriminates among them. Pay particular attention to the asymmetry: personal responsibility is assumed for conduct within the war but denied for participation in it. Can that asymmetry be defended by something other than the moral-equality doctrine — say, soldiers' limited epistemic access to the justice of their war, or the thought that aggression is a crime of states rather than of individual men? Finally, tell me what would actually have to change if the moral equality of combatants were rejected on philosophical grounds: would POW camps become prisons for the wrongfully violent, would veterans' pensions of unjust wars become tainted, would every returning soldier of a bad war owe restitution — or could the whole edifice stand on the weaker justifications alone?
