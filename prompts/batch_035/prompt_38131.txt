Been on lesswrong since the overcoming bias days and the argument about Aumann's agreement theorem is honestly my favorite thing that never goes anywhere. Quick setup so we're on the same page: the theorem says two agents with common priors and common knowledge of each other's information partitions (and of each other's rationality) can't have common knowledge of differing posteriors. Fine as math. The fight is about what it means for actual humans. One side says it's normatively binding in the way decision theory is: when you meet a smart, honest person who disagrees with you after seeing your arguments, their disagreement is itself evidence, and refusing to move is basically an admission you don't think they're processing information rationally. The other side says the assumptions do all the work -- common priors are basically impossible between people raised in different cultures (and if you allow different priors the theorem is trivially false), common knowledge of posteriors almost never holds even between two people arguing face to face, and the models where agents iteratively announce posteriors and converge require them to already agree on the whole causal structure of what each other knows. So the anti-side says the theorem is a theorem about a world with no disagreement in it, and the pro-side says "so update already."

What bugs me is that this has been going on for like 20 years, everyone involved knows every argument on both sides by heart, and nobody has moved an inch. Hanson types still treat disagreement as presumptively dishonest or lazy, the skeptics still roll their eyes, and each side thinks the other missed the point.

So here's what I actually want, and it's not a survey and not a verdict on who's right: diagnose the disagreement. My working hypothesis is that it's not about probability theory at all -- it's a fight over what an idealization is for. One camp treats the idealized agent the way an engineer treats a frictionless plane, as a target you try to approximate, and the other treats it the way a logician treats a model, as a thing that's true of its own little world and carries no weight outside it. Is that right? Or is there a real hidden formal question buried in here, like whether "you should treat my disagreement as evidence" is even well-defined when we don't share a partition and each of us knows that? Or are the two sides just answering different questions -- one about what to DO in an argument, one about what the theorem licenses you to infer -- and talking past each other under the same banner?

Also tell me straight: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here? A sharper formalism, a distinction, some empirical finding about how humans actually transmit evidence, or is this thing going to keep circling forever and if so why.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the diagnosis, one line, no throat clearing -- and then the reasoning. Don't restate my setup back at me and dont open with "great question" or anything like that.
