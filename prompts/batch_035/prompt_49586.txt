I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, writing a chapter on Hacking's looping effects, and my supervisor has told me in no uncertain terms that before I can say anything interesting about interactive kinds I need a criterion for when looping actually occurs. I have a folder of cases on which I have fairly firm verdicts, but the tentative principle I keep writing down doesn't cover them all, and I'd like help either repairing it or replacing it. My current draft principle says: a classification of people loops when the criteria for the classification include things (self-reports, behaviors, expressions) that are themselves shaped by the person's awareness of the classification, so the classification partly constitutes the condition it purports to describe; classifications of properties with criteria fully independent of anyone's knowledge of the classification (a lab value, an anatomical fact) don't loop, however much they change behavior downstream.

Here are the verdicts I want the principle to capture. A patient told she has major depression who then reads the symptom list online, begins describing her inner life in the diagnostic vocabulary, and whose presentation accordingly fits the criteria more fully than before: clear loop, and a worrying one. A child labelled gifted at seven whose whole educational track and self-concept are then organized around the label: also a loop, I think, but not a worrying one — which already puts pressure on my habit of writing as though looping were distorting by nature. Type 2 diabetes: the diagnosis changes how the person eats and monitors, sometimes drastically, but I want to say this is not the same phenomenon, because the classification tracks a glycemic criterion that would obtain whether or not anyone knew the word for it; my principle handles this nicely. But then there are the two cases that keep breaking it. First, actuarial risk labels in sentencing: an offender scored as high-risk by an instrument he never learns exists, whose treatment by parole officers, employers, and police then changes, and whose trajectory changes accordingly — I have the strong intuition that a loop has occurred with no awareness of the classification anywhere in the person's head, which my draft principle, with its awareness clause, seems to rule out. Second, the historical cases my chapter is built around: classifications of kinds of person circulated by experts and institutions for decades before the classified population had any access to them, and I think the kinds were being made in that period, not merely tracked. So either awareness is inessential and the loop runs through institutional treatment, or my verdict on the sentencing case and the historical ones is wrong.

There is one case I keep going back and forth on, and I'd like the principle to settle it rather than me: a teenager who self-diagnoses a condition from social media content, whose self-reports and behaviors then reshape themselves toward the diagnostic criteria, with no clinician involved at any point. Is that a loop in which the classification creates the condition, or accurate self-recognition of a condition a professional-first classification would have missed — or does the principle show the question is badly posed? Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated as precisely as this material allows; say which clause each case motivates; tell me what it predicts about a further case I haven't listed — say, a diagnostic category that clinicians apply and communicate to each other but which patients essentially never encounter, or a country that decriminalizes and medicalizes a behavior, making the category newly available to the people so described; and be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot save, since I suspect at least one of them has to go and I'd rather know which one before my supervisor tells me.
