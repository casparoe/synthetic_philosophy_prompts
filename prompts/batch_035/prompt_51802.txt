I teach a 90-minute session on self-locating belief for a mid-level epistemology seminar, ~14 students. They've already seen the basics of Sleeping Beauty and know the halfer/thirder split on paper, so what I want is an activity where they feel where the disagreement actually bites. Design a game along these lines: some students play "Beauties" — a coin is flipped secretly; heads means one "awakening," tails means two, with the two awakenings made subjectively indistinguishable (no notes, no watches, blindfolded walk between rooms, staggered timings so they can't track elapsed time). Other students play bookmakers offering bets on the coin at each awakening. The part I care most about, and the part everyone skips when they read the paper version: run two settlement conditions side by side. In one condition, a bet placed at an awakening is settled at every awakening — so a Beauty who's woken twice gets paid or loses twice on the same call. In the other condition, only one bet per experiment counts, no matter how many times she woke. Students should end up discovering that the same subjective evidence plus the same coin supports different actions depending on the settlement rule, and then the debrief has to sort out which differences are about credence and which are just artifacts of the betting setup — I want them to see that the halfer/thirder fight is not simply a fight about a number. Please give me: the actual mechanics for faking a memory wipe with 14 people and a couple of rooms, a payoff scheme using tokens (no real money), a timeline that fits in 90 minutes, what will predictably go wrong the first time (Beauties cheating by counting corridor turns, bookmakers accidentally leaking which day it is, students who just recognize each others' voices) and how to handle each, and an honest note on which feature of the real problem the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
