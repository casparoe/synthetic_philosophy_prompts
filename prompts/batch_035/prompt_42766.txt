I produce a half-hour podcast on the history of ideas in anthropology. Next month's guest is a senior anthropologist who has spent decades doing fieldwork among a contemporary foraging people and whose new book revives, in updated form, the Sahlins-style claim that hunter-gatherers lived in the original affluent society — and, going further, that the adoption of agriculture was, on balance, a bad deal for the people who took it up. He is a practiced interviewee with two reliable retreats: when pressed on the empirical record he falls back on the definitional move that affluence means wanting little rather than having much, and when pressed on that definition he says that every welfare concept is contestable, so his is no worse than anyone else's.

Draft eight to ten questions for the recording. They should turn on the substance of his position rather than on his biography or his fieldwork stories, though the stories may be used as levers. At least one question each must press on: (a) the definitional move, and whether framing affluence in terms of wants rather than resources makes the thesis hard to test rather than deep; (b) the demographic evidence — mortality, life expectancy, and the fraction of people who lived to see their grandchildren; and (c) the extrapolation problem — that the foraging peoples ethnographers actually observed mostly survived on land farmers did not want, and so may not tell us about the foragers who existed before agriculture existed to push them anywhere. At least two questions should be built so that either of his two stock replies, if given, makes the next question land harder rather than closing the topic, and the final question should be one he cannot answer with either stock reply — in the neighborhood of what evidence, in principle, could get him to give the thesis up.

Present the output as a table with exactly four columns: Question (worded as I would read it aloud), Presses on (the specific substantive point it targets), Likely reply (the response he is most disposed to give, phrased roughly as he would phrase it), and Follow-up (what I should ask next if he gives that reply). Order the rows in the sequence I should ask them, and after the table add one short paragraph, outside the table, identifying which single question I should cut first if the interview runs long, and why.
