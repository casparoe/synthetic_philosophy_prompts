I'm preparing to teach a unit on virtue ethics for an intro-level philosophy course, and I've been reading about the situationist critique -- the objection, associated with psychologists like Milgram and the people behind the good Samaritan and cookie/diet studies, that so-called character traits barely predict behavior compared to seemingly trivial features of the situation. Rather than answering the critique with yet another thought experiment, I want to confront my students with a literary text they mostly already know, and one real-world counterexample.

The text is Golding's Lord of the Flies (1954). The obvious reading is that the novel is *bad* news for virtue ethics: boys with latent flaws regress under conditions of scarcity, fear, and anonymity, and decent-seeming institutions (the conch, the elected leadership) dissolve. But it's more complicated than that. Golding wrote the book as a dark inversion of Ballantyne's The Coral Island (1857), where the stranded boys -- Ralph, Jack, and Peterkin -- stay steadfastly decent and encounter evil only in the form of pirates and cannibals. In Golding, the evil is internal, and famously he even imports Ballantyne's character names. So in a sense the novel is itself a work of applied situationist psychology avant la lettre: change the situation (remove adults, add fear, add a beast), and Ballantyne's upstanding British schoolboys become hunters and killers. Yet the novel also seems to resist a pure situationism: Piggy and Simon behave with something like stable virtue across wildly shifting situations, Ralph's leadership looks more like a disposition than a response to incentives, and Jack's slide looks like the expression of a character that was always there, visible in the choir's entrance if you reread carefully.

The real-world case I want to set against the novel is the story of the 'Ata castaways: in 1965, six Tongan schoolboys were shipwrecked on the uninhabited island of 'Ata and survived roughly fifteen months before being rescued. By the accounts I've read, they cooperated, divided labor, kept a fire going, resolved a serious quarrel by separating into camps and then negotiating a reconciliation, and even tended an injured boy's wounds. It's become a popular counter-anecdote to Lord of the Flies -- proof, some say, that Golding's vision is parochial to a particular mid-century English construction of boyhood, and that ordinary people in extremis default to cooperation, not savagery.

Please write an essay, suitable as a reading I can assign to my students, that reads Lord of the Flies through the virtue ethics / situationism debate and weighs the 'Ata castaways as a counterexample. Some questions I'd like you to address: Does Golding's novel support the situationist, or the virtue-ethical, reading -- or does it dramatize a more subtle interaction between dispositional and situational factors (e.g., fear and the invented 'beast' as situational triggers that nonetheless work through pre-existing differences among the boys)? Is the 'Ata case genuinely a defeater for the novel's picture of human nature, or does the comparison break down in ways we can specify -- group size, prior relationships, cultural background, the presence of a shared task? What would a defender of virtue ethics, and what would a situationist, each say about the difference between the two sets of boys? And finally: does the comparison teach my students something about the situationist critique itself -- for instance, about how situationists extrapolate from lab experiments while novelists and castaway anecdotes offer different kinds of evidence? Reach a conclusion and defend it; I'd rather you take a position, even a qualified one, than survey the options neutrally. Please don't rely on verbatim quotation from the novel -- refer to scenes and characters by description, since I'll have the students working from memory too.
