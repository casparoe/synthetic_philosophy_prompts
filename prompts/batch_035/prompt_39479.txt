I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on wild animal suffering, and a friend in the agricultural economics group has an argument I can neither accept nor refute. He built it for a food ethics seminar and now uses it to needle the vegans in the department. Each premise looks defensible to me on its own; the conclusion, and what he does with it, do not.

(1) In choosing between diets, other things equal we should prefer the diet that results in the smaller total number of animal deaths, each death counting as one whatever the animal.
(2) Producing plant food kills wild animals: plowing buries burrows and nests, pesticides poison, and combine harvesters kill rodents, birds, amphibians, and reptiles, in numbers that are large even on conservative estimates of field mortality.
(3) Pasture for grass-finished beef involves none of this: the land is not plowed, not sprayed, and not machine-harvested; the animals eat grass that nothing else was living on.
(4) A finished steer yields on the order of half a million calories, purchased by a single death.
(5) Producing half a million calories of crops involves considerably more than one wild animal death, on any estimates I've seen.
(6) So, per calorie, grass-finished beef kills fewer animals than any plant-based diet, and anyone who accepts (1) should prefer it.
(7) Nothing in the reasoning is specific to cattle: the larger-bodied the animal, the fewer deaths per calorie, so the principle ranks diets by the size of the animals they kill, and ranks highest of all any diet built around the largest animal available.

My friend accepts (7) cheerfully and extends it: if a community could draw a generation's calories from one enormous, very long-lived animal, (1) says that diet beats every crop-based diet regardless of the animal's intelligence or the quality of its life, because its death is one death and the count is all that matters. He also derives the corollary that, per calorie consumed, the strictest vegans in the department are the largest-scale animal killers in the food system. I take (7) and its extensions to be a reductio, but I can't locate the break without giving up something I want. I can't reject (2) or (5): field deaths are real, and my whole research program rests on their mattering. I suspect (3) is empirically shaky — pastures are mowed, harrowed, and parasite-dosed, and I doubt grazed land is a deathless idyll — but patching (3) seems to change the numbers, not the shape of the argument. That leaves the equal weighting in (1), or some slide between steps I haven't named.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the flaw is an equivocation — a premise established under one reading and used under another — I'd like that named, since the argument's conclusion seems to me true only under the very reading that generates the reductio. And please price the alternatives: if I give up equal weighting, what weighting do I owe instead, and does any capacity-based weighting simply hand the argument's conclusion back to my friend rather than to the vegans?
