I'm a novelist working on a book in which a secondary character — a retired logician named Miriam, charming at dinner parties, unbearably gloomy about one thing only — is an antinatalist who cites David Benatar's book Better Never to Have Been as the thing that "settled the question" for her late in her career. Her antinatalism needs to be sharp, internally coherent, and frustratingly hard to argue with at the Thanksgiving table, which is the whole point of the character.

My beta reader, a thoughtful non-philosopher who enjoyed the chapters, left me this note about Miriam's position:

"As I understand it, Benatar's argument rests on an asymmetry. The presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good — no surprises there. But when it comes to lives that never exist, things split: the absence of pain counts as good even though there is nobody around to enjoy that absence, while the absence of pleasure counts as merely neutral rather than bad, because there is no person who is thereby deprived of anything. From this he infers that nonexistence is always better for a person than existence, so birth harms the one born in the strongest possible sense: they'd have been better off never existing. He then argues, drawing on our well-documented optimism biases and powers of adaptation, that every actual life is much worse than its owner believes, and that many lives fall below the threshold of being worth continuing at all. That's the real punchline of the view: someone who accepts the asymmetry but keeps on living is being inconsistent, which is why Benatar holds that existing people have a duty to stop procreating and, ideally, to wind things down. Philosophers have pointed out that if death is bad because it deprives us of future pleasures, then by the same logic the absence of pleasure before birth should be bad too — which simply refutes the asymmetry. So the view doesn't really survive scrutiny, and you might want Miriam to be a bit less confident."

I don't have the philosophical training to check this, and the character will fail if her position is a caricature — but she'll also fail if she wins every exchange, so I do need to know where the view is genuinely vulnerable rather than where it's merely been misdescribed.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in population ethics and the literature on the ethics of procreation, and to think carefully and slowly before answering. Please begin with a short brainstorming pass: set out Benatar's asymmetry as precisely as you can, including all four of the comparisons he relies on, before you evaluate anything my reader said. Then go through her note claim by claim and tell me (1) which parts a careful defender of Benatar would accept as accurate, (2) which parts involve subtle conflations or overstatements — for instance, about what kind of comparison is being made between existence and nonexistence, or about how the argument for never starting a life relates to claims about lives worth continuing — and (3) whether the final point about deprivation "simply refuting" the asymmetry is a knockdown or a live objection that Benatar has actually tried to answer. Conclude with practical guidance for the novel: which, if any, of Miriam's lines I should revise, and what the strongest honest objection is that her nephew could raise against her at the table — one she'd have to work to answer rather than one she could bat away.
