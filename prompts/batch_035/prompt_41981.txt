A while back I read that there's a whole faction in philosophy of mind that calls itself illusionist — Dennett, a guy named Frankish, others — whose line is roughly that phenomenal consciousness, the felt what-it's-likeness of experience, is a kind of fiction the brain tells itself, a simplified user-facing model of its own processing. And apparently the phrase they lean on, "user illusion," was borrowed from computing in the first place — the desktop with its folders and trash can being a fiction that deliberately hides implementation. I half-remember there's even a pop-science book with something like that as its title. So the metaphor I use at work every day is apparently the official metaphor for a live philosophical war, and I'd like to actually understand the war in my own terms instead of in theirs.

Here's the job. Interfaces, abstraction layers, virtualization, the distinction between what an API hides and what it misreports, what it means for a model to be "for the user" rather than "of the system." One claim at a time: which of their claims map onto something I'd recognize, and onto what exactly. Then give me the residue report — which parts of the debate survive translation cleanly, which parts you had to bend to fit (and where you bent them), and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. The part I most want you to take on: in my world, a user's model being wrong about the machine is fine, even good, because there's always a fact of the matter at the implementation level, and in principle I can drop to a lower layer and check — I can dump memory, read the source, attach a debugger. My instinct is that the whole philosophical fight is exactly about whether that move is available for consciousness, i.e., whether "drop down a layer and check" even makes sense when the interface is the brain's model of the brain and there's no external user.
