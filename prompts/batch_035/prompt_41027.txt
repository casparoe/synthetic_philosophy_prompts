My type-design reading group has been stuck for months on what should've been a fun question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? We all got into it after reading about Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project, where a letter concept is supposed to be a network of abstract roles rather than a stored shape.

The group has split into two camps that won't budge. The skeleton people say each letter has an underlying topological skeleton — the 'a' is basically a closed bowl plus a vertical stroke with some opening, and a "style" is just one way of fleshing out that skeleton. That, they say, is why a blackletter 'a' and a geometric sans 'a' are recognizably the same letter even though almost no pixels coincide. The no-core people say there is no invariant at all: just look at how 'g' has two completely different shapes (single-storey and double-storey) that everyone reads fine, so letter identity can't be some shared structure — it's more like family resemblance, plus the letter's position in a system of contrasts, so you could drift a form arbitrarily far and it'd still be that letter as long as the contrasts survive. Nobody has moved an inch since spring.

My working hypothesis is that the skeleton people are actually talking about a designer's tool (a useful abstraction for drawing) while the no-core people are talking about the psychology of readers, so they're answering different questions under one heading — but I'm not confident, and a wrinkle came up at the last meeting that I can't fit in: OCR models now recognize 'a's in styles they've never seen, with no explicit skeleton anywhere, and both sides immediately claimed this as evidence.

Diagnose this for me, please, with these sections in this order:

1. "The two positions, stated fairly" — short, ~150 words, no strawmen.
2. "What kind of disagreement this is" — the longest section, ~300 words: verbal, empirical (about what's in a literate reader's head), metaphysical (about whether categories like this have essences at all), or something else. Say which, with reasons from what each side actually claims.
3. "The OCR wrinkle" — ~100 words: whose evidence is it really, given your diagnosis?
4. "What would count as progress" — ~150 words: an experiment, a distinction, a reformulation of the question — or an honest argument that none should be expected.
