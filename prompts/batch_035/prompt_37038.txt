I spent the better part of my career in celestial mechanics — first fitting orbits at a national observatory and later teaching dynamical astronomy — so I have had the number 43 arcseconds per century in my head for most of my life. Since retiring I have been auditing an undergraduate course in philosophy of science at the university down the road, mostly to learn what philosophers actually mean by confirmation, a word my colleagues and I used for decades with more confidence than precision. Because I have time on my hands, the instructor has asked me to help mark the second problem set. I graded laboratory reports and refereed papers for forty years, but I find philosophical grading unfamiliar territory: in my old field an error in a derivation announces itself, whereas here I sometimes cannot tell whether a student has accurately reported the position under discussion, since Glymour and Howson and Urbach are names I met only this term. I would be grateful for your help with one answer in particular.

The question read as follows: "Clark Glymour argued that Bayesian confirmation theory faces a 'problem of old evidence.' (a) State the problem precisely, showing exactly where the mathematics goes wrong. (b) Explain why the case of general relativity and the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion is supposed to be especially embarrassing for the Bayesian. (c) Present one proposed remedy, and one objection to it."

Here is the student's answer, transcribed in full: "(a) Bayesians say that E confirms H when Pr(H|E) > Pr(H). If we already know E, then Pr(E) = 1, and Bayes' theorem gives Pr(H|E) = Pr(H) — the numerator and denominator are the same, so no piece of evidence we are already certain of can ever confirm any hypothesis. That is the problem of old evidence. (b) The perihelion advance of Mercury, about 43 seconds of arc per century left over after Newtonian perturbations, had been known for decades before 1915. When general relativity predicted it exactly, everyone took this as strong confirmation. But the Bayesian cannot say this, because by 1915 the data had probability 1; so the Bayesian must say that the perihelion success confirmed nothing, and that confirmation in science only ever occurs at the moment a new prediction is tested. This shows that Bayesianism fails as a description of how science works. (c) Howson and Urbach's remedy is to conditionalize on the negation of the old evidence: in place of Pr(E), we use Pr(E given not-E), the probability we would have assigned E had we believed not-E instead. For the perihelion data this gives a suitably small number, and confirmation is restored. The standard objection is that not-E may be inconsistent with everything else we know, in which case the conditional probability is undefined and the remedy fails."

Please grade this answer for me: a mark out of ten, followed by the specific point-by-point comments that justify it, written roughly as they would appear on the returned sheet. I would especially like to know whether the student's account of part (a) earns full credit, and how one is supposed to weigh an answer whose mathematical statements check out against the reading but whose handling of the positions in (b) and (c) I cannot certify from my own knowledge — in my profession, describing a rival's model incorrectly while appearing to refute it was the cardinal sin, but I do not know the local custom here, and I would rather not penalize a student for my own unfamiliarity with the literature. If something in the answer is right in substance but wrong in its formulation or attribution, say so explicitly and tell me what it should cost.
