I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator, a radio astronomer, has persuaded himself that the immensity of space and the brevity of human life render absurd everything he loves. Searching for the strongest rejoinder to him, I have been reading Frank Ramsey's 1920s notes on the meaning of life, in which he argues that physical size and duration are simply irrelevant to importance: he sees no reason to feel humble before the vastness of the heavens, and holds that the smallness of the human scale does nothing to diminish what matters on it. I would like you to read Thornton Wilder's Our Town through this Ramseyan lens.

The play deliberately frames tiny Grover's Corners against deep time — the Stage Manager seals a copy of the local paper into the new bank's cornerstone for readers a millennium hence, and lets us feel the town's routines sitting on top of vanished Babylons — and it insists, through the dead Emily's return to her twelfth birthday, that ordinary moments are what count. Does Wilder anticipate Ramsey's claim that scale is no measure of significance, or does he smuggle in a different argument altogether — one resting on the imminence of death and the rarity of attention rather than on the scale-independence of value — that Ramsey would regard as beside the point? Note too where the play resists the reading: it seems to need a vantage point outside ordinary life, the graveyard, from which ordinary life is finally seen to matter, whereas Ramsey wants importance to be visible from within, with no external view to confer it.

Please give a reading faithful to both the play and the philosophy — one that does not reduce Wilder to an illustration of Ramsey, and does not bend Ramsey to fit the play — and end by saying what the play dramatizes that Ramsey's notes cannot. Answer in British English.
