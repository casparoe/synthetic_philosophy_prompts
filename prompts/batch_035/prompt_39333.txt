I write about science and ideas for a living, and a data-scientist friend of mine has been on a "death of expertise" tear lately. Over beers he laid out an argument that big crowds are basically infallible, and I can't find the hole in it even though the conclusion is nuts. Roughly:

(1) Condorcet's jury theorem: take a group voting yes or no on some factual question. If each voter is better than a coin flip at getting the right answer, and their votes are independent, then as you add more voters the probability that the majority is right approaches 1. It works at 51% accuracy, it works at 90% — at 51% it just takes longer.

(2) "Independent" in the theorem just means what it means in everyday life: nobody's vote is influenced by anyone else's vote. No copying, no seeing the running tally, everyone answering on their own.

(3) Take any factual question in the news — did the politician actually say the quote, was that study garbage, whatever — and poll a million people who follow the news even casually. Someone who follows a topic at all is a bit better than a coin flip on questions about it; that's pretty much what "following a topic" means.

(4) A phone poll satisfies (2) trivially: no one sees anyone else's answer before they submit.

(5) So a million-person poll on any newsy factual question is right with probability 1 minus a rounding error.

(6) So forget fact-checkers and expert panels. On any factual question, a big enough poll beats any expert, every time.

I raised the obvious objection that everyone watches the same three channels and reads the same viral thread, and he waved it off: that's shared background, not influence between votes — the theorem cares about whether my vote influences yours, not about whether we watched the same show before voting. Which... sounded right at the time? My instinct since then is to attack (3) instead, on the grounds that people aren't really better than chance on technical questions, but he just says "51 percent" and smiles, and honestly people who read the news probably are at least 51% on news questions. So I'm stuck. Every step sounds fine on its own, and yet the conclusion is insane — Twitter polls about health stuff are wrong constantly.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (1) if at all possible — I checked the math myself and it's definately real.
