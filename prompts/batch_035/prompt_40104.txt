I'm a high school senior whose school lets students propose one elective each year, and this term I'm taking a half-course our math teacher runs called "Probability and Paradox," about puzzles where probability slips into philosophy — the Monty Hall problem, the Sleeping Beauty problem, and so on. We're nearing the end of a unit on the Doomsday argument. Before each test, the teacher has us exchange anonymized practice answers and mark each other's against the official rubric, and the marked sheet goes back to the author, who can appeal to the teacher if they think the grading was unfair. Last cycle two of my classmates appealed marks I gave, and the teacher upheld one of them, so before I hand anything back this time I want a second opinion. What I'd like from you: a mark for the answer below, out of 20, and the specific comments that justify it — keyed to parts (a), (b), and (c), point by point, stating exactly where marks should come off and why, and where the answer deserves full credit. Assume the appeal will be read by someone who knows this material well.

The practice question we were given read: "(a) Explain what the reference class problem is, using the Doomsday argument as your illustration. (b) State the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) precisely enough that someone could tell them apart. (c) Does either assumption solve the reference class problem? Defend your answer. [7 marks, 6 marks, 7 marks.]"

The student's answer reads: "The reference class problem arises whenever we use our own existence as evidence about the world. The Doomsday argument illustrates it. Let n be my birth rank — my position in the ordering of all humans who will ever live. Roughly a hundred billion humans have been born so far, so n is about 10^11. If I regard myself as a random sample from the class of all humans who will ever live, then n is uniformly distributed between 1 and N, where N is the total number of humans there will ever be. Observing an n this small makes small values of N more likely, so the human race is likely to end sooner rather than later. But everything here depended on the class I was sampled from. Had the reference class been all observers, extraterrestrials and uploads included, then perhaps most observers come late in cosmic history, and my early birth is no longer evidence of an early doomsday. Different classes, different conclusions; that is the reference class problem. As for the two assumptions: SSA says to reason as if you were a random sample from all observers who exist at any time. SIA says that, in addition, you should favor hypotheses on which more observers exist in your reference class, since a world containing many observers is more likely to contain someone like me. Does either solve the problem? SSA plainly does not — it leaves the class unspecified. But SIA does. In the Doomsday setup the two factors cancel exactly: the penalty of order 1/N from my birth rank and the boost of order N from SIA leave me with my original opinion about N. So the class drops out — whichever class I pick, the answer comes out the same. The Doomsday argument is thereby refuted, and in fact reversed: since my existence is better explained by observer-rich futures, I should expect humanity to have a long one."
