i run a seminar on philosophy of history for ~14 undergrads, mostly second and third years, one 2-hour block. we just finished Polybius on anacyclosis and Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah story -- rough version: a dynasty is founded by a hard-pressed group with strong internal solidarity, the solidarity frays as wealth and luxury set in, and after three or four generations a hungrier group on the margins takes over, and it starts again. I want them to feel this instead of just taking notes on it.

Design a multi-generation game where the students themselves are the dynasty. Each generation is a small team that inherits the state the previous team left behind -- some resource pool, a cohesion score, a map of rivals, whatever you think does the work -- and makes a set of choices (tax, expand, patronize the arts, buy off the generals, settle the frontier tribes). The rules should be set up so the cycle *emerges* from ordinary self-interested play rather than being scripted in, and the scoreboard shouldn't reward playing the historical script. Printable materials, points not money, and it has to fit in maybe 90 minutes of play with 30 for debrief.

Here's the part I care about most. They've all READ the theory, so they'll walk in trying to escape the cycle on purpose -- keep the cohesion score high, skip the luxury phase, whatever. I want that to be the engine of the session, not a bug: design the mechanics so the escape attempt itself tends to drive decline (though it should be possible, maybe rare, for a group to pull it off). Then the debrief has to land two things: mapping specific moments of play onto specific parts of the theory (which move was the "softening," which was the resentful margin group being created), and the deeper question -- if a cyclical theory is true, does knowing it change anything? is the knowledge itself just another move inside the cycle? I don't want that lectured at them; I want at least one group to notice it on their own.

Also tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing scoring exploits, one generation deliberately sandbagging to ruin the next team's inheritance, students confused about whether they're playing a nation or a ruling family -- and how to handle each. And be upfront about which part of Ibn Khaldun the game simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself. For what it's worth I have one student who will absolutely try to stage a coup against her own teammates, plan for that.
