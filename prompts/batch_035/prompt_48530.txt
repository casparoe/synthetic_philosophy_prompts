I've spent roughly a decade in the rationalist blogosphere — the Sequences, the open threads, the recurring arguments about base rates and adjustment — so I know the standard lecture on confounding by heart. My problem sits one level up from the lecture, and I can't find it addressed anywhere as a working method rather than as a set of classic illustrations.

Here is my situation. I'm the only data analyst at a company of about two hundred people that runs subscription web services. Roughly weekly, someone comes to me with a comparison of rates: churn before and after a price change, conversion under the old and new onboarding flows, support tickets per user across two release versions, cancellation rates in a marketing segment versus the general population. Almost every one of these numbers can be computed either aggregated or sliced — by device, plan tier, acquisition channel, week, market — and the slices routinely disagree with the aggregate, sometimes to the point of reversing the sign. I know the name for the reversal; I've seen the admissions-by-department case and the kidney-stone treatment case many times, and there's a well-known interactive illustration of the phenomenon in the community's canon that I could nearly recite. Naming it doesn't help me. My executives don't want a statistics seminar; they want a number to act on, chosen defensibly enough that the decision survives a Monday-morning meeting with someone holding the opposite number.

What I've pieced together from my reading is that the resolution is causal rather than arithmetic: whether to trust the aggregate or the slices depends on where the slicing variable sits in the causal story — whether it plausibly influences both group membership and the outcome, or sits downstream of the treatment, or of neither. That principle is stated everywhere at the level of textbook examples and almost nowhere as something a working analyst can run on a fresh case in twenty minutes. Slicing can also be gamed: with enough candidate partitions, anyone can find one that flatters their preferred conclusion, and I currently have no principled answer when someone does exactly that.

So, this is my request. Act as a statistician with a serious grounding in the philosophy of causal inference — someone at home with the distinction between an association measure and an estimate of a causal effect, and with the literature on when a measure is collapsible across a partition and when it is not. Before writing anything, brainstorm: list the candidate distinctions from that literature, and decide which of them must survive the translation into a desk procedure and which can be dropped for my use case. Then produce:

1. A decision procedure I can actually run on a fresh comparison of rates: an ordered list of questions, each with what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops and returns "report the aggregate"; where it returns "report the slices"; where it returns "these data do not answer the question being asked"; and where it hands off to me because the question turns on causal background knowledge that no further crunching of these numbers will supply.

2. An explicit statement of the procedure's limits — in particular, that whether a variable is causally upstream is a substantive judgment the procedure cannot make for me, only prompt me to make and record, and that a rule for choosing among multiple candidate partitions is a stopping rule as much as a truth rule.

3. Worked applications to three cases: (a) a new onboarding flow that converts better in the aggregate but worse within both the mobile and the desktop segments, because the traffic mix shifted toward mobile during the test; (b) a price change after which total revenue per cohort rose in the aggregate while revenue fell within every plan tier, where the question on the table is whether the change "worked"; (c) a churn comparison in which slicing by device and slicing by acquisition channel give opposite signs, and no one at the company can say with confidence which of those two variables is causally prior. Let case (b) land wherever it honestly lands, even if that's an answer I'd rather not deliver; don't engineer the cases to come out tidy. For each case, also state what the procedure cannot settle.

Constraints, so that the procedure is realistic rather than aspirational: each call happens under time pressure, typically under half an hour; the stakes are real but bounded — decisions in the tens to low hundreds of thousands of dollars; nobody signs off but me, though my memo can be challenged by anyone with a dashboard and an opinion; and the consumers of my answer have no statistical training and limited patience for caveats, so the procedure's output sentence must be one an executive can repeat in a meeting without mangling it. Where a step is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, say so plainly — I would rather know which parts of my method are taste than believe I have eliminated taste.

Please think carefully, and work through the brainstorming before committing to the procedure's structure.
