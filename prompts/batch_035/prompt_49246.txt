I have spent the last six years on a long novel about a woman who defects from a failing state, and I have a co-writer who is an unrepentant Lewisian. He insists that when the manuscript is finished, we will have created nothing: the woman, her defection, the ending in which she dies at the wrong border post, will all already be real in some concrete world spatiotemporally disconnected from ours, and our book will merely have happened to describe one such world with reasonable accuracy. I find this thought equal parts consoling and appalling. My credence that he is right in the relevant sense — that our finished novel will be true at some concrete possible world — is about 0.15; his is about 0.9. Give me your own number, decomposed: your probability that Lewis-style modal realism is true, with worlds as concrete and isolated as this one rather than as ersatz abstractions; your probability that a typical long novel is internally consistent enough to be true at any possible world at all, given the drift most novels tolerate — a sister's surname shifting between chapters, a journey that cannot be completed in the stated number of days; your probability that the emotional texture we invent, as opposed to the bare events, constrains which worlds our book describes; and any other terms you think belong, with a number and one line of justification for each, then the combination. Then tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, what argument or discovery would move it most, and where my co-writer's 0.9 and my 0.15 most likely diverge. Lead with your number in the very first sentence, then give the decomposition, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
