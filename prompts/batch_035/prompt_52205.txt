The exam question was: "In Book II of Plato's Republic, Glaucon renews the challenge that Thrasymachus raised in Book I. Set out the threefold taxonomy of goods he offers, say where he claims the many place justice, state precisely what he then asks Socrates to demonstrate, and explain how the ring of Gyges story is supposed to support his case."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim: "Glaucon says there are three kinds of goods. Some we want for their own sakes only, like harmless pleasures. Some we want only for what we get out of them, even though they're no fun in themselves — physical training, medical treatment, making money. The best kind is wanted both for itself and for its results — knowledge, sight, health. Socrates puts justice in the best kind, but Glaucon says the many put it in the second kind: something onerous we shoulder purely for the consequences, a good reputation and avoiding punishment, since no one is just willingly. On his telling, justice began as a bargain: everyone would most prefer to commit injustice and get away with it, but being on the receiving end is worse than the profit, so people compromise and enact laws — basically Thrasymachus's view that justice is the advantage of the stronger, restated as a social contract. To support this he tells the story of a shepherd in the service of the king: an earthquake opens a chasm, he climbs down and finds a bronze horse with a corpse inside wearing a gold ring, and when the ring's stone is turned inward he becomes invisible. He seduces the queen, and with her help attacks and kills the king, taking the throne. Glaucon's point is that a just person and an unjust one, each handed such a ring, would behave identically, so justice is only ever practiced under compulsion. Adeimantus then adds that parents and society only ever praise justice for its rewards, and the poets let you buy off the gods with sacrifices, so there's no deterrent in the afterlife either. So the challenge for Socrates is to prove that justice is good for its own sake only, ignoring reputation and consequences entirely, and that it belongs among the first category of goods."
