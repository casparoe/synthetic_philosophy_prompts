"Moore's argument: he holds up first one hand, then the other, and asserts each time that there is a hand before him. Since a hand is a material object existing in space, from these two premises it deductively follows that there are at least two external things. So the argument is valid, and the conclusion Moore draws is fairly modest -- only that at least two external things exist, not that everything common sense believes about the world is true. Moore was responding to Descartes, who famously said it was a scandal of philosophy that the existence of things outside us had never been proved. Moore's three conditions for a rigorous proof are: first, the conclusion must be different from the premise; second, the prover must know the premise; third, the proof must not be circular, i.e. the premise must not be proved by means of the conclusion. Moore concedes he cannot prove his premises and even that he cannot prove he is not dreaming, but he insists that he knows them anyway. Still, this admission is fatal. Since he cannot rule out that he is dreaming, he cannot know that here is a hand, so the proof fails his own second condition. And because the premises cannot be proved, the proof is question-begging -- indeed Moore effectively concedes as much when he admits the premises need proof he can't give. So the proof is valid but circular, and it cannot persuade anyone who doesn't already accept its premises."
