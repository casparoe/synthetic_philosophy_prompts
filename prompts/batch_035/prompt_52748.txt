I'm preparing a discussion case for a course unit on early modern philosophy of mind, and I want one that stresses Descartes' doctrine that non-human animals are mere automata — machines that act through the disposition of their organs, without any thought — in a way Descartes himself would have recognized as pressing rather than as a modern cheap shot. As I read the relevant part of the Discourse on Method, Descartes actually runs two distinguishable tests, and I want a case that pries them apart. The first is the sign test: no animal, however much it can mimic sounds, ever arranges signs flexibly and appropriately to communicate its thoughts, whereas even a cognitively limited human denied speech will improvise some way of making herself understood. The second is the universality test: reason is a universal instrument that copes with any situation, while any mechanism built for a narrow range of tasks will be brittle outside it — animals may outperform us within their own domain, but they never show the adaptability of a mind that can reapply its competence to circumstances utterly unlike those it was made for.

Construct a thought experiment around a single animal that meets all of the following specifications: (a) it fails the sign test outright — the animal never communicates anything, so the case cannot be resolved in its favor by language; (b) it passes the universality test in a pointed way — it combines two independently learned competences to solve a problem of a kind it has never encountered before and that no fixed disposition of its organs seems built to handle, and it does so on the first exposure rather than by visible trial and error; (c) the scenario stays realistic and low-tech, the sort of thing a field researcher could witness and film, with no surgery, no human training regime, and no exotic species invented to make the point; (d) nothing about the animal's inner life may be stipulated — no phrases like it realizes or it intends to describe the behavior itself, since a case that simply asserts the verdict rather than generating it from what an observer could see has cheated. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do the philosophical bookkeeping. First, give the strongest Cartesian response that is actually available: a layered mechanist explanation in terms of cascading evolved heuristics, cue-sensitivity, and generalization from prior learning, and note that Descartes himself never denied that machines can beat us at particular tasks — the mechanist must be a genuine opponent here, not someone who forgets his own doctrine. Second, give the opposing reading, on which the compositional reuse of old skills in a structurally novel situation is exactly the universality Descartes said was the signature of mind. Third, tell me precisely which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work — I suspect it is the first-exposure constraint, since any performance preceded by failed attempts becomes attributable to incremental learning rather than insight, and I want you to confirm or correct that suspicion. Finally, describe the bullet a Cartesian can bite if the mechanist explanation starts to look strained: retreating to the sign test as a constitutive rather than an evidential criterion of mind, so that no behavior, however flexible, could ever count. Say explicitly what that retreat costs the doctrine — in particular, whether it leaves the beast-machine thesis insulated from ethology in a way that should trouble someone who, like Descartes, takes himself to be doing natural philosophy rather than dogmatics.
