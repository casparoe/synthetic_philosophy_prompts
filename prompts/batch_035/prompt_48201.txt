I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory — 12 students, one 90-minute session. Next week we reach the Pasadena game: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip n, the payoff is +2^n/n when n is odd and −2^n/n when n is even. The expected-value series is conditionally convergent, so by rearranging its terms you can make the game "worth" any real number you like. On the page my students nod along; I want them to feel the breakdown rather than just verify the algebra. Design an in-class activity for this.

Requirements. Participants should run into the problem themselves, before any mathematics is put on the board: they should play the game, price it, trade on those prices, and only gradually discover that no stable price is forthcoming. Concretely, I'd like a scheme where students run many trials (coins or dice or a sheet of random digits — no laptops, no real money, points or play money only), track running averages on the board, and hold some kind of market or auction in which they buy and sell plays of the game. At some point a second game should be introduced in which every single payoff is exactly one point larger than the Pasadena game's — so it dominates the first outcome by outcome — and the students should be asked to price the pair and trade between them. The point of that stage should emerge from what happens, not from what I say.

Include: the rules and payoff scheme written out so I can put them on a handout; a minute-by-minute or stage-by-stage timeline; a way of aggregating results across the 12 students that makes the instability visible; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal machinery — the conditional convergence, the Riemann rearrangement result, and crucially the difference between a sample average over finite trials and the expectation itself. Name nothing up front; the phrases "Pasadena game," "undefined expectation," and "conditionally convergent" should appear for the first time in the debrief or later.

Two honest notes I also need. First: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I anticipate, for instance, that in a typical session no one will ever see the large-magnitude outcomes, so the sample data will look tame and possibly priceable, and I need to know how to handle that (more trials? pooled data? seeded outliers?). Second: which features of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief — the rearrangement theorem in particular seems like something no amount of coin-flipping will exhibit, and I want you to be frank about the gap between what the students will have seen and what they will have to take on authority.

Keep the whole plan to 700 words or fewer.
