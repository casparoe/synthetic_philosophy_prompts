I've been cataloguing my gut reactions to paternalistic interventions, and I've noticed they don't line up with the principle I'd naturally offer for them, so I'd like help working out what I actually believe — or discovering that I believe nothing coherent.

My tentative principle runs like this: an intervention is acceptable paternalism when it rearranges how available options are presented — their defaults, prominence, friction, presentation — while leaving every option open, and it crosses a line when it closes options or attaches penalties to them. Steering is fine; gates are not.

Now the verdicts, and you can see the trouble.

Putting the salad bar at the entrance and the fries behind the counter: fine. Plain packaging and graphic warnings on cigarettes: fine. Enrolling new employees in a workplace pension automatically, with a form to fill in if they want out: fine. An employer setting a default that email doesn't arrive on phones after seven in the evening, with an opt-out per employee: fine.

Making adult cyclists wear helmets under legal penalty: I'd say fine, and I notice this already strains my principle, since it plainly closes an option — yet my gut is more comfortable with it than with softer things below.

Banning the sale of sodas over a certain size: not fine. A bar owner who quietly serves half-pour drinks at full price to patrons he judges to have had enough: not fine, and worse than the soda ban even though it does less to restrict anyone.

Here is the pair I can't reconcile. My country withholds a couple of percent extra income tax by default as forced savings, refundable on request but only through a paper form at a tax office: this strikes me as seriously objectionable — presumptuous, collectivist, my money not theirs to hold. Yet it seems structurally identical to the pension auto-enrollment I cheerfully approved three verdicts ago: the same default, the same opt-out, arguably a similar bureaucratic burden in each direction. I've tried telling myself the difference is the employer versus the state, but I can't make myself mind a state-run pension scheme done the same way, so that's not it either.

Two more cases, both involving things that keep every option open yet feel off. A new office building where the elevators are immediate and prominent while the stairwell doors are heavy, unmarked, and painted the color of the walls: everyone can still take the stairs, but I find something creepy about architecture engineered to make one choice unattractive without saying so. And a phone maker whose texting app, by default, holds messages composed after midnight as drafts until morning, sending them then unless the user confirms each one with an extra long-press: everyone I've described it to thinks it's thoughtful, and I half-agree, but it was designed by people who think they know what 2 a.m. me intends better than 2 a.m. me does, and I keep tripping on that.

The motorcycle helmet question — mandatory for adult riders on the same logic as the seatbelt — I go back and forth on more than seems reasonable given how easy I found the seatbelt verdict, so I'd want to know whether whatever principle you extract makes the two come apart or forces them together.

So: is there a single principle behind these verdicts, or a small set of principles that divide the cases at a joint I'd accept? Please test my options-open/options-closed principle honestly — I suspect it's the wrong cut, since it seems to license the stairwell building and forbid the helmet law, both wrongly. Then tell me which of my verdicts any better principle simply cannot save, and what it predicts about a case I haven't decided: a hospital that by default withholds scan results from the online patient portal until a follow-up appointment, reasoning that immediate access causes anxiety and worse decisions, with a box patients can tick at intake to see results right away.
