I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been offered a nine-month fellowship at a policy research institute, for which I need a two-page project proposal. My beat has drifted toward the question Langdon Winner made famous decades ago: whether technological artifacts can themselves have politics. The part of that debate I cannot let go of is this. The canonical illustration — the story that the planner Robert Moses had the parkway bridges to Long Island's beaches built low so that buses, whose riders were mostly poor, could not reach them, while car owners could — has since been challenged by researchers who went back to the record and found that the story does not hold up in its strong form. Yet the wider thesis appears untouched: nearly everyone who believed artifacts can be political has gone on believing it, and nearly everyone who rejected it has gone on rejecting it. That makes me suspect the standard claim is doing something other than empirical work, and it suggests my project: state the artifact-politics thesis in a form that could actually be refuted by facts about an artifact's design and history, and then say, honestly, whether any version of it survives.

Please turn this into a proposal with the following components. First, the question stated precisely: distinguish at least the three readings I keep running into — that a design decision intentionally encodes an authority relation; that an artifact, whatever its designer intended, fits some social arrangements far better than others, so that adopting it settles a political question by default; and that an artifact merely reflects, without reinforcing, the politics of its makers. Second, why the existing positions leave the question open: both the hard-determinist line and the social-constructionist line seem able to accommodate any historical finding whatsoever, which is exactly the property I want to test. Third, the method: I would select a handful of cases in which the design history is unusually well documented and contested, chosen by fixed criteria I state in advance rather than cherry-picked after I know how they will come out, and in each case I would specify, ahead of time, what finding about the documentary record would have to count for and against the political reading. Fourth, the deliverable: not a monograph but two things — a set of working criteria that editors and policy analysts can use to judge when a story of the form 'the technology itself is political' is actually earned, and a long-form essay that explains the criteria through the cases. Fifth, what failure would look like: if every criterion I propose turns out to be satisfiable or violable without changing anyone's verdict, the honest conclusion is that the thesis as normally used is unfalsifiable, and the essay should say so plainly rather than bury it.

Add a closing paragraph on why the institute should fund a journalist rather than a philosopher for this, given that my supposed comparative advantage is forcing abstractions back against the documentary record — which is where, I suspect, this thesis either lives or dies. Omit any bibliography or citations; I will supply the references myself, and for the case studies I would rather you specify the selection criteria and the tests than name particular episodes.
