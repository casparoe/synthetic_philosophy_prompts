I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and in two weeks I'm defending a chapter at a departmental workshop. The chapter's thesis: Popper's famous complaint that the theory of natural selection is close to tautological, followed by his partial recantation, is best read not as a stumble he had to walk back, but as an anticipated consequence of his own mature view of science — one on which Darwinism figures as a metaphysical research programme in something like Lakatos's sense, an unfalsifiable framework that nevertheless disciplines and inspires testable science. On my reading, Popper's initial deflationary verdict and his later, warmer one are two halves of a single position, and the common narrative that he simply capitulated under pressure from biologists misreads both texts.

The audience is the problem. The workshop will be attended by a philosophy of biology group that largely treats Popper as a period piece — a falsificationist whose demarcation criterion was buried by Lakatos and Laudan decades ago — plus two evolutionary biologists who know the actual population-genetic content of the modern synthesis and have no patience for philosophy-of-science commentary on it that doesn't engage the quantitative theory. One of the philosophers has also, in the past, invoked the creationist quote-mining of Popper's tautology remarks as evidence that anything short of crystal-clear demarcation talk does active political harm.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the seven or so objections I should actually expect from this specific room, ordered roughly by how much damage each could do to the chapter, each phrased the way its likely asker would phrase it. For each, give me a short reply I can deliver in two minutes, plus an honest note on where the reply is thin. Please also flag the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — the one that would expose the weakest joint in my reading, which I'd rather walk in prepared to defend than discover at the podium.
