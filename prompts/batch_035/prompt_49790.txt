hey, could you write a satirical set of app release notes — a changelog, like the "what's new in version 3.0" text you'd see on the App Store — for a fictional self-help app called "Dichotomy"? around 500-650 words, numbered entries, deadpan corporate voice throughout.

the gimmick of the app: its core feature is a classifier that sorts everything in the user's life into two buckets, "Up To You" and "Not Up To You." The satire targets the pop-stoicism / wellness-industrial complex — the way it gets stripped down to mood management and hustle quotes and loses the virtue ethics and the cosmopolitanism — so the humor should come from applying the actual doctrine too rigorously, not from inventing wacky app features. A specialist should read it and think the author knows the opening of the Enchiridion cold, without the piece ever winking.

some elements id like (use some or all, no need for everything):

- the 2.0 classifier had a bug where self-help culture's favorite stuff — gym progress, job performance, dating outcomes — was getting tagged "Up To You," and users are furious that 3.0 reassigns it. the 3.0 fix: only your judgments and impulses stay controllable (the prohairesis / faculty-of-assent territory), everything else, including the *outcomes* of your efforts, goes to externals. one release note should explain this "fix" in cheerful corporate language, maybe with a user review complaining that the app just called his marathon "an indifferent."
- a "known issues" section where the support tickets are actually the classic objections to Stoicism — the quietism charge, the "so my grief was up to me the whole time?" complaint — played straight as bug reports.
- the premium tier joke. either direction works: charging $12.99/month for things that are indifferents (streaks, badges, a quote widget), or having moved serenity behind a paywall and the notes defending it with a straight face.
- maybe one beta-tester quote from a Cynic who unsubscribed because the app made things too comfortable, and/or an Epicurean reviewer leaving one star on principle.

dont quote Epictetus or Marcus verbatim — paraphrase is fine, and a fake corporate paraphrase is honestly funnier than the real thing anyway. also, im not looking for it to be mean toward people who genuinely find these apps helpful — the target is the repackaging, not the users, so if the user characters come off as a bit right, even better.
