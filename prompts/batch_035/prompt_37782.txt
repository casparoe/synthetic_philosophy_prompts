Until the 1960s, death simply meant the stopped heart. Then mechanical ventilation made it possible to sustain circulation in patients whose brains had been destroyed, so in 1968 a Harvard committee redefined death as the irreversible loss of all brain function. The biological rationale is straightforward: the brain is the body's master integrator, coordinating respiration, circulation, and endocrine regulation, so once the entire brain is destroyed the body's unity collapses and the organism dies, no matter what machinery is attached to it. Since a patient in this state can neither breathe nor ever regain consciousness, the whole-brain criterion and the higher-brain criterion are really just two ways of describing the same fact: the death of the person as a conscious being. In 1981 the Uniform Determination of Death Act put this into legal form in the United States, with two prongs: you are dead if circulation and respiration have irreversibly ceased, or if the entire brain, including the brainstem, has irreversibly ceased functioning. The circulatory prong remains the uncomplicated case, because the moment the heart stops the body's cells begin dying within minutes, so both prongs are just two routes to the same biological endpoint, namely the moment of death. Today brain death is settled in the law and medicine of every developed country, and the only remaining controversy is over religious accommodation for families who refuse the neurological criterion.
