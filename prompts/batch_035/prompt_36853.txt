I'm a self-taught carpenter who builds custom kitchens and built-ins, and for the past six years I've kept notes on every job I've bid. I've come to think there are two of me doing the estimating. Standing in a client's kitchen, I get a confident number straight from the details — this hinge is the one from the Hendersons' job, that mantel I've built twice before — call that the close-up. Then there's the ledger: across forty-odd jobs, my first guess ran short by about a third, every time, regardless of what the job was like — call that the wide shot. When I'm excited about a job I tend to write the close-up number into the contract, and those are the jobs that eat my winter in unpaid rework. I'd like a procedure I can run alone, in about twenty minutes per bid, that tells me which number to write, under what conditions the close-up is allowed to win, what to do when the ledger contains no comparable jobs, and where the procedure should stop because the question has become how much risk I'll stomach for a job I want — which no checklist can settle for me. It must include the step that makes me write the bigger number even when I want the job badly; please don't leave that step out because it's unwelcome, and don't soften it.

Give me exactly five numbered steps, each one or two sentences. A short example may be folded into a step, but nothing may appear outside the list, and there should be no introduction or conclusion. Within the steps, make it clear how confident you are in each part, and mark the single step you are least sure of, saying why right there in that step.
