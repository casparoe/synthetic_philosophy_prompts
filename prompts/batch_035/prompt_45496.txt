I spent the better part of four decades as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been working through the Bayesian literature on rational belief more or less systematically. I have now reached Jeffrey's rule for updating on uncertain evidence, and I must report a reaction that probably betrays my training: it looks like a solution to a problem I never had. In laboratory life, no observation was ever certain — every reading came with an error bar — and yet we never supposed that this obliged us to revise probabilities by directly installing new values over a partition of hypotheses while discarding the observation itself. The noise was simply more physics: one wrote down a likelihood relating the state of the world to the needle position, then conditioned on the needle position, error bar notwithstanding. Jeffrey conditionalization, by contrast, sets aside the observation proposition altogether and instructs you to redistribute credence directly across a partition in line with how the observation left you feeling. I can see the formal appeal, but it appears to purchase flexibility at a considerable price; I understand, for instance, that successive Jeffrey updates can depend on the order in which they arrive, which seems an odd property for rational learning to have.

Please explain, for someone with my background in measurement and inference but no formal training in philosophy, what problem uncertain evidence genuinely poses that ordinary conditionalization on a noisy observation cannot handle. Then take a definite position: is Jeffrey conditionalization a fundamental improvement on strict conditionalization, a harmless bookkeeping device for those regrettable situations where one cannot articulate the relevant observation proposition, or a mistake of framing — in which case the canonical examples (the fabric inspected by candlelight, the witness of dubious reliability) should really be redescribed so that the uncertainty lives in the likelihoods rather than in the evidence? If you defend Jeffrey's rule, say precisely where my "it is just noise in the likelihoods" response fails; if you side with my instinct, be candid about what the redescription costs, particularly in cases where the agent seems unable to report any determinate observation at all. Please commit to a single verdict rather than surveying the options for me, and flag any step where your argument rests on a contested reading.
