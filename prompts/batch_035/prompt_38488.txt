I help run the math seminar at my university, and this semester we've been working through an introduction to axiomatic set theory, ending with the axiom of choice and its gallery of equivalent forms. For our final session I'd like to close with something a bit less dry than the usual problem set, and it occurred to me that a villanelle is almost suspiciously well suited to the material: a poem built entirely out of two refrains that keep returning in new contexts, never quite the same, until they finally stand side by side in the closing couplet. That's more or less what the chapter on the axiom of choice feels like — Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem, the statement about Cartesian products of nonempty sets, all turning out to be the same sentence wearing different clothes. So I'd like you to write a villanelle in which refrain A is one formulation of the axiom of choice and refrain B is another equivalent formulation (I'd suggest the well-ordering theorem for one of them, but I'll trust your judgment on the pairing; please pick formulations whose equivalence we actually proved in class, not something stronger or adjacent like dependent choice).

A few things I'd like the poem to do. First, the form should do the philosophical work rather than the diction: the poem should never announce that the two refrains are equivalent — the reader should feel it, because the same lines keep surviving every new context the tercets throw at them. Second, I'd love the middle stanzas to take up the classic anxieties about the axiom without resolving into either a sermon or a shrug: the sense that choosing one sock from each of infinitely many indistinguishable pairs demands a rule we don't possess, the feeling that some of the equivalences are proven rather than seen, and maybe Banach–Tarski looming as the strange rent we pay for the convenience. It's fine for the poem to be uneasy about the axiom — most of us are, some of the time — but I don't want it to editorialize crudely, since plenty of working mathematicians use choice without a flicker of doubt and the seminar contains both kinds of person. Finally, the closing couplet, where the two refrains meet for the first time, should carry the weight: whatever happens there is the poem's actual claim about what it means for two statements to be one.

On the accuracy bar: our instructor is a set theorist, and she'll wince at anything wrong, so the mathematics has to be right — but several seminar members are poets with no set theory at all, so it also has to work as a poem for someone who doesn't know what a well-ordering is. If you can make those readers feel the uncanny recursiveness of the form even before they know what it's recursing about, that would be exactly the trick I'm hoping for. Length is the standard villanelle: nineteen lines, two rhymes throughout, refrains in their usual stations.
