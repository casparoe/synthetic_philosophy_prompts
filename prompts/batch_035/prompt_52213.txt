I'd like your help auditing an analogy that does a lot of work in debates I keep finding myself in, and deciding whether the conclusion it is used to support survives scrutiny.

In discussions about technology ethics, a friend of mine makes the following move with remarkable regularity. Every technology, she says, is essentially a tool, and tools are morally inert: a hammer can build a house or crack a skull, a knife can carve a dinner or open an artery, and in every case the moral character of the act lies entirely with the wielder, not the instrument. From this she concludes that asking whether some technology is 'good' or 'bad' — or worse, whether it has a politics — is a category error: the only proper objects of moral and political assessment are people, their intentions, and perhaps their institutions. Tools are picked up and put down; nothing of moral interest happens in between. She deploys this against a whole range of targets: worries about facial recognition, claims that recommender systems shape public discourse, even the suggestion that certain designs of workplace technology favour certain kinds of social arrangement.

I have lately been reading around the opposite position, which seems to have originated with Langdon Winner's 1980 essay 'Do Artifacts Have Politics?'. As I understand him, Winner argues that artifacts can embody political relations in at least two ways. First, a design decision can quietly arrange social life, in a way that may be neither clearly intended nor clearly unintended: his famous — possibly apocryphal, and I may have the details wrong — example is the claim that Robert Moses had the bridges over his Long Island parkways built unusually low, so that cars could pass beneath them but buses could not, with the reported effect that people who could not afford cars, many of them black New Yorkers, were kept off the parkways and away from the beaches they led to. Second, Winner suggests that some technologies structurally require, or at least strongly favour, a particular social order in order to operate at all: a nuclear power plant, on his account, almost demands the centralised, hierarchical, security-minded authority that tends to grow up around it, in a way that, say, a distributed arrangement of solar panels does not; and he points to the mechanical tomato harvester, which (again as I recall it) made sense only on large industrial farms and thereby helped consolidate them, while an alternative programme of plant breeding might instead have benefited smallholders.

What I would like from you is a systematic audit of my friend's analogy — the 'technology is just a tool' picture — as it stands against this opposition. Please do the following:

1. Give the correspondence table explicitly. In the hammer case, spell out what plays the role of the designer, the manufacturer, the wielder, the victim, the surrounding legal system that deters misuse, and so forth — and then map the target case (a large, socio-technical system such as a social media platform or a power grid) onto those roles. I suspect the mapping is where everything goes wrong, so be concrete about where one person's 'wielder' is another's design constraint.

2. Identify the disanalogies, and sort them. The hammer is simple, cheap, transparent in its operation, operable by a single person, produces effects visible at a glance, needs no infrastructure, imposes no demands on bystanders, and does not change its behaviour depending on how many people use it. Most of the technologies my friend's conclusion is applied to lack several of these features. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for her intended use of the analogy, and which are incidental? For instance, does it matter that a platform's 'wielder' is ambiguous (the company? the users? the advertisers?), or that the effects on bystanders who never chose to pick the tool up are central rather than incidental?

3. Say whether the neutrality conclusion survives. Suppose some artifacts can, as Winner claims, have politics 'in them' before anyone wields them, and can lock in a social order around themselves. Does this refute the tool picture for all tools, or only for tools above some threshold of complexity, infrastructure, and reach — in which case can the threshold be drawn on principled grounds rather than case-by-case intuition? And does Winner's stronger claim commit him to technological determinism (artifacts dictating social structure), which he apparently denies, or is there a stable intermediate position? My friend will no doubt reply that even the Moses bridges were designed by a man with intentions, and that the harvester was chosen by somebody; I would like to know whether that reply, which seems to relocate rather than dissolve the politics, leaves her original conclusion intact or quietly abandons it.

4. If the hammer analogy is beyond repair, propose a better one. What would be a comparison that tracks the designer, the system, the social order, and the distribution of burdens and benefits without smuggling in the neutrality it was supposed to establish?

I should say that I am not looking for a bibliography or for textual scholarship on Winner; treat my summary of his position as the statement of the opposing view to be engaged with, and argue it through on the merits.
