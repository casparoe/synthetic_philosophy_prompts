I teach an introductory course in logic and critical reasoning, and this term I'm preparing a unit on epistemic paradoxes, in particular the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. My students usually grasp each case in isolation but miss that the two pull in opposite directions: the lottery case tempts us to say that each individual belief is justified even though their conjunction is not, while the preface case makes us sympathetic to an author who believes every claim in her book and, in the same breath, believes that at least one of those claims is false. To force the comparison, I've written a new case for the problem set, and I'd like help calibrating the answer key.

The new case is an auditor's report. An external auditor, working to standard professional practice, issues a report stating that each individual accounting entry in a company's ledger is free from material misstatement, each claim warranted at the confidence level that sampling supports; and in the very same report, just as honestly, she states that the ledger as a whole almost certainly contains at least one material misstatement somewhere, so the reader should rely on the statements only with that caveat. Structurally, this is meant to sit between the two classics: like the lottery, the individual claims are individually warranted but jointly incredible; like the preface, the speaker asserts the aggregate caveat in her own voice, inside the same document, without any sense of having contradicted herself.

Please rank the following five responses to the classic pair from most to least satisfying as a unified treatment that also covers the auditor case, giving a sentence for each placement on which pre-theoretic judgment it preserves and which it sacrifices: (a) the Kyburg-style response, which blocks the inference from individually justified beliefs to a justified belief in their conjunction; (b) the response that denies the individual lottery beliefs are justified in the first place, so there is nothing to aggregate; (c) the contextualist or threshold-shifting response, on which the standards for justification vary with practical or conversational context, so the same belief can be justified for one purpose and not for another; (d) the probabilist response, which dissolves the paradoxes by replacing all-or-nothing belief with credences; and (e) the tolerant response, on which the preface author, and the auditor, is simply rational in believing all the claims and also believing that not all of them are true, the inconsistency being benign rather than fatal. Then close with one short paragraph on whether the auditor case, as I've described it, genuinely straddles the two classics or merely restates the preface paradox with a ledger in place of a book — if it's the latter, my problem set is less clever than I'd hoped, and I'd rather know now than after I hand it out.
