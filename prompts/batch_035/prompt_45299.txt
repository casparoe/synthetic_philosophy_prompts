I'm a software engineer with no formal background in political philosophy, and I'd like your help evaluating an analogy I keep encountering in discussions of China and Singapore.

The analogy goes roughly as follows: a government is best understood as a service provider, citizens are its customers, and legitimacy is customer satisfaction earned through delivery — safety, functioning infrastructure, rising incomes. On this view, a ruling party that delivers growth and order has earned its position the way a company earns market dominance through good products, and foreigners who complain about the absence of elections are like people insisting that a firm must win a vote among its customers before it is allowed to sell them anything. The conclusion usually drawn is that performance, not process, is what justifies political authority, so long as the goods keep arriving.

Please take on the role of an expert in political philosophy and think carefully before answering, perhaps starting with some brainstorming of the correspondences rather than jumping to a verdict. I'd like a genuine audit of the analogy: a mapping table of what is supposed to correspond to what (citizens, taxes, elections, an opposition party, and anything else the analogy needs), then the points where the mapping breaks down — for example, whether the average customer can switch providers as easily as the analogy's satisfied citizens would need to. Please distinguish the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion about legitimacy from the ones that are just noise. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that a state which delivers prosperity and order holds legitimate authority, so questions of process fall away — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion the whole time; and if a better analogy is available for thinking about performance-based rule, say what it is and what it gets right that this one gets wrong.
