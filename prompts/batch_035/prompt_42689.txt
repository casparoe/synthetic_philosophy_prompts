I've been listening to a philosophy podcast whose host summarized Kant's ethics for beginners roughly as follows: "Kant's real test for right and wrong is surprisingly simple: take whatever you're about to do, generalize it into a rule, and ask what would happen if everyone acted on that rule. If the consequences of universal compliance would be bad — if the practice would break down or backfire — the act is wrong; if the consequences would be fine, the act is permissible. For example, lying fails the test because if everyone lied, trust would collapse and lying would become pointless, so no one could profit by it anymore. Kant's formula is basically the Golden Rule stated with German rigor: before acting, imagine yourself on the receiving end of a world where everyone does what you're about to do, and if you wouldn't accept being treated that way, you may not treat others that way. In the end, Kant's ethics just asks each of us to weigh our private inclinations against the general welfare, and to vote for the course of action that the whole community could endorse."
