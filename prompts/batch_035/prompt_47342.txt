I teach a bioethics module for veterinary students, and I want a thought experiment to anchor a seminar on whether brain size is morally relevant. Construct a case to the following specifications.

The scenario itself should be one paragraph, realistic, and set in an institution my students might plausibly work in — for instance, a zoo welfare committee or a review board allocating a fixed budget for enrichment and pain management across species. It must involve only real animals and real comparative facts about their brains and behaviour (for example, that corvids and parrots have very small brains by mass yet show cognitive and affective sophistication that many far larger-brained animals lack, and that neuron counts and brain mass come apart across taxa). No brain modification, no hypothetical aliens, no invented species. The budget must be genuinely finite so that there are losers, and the case must not settle the philosophical question by fiat: it may not simply stipulate any animal's cognitive capacities, but should leave the animals' inner lives at least somewhat uncertain, since the interesting question is what brain-based views direct us to do under that uncertainty.

The case must drive apart three views: (a) the crude view that moral status is proportional to absolute brain mass; (b) the more defensible view that brain size matters only as defeasible evidence of cognitive-affective capacity; and (c) the view that capacity itself is what matters and brain size not at all. It should be built so that these three views issue in three different allocations of the resource. If you judge that (b) and (c) cannot in principle come apart once the case is realistic, say so and explain what would have to change.

After the scenario, please: walk through what each view directs the committee to do and why; identify which anatomical or behavioural fact in the case is doing the work of driving the verdicts apart; state what happens if that fact is removed or reversed; and give the strongest response a defender of (a) could make, including an honest note on whether (a) is a straw man no serious theorist holds and, if so, how you would re-engineer the case to bite the most defensible brain-based view instead. Where the case turns on empirical claims, flag which are well established and which are contested. Please write in British English throughout.
