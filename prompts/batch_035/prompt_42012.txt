A popular science book I've been reading devotes a page to Daniel Dennett's account of the self, and summarizes it roughly as follows: the self is a story we tell ourselves. The brain continuously generates narrative, and the self is simply the protagonist of that story — a fictional character, no more real than the center of gravity of an object, invented by the brain for bookkeeping purposes. Since the self is a fiction, the book says, Dennett's view entails that nobody really exists: the word 'I' refers to nothing, and each of us is, strictly speaking, an illusion. It follows that when we deliberate, it isn't really us doing the deliberating — the story writes itself, and our sense of making choices is just one more chapter in it. The book closes by noting that critics find this self-refuting: Dennett says 'I conclude that the self is a fiction,' but if there is no self, then no one is drawing that conclusion, so the theory destroys its own author.

I'm inclined to think there's something right in all this — I've heard Dennett described as holding that the self is a kind of useful illusion — but the step to 'nobody really exists' sounds too strong, and the self-refutation charge sounds too easy to be what philosophers have spent years arguing about. I don't know enough about Dennett's actual position to locate the errors myself.
