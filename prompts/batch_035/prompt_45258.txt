My advisor wants me to write the Black's spheres chapter of my dissertation, and a friend in the logic group just walked me into an argument I can't get out of, which either sinks the chapter or sinks the identity of indiscernibles outright. Help me find the exit. (1) Black's universe is just two iron spheres a mile apart and nothing else, and it's perfectly symmetrical: swap the spheres and every purely qualitative truth about the world stays exactly the same. (2) If a permutation of objects fixes all the qualitative truths, then the objects it swaps share every purely qualitative property -- that's basically what a symmetry amounts to. (3) PII says sharing all purely qualitative properties gets you identity. (4) So the two spheres are identical, and what I was calling "a two-sphere world" is really a one-sphere world that sloppy people like me miscount. My friend is fine biting that bullet -- he says Black's world is impossible and the intuition that it's coherent is exactly the confusion PII is supposed to diagnose. (5) But now take one of the spheres on it's own. It's homogenous and rotationally symmetric: rotating it fixes every qualitative truth, and from a certain point of view a rotation just is a permutation of its parts, shuffling the points around while the qualitative facts sit still. (6) By the same reasoning as (2)-(4), those parts share every purely qualitative property, so by PII they're identical. (7) So a perfectly homogeneous sphere has no distinct parts at all -- it's one unextended thing that only looks like a big ball -- and running the same argument on any homogeneous region of space, distinct points collapse too, so space itself can't be homogeneous and nothing symmetric can exist. (8) So the symmetry argument for PII proves way too much, and something in it has to be broken.

I can see three outs and I don't love any of them. One: deny that an object's parts are "objects" that PII applies to, so (5)-(6) involve a scope error -- but that looks like giving mereology a free pass, and it doesn't obviously help if I run the same argument on two disconnected identical droplets instead of parts of one sphere. Two: say the spheres in Black's world were discernible all along via some irreflexive relation, like being a mile from -- each sphere stands in that relation to the other but not to itself. Three: keep (1)-(4) and bite the bullet that symmetry itself is metaphysically impossible, which feels like reductio territory but my friend genuinely seems willing to consider it. And if you're tempted by the irreflexive-relation move, please explain how it doesn't rescue the sphere's parts too -- distinct points on the sphere are also related by irreflexive distance relations, so it seems to save Black's world and the homogenous sphere together or not at all, which can't be what the anti-Black crowd wants. Which premise or step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the whole thing turns on an equivocation somewhere -- like on what counts as "purely qualitative," or on which things PII quantifies over -- tell me where the meaning shifts between the steps, because I can almost feel it and I can't name it.
