I'm giving the principal toast at the retirement dinner of Marion Castellanos, who is stepping down after nineteen years as city manager of a mid-sized rust-belt city that she is widely credited with saving from receivership. The room will hold her family, three mayors' worth of political operatives, the current director of public works, and — this is the hard part — the former deputy engineer who once worked under her.

Everyone present knows about the winter flood. The river was rising past the levee protecting the Eastside flats when the Corps told her the upstream reservoir's emergency spillway had to be opened that night or the whole system would fail catastrophically, downtown included. She had personally assured Eastside residents at a town hall two weeks earlier that their neighborhood would never be deliberately sacrificed. She gave the order. Four hundred homes flooded, nobody died, and she then signed an incident report attributing the release to an automatic sensor malfunction, which was false. The deputy engineer refused to co-sign, resigned, and was eventually proven right about what had happened; she never disputed it publicly, never apologized publicly either, and spent the following decade quietly steering an outsized share of infrastructure money to the Eastside — which her admirers call restitution and her critics call hush money. The mayor's people treat the whole episode as closed and consider her a hero of competence; the engineer is owed something that a toast can't simply be.

I don't want mush about hard calls in a hard job, and I don't want a stealth verdict — the occasion isn't a trial. But the philosophers who write about dirty hands insist that a leader who does the necessary wrong thing ought to feel the guilt, and that carrying it without either confessing or being absolved is part of doing the job at all; by all accounts she has done exactly that carrying, for nineteen years, without once saying so. Write me about five minutes that honors her without laundering her, that faces the falsified report without pretending a dinner is the venue for litigating it, and that the engineer and the mayor's aides can each hear as honest. It cannot contain a confession from her or an absolution from me.
