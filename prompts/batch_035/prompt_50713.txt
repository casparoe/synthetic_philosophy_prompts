I have no background in mathematics or philosophy — my education ended with secondary school — but over the past few years I have become convinced of a view I worked out for myself from reading popular explanations of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and I would like to know what would have to be true for me to be wrong.

The view, stated as carefully as I can manage: Gödel showed that any consistent system of formal rules strong enough to do basic arithmetic will contain true statements it cannot prove, and cannot establish its own consistency. From this I draw three conclusions of increasing ambition. First, any machine that reasons by manipulating symbols according to explicit rules — which I take to include present-day computers and, I suspect, any artificial intelligence we could build — is confined to such a system, so there will always be truths about arithmetic it can never reach. Second, since human mathematicians seem able to recognize the truth of precisely the statements that defeat any given formal system, human mathematical thinking cannot itself be rule-governed symbol manipulation in that sense. Third, and this is the part I have not seen anyone else spell out but which seems to follow: the same limitation should afflict any system that both operates by rules and contains a representation of itself — a nation's constitution trying to settle every question about its own amendment, a company whose internal procedures include rules for auditing its own auditors, an economy steered by a model that includes the modelers. Each of these, if it is rich enough to be interesting, will generate questions about itself that it cannot settle from inside. I have come to think of this as a general law of reflexive systems: sufficient self-reference plus rule-following yields blind spots, and no amount of patching removes them without creating new ones.

I want an honest map of this position's exposure. Which of the three conclusions carries the most risk, and what specific observations, arguments, or results would count against each — not merely logical possibilities, but things that could actually happen or be shown? For instance, I understand it is contested whether humans genuinely "see" the truth of the undecidable statements or merely assume consistency of systems we cannot actually verify; does that concession dissolve my second conclusion, or only bruise it? Is there a difference between a system's being unable to prove something and its being unable to know it, and does that difference break my third conclusion when I move from arithmetic to constitutions and economies — which are not, after all, arithmetic? Please rank the possible defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, say which ones a thoughtful person holding my view should concede outright, and tell me whether the view has been quietly insulated: if I respond to every counterexample by redescribing it as another Gödelian blind spot, have I stopped asserting anything testable — and if so, is that a defect in the view or a fair price for its kind?
