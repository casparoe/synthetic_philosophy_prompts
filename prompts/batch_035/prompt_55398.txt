I'm preparing to defend act consequentialism in my department's reading group, and the objection I'm least sure how to handle is the cluelessness problem. Rather than rehearse alone, I want a live opponent. Please play a committed act consequentialist — sharp, well-read in the literature, willing to concede that our empirical forecasts are shaky and even that most expected-value comparisons are wildly uncertain, but absolutely unwilling to concede that cluelessness undermines consequentialist decision-making itself. You may use whatever resources contemporary consequentialists actually deploy — fat-tailed expected value, symmetry arguments, robustness constraints on choice — but you may not quietly slide into rule consequentialism or "do what generally works" as a way of changing the subject. Stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph of at most 150 words.

Here is my opening move. Take the most mundane good deed imaginable: you hand a beggar five pounds. He buys a meal, walks home two minutes later than he otherwise would, nods to a different passer-by, and from that moment the causal ripples never stop. Conversations shift by seconds, meetings happen or fail to happen, and within a few generations the timing of conceptions is scrambled, so that entirely different people exist — different children, different careers, different atrocities and cures. Whatever expected value you assign to the immediate transfer, its magnitude is dwarfed by the variance in these untraceable remote effects, and you have no idea even of the sign. Your decision procedure instructs you to maximize a quantity that is, as far as you can tell, pure noise. So tell me: on your own theory, what exactly is the difference between giving him the five pounds and keeping it?
