So every couple years I teach the grad seminar on rational choice, and this term I'm supposed to hand out a one-page "where I stand" sheet before the decision theory unit, partly so the students can take shots at me for the rest of the semester. I drafted it last night and now that I look at it again I suspect there's something rotten in there somewhere, but I genuinely cant locate it. Here's roughly the list:

(1) In the one-shot Parfit's hitchhiker case — you're dying in the desert, the driver is a near-perfect predictor who'll only take you if she predicts you'll pay on arrival, and there are zero reputation effects or future interactions — you should pay, and paying is rational, full stop.

(2) In Kavka's toxin case — you get a million dollars tonight if at midnight you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow, the toxin makes you sick but won't kill you, and the billionaire can read your mind — it's rational to intend to drink, but come tomorrow, actually drinking the toxin is irrational. You've got the money, the intention did its job, drink nothing.

(3) Rationality is not a property of acts at moments. It's a property of whole plans or policies. An act at a time is rational only insofar as it belongs to the best plan that was available to you when you committed, and it can be rational to carry through on a step of the plan that looks terrible from inside that moment.

(4) Rational irrationality is a real phenomenon and not just a cute phrase. Sometimes the rational thing is to deliberately install a genuinely irrational disposition and then act from it — Schelling's point about the bargaining power of the leader who's half-mad, or the person who takes a pill that makes her incapable of reneging on a threat she's issued. Once installed, acting on the temper or the threat is itself rational.

(5) I'm an act consequentialist about moral rightness. What you morally ought to do is whatever act has the best consequences, no loopholes for past promises or dispositions.

(6) I've never bought Gauthier's constrained maximization. It always looked to me like an elaborate device for smuggling morality into rationality by fiat, and I explain whatever's right about it some other way.

(7) Sunk costs are never reasons. Past expenditure, past benefit recieved, effort already sunk — none of it can by itself make any difference to what there's reason to do now.

My problem: a couple of my own grad students have told me (smugly, I might add) that this is "not a package" but a drawer of mismatched parts, and when I pressed them on which parts clash they gave me three different answers. So I want a real audit, not a vibe. Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and where they really clash, what exactly has to give — and what adopting the fix costs me elsewhere on the list. Please rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally fatal, and be concrete about which commitment is doing the damage in each case. If some pair looks like it conflicts but actually doesnt, say so and show the work, because I'd rather keep most of these if I honestly can.
