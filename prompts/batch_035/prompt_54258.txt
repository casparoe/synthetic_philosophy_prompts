I'd like a careful explanation of a conceptual problem that arises in the theory of mechanistic interpretability, aimed at a reader who knows how neural networks work but knows nothing about interpretability research as a field.

The background is this. A recurring ambition in that field — it motivates, for example, the agenda of "mechanistic anomaly detection" — is the idea that a model fails on out-of-distribution inputs not merely because its outputs are wrong, but because it has switched from the mechanism that handled in-distribution inputs to some different mechanism that happens to produce output anyway; and that this switch could be detected by testing, on a fresh input, whether the model is still using the same mechanism it used in training. The whole idea depends on making "the same mechanism" precise, and when I try to state a criterion of mechanism identity, I fail at both ends of a spectrum.

At one end, an extensional criterion: two contexts involve the same mechanism iff the same input–output mapping is being computed. This is far too coarse. A giant lookup table and a compact algorithm compute the same function, so on this criterion they share a mechanism — which collapses exactly the distinction the anomaly-detection idea needs. "The model is doing something different on this input" is supposed to be a non-trivial claim; it cannot be if the only sameness on offer is sameness of input–output behavior.

At the other end, a token-physical criterion: two contexts involve the same mechanism iff the very same weights are read in the very same way, so that the intermediate activations are numerically identical. This is far too fine. No two distinct inputs share a mechanism on this reading, since activations differ numerically even between two ordinary in-distribution examples; the notion would be useless for comparing any two separate forward passes, which is the comparison the agenda is all about.

I am therefore drawn to an interventionist middle criterion: two contexts involve the same mechanism iff, in each, the output depends on the input through corresponding causal paths over corresponding intermediate variables, so that corresponding interventions — ablations, or patching activations from one context into the other — produce corresponding changes in the output. But two problems stop me from simply adopting it, and what I most want explained is what to do about them.

The first is a correspondence problem. The criterion presupposes that we can say which intermediate variable in context A corresponds to which in context B, and this does not come for free. It is familiar from interpretability work that a feature can split into two, or two features merge into one, across changes of context or across training; and in the limiting case the "corresponding variable" in B might be a pattern distributed across many units where in A it was localized in one. Unless there is an independent fact about which parts correspond, the interventionist criterion cannot even be applied — and I do not know what would make it true that a scattered pattern in B corresponds to a localized feature in A, rather than there being no fact of the matter at all.

The second is a granularity problem. Whether two contexts count as mechanistically the same seems to depend on how finely the mechanism is described: at a coarse grain, "compute the answer with a weighted sum" may be shared by both contexts; at a fine grain, nothing ever is. If "same mechanism" is thus partly a function of the describer's chosen level of analysis, then either the notion is objective and there is a principled way to fix the grain, or it is a convention, and the empirical-sounding claim "the model switched mechanisms" is really a claim about which descriptive conventions we find useful. I would like to know which, and what the choice would turn on.

With that setup in place, please explain three things.

(a) Why the extensional and token-physical criteria fail in the ways described, and what work the interventionist middle criterion is supposed to do — in particular, why it is supposed to carve the phenomenon at a joint that the input–output mapping does not reveal, and what kind of evidence (for instance, from ablations or activation patching) is supposed to bear on mechanistic sameness without collapsing into behavioral sameness.

(b) How the correspondence problem relates to the classic philosophical discussion of functional individuation and multiple realizability. The obvious suggestion is that "same mechanism" is a matter of type identity rather than token identity: two forward passes involve the same mechanism when both instantiate a single mechanism-type, just as physically different computers can instantiate the same algorithm. But functionalists have long known that type identity across different realizers requires a principled way of matching functional roles, and that the same matching problem reappears one level up — matching roles requires matching their parts, whose individuation raises the same question again. I want to know whether this regress is benign, terminating in some natural level of description, or vicious; and what an interpretability researcher should conclude about whether mechanism identity is a natural-kind matter or a bookkeeping convenience.

(c) Whether "same mechanism" should be treated as graded rather than binary. It is tempting to say that pairs of contexts lie on a spectrum — same pathway, somewhat different features, entirely different circuit — and that an anomaly detector should report degrees of mechanistic divergence rather than verdicts. Please explain what would be lost and what gained by going graded, and whether the interventionist criterion can be made to output a similarity ordering rather than a yes/no answer without quietly collapsing back into a measure of behavioral similarity.

In answering, please do not lean on any particular empirical paper or remembered result from the interpretability literature; nothing should depend on recalling specific findings. Work from the setup as I have stated it, general knowledge of how neural networks compute, and the philosophical material on functionalism, multiple realizability, and type/token distinctions. Formalism is welcome where it clarifies, but the explanation should remain followable by a reader with no interpretability background.
