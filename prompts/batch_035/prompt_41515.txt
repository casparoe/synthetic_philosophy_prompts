I'm in a philosophy of science reading group that's been stuck on the same fight about Kuhnian incommensurability for basically the whole semester, and I'm starting to suspect we're not even having a real disagreement anymore. The setup, roughly: one faction (call them the historians) insists incommensurability is just an obvious fact about science — the Newtonian term "mass" doesn't map cleanly onto relativistic mass or rest mass, phlogiston-era "dephlogisticated air" wasn't oxygen under a different description, and the practitioners of rival paradigms genuinely had trouble understanding each other. The other faction (the philosophers of language, basically) says that's all overblown: if two communities couldn't understand each other at all, we couldn't even recognize them as disagreeing, let alone describe one theory as replacing another; and once you grant any translation at all, the strong incommensurability thesis collapses into the boring observation that translation is lossy and dictionaries are imperfect. Late Kuhn's retreat to a *local* version — incommensurability only in bits of the vocabulary, tied to how terms get their meaning from their place in a lexical structure — was supposed to split the difference, but in our group it just gave both sides new ammunition to keep saying the same things.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to actually evaluate rather than just nod at, is this: the two camps are using "translation" to mean two different things. The philosophers of language implicitly demand something like full substitutability in all contexts plus preservation of inferential role, find that nowhere in science, and declare incommensurability false or trivial. The historians implicitly work with something much weaker — good-enough-for-purposes translation, the kind that lets a textbook footnote gloss one term with another — find that this fails in specific, historically locatable spots, and declare incommensurability real. If that's right, the "debate" is partly verbal, but maybe not *only* verbal, because there might be a substantive residue: whether the local translation failures cluster in ways that matter for the big Kuhnian claims about theory choice, or whether they're just noise that would appear in any comparison of technical vocabularies, scientific or not (is the gap between two paradigms really bigger than the gap between, like, two subspecialties of contract law that also use overlapping words differently?).

So: diagnose this thing properly. Is it a verbal dispute, two different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical question about the actual history, or a genuine substantive disagreement about how meaning works in science? Use what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature (I'll track down references myself). And be honest about whether anything could count as progress here — a sharper typology of translation failure, some specific historical case worked in enough detail to force one side to bite a bullet, a formal account of partial intertranslatability — or whether the right move is to conclude that incommensurability was always a grab-bag of distinct claims that should have been separated out from the start. If your diagnosis differs from my hypothesis, say so and say why mine is wrong. Also feel free to tell me if my framing of the local/global retreat misses something, but dont make that the whole answer.
