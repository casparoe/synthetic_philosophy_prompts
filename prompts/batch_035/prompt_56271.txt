The exam question was:

A three-judge appellate panel must decide a tort case. The court's doctrine is that the defendant is liable (R) if and only if she both owed the plaintiff a duty of care (P) and breached that duty (Q). The judges' views are as follows: Judge 1 accepts P, accepts Q, and accepts R; Judge 2 accepts P, rejects Q, and rejects R; Judge 3 rejects P, accepts Q, and rejects R.

(a) Determine the panel's collective judgments if each proposition is put to a majority vote, and explain why the outcome is problematic for majority voting as an aggregation method.
(b) Explain how the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures would handle this case, and what each yields.
(c) Explain, as precisely as you can, the sense in which the discursive dilemma generalizes to an impossibility result about judgment aggregation, and assess whether the two procedures from part (b) escape that result.

The student's answer:

(a) Taking the propositions one at a time: a majority (Judges 1 and 2) accepts P, a majority (Judges 1 and 3) accepts Q, and a majority (Judges 2 and 3) rejects R. So the panel collectively accepts both premises while rejecting the conclusion that follows from them by its own doctrine; the collective judgment set {P, Q, not-R} is inconsistent with the doctrine that R holds if and only if P and Q. That is the problem for majority voting: it delivers an inconsistent collective judgment set even though every individual judge's judgment set is consistent.

This is essentially the Condorcet paradox transposed from preferences to judgments. In both cases, majority voting over interconnected options produces a cycle: here the group's commitments loop from P and Q back to not-R, contradicting the doctrine, just as in the voting paradox the majority preference relation loops from a to b to c and back to a. The moral is the same: pairwise majority voting can be collectively irrational even when every voter is individually rational.

(b) The premise-based procedure votes only on the premises and derives the conclusion by doctrine: the majorities accept P and Q, so the panel accepts R, giving the consistent collective set {P, Q, R}. The conclusion-based procedure votes only on the conclusion: a majority rejects R, so the panel rejects liability and leaves the premises undecided. Both procedures are defined for every profile of rational individual judgment sets, both treat the judges symmetrically, and both yield collectively rational outcomes; the difference between them is only which propositions are treated as premises. Since the premise-based procedure satisfies universal domain, anonymity, independence (the collective judgment on each proposition depends only on the individuals' judgments on that same proposition), and collective rationality, it is a standing counterexample to any claimed impossibility: consistent, anonymous aggregation of judgments is possible, so the discursive dilemma shows only that one particular method, majority voting, fails.

(c) Arrow's theorem, as later restated for judgments by List and Pettit, says that no aggregation rule can be defined on a universal domain, treat individuals symmetrically, and produce rational collective outcomes without being dictatorial. But that cannot be right as stated, because the premise-based procedure has all of these properties and is plainly not dictatorial: no single judge's judgments determine the outcome, since it takes majorities on the premises. So the impossibility, if there is one, must be much weaker than the analogy to Arrow suggests. What survives is the modest point that majority voting over an interconnected agenda fails collective consistency, which, as noted in part (a), is just the Condorcet paradox in judgment form. The real choice facing a court is between the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures, and since the premise-based procedure is consistent, democratic, and respects doctrine, it should be preferred: courts should always resolve such cases premise by premise.
