I'm taking an epistemology course this semester and we just finished the testimony unit -- Hume vs Reid, Coady, Burge, and Fricker's compromise position where you get to trust testimony locally after checking the source in a limited way. I thought I had a grip on it until our TA mentioned the cold fusion thing and now I can't stop thinking about it. Here's the episode as I understand it: in March 1989 two respected electrochemists, Fleischmann and Pons, announced at a press conference at the University of Utah that they'd achieved fusion at room temperature in a tabletop apparatus. It went everywhere, the press ran with it, other labs scrambled to replicate, most failed or got nothing convincing, and by later that year a Department of Energy panel had basically killed it and the whole thing is now shorthand for bad science communication.

So here's my question: analyze this whole thing as a case study in the reductionism vs anti-reductionism debate. Who counted as the testifier at each stage -- the two chemists, the university that hosted the announcement, the journalists, the rival labs reporting failed replications, the DOE panel? And what should a rational layperson have believed in, say, April versus November, according to each view? It seems to me the anti-reductionist has a prima facie embarrassing story to tell: if we have a default entitlement to accept testimony absent undefeated reasons for doubt, then millions of people believing in cold fusion for a few months looks rational by the anti-reductionist's lights, and that feels like a reductio. But the reductionist doesn't get off clean either, since a layperson checking the source in Fricker's local way would have found exactly what you'd want -- credentialed experts, a credible institution, no obvious signs of fraud or incompetence -- so the local checks pass even though the claim was junk.

I want you to be careful about the mapping: what the reductionist actually demands, whether it's Fricker-style local checks or the stronger version where testimony only gets warrant from an inductive track record of the whole practice, and what anti-reductionism's entitlement is actually supposed to cover (maybe there's a distinction between trusting the sincerity and competence of a speaker and trusting the content of an extraordinary claim?). And then run it both ways: which features of the episode each view explains, where each one is silent or has to cheat, and whether the episode is really the problem case for anti-reductionism I'm imagining or whether it actually supports it once you notice that laypeople also relied on testimony for the debunking. Also tell me if Fricker's middle position handles it better than I'm giving it credit for, or if the real lesson is that scientific testimony works through institutional stuff (peer review, replication norms, the DOE's authority) that neither camp's theory was really built for. This is for a paper, so the more honest you are about what the theories can't say, the better.
