You are to act as a philosopher of information with a historian's sensitivity to how records get made. Please think carefully and, before committing to a line of argument, briefly brainstorm alternative reconstructions of the practice described below and say why you settle on the one you defend.

Consider Wikipedia as an archive, and in particular the interaction of two of its core editorial policies. The first, usually summarized as "verifiability, not truth," holds that statements must be attributable to published, independent, reliable sources — an editor's own knowledge, however sound, doesn't count. The second, notability, holds that a topic merits an article only if it has already received significant coverage in such sources. Note what these rules do together: a subject becomes archivable only once it has been archived elsewhere, and the people best positioned to know about it (participants, community members, practitioners) are precisely the ones whose testimony the rules discount, because they are not independent.

My question is what theory of knowledge and of the knowable this pair of policies presupposes. Some sub-questions to structure your answer, though you should reorganize them if you see a better structure:

1. Does the verifiability rule presuppose that testimony from involved parties is defective as evidence, or merely that it is hard for a distributed, anonymous volunteer corps to adjudicate? That is, is the implicit epistemology a claim about what knowledge is, or a claim about what is feasible at scale — and can those two readings be kept apart?

2. Does notability presuppose a "trickle-down" model of significance, on which importance flows from published attention downward to the encyclopedia? Does that quietly identify the boundary of the knowable with the boundary of the already-recorded, and if so, what does it imply about oral traditions, unwritten practices, and communities whose lives generate few third-party texts?

3. Are the two policies' presuppositions consistent with each other? Verifiability looks like a rule about evidence; notability looks like a rule about which subjects exist for the archive at all. It is at least conceivable that one is epistemological while the other is primarily economic — a device for rationing scarce editorial attention — while wearing epistemological dress. Explore whether the practice is underdetermined in this way, such that several quite different theories would license it.

4. There is a further wrinkle: the archive is now so widely consulted that journalists and other writers routinely take their facts from it, so Wikipedia has begun to generate the very published, independent coverage that its own rules require. What does this circularity do to your reconstruction? Does the presupposed epistemology (if there is one) survive a regime in which the archive manufactures its own sources, and does this transform a policy that merely mirrors existing power in the source base into one that actively amplifies it?

In your answer, please distinguish what the policies strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and describe concretely what would have to change — in the rules, in what counts as a source, or in who counts as an authority — if the implicit view were replaced by one in which insider testimony and oral transmission counted as evidence in their own right. You may also address, if you think it matters, whether the volunteers who wrote these policies were right to think that any other rule set would collapse the project into a battlefield of unverifiable claims.
