I am the editor of the philosophy and ideas section of my high school's student-run literary magazine, and our upcoming issue is themed "Impossible Objects." I have already commissioned pieces on the liar paradox and on Gödel sentences, and I want the centerpiece to be a poem on the grandfather paradox — but handled the way I think it deserves, rather than the way it is usually done.

Here is what I have in mind. The grandfather paradox is almost always staged as a dramatic standoff: the time traveler raises the gun, and the universe is supposed to buckle. But my philosophy teacher pointed me toward David Lewis's paper "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" (1976), and the reading that stuck with me is the unglamorous one: nothing buckles, nothing intervenes, the murder simply fails the way things fail on any other day — the rifle jams, the tram is missed, the traveler's hand shakes. The impossibility never announces itself; it hides inside an ordinary run of bad luck. Meanwhile, the traveler's ability is ambiguous: in one sense he is perfectly able to kill his grandfather, and in another sense he is no more able to than you or I are able to do what has already failed to happen.

I would like a sestina that carries this. The narrator should be the traveler, on the day of the attempt, in the past, waiting for the moment that keeps not arriving; each stanza should contain another failure, each one mundane and each one slightly wrong — a wrongness the narrator senses but cannot name. The philosophical work should be done by the form: the six end-words return in their fixed rotation, exactly as before, never in a new order, and the reader should gradually feel that the murder fails not because anything stops it but because the sequence cannot contain it — the way a sestina cannot contain a seventh end-word, the way the pattern excludes it without ever mentioning it. The envoi should gather all six words and close the loop without resolving it: the traveler should come away suspecting the pattern, counting the coincidences, but unable to point to any law being enforced, or to any moment he could have acted otherwise.

Two requirements on accuracy. Someone who knows the philosophy of time travel should recognize exactly what view of the paradox the poem is taking — the mundane-failure reading, not a guardian-of-the-timeline reading, and not a branching-worlds reading — and should not be able to find a line that smuggles in cosmic intervention. Someone who knows nothing of the debate should simply read it as an unsettling poem about a day that keeps refusing to happen. If you want meter, unobtrusive pentameter is fine, but the rotation of the end-words and the logic of the failures matter far more than strict syllable counts.

One last note: I would rather the poem end in suspicion than in revelation. A closing stanza in which the traveler finally understands what the universe is doing would, I think, betray the idea; the whole point, as I understand Lewis, is that there is no moment of understanding to be had — only a pattern visible from outside that is invisible from inside. Please respect that.
