I'm a junior taking an elective called History of Modern Philosophy, and we just finished our unit on George Berkeley. I mostly followed the master argument — the one where he tries to show you can't even coherently conceive of an unperceived object, because the moment you try to imagine, say, a tree standing alone in a forest with nobody around, you've turned yourself into the observer who is perceiving it, so what you actually conceived was a perceived tree, not an unperceived one. My teacher mentioned that Berkeley also had God perpetually perceiving everything as his way of keeping objects from blinking out of existence whenever we leave the room, which honestly seems like it does a lot of quiet work in his system.

Here's what I keep wondering about, though. Last week my friend and I were looking at pictures from the James Webb Space Telescope, and she pointed out that some of that light left its galaxy before there were any minds anywhere in the universe (assuming there's no God, which she does). She says the fact that the light traveled all that way, and that we can now reconstruct exactly what the galaxy looked like, is basically a slam dunk against Berkeley — a world running along unconceived for billions of years. I have a feeling Berkeley wouldn't have been fazed, since he could just invoke divine perception or something about the whole thing being ideas in God's mind, but I'm not sure that's a fair reconstruction rather than a caricature. If Berkeley were alive today and shown the Webb images alongside our modern cosmology of a mindless early universe, how do you think he would respond, and would any of his responses actually engage with the astronomical evidence or just sidestep it? For what it's worth, my friend also claims modern people who believe we live in a simulation are basically Berkeley's descendants, and I'd like to know whether that comparison holds up at all or whether it flattens some important difference between his idealism and the simulation hypothesis.
