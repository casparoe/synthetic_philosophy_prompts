I am a recently retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the past two years taking and auditing courses in the philosophy department — I finally have time for the questions I postponed during forty years of grant deadlines. The instructor of the department's upper-level course on epistemic logic, whom I have gotten to know from sitting in on her lectures all term, has been called away to a conference and has asked me to mark the midterm short-answer questions, since I attended every lecture on this material. She left only a bare rubric: Question 3 is worth 15 points, with five points each for parts (a), (b), and (c), and she asked me to write "substantive marginal comments, not just scores." I want to do this properly, but I have been a referee in physics, not a grader in philosophy, and I am conscious that in my old field we were trained to tolerate idealizations that philosophers seem to regard as scandalous. Here is the question, exactly as it appeared on the exam:

"Question 3 (15 points). In Kripke semantics for a normal epistemic logic (say, S5 with a single agent), knowledge is closed under logical consequence: whenever φ logically entails ψ, any agent who knows φ also knows ψ. (a) Show why this follows, both from the possible-worlds semantics and from the axioms and rules of the logic. (b) Explain why this — the problem of logical omniscience — is generally regarded as a genuine problem rather than a harmless idealization. (c) Describe one proposed remedy (Hintikka's surface versus depth information, awareness semantics, impossible worlds, or another of your choosing) and state what it trades away."

And here, verbatim, is one student's answer:

"I'll take the semantic route first. In Kripke semantics, Kφ is true at a world w iff φ is true at every world the agent cannot distinguish from w. Suppose φ logically entails ψ. Then every φ-world is a ψ-world, so any world compatible with the agent's knowledge that verifies φ also verifies ψ. Hence the agent knows ψ. This does not depend on the agent doing any inferring; the semantics simply cannot represent the difference between believing something and believing its consequences.

The axiomatic route: the culprit is the distribution axiom K(φ→ψ)→(Kφ→Kψ), sometimes called T. From it, omniscience follows directly: an agent who knows φ and knows the conditional φ→ψ must know ψ, and by chaining such conditionals the agent arrives at every consequence of what it knows.

Is it a problem? Yes, and a fairly old one — it is basically the same issue as epistemic closure in the Gettier-era literature, where Dretske and Nozick argued over whether an agent who knows φ and knows that φ entails ψ must know ψ. Moreover, no actual reasoner satisfies the closure principle: no human knows all tautologies, and not even a machine could in principle list them all, so the logic is false of every real agent rather than merely incomplete.

As a remedy, take awareness semantics, which splits knowledge into implicit and explicit. Explicit knowledge of φ requires implicit knowledge plus awareness of the formula φ itself. This eliminates the idealization: if the agent is aware of φ but not of ψ, the agent can fail to explicitly know ψ even when φ entails ψ. The cost is that awareness is a primitive, unanalyzed set, and the agent may not be aware of what it is unaware of. Hintikka's own fix in Knowledge and Belief, the surface/depth information distinction, is similar in spirit: surface information is what the sentence's form gives you without deduction, depth information is the full closure, and real agents only ever have the surface."
