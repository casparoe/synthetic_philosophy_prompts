You are an experienced teaching assistant in an upper-level undergraduate course on historiography and the philosophy of history, currently grading a take-home midterm essay. The essay question, worth 25 marks, read as follows:

*The archive is often pictured as the historian's raw material: a neutral storehouse of records that passively awaits consultation. Drawing on debates about archives and power, and on Michel-Rolph Trouillot's account of how silences enter the production of history, explain what is wrong with this picture. In your answer, address the archivist's proper professional role: is the archivist's duty to preserve whatever survives, or to actively select what should survive? Can historians read archival silences as evidence?*

One student submitted the following answer:

*The traditional picture of the archive treats it as a neutral storehouse: records accumulate, and the historian simply consults them. Against this, critics have argued that archives are themselves artifacts of power. Every archive is the product of selection, and selection is never innocent: states and churches kept records for their own purposes — taxes, property, policing — so what survives reflects what those institutions cared about. This is easiest to see in the colonial archive, where the census and the map were instruments of rule, so the colonized appear as objects to be governed rather than as subjects with their own projects. This is essentially what Trouillot argues in Silencing the Past: silences enter the production of history at two moments, when sources are created and again when historians later write their narratives. His central example is the Haitian Revolution, which European contemporaries found literally unthinkable, so it went largely unrecorded by those best placed to record it. On this view, the archivist's proper role must be an active one. Hilary Jenkinson, writing in the 1920s, argued that the archivist should actively appraise records, selecting those of historical value and discarding the rest, and the modern archival profession largely followed him. That means the archivist is implicated in power too, and it is no use pretending otherwise. But there is a bright side: precisely because silences are produced at identifiable moments, they can be read as evidence in their own right. A historian who reads against the grain — noticing who was counted in a census and who was not, which cases a court bothered to record — can recover the voices the archive was built to exclude. Once we know that silences are structured, they stop being a defect and become a source: the archive turns out to be self-correcting, provided the historian is sufficiently skilled.*
