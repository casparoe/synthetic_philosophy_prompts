My oldest friend and I argued late into the night about whether ideas can be owned, and I finished the evening with a set of verdicts I can't yet reconcile. He kept insisting that the entire edifice of intellectual property is just an incentive scheme — that talk of deserving to own one's ideas is confused nostalgia for labour — and that if I would only accept this, everything would fall into place. It didn't, because my intuitions don't seem to want a single home. Here they are. A pharmaceutical company that discovers a genuinely novel molecule and charges a hundred times the production cost for the term of its patent: broadly permissible, since the reward tracks a real achievement. An engineer who independently invents, months later and wholly unaware of the first inventor, the same hinge mechanism, only to be shut out entirely by the first-to-file rule: wrongly treated, since she did nothing the winner did not do. Fashion houses freely copying each season's silhouettes and hemlines, as the law permits: fine, and probably healthy for the industry. A younger comedian lifting another comedian's tightly crafted closing bit wholesale: wrong, even though a bit is mostly premise and structure — that is, ideas rather than expression. Copyright subsisting for seventy years after an author's death, so that a pop song stays locked up for a century: far too long; a decade or two would be plenty. Coca-Cola guarding its formula as a trade secret indefinitely: perfectly legitimate. An indigenous community insisting that outsiders not print its sacred designs on garments, in perpetuity: also legitimate, and I confess I cannot see a principled stopping date for that one at all. Generic manufacturers reverse-engineering a drug the day its patent lapses: obviously fine. A student submitting a purchased essay: a serious wrong, though I would hesitate to call it theft. Is there a single principle — desert, personality, incentive, or something stranger — that captures all of these? Please state it precisely if it exists, tell me what it recommends for a case I have not listed (a magician who invents a new illusion and keeps the method secret, only to see it exposed and then performed by rivals), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and whether the friend's incentive story could save them instead. Please answer in British English.
