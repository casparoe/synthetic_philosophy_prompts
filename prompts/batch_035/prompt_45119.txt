For my junior-year extended essay I have to read a film through a philosophical lens, and I have chosen Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006), read through the debate over mind uploading and gradual replacement. I would appreciate a serious critical reading rather than a summary, and I need the answer written in American English.

Here is why I think the pairing works. In the film, Tesla's machine does not teleport Robert Angier; it duplicates him. Every night, the man standing on the platform falls through the trapdoor into a locked water tank and drowns, while a copy appears at the back of the hall to take the applause. Near the end, the dying Angier says that what the trick really took was courage: he climbed into that machine every night without knowing whether he would be the man in the box or the prestige. Meanwhile his rival Borden achieves the same trick by the opposite method: two men, twins, continuously living one shared life, alternating between the performer and the disguised Fallon. And late in the film Cutter withdraws the comforting story he once told Angier about a sailor who said drowning felt like going home — he admits he lied, and that the sailor said it was agony.

I want the reading organized around the following questions:

1. The uploading debate distinguishes destructive instant copying, where the original is scanned and destroyed and a duplicate built elsewhere, from gradual replacement, where neurons are replaced one at a time by functionally equivalent silicon, so that there is never a moment at which a man drowns. Angier's machine is the paradigm of the first kind, and its horror is exactly the horror gradual replacement was invented to avoid. So is the film attacking a straw man, or does it show that even the gradual case inherits something the standard arguments miss?

2. Angier frames his gamble as a lottery he might lose: not knowing which one he would be presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about which of the two men is him. Does the film endorse that presupposition, or does it instead suggest, in the spirit of Parfit, that personal identity is not what matters? Notice that Cutter's final admission answers a welfare question, not an identity question: drowning is agony, which is true of the man in the tank whether or not he is Angier.

3. Borden's doubled life is a case of two people continuously sharing one public identity, while Angier's trick makes one person discontinuously into two. Does the film use this contrast to suggest that continuity of a life, rather than continuity of consciousness, is what the survival debate actually trades on?

4. Where does the film resist this reading? An honest critic should tell me whether the metaphysics is doing real work or is only a device for the story about obsession and sacrifice — whether, once the audience learns the trick, the identity question is subordinate to the ethics of what each magician is willing to do to his rival, to his family, and to himself.

Please point me to specific scenes to feature in the essay: the drowning, Tesla's demonstration with the duplicated hats, the scenes in which Sarah senses that some days Borden truly loves her and other days does not, and the final row of water tanks. And conclude by saying what the film might add to the uploading debate that the arguments alone do not — for instance, whether making the audience root for a man who drowns a hundred times, and revealing the cost only in the last minutes, dramatizes something the thought experiments cannot. My supervisor insists that the reading be faithful in both directions: the film must not be reduced to an illustration, and the philosophy must not be bent to fit the plot. Please tell me plainly if any of my questions beg the answer.
