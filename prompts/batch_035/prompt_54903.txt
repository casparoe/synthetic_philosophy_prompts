You are a professional philosopher who works on collective responsibility and the ethics of harm. Please think carefully and methodically: before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm the candidate ways the argument below could fail, then settle on what you take to be the strongest reading of where it goes wrong, or on which premise is least costly to reject.

For three weeks my ethics seminar has been stuck on an argument from a classmate, call him J., about whether individual consumption choices can be wrong. J.'s argument keeps winning, and its conclusion keeps striking most of us as absurd. I want a real diagnosis, not a shrug.

The argument, directed at someone buying chicken at the supermarket:

(1) An act is wrong only if it makes a difference to how the world goes. If the world would have been exactly the same, in every morally relevant respect, had you not performed the act, then the act did not wrong anyone, and it is not wrong.

(2) Producers in industrial animal agriculture set production levels according to demand forecasts, retail orders, and processing batches — not according to individual retail purchases. So for any given purchase of chicken, it is overwhelmingly likely that the same number of chickens is bred, raised, and slaughtered whether or not that purchase happens. (J. concedes a small probability of crossing some threshold — an order quantity, a forecast revision — but says it is negligible.)

(3) So, with respect to animal suffering, buying the chicken is overwhelmingly likely to make no difference at all.

(4) An act that certainly makes no difference cannot be wrong; and a probability small enough to neglect for all practical purposes can be treated, for the purposes of moral assessment, as certainty.

(5) Therefore buying the chicken is not wrong — and by the same reasoning, an individual's flight, an individual's vote in a large election, an individual's plastic waste, and an individual firm's emissions among millions of firms are all permissible, since each is overwhelmingly likely to make no difference.

(6) And in that case, the trillion chickens slaughtered annually and the gigatons of CO2 emitted are the result of a billion individually permissible acts: the harm exists, but nobody did anything wrong in producing it.

When we pushed back with overdetermination cases — a hundred-man firing squad where the prisoner dies no matter which single riflemen abstain, or ten poisoners each adding a lethal dose to a well where any one dose would suffice — J. just grinned and said that these are not reductios of his argument but illustrations of it: if we think the individual riflemen and poisoners are still doing something wrong, that shows we should doubt (1), and he is happy to concede (1) for the special case where the victim dies anyway. He insists the core of his argument is elsewhere.

Here is my predicament. I would most like to keep (1): difference-making seems like the backbone of any sane account of individual accountability, and I don't want to say people are wronged by acts that change nothing for them. I also can't see how to deny (2); it looks like plain industrial economics. Yet I refuse to accept (6), and something about (4) makes me uneasy without my being able to say exactly what.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please be precise about whether the flaw you identify, if there is one, is a slip between two steps or a premise that was only ever plausible under a reading the rest of the argument doesn't support — and say what J. would still be right about even after your diagnosis, since he does seem right about something.
