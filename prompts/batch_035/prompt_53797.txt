In September 1713, Nicolas Bernoulli described, in a letter to Pierre de Montmort, a game in which a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on the nth flip, the player wins 2 to the nth power ducats. The expected value of this game is infinite, yet, as Bernoulli observed, no sensible person would pay more than a few ducats to play it. Pascal had been dead for half a century and never saw this puzzle — but it bears directly on his own wager, which likewise trades a modest cost against a small probability of an infinite payoff. Please predict his reaction. Drawing on the wager, his distinction between the reasons of the heart and those of reason, and his view of the infinite distance separating our finite condition from God, explain how Pascal would likely answer the St. Petersburg puzzle: would he insist that the game is genuinely worth any finite price, would he deny that mathematical expectation is the correct measure when infinity enters the arithmetic, or would he draw some third line? And would he regard Bernoulli's puzzle as a threat to his wager — since whatever reasoning deflates the value of the game might also deflate the wager — or as an unexpected ally that vindicates reasoning with infinities? Conclude by stepping outside the historical reconstruction and assessing whether Pascal's likely position, so extended, is actually defensible.
