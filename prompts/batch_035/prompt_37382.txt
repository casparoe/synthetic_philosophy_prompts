I've been pretty sympathetic to the quining qualia position for a while now -- the Dennett line, fleshed out with that thought experiment about the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, where you realize you can't ever distinguish between your tastes changing, your reaction to the same tastes changing, and the raw experiences themselves changing, so the idea of qualia as intrinsic, private, ineffable somethings starts to look like a posit that can't do any work. At this point I'd basically call myself a deflationist: qualia as classically conceived are a confused concept, and everything real about experience can be redescribed in terms of discriminations, reactions, dispositions, and reports.

What would count against that? I don't want the standard zombie finger-wagging or someone just re-asserting that they know what red looks like to them. I want the serious defeaters: the cases and arguments the deflationist camp itself would wince at, the ones a thoughtful defender would concede actually put pressure on the view rather than dismissing them as confusion. Things like -- would a genuinely predictive psychophysical bridge law, where we could map neural states to color reports well enough to predict novel discriminations, vindicate qualia-talk or dissolve it further? Does the inverted spectrum scenario, once cleaned up, really reduce to the coffee-taster worries, or is there a version of it the deflationist can't handle? Where does the view stand or fall on introspection being unreliable in specific ways -- if introspection turned out to be much more reliable than the change-blindness and confabulation literature suggests, how bad is that for me?

Also: has quining qualia drifted into illusionism-vs-eliminativism hair-splitting that makes it unfalsifiable in practice? Is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it fine because it's a conceptual claim, not an empirical one? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest take on which rival views gain ground if each one lands.

One more thing: keep clear about what's actually settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. I know deflationism is a minority taste, so don't present it as the consensus view of consciousness researchers, and don't present its critics' strongest points as if they were fringe either -- I want to know where the real live disputes are, not get a sales pitch for the side I already picked.
