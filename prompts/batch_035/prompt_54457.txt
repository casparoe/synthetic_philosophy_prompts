I'm writing the background chapter of my thesis on the nature of pleasure, and I've become convinced that the heterogeneity problem is usually stated too quickly: the pleasures of a hot bath, of finally cracking a proof, of hearing that a friend's operation went well — what do all these mental episodes share in virtue of which they are pleasures? Philosophers tend to argue for or against a candidate answer by pointing at a single case, and I want the full specification before I commit to anything. Please set out the desiderata any adequate answer must meet. Among the things I'd want covered, at least tentatively: the cases it must classify correctly (unnoticed enjoyments, where one only realises later that one was enjoying oneself; pleasures of anticipation; guilty pleasures, where the unpleasantness seems partly constitutive of the pleasure); the distinctions it must respect (between something's feeling pleasant and its being wanted, and between the pleasantness of a sensation and of an intentional object); whether the answer must be reductive or may legitimately be disjunctive; and, if you think it genuinely constrains the philosophy rather than merely re-describing it, consistency with the empirical dissociation between liking and wanting. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is what I most want to see — and identify which single desideratum excludes the most prominent accounts (a shared phenomenal quality, an attitude of enjoyment, pleasure as being pleased that p). Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases motivating them; assess the contenders only at the end, and briefly, as I'll do the fuller evaluation in the thesis itself.
