Philosophy PhD student here who got pulled into a policy modeling group, and there's a move the economists make that I can't find a name for. They estimate what a household buys at different prices, fit a smooth function to that, and then report things like "this policy costs the average household $340 in lost wellbeing" — sometimes summed across the population to say whether the policy is worth it overall. The thing I keep noticing: the function was constructed to fit the very choices that are supposed to be the evidence for it. So if nothing about a household's behavior changes, the model is forced to say its wellbeing didn't change, no matter what else we learn about them (say they're miserable but still buying the same groceries); and when behavior does change, the size of the "welfare effect" depends on the functional form they picked, which fits the observed data equally well at every point we can actually check. It's not just the ordinal/cardinal business — I know they know utility has no units. It's more that the same observations get used twice: once as the input the model is fit to, and again as the output the model is claimed to measure, with nothing new in between. Is there an established term for this — reusing your evidence as your measurement? Or for the broader pattern where a model's output is presented as an independent finding about wellbeing when it's really just a re-encoding of its inputs? If something exists, tell me how it differs from plain unfalsifiability and from the standard complaints about interpersonal comparability; if nothing fits exactly, say so plainly and coin something with a definition and edge cases. I want your actual verdict on which nearby concept does the most work here — don't survey the options and leave the picking to me. And please write in American English.
