I write novels for a living, so I spend my days making things up, and lately political communication reads uncomfortably familiar to me. Watch a government handle a scandal. The first move is never "what's true," it's what consultants call the narrative: get ahead of the story, land the message, don't let the other side define the frame. When a politician gets caught saying something false, the pundits score it as a communications problem, and the analysis is mostly about whether the base will care, not about whether it was true. And there's the classic non-apology: mistakes were made, lessons have been learned, regret is expressed -- a whole grammar built to admit nothing while sounding like it admits everything. So what I want to know is: what view of truth in politics does this entire practice presuppose? My own trade runs on an open compact -- my readers know the characters aren't real, and because they know, I'm not deceiving anybody. Does narrative politics presuppose some analogous compact, where the public knows it's being spun and consents, so the spin isn't properly a lie at all? Or does it presuppose something darker -- that factual truth just doesn't do any work in politics anymore, and all that matters is which story sticks? I keep going back and forth on this. If it's the compact version, I'd like to know exactly where the analogy with the novel breaks -- because a government's stories get cashed out into laws and taxes that hit people whether they beleived the story or not, and mine don't. And one more thing: is the practice even coherent? Can a public sphere keep running on statements everyone is expected to discount?
