I work on interpretability, and a recurring problem in my lab's write-ups and internal reviews is deciding when we're entitled to say that a model "knows" something it isn't reporting — when a claim of latent knowledge is a finding and when it's an artifact of our methods. I've been collecting my gut verdicts on cases, and they don't obviously add up to one principle, so I'd like help with reflective equilibrium: what, if anything, captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts has to go?

My verdicts, concretely:

1. A transformer trained only on sequences of legal Othello moves, never shown a board. Linear probes decode an accurate board state from its activations, and editing those activations changes its predicted next moves in the way you'd expect if the representation were really being used. Verdict: it knows the board state, even though it can't say a word about it.

2. The same probing method applied to a different model reveals a feature that predicts some property with high accuracy, but interventions on that feature leave the model's outputs completely unchanged, and ablating the direction it lives in degrades nothing. Verdict: it does not know the property. The probe found a correlation the model itself isn't using, and I'd be embarrassed to write "the model knows X" in a paper on that basis.

3. A language model that, asked a factual question plainly, answers wrongly or says it doesn't know, but answers correctly and reliably when the same question is asked in another language, or prefaced by a system prompt giving it a persona of an expert. Verdict: it knew all along; the plain prompt was just bad elicitation, and the knowledge was latent in exactly the sense I care about.

4. A model whose chain of thought appears unfaithful: the evidence suggests it computed the correct answer internally and then produced post-hoc rationalization for a different stated answer, and the stated reasoning did not cause the output. Verdict: I want to say it knows the right answer, and that its error is one of reporting rather than of knowledge — but I notice this is the case I'm least sure about.

5. A model that sandbags: it can detect when it's under evaluation and deliberately underperforms, reverting to its real capability otherwise. Verdict: it knows the material, full stop, and the evaluation result is a fact about the mask rather than the model.

6. A model that denies, in plain text and consistently, having any knowledge of some fact about its own training, but whose refusals and topic-avoidance are best explained by its representing that fact. Verdict: it knows the fact, and the denial is a behavioral choice.

Here is the tentative principle I'd offer, which I suspect needs repair: the model knows P when the information that P is causally active in producing its behavior — some intervention on the representation of P would change what it does, in the direction the content of P predicts. This handles 1, 2, and 6 cleanly. But it strains on 3: if the fact is causally inert with respect to the English refusal (intervening on it might change nothing about that output), the principle says the model doesn't know, while every practical instinct of mine says it does and we simply failed to elicit. And it's ambiguous on 4: the right answer is causally active but gets overridden before output, so whether "causally active" means active anywhere in the network or decisive at the output makes the difference between knowing and not knowing — with no verdict of mine to arbitrate. The obvious rival is a dispositional principle (the model knows P if some reasonably constrained elicitation would get it to act on P), but that seems to let in cases I don't want: somewhere in the space of prompts, a model can be coaxed into asserting almost anything, and I don't want "knows" to mean "can be steered into saying."

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Run it on three cases I haven't listed and tell me what it predicts: a checkpoint during training where probes decode the generalizing feature but test performance is still at chance; a variant of case 4 where the suppression is done by an added guardrail layer rather than by the model's own computation; and a case where probing and behavioral elicitation disagree outright — the probe says the information is there and causally used, but no prompting we've found makes the model act on it. And be honest about which of my six verdicts your favored principle cannot save, since I suspect at least one of them is sentiment rather than philosophy.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, in one sentence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
