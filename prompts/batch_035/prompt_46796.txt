I've been unable to shake an argument a mathematician friend of mine laid on me over a long dinner last weekend, and I'd like a careful, professional diagnosis of it. I'm a graduate student in philosophy, reasonably comfortable with decision theory but out of my depth on the mathematics of infinite series, so please respond as an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of probability: start with some brainstorming of where the pressure points might be before you commit to a verdict, reason step by step, and write in polished American English. Here is the argument as he gave it to me. (1) An act's expected value is the sum, over all the ways the act could turn out, of each outcome's value weighted by its probability. (2) Such a sum deserves to be called *the* expected value only if it is independent of the order in which we happen to list the outcomes; if different orderings yield different sums, there is simply no fact of the matter about how good the act is. (3) By Riemann's rearrangement theorem, a series that converges but not absolutely can be reordered so as to converge to any number whatsoever, or to diverge. (4) The Pasadena game — a St. Petersburg variant where a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads on toss n, with a payoff of +2^n/n dollars when n is odd and −2^n/n dollars when n is even — has an expectation series of 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges but not absolutely, so by (2) and (3) it has no expected value at all. So far I'm willing to follow him. (5) Now consider any real act: ordering a coffee, crossing a street, accepting a job offer. It has infinitely many possible outcomes, and their payoffs are unbounded in both directions — for any n, there is some fantastically improbable scenario in which the act leads to a windfall or a catastrophe of magnitude at least 2^n dollars. (6) For any such act, he claims, the outcomes can be listed so that the probability-weighted values form a series that converges but not absolutely — roughly, one can always find outcomes whose payoffs grow as fast as their probabilities shrink, arranged so the signs alternate, exactly as in the Pasadena game. (7) So by the same reasoning, no real act has an expected value. (8) Therefore expected-value maximization applies only to artificial finite gambles; as a theory of actual choice it is empty, and decision theory, taken at face value, refutes itself.

My friend is delighted by this conclusion and I am not. My suspicion is that something slips between (6) and (7) — perhaps between "the outcomes *can be listed* so that the series behaves badly" and "there is *no fact of the matter* about the act's value" — but I can't put my finger on exactly what kind of mistake that would be, whether it's an equivocation, a quantifier shift, or something about which mathematical facts are sensitive to our labeling and which are not. Yet when I try to dismiss (6), he asks me what guarantees that a real act's payoff distribution has a well-behaved tail at all, and I don't have a good answer. So here is what I want from you. Tell me precisely which step to reject and why that step looked so fine. Then price the alternatives honestly: if I reject (6), what am I committed to saying about real-world probability distributions, and is there any class of genuinely high-stakes, heavy-tailed decisions — say, involving tiny probabilities of enormous harms — where his argument retains real force? If instead the flaw is in (2) or (3) — say, in the assumption that no ordering of outcomes is privileged — what would a principled, non-arbitrary way of ordering the terms look like, and what does adopting it cost? For what it's worth, I would dearly like to keep (2) and the ordinary thought that my morning coffee has an expected value; if your diagnosis forces me to give up either, say so plainly and explain what I take on instead.
