I've noticed an odd combination of norms within professional historiography, and I'd like to understand what theory of explanation the discipline is implicitly committed to — or whether it's committed to anything coherent at all.

Consider the following cluster of practices and norms, all of which are standard:

1. Training, methodology sections, and peer review treat "hindsight," "presentism," "anachronism," "outcome bias," and "Whig history" as cardinal sins. The historian is faulted for explaining the outbreak of the First World War as though contemporaries could see it coming, or for treating a decision made under deep uncertainty as though its consequences were visible to the person making it. The agent's open future is supposed to be respected.

2. At the same time, the discipline's flagship genre is outcome-driven in title and design: books and courses organized as the origins, the road to, the collapse of, the making of some known result. Historians select topics because of how things turned out, enter archives already knowing the ending, and pick from the record the threads that run forward to it. Nobody proposes to research the run-up to a crisis that never happened, and no press would publish it.

3. Referees add a further, apparently independent demand: that the author demonstrate contingency — that at some juncture things could plausibly have gone otherwise — while also insisting that the cited causes genuinely carry the outcome rather than merely accompany it. The monograph must make the result both explicable and avoidable.

4. The publication market runs on narrative shape. A book whose conclusion is that the outcome issued from an accumulation of low-grade frictions, none individually decisive, several of which nearly failed to occur, is hard to publish and harder to assign in a seminar; a book with an arc — mounting pressure, a turning point, resolution — is not. Individual historians are told to resist the narrative fallacy, yet the collective structure of the discipline seems to guarantee it.

What I want to know is what this cluster presupposes about historical explanation. Some candidate readings, none of which I'm confident in:

- Perhaps the discipline presupposes a distinction borrowed from statistics: using the outcome to generate hypotheses is legitimate, using it as a premise of inference is not. But historians never receive fresh data — they go back to the same outcome-loaded archives — so there is no held-out set on which a retrospective conjecture could be tested. Can the generate/test distinction survive the absence of anything like a second sample?
- Perhaps it presupposes a kind of double vision: a good narrative must render the outcome intelligible to the reader, who knows it was coming, while accurately representing it as an open question to the agents, who did not. The genre title ("the road to X") then encodes both perspectives at once. But is that a theory of explanation, or just a description of a rhetorical accomplishment — and is it even achievable, since selection of detail is itself retrospective?
- Perhaps the anti-hindsight norm is not an epistemic norm at all but a norm of display — don't let the reader see the outcome shaping your inquiry — and the discipline knows, at the aggregate level, that outcome-shaped topics and narrative-shaped publications will produce hindsight bias regardless of individual historians' virtues, much as a casino profits while individual gamblers sincerely intend to win.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as best it can be reconstructed: which parts of the practice are strictly required by that theory, which merely fit well with it, and where the practice is underdetermined such that rival theories of explanation would license it too. Then tell me what a historiography built on a rival theory would have to change — for instance, one that treated every retrospective narrative as merely one of several accounts equally well supported by the record, or one that explained non-events with the same seriousness as events. Finally, if you think the puzzle is badly posed — say, because using the outcome to select what to explain is different enough from hindsight bias that no single implicit view needs to cover both, or because the contingency demand in (3) simply tracks a different norm than the causal demand does — please say so plainly rather than manufacturing a unified theory where the practice may simply be making incompatible demands.
