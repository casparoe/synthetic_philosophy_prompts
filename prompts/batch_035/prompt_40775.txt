The course covered Paul Churchland's case for eliminativism, Jerry Fodor's defense of belief–desire psychology, and Daniel Dennett's treatment of beliefs as real patterns, so students were expected to be able to keep these positions and their proponents straight. Question 3. Eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes.
(a) Explain the eliminative materialist's claim about beliefs and desires, and why we are supposed to arrive at that claim by treating folk psychology as an empirical theory. Make clear what work the analogy with discarded theoretical posits such as phlogiston is doing.
(b) Explain carefully how eliminative materialism differs from the type-identity theory's treatment of mental states.
(c) State the self-refutation objection to eliminative materialism and assess whether it succeeds.

The student's answer to Question 3, transcribed in full:

"The eliminative materialist claims that beliefs and desires, as commonsense psychology understands them — internal states with content, capable of standing in rational relations and of causing behavior — do not exist. This follows from treating folk psychology as an empirical theory on all fours with any other: it posits unobservable entities to explain and predict observable phenomena, here the behavior of intelligent creatures. Like any theory it must be judged by its explanatory record, and the record is poor: for all its everyday successes, folk psychology has been essentially stagnant for some twenty-five centuries, and it is silent or nearly so about sleep, creative imagination, the differences between the brains of the mentally ill and the brains of the healthy, and the physical mechanisms of learning. Fodor pressed this point: folk psychology is a research program that has stopped producing. It is therefore apt to share the fate of phlogiston, and a mature neuroscience will deploy an entirely different set of categories.

(b) The difference from the identity theory is sharp. The identity theorist is a reductionist: beliefs exist and will turn out to be identical to neural states, in the way water turned out to be H2O and temperature turned out to be mean molecular kinetic energy, so ordinary belief-talk will be retained, perhaps refined, once the underlying states are identified. The eliminativist denies this: the reduction will not go through. Where the identity theorist smooths the old vocabulary into the new science, the eliminativist expects it to be dropped, as chemists dropped talk of phlogiston rather than identifying phlogiston with oxygen. Dennett's real patterns position was an early version of this radical stance: beliefs are at bottom only patterns in behavior and dispositions to behave, so strictly speaking there are no beliefs, only the patterns. Still, the difference can be overstated. Both parties agree that the underlying neural states are real; the eliminativist's complaint is really about our current vocabulary, which a more accurate neuroscience will replace while leaving the phenomena themselves — properly redescribed — where they always were.

(c) The self-refutation objection runs as follows. If eliminative materialism is true, no one believes anything, in which case no one believes eliminative materialism; yet the thesis is asserted by its proponents, who presumably believe it; so the thesis cannot be true. I think this objection succeeds. The standard reply is that the eliminativist merely asserts the theory without believing it, but that reply looks like a verbal maneuver: assertion is the paradigm expression of belief, so the reply presupposes exactly the concept it is meant to discharge. Unless the eliminativist can say, without begging the question, what remains of the difference between asserting and believing once beliefs are gone, the objection stands and eliminative materialism is refuted by its own content."
