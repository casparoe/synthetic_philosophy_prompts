So, context: I'm a grad student, and in two weeks I'm giving a colloquium talk defending (most of) Anscombe's position on Truman and the atomic bomb. The occasion is the 1956 Oxford business: the university planned to give Truman an honorary doctorate, Anscombe and a couple of others forced a public vote on it, she got crushed, and afterwards she wrote a short pamphlet explaining why she'd done it. My talk reconstructs her actual argument and tries to make it hold up, roughly along these lines: (1) the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings were not a case of foreseen-but-unintended civilian deaths -- killing large numbers of noncombatants was the point of the act, chosen as a means to force surrender, so the intention/foresight distinction that legitimizes collateral damage in war doesn't apply; (2) the intentional killing of the innocent is murder, so Truman ordered a mass murder, whatever the outcome; and (3) giving such a man an honor was disgraceful, and the only defense anyone offered -- that he acted for the sake of good consequences -- is, by her analogy, about as convincing as a judge defending himself for taking bribes by insisting he only ever took bribes in good causes. I also want to say something in her favor about the vote itself: that voting can be the expression of one's judgment rather than an attempt to produce an outcome, which is why voting for a losing protest motion isn't pointless, but this part I'm least sure of.

The audience is the problem. It's our departmental colloquium, and the room will be mostly mainstream analytic ethicists, several of them straightforward consequentialists who think the bombings were justified by the invasion alternative, plus a couple of just war theorists who in principle accept double effect but will want to know if I'm applying it consistently, and one historian who knows the end-of-the-Pacific-war literature cold (surrender negotiations, Soviet entry, the firebombings, etc.) and loves puncturing philosophers who gesture at "the historical record." No one is a Catholic Thomist, so nobody in the room will be predisposed to like Anscombe -- some will come in assuming the whole position is a religious reflex dressed up as argument. I do not want to get up there with a prep sheet full of strawmen; I want the objections as this room would actually raise them, in their vocabulary.

So please act as a senior moral philosopher who's sat through a lot of these colloquia. Start with a little brainstorming about what each faction in that audience will reach for, then give me a prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I'm most likely to get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede upfront rather than fight (e.g. am I really prepared to bite the bullet on the firebombings too, and on whether her view collapses into pacifism, which she denied?). Mark which objections have real force that I can't fully answer and where the best move is graceful partial concession. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect I know which one it is, but I'd rather see if you pick out the same one. Keep quotes out of it; paraphrase her views, don't quote them, since I'll check any wording against the texts myself before I put it in the talk.
