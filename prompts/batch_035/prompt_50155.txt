I teach a cross-listed phil-bio course, 14 students, one 80-minute session coming up on the units of selection debate. The students walk in with the folk history baked in: Wynne-Edwards overreached, Williams cleaned it up in 1966, Dawkins finished the job, and group selection is now for people who don't understand selfishness. I've lectured this at students before and it slides right off. What I want is an in-class game that makes them feel the actual hard part, which I'd put like this: the same population dynamics can be booked at more than one level, all the bookkeepings give the right answer about what changes, and so the fight has to be about something other than who's right about the outcome.

So design the game: rules, roles, tokens, a reproduction scheme, timings. The core should be that within any one group defecting beats cooperating, while groups with more cooperators contribute more to the next generation, so the students are caught between the two pressures and can watch which one wins under which setup. And somewhere in there I want them to compute the same run twice — once counting success by individual organisms and their payoffs, once counting by allele copies (or by whole groups, whichever you think lands better) — and get the same trajectory both times. That moment, where the two accountings agree, should be the moment the folk story wobbles for them. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the within-group and between-group components of change, says what the remaining dispute could even be about once the bookkeeping equivalence is conceded (causation? selection for versus selection of?), and what it costs to keep insisting one level is the "real" one.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that students will collude across groups, or that the per-round arithmetic eats the whole session, but you tell me — and be honest about which feature of the debate the game definately cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself at the whiteboard. Don't have them read anything beforehand; they should walk in with just the folk story and leave having broken it themselves.
