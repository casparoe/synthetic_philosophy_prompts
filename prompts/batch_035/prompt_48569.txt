Draft the joint statement that a defender of the simple conditional analysis of dispositions ("a glass is fragile iff it would break if struck") and an opponent armed with finkish and masking cases could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. By a fink I mean a case in the style of Martin's sorcerer, who is poised to render a fragile glass no longer fragile the instant it is struck, so that it would not break though it plainly is fragile; by a mask I mean an interposed blocker, such as packing foam or an antidote, that stops a genuinely disposed object from manifesting when the stimulus occurs. The document should contain: (i) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, including about what any adequate account of dispositions must deliver, about the direction of explanation between dispositions and counterfactuals, and about which standard counterexamples actually trouble the simple analysis rather than merely dramatizing its scope conditions; (ii) a table of the residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it accepts the wording — covering, at minimum, whether Lewis-style emendations in terms of what intrinsic duplicates in fink-free surroundings would do are principled repairs or ad hoc buttressing, and whether masks show the analysis is false or only that it must be qualified; (iii) the cruxes, at least one of which should be whether all the counterexamples — finks, masks, antidotes, and the reverse cases, where the sorcerer would make an indestructible glass fragile the instant it is struck — share a common structure or are merely analogous; and (iv) for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal — for instance, over whether "disposition" is a technical term to be fixed by analysis or an ordinary concept fixed by use — say which part, and why.
