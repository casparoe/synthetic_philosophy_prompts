I'm a PhD student TAing an upper-level epistemology course, and I run the 50-minute discussion section of about 16 students. Next week is peer disagreement, and I'd like an in-class game they play before doing any of the reading, so that conciliationism and steadfastness arrive as names for positions they've already occupied rather than as positions on a syllabus. Here's the shape I have in mind, but please work out the details. Students privately commit credences on a series of estimable quantities (populations, lengths, dates — nothing checkable by phone). They're then paired up, each sees their partner's credence on the same item plus a partner "track record" of past performance. Unbeknownst to them, half the track records are genuine and half were drawn at random from a shuffled stack, so for some pairs the "peer" is credentialed by nothing. Post-exposure credences are scored with a proper scoring rule on points, which matters because it makes splitting the difference a real gamble rather than safe hedging: moving halfway to your partner's answer should cost you points when they're wrong and gain you points when they're right, and bland retreat toward the middle is punished either way. I'd also like a final round in which the quantity to estimate is which member of the pair was more accurate in the earlier rounds, so that peerhood itself becomes the thing they disagree about, with each side holding a track record the other side disputes. Design the full activity: exact rounds, timing, materials, how to construct the track records so the genuine and fake ones aren't distinguishable by formatting, the scoring, and how to handle the reveal. Then the debrief: map what happened onto the distinction between revising your credence and revising your view of your partner, whether students who discover their partner's credentials were random end up retroactively distrusting the genuine ones, and where in the game someone acting as a steadfast theorist would have actually won points. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run, and be candid about what the game cannot reproduce — real peer disagreement usually involves shared evidence with no external oracle, whereas here there's a fact of the matter I reveal at the end — and what I should supply in the last ten minutes once the names conciliationism and steadfastness finally come out.
