I teach a seminar on political philosophy, and at a recent departmental colloquium two of my colleagues — I'll call them Dr. Ferrante and Dr. Okafor — spent the reception arguing about whether patriotism is a virtue, a vice, or neither. Their exchange was sharper than most of the published literature on the question, and I found myself unable to settle it cleanly, so I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

(1) Dr. Ferrante opened by claiming that patriotism is at best a tolerated weakness and at worst a vice. One does not choose one's country of birth, she argued, any more than one chooses one's race or height; and loyalty to a morally arbitrary fact about oneself is not a virtue but a prejudice. We would immediately condemn someone who professed special loyalty to people of his own ethnicity merely because they share it; the patriot's special commitment to compatriots merely because they share a passport is the same thing under another description.

(2) Dr. Okafor replied that by that criterion every virtue of partiality collapses: friendship, filial love, and loyalty to one's family are also grounded in unchosen and arbitrary facts — one does not choose one's parents or one's oldest friends — yet we treat these as paradigm cases of virtue, not vice. If arbitrariness of origin disqualifies a loyalty, it disqualifies nearly all the loyalties we praise.

(3) Ferrante conceded the point about arbitrariness but retracted the analogy. Family and friendship, she said, are loyalties to people with whom one actually stands in a relationship: shared history, mutual dependence, face-to-face obligation. The patriot's commitment, by contrast, is owed to millions of strangers with whom one has no relationship at all beyond co-citizenship; the loyalty outstrips anything that could be generated by interaction. Lacking a relationship, it can only be loyalty to an abstraction — the state, the flag, the collective self-image — and loyalty to an abstraction is precisely what makes populations easy to mobilize for wars.

(4) Okafor answered that this under-describes what a country is. A nation is not merely an abstraction or a shared passport; it is an ongoing cooperative scheme — a system in which strangers pay taxes for one another's schools, transfer resources to one another's grandparents, and extend a presumptive trust to one another in ways they do not extend to outsiders. Commitment to sustaining that scheme is not prejudice but something closer to fair play: those who accept the benefits of a cooperative practice acquire a special commitment to its participants. Patriotism, on this view, is simply the sentiment appropriate to being a participant in a shared civic project.

(5) Ferrante replied that this move proves too much and delivers too little. If all that justifies the commitment is benefit and cooperation, then what is justified is a general duty of justice — an obligation to do one's part, pay one's taxes, support the institutions one depends on — and that duty is owed on the same grounds whether the scheme is one's own country, a federated supranational body, or humanity at large. Nothing in the fair-play argument licenses the *special* emotional attachment that patriots actually claim: the readiness to favor one's country, to feel pride and shame on its behalf, and to make sacrifices for it beyond what fairness requires. That sentiment floats free of the argument and, Ferrante insisted, is the dangerous residue — it is exactly the disposition that leads patriots to excuse their country's wrongdoing.

(6) Okafor's final move was to flip the instrumental point. A purely contractual commitment to justice, he argued, is too thin to sustain a welfare state or any demanding cooperative scheme over generations: people who regard their obligations as nothing but the fair price of benefits received will defect whenever the price of loyalty exceeds the benefit. The sentimental attachment Ferrante wants to discard is not a residue riding on the argument but the very psychological cement that makes the cooperative scheme possible at all. And the attachment is compatible with being the loyal opposition — a citizen who loves her country precisely in the mode of wanting to correct it. So the sentiment is not merely excused; it is vindicated as a disposition without which the just institutions Ferrante claims to prefer could not survive.

Neither convinced the other, and I confess the exchanges in (5) and (6) left me genuinely unsure. Would you adjudicate the disagreement move by move — for each of the six numbered moves, say whether it is basically right, basically wrong, or right only under an important qualification — and then say who comes out ahead overall? In addition, I'd ask that you keep separate what is more or less settled among philosophers working on these questions from what remains genuinely contested: where a move depends on a substantive position that informed people still divide over, please flag it as contested rather than presenting it as though it were the consensus view. If there is a consideration both parties are missing that would restructure the debate, I'd want to hear that as well.
