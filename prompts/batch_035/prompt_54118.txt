I have been reading around the bounded rationality dispute for the better part of fifteen years — first the heuristics-and-biases material that saturated the rationalist blogosphere, then, when I finally went to the primary sources, the fast-and-frugal heuristics program that Gigerenzer and the ABC Research Group built as a direct rival to it. And I have come to suspect the fight is structurally stalled in a way its participants rarely acknowledge. The data points are old and mostly undisputed: the Linda conjunction effect, the finding that Take The Best can beat linear regression out of sample, the demonstration that frequency formats dissolve many of the classic probability errors. Gigerenzer reads the same results Kahneman does and concludes that the heuristics are ecologically rational rather than defective; the heuristics-and-biases side reads them and concludes the heuristics are reliable enough to be dangerous. Meanwhile the original Simon-style question — whether satisficing is a genuine rival normative ideal or merely maximization with search costs folded into the objective function — keeps getting "resolved" by the deflationary move that any satisficer is just a constrained optimizer in disguise, and both sides keep talking past that resolution rather than accepting it. More recently the resource-rationality program has tried to launder the whole thing by deriving heuristics as optimal under computational constraints, and, as far as I can tell, neither camp has treated that as the dissolution it was advertised to be.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement was never really empirical. Both camps can predict each other's results. What they disagree about is the scoreboard — whether individual cognition is to be scored against a coherence standard set by ideal agents with unbounded time and information, or against a correspondence standard set by performance in the environment the organism actually inhabits. If that's right, then a lot of the apparent factual dispute (do heuristics cause errors?) is a normative dispute about what an error even is, wearing an empirical costume, and the deflationary "satisficing is just constrained maximizing" argument doesn't dissolve anything because the two camps never agreed on what the constraint set is or who gets to fix it. It also suggests the resource-rationality synthesis failed to land not because it's wrong but because it quietly takes a side — it treats the ideal-agent standard as the standard and recomputes bounded behavior as approximation, which is precisely what the ecological-rationality camp denies one should do.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, two research programs answering different questions (mechanism, norm, prescription) under a single heading, or a genuine normative standoff about the standard of evaluation that no data can touch? Support whatever diagnosis you give with what each side actually claims, say what the deflationary move about satisficing really accomplishes, and tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a sharper distinction, a class of environments where the two standards provably diverge, a change of question — or whether the stalemate should be understood as the permanent cost of an ill-posed question. I'd particularly like you to address the move our own community makes constantly: the injunction to "shut up and multiply" over expected utility on the one hand, and the acknowledgment that we are bounded agents who cannot actually run the full decision theory on the other. Does holding both of those at once commit us to one side of this dispute, or is that combination itself a fourth position nobody in the academic literature has properly named?
