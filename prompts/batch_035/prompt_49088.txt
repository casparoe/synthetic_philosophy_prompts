Can you compare the Nash bargaining solution with the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution? I get that both pick a point on the Pareto frontier of a two person bargaining problem, but they come apart once the feasible set isn't symmetric. From what I understand the whole fight between them comes down to monotonicity: KS guarantees that if the feasible set expands in a way that only raises one player's maximum possible payoff, that player shouldn't be made worse off, and Nash doesn't care. But then Nash buys independence of irrelevant alternatives and KS loses it, since the ideal point itself moves whenever the frontier moves. So two questions. First, which axiom do you think is actually more costly to give up -- can you give a concrete-feeling example where Nash gives a pretty bonkers outcome, or one where KS does? Second, I've seen teh KS point described as the outcome where both sides make equal relative concessions, meaning each player gives up the same fraction of the gap between their disagreement payoff and their ideal payoff. Is that characterization exactly right, and if so, does it give KS a real fairness story that Nash never had, given that Nash only normalizes utilities and then splits the surplus 50/50? I'd like your actual opinion at the end about which solution you'd endorse for, say, wage negotiations -- not just a survey of the two.
