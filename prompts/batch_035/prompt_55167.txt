I am a retired vision scientist who has lately been reading the early moderns, and I have become stuck on a question about the way my old field handles Molyneux's problem. Molyneux asked whether a person born blind, upon gaining sight, could distinguish a sphere from a cube by sight alone before touching them. Philosophers debated this for three centuries, but in my own field it has largely been treated as an empirical question awaiting the right patients. There is a long tradition here: an eighteenth-century surgeon's report of a boy whose sight was restored by couching his cataracts was taken up eagerly by both sides of the debate, and more recently a surgical programme that restores sight to congenitally blind children has tested them within a day or two of the operation, using forced-choice discrimination between objects that differ in shape, and then tested them again days later. The usual finding is near-chance performance very early on and rapid improvement within a few days, and one frequently reads that Molyneux's question has now been answered in the negative by experiment.

What I want to understand is what this research programme takes for granted philosophically, because I suspect the experiments presuppose rather more than their investigators acknowledge. It seems to presuppose, first, a view of what it is to possess a spatial concept such as sphere or cube: that recognising a shape is a determinate capacity that either transfers across modalities or does not, measurable at a moment, rather than something that comes in degrees or develops over time. It seems to presuppose, second, a view of what counts as recognition: the original question, as I read it, concerned what a newly sighted person could say with confidence, yet the experiments score above-chance guessing on a two-alternative task, and treat chance performance as an answer. And it seems to presuppose, third, that the question is settled by when the patient is tested, even though the interval between surgery and test looks like an experimental variable rather than something the philosophical question itself fixes.

Could you reconstruct the implicit theory of concepts and perception that this practice rests on, and say whether the several parts of it are mutually consistent? Which of these commitments does the practice strictly require, and which are merely conventional choices it could survive without? Is the programme underdetermined in the sense that several quite different theories of cross-modal concepts would license the same design, and could one instead argue that the experiment answers a different question from Molyneux's altogether? And if we replaced the presupposed view with, say, a theory on which spatial concepts are abilities that develop and partially overlap across modalities, what specifically would have to change in how such patients are tested and in what the results are allowed to mean? I would be grateful for a careful answer, written in British English.
