I run the disability services office at a mid-sized community college, and every exam season my staff and I make something like thirty decisions about what a student may keep with them in a proctored exam: a paper notebook the student carries everywhere, a phone full of notes, a human note-taker, a smartwatch that prompts them through steps. Our official standard -- accommodations that are reasonable and don't fundamentally alter what the exam measures -- gives us almost nothing to work with, and instructors appeal half of what we approve, which puts my staff in the position of defending gut calls to a Dean who wants reasons.

Some years ago I read a popular piece about a philosopher who argued that if a patient with memory damage wrote everything in a notebook and always had it with him, the notebook was doing the same job his brain used to do, so calling it "not really his memory" was arbitrary. I have no philosophy training, but this struck me as the only principled handle we have: for some students, the object they're asking to bring in is genuinely part of how they think, and taking it away isn't removing a crutch, it's removing part of the mind doing the exam. For other students it's plainly a shortcut around what the exam is supposed to measure. My staff can't tell which is which by intuition, and neither can the instructors, and I'd like to stop pretending we can.

Please turn that idea into a procedure my staff can actually run, in about fifteen minutes per request: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a firm no (the aid is clearly not part of the student's thinking), where it stops with a firm yes (the aid clearly is, so banning it changes what the exam measures rather than preserving it), and where it must hand the decision to the accommodations committee because the remaining question is about what the course is for rather than about the student. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of mind actually says about when an external thing becomes part of someone's cognition, but keep the questions plain enough for a staff member without any theory.

Then run it on four requests from last spring: (1) a student with a brain injury who carries a paper memory notebook everywhere, relies on it completely, and wants it in a closed-book history exam; (2) the same student in a math exam where the instructor insists the course goal is performing without reference material; (3) a student with severe ADHD whose phone holds notes she wrote herself but half-trusts, asking to keep the phone during a paper exam; (4) a student asking to use an AI assistant during a closed-book philosophy exam because, he says, it is now simply how he thinks. Flag which of these the procedure settles and which it can't, and be blunt about where you think the verdict will be one I won't like.

Finally, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire, and what would have to be true -- about the philosophy, or about our students, or about what exams are for -- for the verdicts to come out the other way. If the underlying idea about minds and their tools is less settled than the popular version I read, I would rather know that than have a procedure that pretends otherwise.
