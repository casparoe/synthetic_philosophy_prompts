So the geologists finally ruled on the Anthropocene, and it got rejected. My understanding of the episode: the Anthropocene Working Group spent like 15 years on this, picked the sediments of Crawford Lake in Canada as the physical reference site, and put the start of the epoch at the plutonium layer from the early hydrogen bomb tests (early 1950s). Then in 2024 the relevant subcommission voted the whole proposal down by a wide margin, and the IUGS leadership confirmed it, with a statement that basically said: the term is too useful as a general descriptor, used by everyone from ecologists to politicians, to be boxed into a formal unit of the geologic timescale. From the outside it looks like a standards body rejecting a spec that everyone already depends on.

Analyze this episode through the lens of periodization as a question of standing: who gets to draw the lines, and by what procedure? Geology has an actual gatekeeping mechanism -- a marker site, a committee, a vote -- for saying when an epoch begins. Historians have no procedure at all: nobody ever ratified "the Renaissance" or "the Industrial Revolution," and those concepts seem to get along fine without a governing body.
