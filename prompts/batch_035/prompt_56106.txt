I teach political philosophy, and I've been drafting a short essay for a general-interest journal on whether the left/right distinction carves anything at nature's joints — not "is it useful" but "what, if anything, holds each side together," given how heterogeneous each coalition is. My working thesis is a two-part diagnosis argument, sketched below, and I'd appreciate candid comments on it before I commit to the full draft.

The essay's argument, in compressed form: (1) The standard answer — that the left is united by commitment to equality and the right by acceptance of hierarchy — fails, because both sides claim egalitarian credentials of some kind: the right appeals to equality before God, equality of opportunity, equality before the law, and a nationalist "we're all in the same boat," while parts of the left accept hierarchies they decline to attack (of expertise, of party, of the charismatic leader). So equality doesn't sort the sides; it just gets defined differently by each. (2) What actually unifies each side, I want to argue, is not a value but a diagnosis — an answer to the question of what the paradigmatic political evil is. The left's unifying commitment is that the worst thing politics does is subordination: people being made lesser by systems of power, whether economic, racial, gendered, or cultural. The right's unifying commitment is that the worst thing politics does is disintegration: the dissolving of the boundaries and bonds — family, nation, faith, property, inherited custom — without which collective life decays into chaos or sterility. Values, on my account, are downstream of diagnoses: the left values equality because equality is the remedy for subordination, and the right values order and authority because authority is the remedy for disintegration. The payoff I want to claim is that this explains otherwise puzzling coalitions (why libertarians sit on the right despite caring little for tradition: they see the state as the chief disintegrating force; why parts of the left tolerate quite steep internal hierarchies: those hierarchies aren't experienced as subordinating the people who matter), and it predicts that each side will systematically misread the other as celebrating the evil it diagnoses — the right reads the left as wanting disorder, the left reads the right as wanting cruelty — when in fact each is remedying a different disease.

Three specific things I'd value comments on. First, fairness: is my dismissal of the equality/hierarchy account in section (1) a genuine refutation or a strawman — is there a version of the equality thesis that survives my "both sides claim equality" objection, perhaps by distinguishing first-order commitments from rhetorical window dressing, and if so, does that distinction do real work or just relabel the problem? Second, the diagnosis thesis itself: does it handle the cases that most obviously strain it — the authoritarian left (which seems to value subordination-remedies less than its own grip on power), the communitarian or "post-liberal" right (which is anti-market and pro-hierarchy in ways my framework may predict but may also just absorb everything), and green politics (which sits everywhere)? I'm particularly worried that "diagnosis of the political evil" is elastic enough to accommodate any counterexample, which would make the thesis unfalsifiable in a way that makes it merely verbal. Third, and most candidly: is this interesting, or is it a repackaging of familiar points about each side's picture of the fallen world with new labels attached? If you think a reviewer would say "this is just X in different words," I'd rather hear it now. Comments on any or all of the three, and on anything structural I've missed, would be most welcome.
