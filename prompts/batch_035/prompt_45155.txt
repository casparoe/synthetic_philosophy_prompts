I would like a careful comparison of two ways of responding to Newton's bucket experiment: Newton's own conclusion that the water's rotation must be rotation with respect to absolute space, and the relational alternative descending from Mach, on which the water rotates relative to the distant masses of the universe rather than to space itself.

There is a subtlety I want you to take seriously rather than brushing aside. A common textbook rendering says the bucket shows rotation is absolute, and therefore absolute space exists. But the experiment, described carefully, seems to establish something weaker: only that the water's state of motion cannot be constituted by its motion relative to the bucket, the body immediately surrounding it. This leaves at least three options open: (i) rotation relative to absolute space; (ii) rotation relative to the total distribution of matter in the universe, as Mach suggested; and (iii) a more austere view on which no body stands in any privileged reference relation at all, but acceleration, unlike velocity, is a primitive and irreducible feature of the histories of bodies or of their pairwise relations. On this third view, the concave surface of the water reflects the water's intrinsic acceleration history, and nothing about a container called space.

Please assess the following: (1) Which, if any, of these three options does the bucket experiment itself actually favor, as opposed to broader theoretical considerations that might be brought to bear on top of it? (2) Do options (i) and (iii) differ in any empirical or metaphysical substance, or merely in how much ontology each packs into the word "space"? (3) Can Mach's option (ii) be reformulated so that it does not depend on the contingent fact that the universe happens to contain distant heavy bodies — in other words, does a Machian have a good answer to the question of what would happen to a lone bucket in an otherwise empty universe?

On all three points, please clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of physics. Where the literature is divided, present the competing positions as competing positions rather than settling on one and presenting it as the standard view; where something is settled — for instance, about what is observed at each stage of the experiment itself — say so plainly.
