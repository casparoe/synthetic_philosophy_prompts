For the past six years I've been the sole Rules Judge in a long-running Nomic-style game some friends and I have kept going — loosely inspired by Peter Suber's, but with a ruleset of our own devising: roughly forty mutable rules, a core of fourteen immutable ones, and a transmutation rule for moving a rule between tiers. Disputes over a proposal's validity come to me, I rule alone, and I've become mildly obsessed with staying consistent with my own precedents. My track record on questions of self-reference is as follows:

- Proposal 114 amended our quorum rule, a mutable rule regulating how proposals are voted on. I upheld it. The change was purely prospective, and nobody suggested it bootstrapped itself.
- Proposal 131 amended the rule that defines what counts as a rule-change (enacting, repealing, amending). I upheld it: the definitional rule is mutable, and nothing in the change touched that proposal's own adoption.
- Proposal 147 was a single proposal that lowered the voting threshold and declared that it had passed under the new, lower threshold. I struck it down, on the ground that an act cannot supply the conditions of its own validity. It was then resubmitted as two sequential proposals, each adopted under the law in force at the time, and I had no objection to that.
- Proposal 162 added to a mutable rule the sentence "this rule may never be amended or repealed." I held the entrenchment clause void — a mutable rule cannot make itself immutable one clause at a time — and the rule stayed mutable.
- Proposal 175 sought to amend one of the fourteen immutable rules outright. I struck it down, unremarkably.

Next session brings Proposal 190, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I rule. It is a single, clean amendment to Rule 20 — the rule that authorizes all rule-changes, currently providing that rule-changes are adopted by a simple majority of votes cast, and itself mutable — replacing majority adoption with approval by the sitting Speaker. The proposal was itself adopted by simple majority, does not claim to rely on the Speaker-approval regime for its own adoption, and would operate purely prospectively. Its opponents argue that it is the paradigm self-amendment: the rule that makes this proposal law is the very rule it abolishes, and if an act cannot confer its own validity directly, it cannot do so at one remove.

Please give me five things. First, lay out the readings of my track record that are genuinely live. I can see at least two: a plenary-authority reading, on which the rule-changing power reaches every rule it does not expressly exempt, itself included, provided the proposal's own adoption satisfied the law in force when it passed; and a no-self-application reading, on which an amendment to the rule that authorizes amendments is void in the same way the bootstrapping clause in 147 was. Tell me whether there are other doctrines that fit the five precedents as well as these do, and whether any of them fit the precedents better. Second, identify my closest precedent and say honestly whether it decides this case or merely resembles it. Third, recommend the verdict that coheres best with my own record, with your best guess at what I would say and the reasons for preferring that rule to its rivals. Fourth, tell me what principle I would be adopting with each available choice — including what each commits me to on entrenchment and on the immutable tier, and specifically whether each doctrine explains my ruling on Proposal 162 for the right reasons or only by coincidence. Fifth, name the further case, ideally one our game could actually generate, that would most cleanly divide the live readings — I suspect something like a proposal that amends Rule 20 only for future proposals that do not themselves concern Rule 20, or a deliberate one-step-versus-two-step comparison, but I want you to reason it out rather than accept my suggestion.
