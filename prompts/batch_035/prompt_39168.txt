You are a professional philosopher with expertise in aesthetics, the ontology of art, and value theory. Please think carefully before answering: I'd rather you brainstorm the possibility space first and then commit to a ranked list, with your reasoning visible at each step.

I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and the adjacent blogosphere, and I've noticed our community is oddly split on fakes and forgeries. The standard rationalist line treats the premium on originals as a bias: if a duplicate is molecule-for-molecule indistinguishable from the 'original' painting, preferring the original seems like the same error as caring whether you're the original or the copy in a teleporter scenario. Yet I find myself unpersuaded. The van Meegeren Vermeers became worse paintings to most viewers the moment their provenance was exposed, and I've never seen a clean account of why that reaction is irrational rather than the correct tracking of something real — say, the object's causal-historical relation to an artist's intention and skill.

I've been asked to advise a small foundation that can fund about eight philosophers for three years on the conceptual side of this debate — ontology and value theory, not art history, forensics, or authentication technology. Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order?

Here's my first pass at what's on the table, though I suspect it's incomplete: (a) whether aesthetic value supervenes on an object's intrinsic properties alone, or partly on its history of making; (b) the status of my own pet hypothesis, that the value of originals is largely positional — authenticity working as a Schelling point for status, so that debunking the preference wouldn't create or destroy value, merely redistribute it, which matters a lot for whether a rational agent ought to revise what she cares about; (c) whether the pleasure we take in originals is epistemically justified, in the sense of tracking reliable information about skill and effort, in which case van Meegeren-style reactions are Bayesian updating rather than snobbery; and (d) how all of this interacts with perfect duplication, from digital files to hypothetical atom-for-atom copies of physical works.

I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning behind it. For each item, tell me: why it's upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is with armchair methods versus requiring empirical psychology, and what would have to turn out to be true for your ordering to change. Finally, be explicit about which of these, if settled, would actually change anything for a reflective agent deciding what to value — versus which would merely relocate the mystery one level up.
