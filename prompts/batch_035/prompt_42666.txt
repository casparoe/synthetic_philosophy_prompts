I handle grantmaking for a small effective-giving advisory that steers a few million dollars a year from roughly thirty major donors. Next month I have to present to our donor-advisory board the case for overhauling how we allocate: instead of our current practice of ranking charities directly by whatever cost-effectiveness numbers the charities themselves report, I want us to adopt a two-stage process — first a cause-level screen using importance, tractability, and neglectedness, and only then comparisons between interventions inside a cause that has passed the screen. The board is a specific group and I want to be prepared for them specifically, not for a generic audience: there's a development economist who spent a decade at a multilateral agency and believes retrospective evaluations of concrete programs are the only honest evidence there is; a physician who runs a community health nonprofit and privately thinks the identifiability and proximity of beneficiaries matter morally, not just the number helped; the family's longtime estate lawyer, who is our institutional memory and is suspicious of anything that arrives as a spreadsheet with a hidden agenda; and one donor's adult child, strongly EA-adjacent, who will want me to admit that a consistent application of the framework ranks existential-risk work above everything else, and that if I flinch from that conclusion the framework is being applied dishonestly.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive, phrased in each person's own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each one can do, each with what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Please make sure the sheet covers at least these, since I already half-know they're coming: the endogeneity worry that causes are neglected *because* they are bad, and that tractability and neglectedness aren't independent axes at all but re-encodings of each other and of expected value, so the three-factor screen is just cost-effectiveness analysis with worse arithmetic; the demand that importance be commensurable across wildly different causes, which is the point where I least trust my own answer; the economist's preference for the charity-level numbers on the grounds that at least someone measured something; and the physician's objection that the framework launders moral choices about whose lives count into an apparently neutral bookkeeping exercise. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect the lawyer will ask it anyway — and tell me how to survive it.
