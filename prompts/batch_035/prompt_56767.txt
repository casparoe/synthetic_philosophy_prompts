I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I am preparing a column on supertasks built around Thomson's lamp. The standard treatments leave readers with the vague sense that infinitely many switchings somehow make the final state indeterminate, and I want a pair of engineered cases that pins down exactly what does the work — the piece's point being to distinguish puzzles generated by infinity itself from puzzles generated by gaps in our descriptions.

Construct two supertasks. In the first, the classic bouncing ball: dropped from a fixed height, it bounces at geometrically shrinking intervals and comes to rest at a definite point at a definite time, its final state fixed. In the second, design a variant of Thomson's lamp — call it the vibrating-switch lamp — in which the switch lever itself oscillates across the on/off threshold in geometrically shrinking swings, so that every physical variable of the mechanism converges to a definite limit, yet whether the lamp is on or off at the limit time remains undetermined. Constraints: each scenario should be a single realistic paragraph, driven by ordinary mechanics, with no agent flipping switches and no exotic physics; and the underdetermination must be generated by the construction, never stipulated.

After the two scenarios, demonstrate that the design works: show, case by case, which quantities converge and which do not; identify the single feature that makes the difference between the two verdicts; and say what happens to the pair of cases if that feature is removed. Include the strongest line of attack from someone who holds that Thomson's lamp shows such supertasks to be physically impossible. Finally — and here I want you committed rather than balanced — take a definite position on whether the pair of cases shows the lamp's indeterminacy to be a feature of our description rather than of the world, and defend that verdict against the alternative reading instead of leaving both standing.
