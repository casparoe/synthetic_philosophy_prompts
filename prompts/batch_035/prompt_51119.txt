So I've been reading rationalist-adjacent stuff on effective altruism forever, and the standard-issue film discussion is always The Ones Who Walk Away From Omelas, which at this point feels like the "imagine a McDonalds in Atlantis" of moral philosophy. But I've never seen anyone do It's a Wonderful Life vs. Singer's drowning child argument, and I think it's actually a much more interesting matchup, so I'd love a reading of the film through that lens.

George Bailey basically lives inside a Singer thought experiment for two hours: Harry falls through the ice and George jumps in, Mr. Gower's poisoned capsule, the bank run, Mary offering their honeymoon money. He is constantly rescuing nearby, identifiable people at the cost of every distant ambition he has -- travel, college, building things far from Bedford Falls. And the film frames that as a good life! Meanwhile Singer's whole point is that distance and identifiability shouldn't matter, so by Singer's lights George is making exactly the right trade every time, but for the wrong reasons (he can see them, he knows their names), and Potter is the villain for hoarding rather than for not giving globally. And then the ending inverts everything: the man who spent his life as the rescuer becomes the drowning child, and the community bails him out to the tune of $8,000 that, by Singer's arithmetic, would've saved several actual children from malaria. Does the film have an answer to that, or just warm fuzzies? Is there something structurally missing from Singer's argument that the movie captures -- e.g. that the rescuer is also someone who can need rescuing, that a life of obligation needs to be a life someone would want -- or is that just rationlizing partiality?

Be honest about where the film resists the reading too, I don't want it flattened into an EA thinkpiece. And since you can't rewatch it to check details: tell me how confident you are in the scene-level claims, and flag which single point in your reading you're least sure of.
