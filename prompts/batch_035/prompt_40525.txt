i'm a novelist, two years deep into a book set in a provincial town in the 1870s, and the research has quietly wrecked an assumption I didn't know I was writing from: that the villagers would experience "the nation" the way my readers do, as something ancient and self-evident. the more I read around, the more it seems like half of what looks eternal about national life -- folk costumes, anthem rituals, the whole vibe of "we have always been a people" -- got assembled shockingly recently, roughly in the century between my setting and now. some historians basically claim that the nation itself is a modern invention, a product of mass literacy and print and bureaucratic censuses rather than something waiting patiently in the soil for centuries.

so heres what I want, and I want it in British English please. give me your actual credence that this claim, stated carefully as: "a randomly chosen tradition that a present-day nation-state treats as evidence of its ancient national character exists in a recognisably continuous form from before roughly 1800" is true. a number, not a hedge. and then decompose it for me, because the bare number won't help me with the novel: how much of it rides on whether the big picture theory (nations as modern constructions, imagined through print and mass politics, traditions manufactured retrospectively) is basically right; how much rides on the definitional question of what "recognisably continuous form" even means, since almost everything has *some* older ingredient even if its current packaging is new; how much on the selection effect, i.e. whether the traditions states actually showcase are a biased sample of the genuinely old ones; and anything else you think belongs in the sum. give me a rough probability and one line of reasoning for each piece, then put them together.

then tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what would actually move it -- archival stuff? a particular kind of counterexample, like a tradition with genuinely unbroken documentation? -- and be honest if you think my framing smuggles in a false dichotomy between "ancient and real" versus "recently assembled and fake", because its occuring to me as I write this that maybe the manufacturing process doesn't make the thing less real, and if that thought is doing hidden work in the question I want to know.

bonus if you have views: for my protagonist, a schoolteacher in the 1870s, should I write him as experiencing his national identity as primarily ethnic-linguistic, or as dynastic-religious-local with the national layer still thin and new? tell me how your credence above bears on that choice, if it does at all.
