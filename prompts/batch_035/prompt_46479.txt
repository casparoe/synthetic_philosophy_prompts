I've never studied philosophy, but for the past few months I've been thinking about consciousness on my own, mostly during late-night walks, and I've run into something I can't settle. As I understand it, there are really two problems here. The obvious one is the hard problem: why all of this processing in the brain feels like anything from the inside at all. The second, which I gather people call the meta-problem, looks much more tractable: why do we go around saying there's a hard problem in the first place? Why do we report an "explanatory gap," insist that our experiences have features that science can't capture, and keep complaining that something is being left out? That second question is just a question about human behavior and reports, so psychology and neuroscience can in principle study it the way they study any other behavior — no spooky first-person data required.

Here is what has been eating me. Suppose science someday arrives at a complete story of why I keep insisting something is left out — some quirk of how a brain models itself, say, that predictably generates these protests. Once I understand that story, don't I have everything I could reasonably want? If I still feel that something is missing, I would at that point know exactly why I feel that way, and the feeling itself would be explained rather than pointing past the explanation to some further fact. The remaining complaint seems to me like watching a stage magician, learning exactly how the trick works, and still insisting the woman was genuinely sawed in half. My friends push back in two opposite directions, and both sound plausible to me. One says no — the explanation of the reports leaves the thing itself completely untouched, so the mystery is exactly as large as before, and my magician analogy smuggles in the conclusion. The other says the analogy is right: the felt mystery was all the mystery there ever was, and once we see where the feeling comes from there is nothing left to explain.

So my question is: when science explains why we claim there's a hard problem, what does that explanation actually do to the hard problem itself — dissolve it, shrink it, or simply fail to touch it? Is there a principled way to decide among these, or does the answer depend on things we can't yet see? And connectedly: at what point, if ever, does it become irrational to keep saying something is missing, once I understand precisely why I keep saying it? I'd appreciate it if you could keep clear about which parts of this are genuinely contested among people who work on these questions and which parts are more or less agreed, since I've gathered that self-described illusionists and their critics part ways exactly here, and I don't want to be handed one side's answer as though it were the settled one.
