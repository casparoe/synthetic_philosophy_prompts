Construct a thought experiment on the theme of the Ship of Theseus in which the traditional worry is subordinated to a sharper one: a collision between the transitivity of identity and our tolerance for small changes in artifacts. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic (a single working shipyard across successive refit seasons; no teleporters, demons, or perfect records), no longer than one paragraph, and it must not stipulate its verdicts — the tension must be generated by the replacement schedule itself. The schedule should be engineered so that (i) between any two adjacent states of the vessel, the change is matched or exceeded by a control change that we independently and confidently judge identity-preserving on a different ship, and (ii) the first and last states differ in every plank and in overall hull form, so that no plausible criterion counts them identical. Note what the case is for: it is a stress test of whether artifact identity can be transitive, not of which of two rival ships is the original — a scenario that answers the second question by stipulation fails. After the scenario, demonstrate that each specification is met; then explain, for each of three views — the perdurantist's temporal-parts account, a closest-continuer theory, and epistemicism about vagueness — which state, if any, counts as the same ship as the first, and why. Finish by identifying which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if that feature is removed, and how a defender of transitivity plus classical identity would attack the case (for instance, by rejecting one of the control judgments, or by denying that smallness of change ever suffices for identity rather than merely excusing our inability to tell). State finally whether you think the case is just a sorites in disguise, and what would have to be built into it to make it more than that.
