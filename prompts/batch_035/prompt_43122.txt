I run an evening philosophy discussion group of about fifteen people from mixed backgrounds, and I want a sorting quiz titled something like "which view on the ethics of belief do you actually hold?" — aimed at the Clifford–James debate, but sorting by cases rather than by labels. Write eight to ten questions, each a concrete situation in which someone is tempted to believe ahead of the evidence: e.g., a climber who will survive a leap only if he genuinely believes he can make it; a person deciding whether to trust a new colleague against a rumor; an entrepreneur whose confidence may be self-fulfilling; someone weighing Pascal-style prudential reasons to believe in God; a patient hoping for recovery beyond what the prognosis supports; a juror sympathetically disposed toward a defendant. Each question should offer two or three answer options phrased as what the person should do, is permitted to do, or cannot help doing — never as which theory the person endorses.

Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the following leaves: strict evidentialism in Clifford's spirit (it is always wrong to believe on insufficient evidence); James's permission (believing is legitimate when the option is live, forced, and momentous and the evidence cannot decide it); the dissolutionist view that what is under our control is acceptance, action, and policy rather than belief itself, so the whole dispute rests on a mistake about doxastic voluntarism; and an epistemic-consequentialist middle position on which believing ahead of evidence is permissible when the risk to others and to one's own epistemic habits is contained. Build the key from the underlying diagnostic questions — Is belief under direct voluntary control here? Is the option genuinely forced, or could it be postponed? Is the belief self-fulfilling or evidentially inert? Does believing risk harm beyond the believer? — and only then locate the named positions in that space. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot really separate (for instance, James's permission and the acceptance view may predict the same answers to every question — if so, say what extra question would be needed, or admit none exists). Note any combination of answers that you suspect no one occupies, with one sentence diagnosing whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, make sure the debrief for my group explains how a respondent's pattern would change if the same cases were restated with third-person stakes (someone else's welfare riding on the belief) — I want to know whether the quiz sorts people into a single stable position or into one per framing.
