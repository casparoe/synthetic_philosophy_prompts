A longstanding dispute flared up again at my local rationalist meetup last week, this time about dispositions, and I found myself unable to referee it cleanly. Since the issues came up in the context of a discussion about AI safety claims, I'd like an outside adjudication. Background, for context: the argument began when someone mentioned the standard counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions — the thought that to be fragile just is to break if struck, to be irascible just is to snap if provoked, and so on. The two participants, Ada and Ben, got into it over a glass swaddled in packing straw, and the exchange ran as follows.

(1) Ada argued that the straw-wrapped glass is still fragile even though it would never break when struck: the straw masks the manifestation of the disposition rather than removing the disposition itself. If you insist on the conditional analysis, she said, you get the absurd result that wrapping a glass changes what it's made of, dispositionally speaking.

(2) Ben countered that this makes dispositions into invisible baggage. A disposition with no possible manifestation does no explanatory or predictive work, so the claim that the glass is "still fragile" is either a claim about its microstructure — in which case just say that — or it's a claim about nothing. On his view, if the glass is never going to break when struck, calling it fragile is a holdover from a pre-scientific way of talking.

(3) Ada replied that the same move would force Ben to say an irascible man who has been slipped a fast-acting tranquilizer isn't irascible while the drug is in his system, since he never actually snaps at provocation. That seems clearly wrong, she said: we interrupt the expression of traits all the time without thinking the traits vanish, and the conditional analysis was supposed to capture what we mean by the trait, not silently rewrite it.

(4) Ben answered that he can bite that bullet, because character traits like irascibility are individuated behaviorally in a way brittleness is not — a personality trait just is a pattern of reactions, so a fully and permanently tranquillized man has, in the operative sense, lost the trait, whereas the glass's molecular structure is still there to be described directly. He added that Ada's position faces a dilemma: either "fragile" names the categorical microstructure, in which case it's redundant with chemistry, or it names some extra dispositional layer, in which case he wants to know what that layer does besides keep the old word in circulation.

(5) Ada's final move was to distinguish masking from finking, on the theory that Ben was equivocating between them. The straw is a mask: given the stimulus, the disposition is still present but its manifestation is blocked. A fink is different: it removes or replaces the disposition itself when the stimulus occurs — as in the classic case of a live wire connected to a device that instantly renders it dead whenever it's probed. The wire is genuinely live, yet it would never shock anyone. Finks show that even an intrinsic-property-friendly account of dispositions has to handle stimulus-conditional interventions, and masks show that manifestation-conditional analyses fail — and Ada claimed Ben's arguments conflate the two failure modes, which is why his tranquilizer bullet-biting and his redundancy dilemma don't both go through.

At this point the organizer had to break it up because we'd overrun the venue's booking, but they then pivoted to the question that started all this: if a deployed AI system never behaves badly because it is continuously monitored and constrained, is it "safe" in the dispositional sense, or is the monitoring merely a mask (or a fink?) over a dangerous underlying disposition — and does that distinction even matter for what the lab is entitled to claim about it? Ada and Ben split on this too, in exactly the ways you'd predict from their positions above.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right-about-part-of-it, and justify your ratings. Then say who is more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing — including, if you can, whether the AI application is a genuine instance of masking or finking or neither. Finally, please state how confident you are in each of your main verdicts and single out the point you're least sure about, since I'd like to know where to push back when this inevitably comes up again at next month's meetup.
