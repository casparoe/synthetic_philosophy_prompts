For the last session of my graduate seminar on philosophy of language, which has run from Frege through Kripke and ends with Quine's indeterminacy of translation, I'd like to close not with a summary but with a poem to read aloud. Write a sestina — six six-line stanzas plus a three-line envoi, with the six end-words cycling through their proper permutations — from the point of view of Quine's field linguist, newly arrived among speakers of a language for which he has no key. The end-words should be gavagai, rabbit, part, stage, pointing, and assent: the native word, the rival candidate referents, and the two kinds of behavioral evidence the linguist has to work with. Let the form carry the argument: the same six words return in new arrangements stanza after stanza, just as the same stream of evidence supports each rival translation manual equally well, so no stanza may quietly smuggle in a seventh word that settles the matter. Pointing in particular must fail as disambiguation — the linguist points at the same spot whether the native means the whole animal, an undetached part, or a fleeting stage, and the native assents every time. The envoi should gather all six end-words and land on the point the seminar has built toward: that nothing in the behavior, however long he stays, would decide between the manuals, so any resolution is a choice rather than a discovery — though this should be said in the poem's own voice, not the seminar's. Someone who has actually read Word and Object should recognize stimulus meaning, the role of assent and dissent, and the underdetermination of manual by evidence, not just the gavagai anecdote; someone who hasn't should still come away with a good poem about a stranger trying to pin down a meaning that will not hold still.
