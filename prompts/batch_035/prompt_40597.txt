I run a monthly philosophy evening at my local library, a standing group of about fifteen curious adults with no philosophical training beyond what previous sessions have given them; last month we worked through Gettier-style cases, so they already distrust the justified-true-belief formula and are keen to see whether anything repairs it. Next month I have forty-five minutes and a flip chart for Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and, more delicately, its consequence that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. I don't want you to explain the theory to me; I want a plan for explaining it. Work out the order the pieces have to arrive in — my own suspicion is that the two counterfactual conditions have to be solidly in place before the closure result can even be stated as something surprising rather than as a technicality, but tell me if that's wrong and what depends on what. Tell me what single case should carry the whole session, and in particular whether the demented grandmother case — the one where the family tells her the grandchildren are doing well whenever she asks, and the one time a grandchild is seriously ill they distract her with news of the others, so her true belief that they are all well fails the sensitivity condition — earns its place or merely teaches the group that tracking means something like never having been lied to; if there is a better carrier, say what it must contain and what it must be kept free of. Then give me a map of the misconceptions I should expect: I can already predict that several will hear the tracking conditions as plain reliability, that someone will object that Nozick has rejected modus ponens itself, and that a faction will walk out convinced his view implies we know nothing about the external world — tell me where in the sequence each of these should be intercepted, and add the ones you would predict that I haven't. Give me one question I can put to the group in the last five minutes whose answer would show they have genuinely understood the closure denial rather than merely tolerated it. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level — whether safety conditions, contextualism, or the wider literature on sensitivity belong in that category, you judge — and how to say so in a way that leaves the group feeling they got the real thing rather than a diluted one. Please write the whole plan as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
