I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form essay on the politics of luck: which consequences of fortune a society may fairly leave with the individual, and which it must absorb collectively. Before I begin interviewing people, I want the specification in front of me. Rather than a tour of positions, I want the requirements list first: what any adequate theory of distributive justice that takes luck seriously must deliver.

Please set out those desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. Among the cases I would expect the list to handle — do add others as you see fit:

- Two equally careless drivers, of whom only one happens to injure a pedestrian: identical choices, divergent outcomes, and the question of whether the difference in outcome is a difference justice should register.
- The person who was properly informed of a medical risk, declined precaution, and fell ill; and the person who was never told, or was misled.
- Someone who deliberately risked a ruinous loss and declined to insure, against someone who could not afford the premium — so that the same failure to be covered is a choice for one person and a circumstance for another.
- Expensive tastes versus expensive needs: the case where the theory must say something different about the gourmet and the dialysis patient, or explain why it need not.
- Natural endowments versus cultivated skill: whether being born without a talent is to be treated like being born with an illness, or like having declined to develop a capacity.
- A requirement the theory must not violate, or must explicitly reject: that holding someone responsible for a bad outcome is one thing, and the state expressing contempt for them, or leaving them without a safety net, is another. I understand there is a well-known objection that some theories effectively abandon the victims of their own choices to harsh treatment; I want to know whether that objection states a genuine requirement of any adequate theory or merely a preference of one camp.

Then, please tell me:

1. Which of the requirements are non-negotiable features of any adequate theory, and which are preferences of particular camps presented as universal demands.
2. Whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether holding people responsible for their choices would require information about those choices that no institution could actually possess, or whether compensating bad luck without penalising the prudent is achievable at all, or only at some cost the theory must acknowledge.
3. Which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and why.

Close with one short paragraph on whether the two leading families of views — the one under which justice is principally about neutralising the effects of luck, and the one under which it is principally about the quality of relations among citizens — could in principle satisfy the list. But the criteria, not the scorecard, are the part I want; keep the weight there. Please write your reply in British English.
