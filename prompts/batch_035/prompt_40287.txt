I'm grading an exam from a metaphysics course on dispositions, and I'd like a second opinion on one answer. The question was:

"State the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. Then explain, with an example of each, the two principal kinds of counterexample to it: C.B. Martin's finks and Mark Johnston's masks. Finally, assess whether Lewis's revised analysis, with its 'rather prone' clause, avoids both kinds of counterexample."

The student wrote:

"The simple conditional analysis holds that for an object to be disposed to give manifestation M under stimulus S is just for a certain conditional to be true of it: if S were to obtain, M would occur. Thus a glass is fragile just in case, were it struck, it would break.

Finks are counterexamples to this. Lewis's famous example is the live wire: a wire is connected to an electro-fink, a device that, the instant it detects anyone touching the wire, would render the wire dead before any shock could be delivered. The wire is live, and so disposed to shock when touched; yet the conditional 'if touched, it would shock' is false, since the fink would remove the disposition — or at any rate its basis — before the manifestation could occur. Finks therefore show that a disposition cannot be identified with its manifestation conditional.

Masks operate on the same principle; they are really a subspecies of fink. A mask is a standing condition that would be activated by the stimulus and remove the disposition before it can manifest. Johnston's example is the glass packed in stiff foam: were the glass struck, the foam would get between the blow and the glass, robbing the glass of its fragility at that moment, so the glass is fragile though it would not break if struck.

Lewis's revised analysis adds a 'rather prone' clause: the glass is fragile iff, if it were struck, and it remained rather prone to break (and nothing else intervened), it would break. Since the clause requires the object to stay prone, the electro-fink is excluded — the fink works precisely by making the wire no longer prone to shock. And for the same reason masks are excluded too: the foam is filtered out by the clause just as the reverse fuse is. So both counterexamples are neutralized, and the conditional analysis, in its revised form, survives."

Please assign a grade (letter or numerical, as you prefer) and give the specific comments that justify it. I'd like this point by point — where the answer is right, where it goes wrong, and how serious each error is — rather than a general impression of quality.
