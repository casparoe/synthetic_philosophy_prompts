I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and my supervisor keeps telling me my latest argument "proves too much" and I think she's right but I can't see exactly where it breaks. It's meant to be a new twist on the pessimistic meta-induction. Goes like this:

(1) A method of forming beliefs is unreliable if most of its past outputs have turned out to be false.
(2) Inference to the best explanation, applied to unobservable entities, has produced mostly false outputs in the past: crystalline spheres, phlogiston, caloric fluid, the optical ether, miasma.
(3) So IBE about unobservables is unreliable.
(4) Our belief in electrons, genes, tectonic plates and so on rests on IBE about unobservables.
(5) So we have no rational basis for believing in electrons.

So far so standard — that's just the pessimistic induction, and there are the usual replies. Here's my twist though:

(6) Premise (2) is itself known only through historical reconstruction — we know phlogiston theory failed by inferring to the best explanation of documents, lab reports, instruments and the trajectory of chemistry. Nobody directly observed phlogiston's absence; historians (and the rest of us) concluded it via IBE.
(7) By the same reasoning as (1)-(3), IBE is unreliable as applied to the historical record too.
(8) So we lack a rational basis for believing premise (2), the very record of past failures.
(9) So the whole package — PMI plus my extension — is self-defeating, which suggests something in it has gone wrong.

My problem is I genuinely cant decide whether the right diagnosis is that the unreliability verdict in (3) is domain-specific and doesn't transfer the way (7) needs, or that (1) is too crude a reliabilist principle, or that (6)-(7) commit a scope error — the method that failed was IBE about unobservables, whereas the history of chemistry is inference from observables (documents, instruments), so maybe the record is safer than the theories it buries. But that last response worries me, because it seems to hand the realist a win: if historical IBE about observables is trustworthy, why isn't current IBE about unobservables? If we can tell retrospectively that phlogiston was a mistake using methods we still trust, doesn't that mean some part of our method has been tracking truth all along — which is roughly what the structural realist and the selective realist want to say?

So: which premise do you reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd honestly rather keep something like (1), I like the reliabilist flavour of it, but I suspect keeping it intact is exactly what generates the self-defeat. Also tell me whether the argument is even valid as I've laid it out — I have a nagging feeling there's a slide between "unreliable" meaning "mostly false outputs" and "gives no rational basis for belief", but I can't pin it down. One more thing: please answer in British English (sceptic, not skeptic, etc.) — I keep getting American spellings back and its doing my head in.
