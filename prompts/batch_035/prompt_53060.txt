Two members of my philosophy of physics reading group got into a long disagreement about quantum immortality. (1) Dan claimed that on the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation, any lethal setup with a nonzero chance of survival — a quantum Russian roulette, say — will branch into worlds where he survives, so some version of him always goes on experiencing things; and since he can never observe his own death, because observing requires being alive, he should expect to survive every such trial. (2) Elena replied that this confuses the mere existence of a surviving branch with what he should expect: if the survival amplitude is one in a million, branching changes nothing about the fact that the overwhelmingly greater part of his future selves die, so his credence in survival should still track the Born-rule weights. (3) Dan answered that expectation is about self-location: the only future selves he could ever find himself as are the surviving ones, so conditional on there being a continuer of him at all, survival is certain — anthropic reasoning guarantees it. (4) Elena said that even granting the self-location point, it buys him almost nothing: the far more probable survivors are the maimed and brain-damaged ones, so the argument at best promises continued consciousness of some degraded kind rather than immortality; and death by aging isn't a single quantum measurement with clean survival branches at all, but decoherence playing out across trillions of microscopic events, leaving no healthy branch worth counting as 'him'. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the four numbered claims, say whether it is right, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
