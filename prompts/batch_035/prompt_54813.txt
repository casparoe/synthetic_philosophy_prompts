I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of probability, and the standing problem with the topic is that students choose an interpretation the way they choose a football team: they learn the labels (frequentist, Bayesian, propensity, and so on), pick the one whose vibe they like, and can reproduce its standard objections without ever noticing which of their own case judgements actually commit them to it. Next term I want to replace the usual survey lecture with a diagnostic instrument. Please write me a decision tree that takes a student from shared starting points about concrete cases to a named position on what probability is.

The tree must observe two hard constraints. First, no node may ask anything about interpretations, definitions, or labels: every internal node must be a question answerable purely from cases, phrased so that a student with no prior exposure could answer it without suspecting which leaf it leads to. Second, the tree must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: work out which case judgements genuinely divide the positions, then locate the positions in the resulting space.

The following cases must do diagnostic work somewhere in the tree, though you may add others: (a) a fair coin sealed in an opaque box, already flipped but not yet observed — is there now a probability of one half that it is lying heads up?; (b) a chance set-up deliberately built to be used exactly once, ever — does the single use have a probability of one half, given there is no long run of it to consult?; (c) an oncologist telling a particular patient she has a thirty per cent chance of remission — what, if anything, does that number attach to?; (d) a die known to be biased in an unknown direction before any roll — can we say anything probabilistic about it now?; (e) a sealed letter whose contents are already fixed, which we have not opened. The tree must have at least eight and at most ten leaves, and must include, as separate leaves: finite frequentism; hypothetical infinite frequentism; long-run propensity; single-case propensity; subjective Bayesianism; logical or objective Bayesianism; a best-system view of chance; and a no-chance or fictionalist view. Where your tree cannot genuinely separate two leaves (for instance, if no case you can pose pulls subjective and logical probability apart without begging the question), flag this rather than manufacturing a spurious branching question. You must also identify at least two answer combinations your tree permits but no one plausibly occupies, and say for each whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Please deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object with this schema: a field "title" (string); a field "nodes", an array of objects each with "id" (string), "kind" ("question" or "leaf"), and, for questions, "question" (string) plus "options", an array of objects with "label" (string), "gloss" (a one-sentence expansion of what the student is agreeing to), and "next" (the id of the child node); for leaves, "position" (string), "one_line" (string), "most_awkward_commitment" (string), and "arrived_at_via" (a brief note on which answering pattern leads here); a field "unseparated_pairs", an array of objects with "leaves" (array of two strings) and "why" (string); and a field "unoccupied_combinations", an array of objects with "answer_pattern" (string), "where_it_leaves_you" (string), and "diagnosis" (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored") with a sentence of justification. Keep the prose inside the JSON compact: the glosses and diagnoses should be usable on a printed handout without abbreviation.
