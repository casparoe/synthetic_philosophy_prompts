I write rapid-response briefs for a state legislative research office. Whenever a bill or a news story leans on a heritability number, a lawmaker's office wants a two-page note within a day saying whether the claim holds up. It's always some version of "the study says trait X is 60% heritable, so this program won't work" or "the gap must be genetic, twin studies show high heritability." I know the textbook caveats — heritability is a property of a population in an environment, it's about variance not individuals, it can go UP when you equalize environments, high within-group heritability says nothing about between-group differences — but under deadline I keep fumbling which caveat actually kills which claim. Can you turn all of this into a decision procedure I can actually run in an afternoon: the questions to ask of any heritability-based claim in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, which misreadings should make me stop and write "the number is being used for something it can't support," and which cases the procedure can't settle because they're live empirical questions no shortcut answers — plus where it's genuinely a values call that should get handed back to the legislator instead of me. Then run it on three claims: (1) "reading ability is around 70% heritable, so universal pre-K spending is a waste," (2) a legislator arguing "height is nearly fully heritable in well-nourished populations, yet the Dutch grew a foot in a century, so heritability estimates must be basically meaningless," and (3) one from our own side that I'd rather were true — "childhood obesity has high heritability, but that just means the environment is switching the genes on, so school food policy is exactly the right lever." Tell me honestly if the procedure kills that one too.
