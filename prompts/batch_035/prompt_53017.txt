I sit on the grants panel of a mid-sized research foundation that awards about thirty early-career fellowships a year. Fourteen of us, two-day panel meetings, and the scoring works like this: we go around the table in seat order, each person says a number out loud before the next person speaks, and then we discuss. Everyone has quietly noticed that whoever sits in the first two seats basically decides the fate of half the applications -- if the first two people say 8, the rest of the room says 7 or 8; if they say 4, the room says 4 or 5. One of our members, a decision theorist, keeps telling us this is a textbook information cascade: early public signals swamp private judgement because nobody wants to be the person who dissents from two confident colleagues on an application only they read carefully. Another member, an economist, pushes back just as hard: he says the cascade literature cuts the other way, since following early speakers can be perfectly rational Bayesian behavior when you suspect they have information you lack -- it's not groupthink, it's learning -- and if we wall ourselves off from each other's views we throw away exactly the information aggregation that makes a panel better than a stack of individual reviewers. So there's no consensus, and the next meeting is in three weeks.

There are two proposals actually on the table and we have to vote. Proposal A: sealed first-pass scores logged on tablets before any discussion, revealed all at once, followed by open discussion as usual. Proposal B: no discussion at all, scores submitted independently, averaged, done -- which would cut our meetings to one day and save real money. A third member has informally floated a variant of A she calls the canary: same sealed scores, but before open discussion one randomly chosen panelist is required to argue the opposite of their own initial score. Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action (A, B, the canary variant, or rejecting everything and keeping the current round-the-table format), give the reasoning in terms the panel would actually engage with, and treat the economist's objection seriously rather than dismissing it -- he's right that there's something genuinely valuable in people hearing each other out, and the memo loses if it pretends cascades are nothing but bias. Also flag whatever risks or failure modes you see with the option you recommend, including how the panel could tell within two funding cycles whether it backfired. Keep it under 900 words and make the bottom line unambiguous so I know how to vote.
