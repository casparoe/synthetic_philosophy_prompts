I'm in a philosophy of probability seminar and for my final project I want to build a "which position on the reference class problem do you actually hold?" quiz for my classmates. Ten-ish questions, each one a concrete case -- e.g. a newlywed asking an actuary for their probability of divorce (young? college-educated? married in this state? where do you stop narrowing?), a prisoner who just arrived and wants to estimate the median sentence at their prison, something doomsday-argument-flavored where the candidate classes include people who haven't been born yet, and maybe a self-locating case like waking up in a hotel room not knowing if the place has 10 rooms or 1000. Each question gets two or three answer options, then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves have to include at least: the "use the narrowest reference class that still has decent statistics" rule, the "there's no fact of the matter and rational credence is a whole range" view, the "the right class is fixed by the purpose of the inquiry" position, the Bayesian "put priors over candidate classes and average" line, and the "this whole mess is a symptom that single-case frequentist probability doesn't exist" take. The quiz has to sort people by what they say about the cases and not by which label they like -- so no question may just ask "do you believe in objective probabilities?" Finally, tell me honestly which pairs of leaves the quiz can't really seperate, and flag any answer combination that dumps you at no leaf: is nobody at that combination because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?
