I help run a decision theory reading group (mostly advanced undergrads, a few hangers-on) and we just did the St. Petersburg paradox. The discussion made it obvious that people who all said "I wouldn't pay much for that lottery" were refusing for totally different reasons, but nobody could articulate the fault lines. So: write me a decision tree handout, one page-ish, that sorts a person from a starting commitment to a named position.

The starting point should be concrete: "Someone offers you a single play of the St. Petersburg lottery for $100" — and let me be able to re-run the same tree at $10,000 and at a million, since price sensitivity is part of the sorting.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Branch nodes should be questions someone can answer without knowing any of the labels: e.g. does the fact that the game's expected value is infinite settle anything about what a rational person should pay; is your utility for money bounded or unbounded; should sufficiently small probabilities be discounted, ignored as "morally impossible," or taken at face value; does the answer change if you get 1000 plays instead of one; does it change if the casino demonstrably can only cover payouts up to some finite bankroll; is an infinite expectation even the kind of thing that can guide a one-shot choice. Don't have any node be a disguised "are you a Bernoullian?" — the whole point is sorting people by what they say about cases.

Leaves that need to be in there: the pay-any-finite-price expected value maximizer; the diminishing marginal utility fix and, crucially, its vulnerability to the modified version where payoffs get inflated so that utility is still unbounded (Petrograd-style); bounded utility views; truncation views in the spirit of Buffon and d'Alembert, where tiny probabilities get rounded to zero; Easwaran-style views on which infinitely many plays converge to a finite per-play average, so single-shot and repeated versions come apart; views that reject the setup outright because no actual casino can honor unbounded payouts or because only finitely many coin flips fit in the physical universe; and a deflationary leaf that treats the whole thing as a theorem about expectations rather than advice.

Two more requirements. First, note unoccupied combinations of answers — say for each whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually seperate (I suspect bounded utility and small-probability discounting come apart only on cases my node questions don't probe — if so, either add a splitting question, like a lottery with a one-in-a-quadrillion shot at a guaranteed $10^15, or admit the tree can't cut there).

Also, at least one node has to use the concrete case where the house's insurer caps the bankroll at $10^12 — I want to see which leaves change their verdict there and which shrug and keep their original answer, since that's where the group argument actually started.

Keep the math light enough for undergrads but dont dumb down the structure — they've all seen expectation calculations before. And phrase everything in second person ("would you pay..."), so I can literally hand it to someone as a quiz.
