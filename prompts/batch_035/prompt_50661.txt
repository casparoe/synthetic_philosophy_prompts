I help run a philosophy of mind reading group that spent this term on the modal epistemology of consciousness: Kripke's treatment of pain and the firing of C-fibers in Naming and Necessity, the zombie argument, and the exchange over whether what we can conceive tells us anything about what is possible. Our final session is next week, and I want to close it with a poem rather than a summary, because the discussion never actually resolved — it just got more precise about where the two sides part ways. I have a formal idea for the poem and I'd like you to execute it.

The conceit is rhyme as identity. The poem should be in rhymed couplets, roughly fourteen to eighteen lines, and it should work through the two kinds of identity claim the term covered. For the identities that science actually discovered and we now accept as necessary a posteriori — water and H2O, heat and molecular motion — give the lines true rhymes, pairs that sound as matched on the page as they are when spoken aloud. These are the successes: the correlation was found, and the rhyme holds in every register. But when the speaker turns to the proposed identity of pain with the firing of C-fibers, the only rhyme on offer should be an eye-rhyme — pairs of the love/prove sort, which look as if they must chime when you see them and then go silent the moment the poem is read aloud. The physical description is complete on paper; the sound doesn't come. And the ending should leave the reader with the question our group ended on: whether that silence is the absence of the phenomenal, or just the limit of what rhyme can be asked to carry. Please don't resolve it — a resolution would betray the term we just had.

Since a poem can smuggle in a verdict under cover of form, please append a short prose note (a paragraph is enough) on what the device presupposes. Specifically: which side of the conceivability debate the eye-rhyme trick tacitly favors, and where it begs the question rather than showing it — a defender of the phenomenal concept strategy would presumably say the rhyme fails only because we lack a completed physical vocabulary, not because something is missing from the world, and I'd like the note to state that objection at its strongest. And then tell me what would lead you to abandon the conceit: what a successful physicalist completion of the poem's rhymes would have to look like, or what argument would show the silence in the pain couplet to be an artifact of English versification rather than a guide to possibility. If you think the device can't be made to do this work at all, say so plainly rather than saving the poem.
