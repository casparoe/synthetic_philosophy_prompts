I serve on the Standing Committee on Strategic Posture of the Federal Republic of Askeland, a mid-sized nuclear power whose doctrine has long relied on deliberate ambiguity about the circumstances in which it would use nuclear weapons first. Our principal rival, the Coalition of Veymara states, has begun fielding missile-defense interceptors that, in a crisis, could plausibly blunt a small retaliatory strike but not a full one, and its forces have repeatedly probed our maritime exclusion zone with conventional incursions that fall well below any plausible nuclear threshold.

A mandated posture review is due before the Defence Minister next month, and three positions have crystallized on the committee. The first faction urges a public declaration of no first use, arguing that ambiguity invites the very probing we are suffering and that our deterrence needs only to punish, never to preempt. The second wants part of the arsenal moved to a hair-trigger posture in which launch is authorized on warning of incoming attack, before impact can be confirmed. The third position comes from a junior analyst and has unsettled the rest of us: she proposes that we openly publish a crisis procedure under which, once the adversary crosses a clearly stated line, the decision to escalate would be partly surrendered to chance — for instance, a randomizing mechanism that would, with some declared probability, delegate launch authority to a council that could not be predicted or lobbied in advance. Her argument is that a threat our adversary can model as the output of our own rational calculation will always be discounted by a rational adversary, whereas a threat genuinely hostage to chance cannot be; she concedes this borrows from Schelling's observation that credibility can come from deliberately giving up control.

Draft the committee's decision memo for circulation before the review. It should recommend one posture (or a defensible hybrid) as our formal advice to the Minister, with whatever concrete qualifications the recommendation needs. In reaching that bottom line, the memo should engage the structural paradoxes honestly rather than gesturing at them: whether a threat it would be irrational to carry out can still deter; whether no-first-use declarations are self-undermining precisely because they can be revised in the crisis they were meant to prevent; whether the rival's lower-level aggression is a predictable consequence of stable deterrence at the top, as the stability-instability paradox would suggest, and what that implies for which posture actually protects us; and whether injecting genuine randomness into launch authority strengthens deterrence or simply converts the probability of accidental catastrophe into an instrument of policy. The memo must also give a fair hearing to the strongest consideration on the side of each option it rejects, stated strongly enough that a proponent of that option would recognize it, and should close with the single most important caveat the Minister should carry into her decision.
