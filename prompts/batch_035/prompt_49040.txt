Each year my graduate seminar in philosophy of biology reaches the species problem, and each year the unit ends in exactly the same place: everyone agrees on the underlying biology, and no one moves an inch. The interbreeding-based concept (Mayr's line) carves out reproductively isolated populations, which works tolerably for birds and hopeless for bacteria, which trade genes laterally with abandon. The phylogenetic concepts carve out the smallest diagnosable clades, which makes species branches of the tree of life but multiplies taxa beyond what most working biologists will use. The morphological concepts work on fossils and museum drawers but float free of any single evolutionary process. And above the fray stand the pluralists, who say this multiplicity is no embarrassment — species are whatever counts as a species relative to a scientific purpose — facing off against the monists, who insist that if there is one tree of life there had better be one right way to cut it. We can spend an afternoon on ring species, asexual lineages, and hybrid swarms, and the positions at the end are the positions at the beginning. A century and a half of this is enough.

My own working hypothesis, which I want you to take seriously but not to defer to, is that this is not a disagreement about organisms at all. It is a disagreement about what taxonomy is for — whether a classification is an inference tool that should be tailored to each research purpose, or a metaphysical map of a real structure in nature that we are discovering rather than deciding. On the first reading the dispute is symptomatic of an unstated question about aims; on the second it is substantive in the way a dispute about whether there are mountains on a planet we are surveying would be substantive, with the pluralists simply changing the subject. But I can also imagine a third possibility: that there is a hidden formal result waiting to be articulated — something like a demonstrated trade-off showing that no single partition can satisfy the desiderata all the concepts partially satisfy, in which case the monist position is not so much false as incoherent.

So here is what I want, and I want it in a form I can hand to the seminar. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal (a fight about the word 'species' dressed up as a fight about nature), substantive (a real question about whether lineages have a joint structure independent of our classificatory purposes), a matter of the two camps answering different questions under one name, or something my three options fail to capture? Support the diagnosis with what the positions actually say and how they behave on the hard cases, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Do not give me a balanced survey of the options followed by an invitation to choose; you take the diagnosis you think correct, defend it, and say plainly where the reasoning is weakest, since I will be arguing back at the seminar. Close with a single line of the form \diagnosis{...}, containing the label you are committing yourself to, so the students can hold you to it.
