I am a high school senior writing a long essay for an interdisciplinary course on medicine and society, and I would like you to respond as an expert in decision theory who also knows a good deal of medical ethics. Please think carefully and take the question seriously rather than giving me a textbook summary; before you write your answer, please brainstorm several candidate explanations and choose among them deliberately, though send me only the finished answer, not the brainstorming. Here is my question, stated as precisely as I can.

Modern medicine treats information about your own future in two strikingly different ways. On one hand, if my grandmother's doctors discover that her cancer is terminal, they are professionally and often legally expected to tell her; concealing a diagnosis is treated as paternalism, and the whole apparatus of informed consent rests on the idea that a patient who knows more is better positioned than one who knows less. On the other hand, for genetic tests that reveal the probability of an incurable late-onset disease, the prevailing norm is nearly the reverse: testing is strictly voluntary, pre-test counseling involves asking whether the person even wants to know, and clinicians openly worry that a positive result could inflict decades of dread about something the patient cannot currently prevent or treat. So the same medical establishment that insists on disclosure in one setting actively counsels hesitation, and sometimes counsels not knowing, in another. My essay is supposed to figure out what these two practices, taken together, presuppose about the value of information.

The reason I think this is a real philosophical puzzle rather than just an inconsistency is a result in decision theory that I have been reading about, which I believe goes back to the statistician I. J. Good: if a piece of information is costless to obtain, and if you will use it sensibly when deciding what to do, then learning it can never make your expected situation worse. The reasoning, as I understand it, is that before you learn anything, you can always plan to ignore the news if ignoring turns out to be the better course; a free option cannot have negative expected value. If that theorem is right, and both my grandmother's diagnosis and a genetic risk score are cases where the information itself is free and no effective treatment exists in either case, then the different norms cannot simply rest on the theorem. Something else must be doing the work.

So here is what I would like from you. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the value of information that the two-practice pattern presupposes, if any single theory can cover both. Say clearly which rival views of information's value each practice quietly rules out. Then distinguish what the pattern strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably: for instance, would it be enough to hold that information is good when it can be acted on and bad when it cannot, or does the terminal-diagnosis practice presuppose something stronger, something about autonomy or dignity that has nothing to do with acting on the news at all? Also tell me where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, meaning where several quite different theories of information's value would each license what doctors actually do, so that the practice alone cannot tell us which theory is right. Finally, explain what would have to change in medical practice if the decision-theoretic view were applied rigorously and literally, and say whether you think the resulting medicine would be better or worse, and why.

Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables of any kind, written the way one would compose a careful letter. Length is up to you, but I would rather have a thorough answer than a short one, and I would rather you admit where the theory runs out than force a tidy conclusion.
