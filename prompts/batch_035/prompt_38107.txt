Got into a long argument with my roommate the other night (fittingly, over cheap wine) that started when she said "in vino veritas" to justify taking a drunk friend's rambling confession as his real opinion — the stuff a person would never say sober is supposedly the true them, and everything polite and considered is the mask. I objected that drunk people say all sorts of garbage they don't believe, and she came back that alcohol doesn't invent anything, it just lowers the guard. And honestly neither of us could pin down what the proverb is even asserting, we just kept talking past each other. Later I half-remembered something from a Plato course that seems relevant: in the Laws there's a stretch where the Athenian stranger treats wine almost like an instrument or a test, the idea being that watching how someone behaves when loosened up at a drinking party reveals their character and tells you something about whether they can be trusted with responsibility. But that seems like a really different sense of "truth" than what my roommate meant — something about conduct and self-control rather than about the words coming out of a drunk person's mouth.

So I want the whole slogan taken apart. What are the distinct precise claims packed into "in vino veritas" — from the trivial reading (people are more talkative when disinhibited) through stronger ones (drunk speech gives privileged access to belief, or to character), including whatever the Plato-style reading amounts to and any deflationary reading where drunken talk is basically noise the proverb gets backwards — and for each, say whether it's plausible, whether it's just false, and whether it would actually support what my roommate used it for, namely treating drunk confessions as *more* probative than sober ones. Give me exactly five readings as a numbered list, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion — I want to be able to read it out at dinner and finally settle which of us the proverb is actually backing.
