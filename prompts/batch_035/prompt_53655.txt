For the past several years I've been part of an informal reading group that grew out of the rationalist and AI-alignment blogosphere, and two of our members have been locked in the same argument for over a year now. One, whom the rest of us think of as the penalty camp, maintains that avoiding negative side effects can be turned into a precise, value-agnostic objective: build the agent's reward function to include a penalty term that measures how much the agent's presence changes the world relative to a counterfactual baseline — the world as it would have gone had the agent done nothing, or never existed — with the change scored by how the agent's actions alter the attainability of a large, diverse family of auxiliary goals, so that the agent is discouraged from foreclosing possibilities or rearranging the world regardless of whether anyone happens to care about the particular change. The other, the specification camp, holds that the whole project is confused at the root: impact is not a physical magnitude like mass or charge. Whether an outcome counts as a side effect rather than the intended effect — indeed, whether a change is bad rather than good — depends on what we value, so any penalty term is either a disguised partial utility function, inheriting all the usual misspecification problems, or else so indiscriminate that it forbids doing anything at all: an agent that treats all change as costly will dither, avoid completing its task, or even offset its own good deeds later in order to shrink its measured distance from the baseline, the way an agent rewarded for watering a plant might later uproot it just to reduce its recorded impact.

Their exchanges have become repetitive, and several of us suspect that part of the disagreement is verbal while part of it is real and important. I'd like to commission the document the two of them should have produced long ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. Please give me (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — about what a baseline-penalty objective actually computes, about the cases where everyone agrees it misbehaves (for instance, that an agent maximizing such a penalty term alone would do nothing, or switch itself off), and about the fact that any choice of auxiliary goals or of baseline counterfactual is itself a design decision with consequences someone has to answer for; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording as fair to it; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a baseline counterfactual is even well-defined, given that inaction itself has consequences and the agent's mere existence already perturbs the world, and another is whether a measure that averages over many auxiliary objectives is genuinely value-neutral or merely averages over the biases baked into the choice of those objectives; and (4) for each crux, a concrete toy case both camps would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. For the toy cases, consider setups like a delivery robot that can reach its destination either by a shortcut that disturbs a laboratory or by a longer route that delays a medical sample, and whether an agent should be permitted to break a cheap object in order to prevent an expensive one from being lost. If you conclude that some part of their dispute is purely verbal — perhaps over what side effect even means — please say which part and why.
