I teach a graduate seminar in population ethics, and this term the recurring flashpoint has been the procreation asymmetry. Let me fix the target precisely, since the literature slides between several formulations. By the wide asymmetry I mean the claim that while there is a moral reason against creating a life not worth living, there is no commensurate moral reason in favor of creating a happy life: a world in which additional worthwhile lives exist is not, in virtue of those lives alone, a better world, other things equal. This is stronger than the narrow asymmetry, on which creating a happy life is merely not good for anyone in particular — no person is better off — while leaving open whether the outcome is impersonally improved. My question concerns the wide version, and I want a credence for it specifically.

I hold this at roughly 0.65; a colleague with whom I run the seminar puts it at 0.25. Give me your own number, but the number is the least of it. Decompose the question into the sub-claims the wide asymmetry depends on, with a probability and a one-line justification for each. At minimum I would expect terms for the following: the probability that some person-affecting restriction on moral claims is correct, such that claims about who exists can only be assessed relative to particular people; the conditional probability that the wide asymmetry holds given such a restriction (the step from not good for anyone to not better simpliciter is where most of the action is, and it is not a trivial step); the probability that the wide asymmetry survives even if impersonal values are admitted — some defenders concede impersonal value in principle while denying that it generates reasons to create; and, as a separate lever, the probability that the best explanation of our reluctance to accept the Sadistic Conclusion — the conclusion that when adding lives would lower the average, it can be better to add lives of negative welfare instead — is itself the wide asymmetry, in which case using that reluctance as evidence for the asymmetry would be circular, and that term should be discounted accordingly. Combine the terms however you judge correct, show the combination, and then tell me two things: which single term you are least confident in and what argument or kind of case would move it most; and where you would locate the disagreement between my 0.65 and my colleague's 0.25 — whether it is most likely a dispute about the person-affecting restriction itself, about the transition from the narrow to the wide reading, or about how much weight the Sadistic Conclusion consideration can carry once the circularity worry is on the table.
