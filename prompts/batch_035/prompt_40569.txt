I've been reading Herodotus for the first time -- a paperback translation someone gave me, a few pages each night before bed -- and what's slowly hooking me isn't the battles or the giant ants that dig up gold, it's the way he tells you where everything comes from. He's forever flagging the channel: this is what the Egyptians say, here's a second and less believable version, the Spartans tell it differently, and every so often he more or less shrugs and says his job is to pass on what he was told, not to vouch for it. I gather the professionals have a whole discipline about this, source criticism, and that Thucydides was the one who started being haughty about it, scorning poets and rumor in favor of eyewitnesses and checking the speeches. My only credentials are a book club and an uncle whose war stories grew a little grander every Thanksgiving, so take my framing for what it's worth.

Something occurred to me that I'd like a poem to carry, because I can't get it to come out right in prose. The thought is that every retelling of an event loses something and adds something at the same time -- numbers get rounder, hedges harden into facts, someone's name becomes a role, a motive quietly gets invented -- and you can't get back to the original by subtraction, because the original wasn't pure truth either; it was just the first version, already a story told by someone with a stake in it. I want the poem to enact that transmission rather than merely describe it, and I want it to stay fair: the distortions are real losses, but without them nothing would have survived at all.

Here's the shape I have in mind, though adjust it if you see something better. Four stanzas of six or seven lines each, and every stanza tells the same small event -- let's say: a young soldier drowned when a ferry sank in a storm crossing a river, and his commander afterward gave his own cloak to the soldier's brother. The first stanza should be plain and a little hesitant, the voice of an eyewitness who admits there's a part of the story he didn't see clearly. Each later stanza is a retelling down the chain: same event, but shorter, with the uncertain detail hardened into certainty, what was once a handful of passengers becoming forty, the cloak perhaps turning into a sword or a strip of land, and the tone growing more confident and more moralized as the facts get worse -- by the end the commander should be either a saint or a villain, your call. In the final stanza the teller should be absolutely sure and demonstrably wrong about one specific thing, and shouldn't know it. If you can manage two closing lines in a different voice -- the historian, centuries later, weighing the versions against each other -- I'd like them not to sneer at the tradition but not to vouch for it either, since that's honestly where I've ended up after a year of Herodotus at bedtime: I don't trust any single telling, but without the retellings I would know nothing at all.
