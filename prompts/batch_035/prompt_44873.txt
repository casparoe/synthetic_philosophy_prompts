Quick background, then the ask. I'm a recently retired physicist -- condensed matter, thirty-some years, mostly superconductors and dirty interfaces, the kind of career where you use quantum mechanics every day and never once ask what it means. Since retiring I've gotten properly into philosophy of physics, and I've gone from "shut up and calculate" to being that annoying guy at dinner parties about Bell. I've been asked to give a talk to a philosophy-of-science reading group (about 15 people) at the university near where I now live, and I want you to prep me for the Q&A.

My thesis, roughly: the popular framing of Bell's theorem as "you must give up either locality or realism" is a fudge. Once you state Bell's assumption the way Bell himself eventually did -- as local causality, factorization of probabilities -- the "realism" horn of the dilemma collapses into the locality horn, and what's left is a genuine no-go theorem against local causality, full stop. I want to defend that, and also push the more contentious line that Many-Worlds doesn't dodge the conclusion the way its fans claim: yes, there's no collapse, but "the wavefunction evolves locally" is doing a lot of unearned work there, since the locality we lost in Bell is a locality about the consequences of distant settings, and a decohered world where my distant colleague's outcome is not a fact until it branches is not obviously local in that sense.

The audience: mostly general philosophers of science, sharp but not specialists. Two committed Everettians, one of whom knows the decision-theoretic Born rule literature inside out. One guy who I suspect is quietly Bohmian. A postdoc who works on quantum foundations and will know all the jarrett-style decompositions of the Bell assumption into parameter independence and outcome independence, and the Fine-style theorems about what the inequalities actually follow from. Possibly a superdeterminism-curious contrarian. No dummies, but also nobody who needs the derivation shown to them twice.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of them would actually phrase it -- the Everettian's version of "MWI is local in the only sense that matters" will not sound like the contrarian's "you just assumed the settings were free." For each: how much damage it does, a two-minute reply I could give out loud, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Sort them by how badly they'd hurt me if I fumble them, not by how famous they are. Include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.

One more thing, and I mean this: I'm new enough to this field that I can't always tell settled ground from a live fight, and I do not want to confidently repeat somebody's contested line as if it were consensus from a podium -- I gather even the question of what exactly Bell's theorem assumes is itself disputed, and I've already been burned once at dinner arguing about superdeterminism like it was obviously crazy when apparently that's not obvious to people who know more than me. So as you write the prep sheet, flag clearly which parts of my position (and which parts of each reply) are genuinely contested among people who know this stuff, versus which parts are just math and nobody serious doubts them. Where something is a live fight, tell me what the main camps actually are so I can hedge accurately instead of accidentally picking a side I didn't mean to pick. I'm seventy-one, I have nothing to prove, but I'd also rather not be politely taken apart by a postdoc.
