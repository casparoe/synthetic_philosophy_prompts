I'm taking a course on American philosophy this term and we've just spent two weeks on Peirce versus James on truth, and at the same time I've been going down a rabbit hole about the cold fusion fiasco, and I keep thinking they'd make a perfect case study for each other. Quick refresher on the episode so we're on the same page: March 1989, Fleischmann and Pons hold a press conference at the University of Utah announcing excess heat from palladium electrodes in heavy water -- announced before peer review, with patents pending, the Utah legislature putting up millions, a worldwide scramble to replicate, most attempts coming up with nothing, and by the end of the year a DOE panel bascially kills it. And yet a small community of researchers has kept at it for decades, still publishing, still convinced. So here's my ask: analyse this whole episode as a case study in the pragmatist theories of truth, treating Peirce and James as genuinely different theories rather than one happy family.

For Peirce, truth is what the community of inquirers is fated to converge on in the long run -- the opinion that investigation, indefinitely prolonged, would ultimately settle into. So was it already true, in Peirce's sense, that cold fusion was bogus, back in April 1989 when the physics was still in play? Or take the awkward bit: the community of investigators never merged back into one -- a mainstream that closed the case and a remnant that never did -- so what happens to the long-run picture when the run goes on and convergence just doesn't arrive? Does the persistent believer community count as inquiry at all, or did they stop investigating somewhere along the way? Then run it through James, who literally asks what he calls the truth's cash-value in experiential terms and says that true ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify. For Fleischmann and Pons, belief in cold fusion had enormous cash value for a while -- funding, fame, a shot at patents -- while being verifiable by nobody else. Does that show James's account licenses exactly the pathology we watched unfold, or does James have the resources to say the belief was false all along precisely because it stopped leading anyone prosperously through experience?

And then run it back the other way: what does the episode do to each theory? Does cold fusion flatter one of them, embarrass one, or expose a kind of case that neither of them anticipated -- something like a question where the short run is all there is, or where the practical effects of a belief and its correspondence to reality come apart for decades? I don't want the regurgitated spiel about how pragmatism means whatever works for you; I want a careful mapping of what each man's theory actually commits you to in this concrete situation, and honesty about where each theory goes quiet rather than forcing it to speak.
