I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and the running theme of my thesis is time. Over the course of writing my first chapter I've drifted into a fairly confident eternalism: I take the Rietdijk–Putnam style argument from special relativity seriously, I don't believe in a metaphysically privileged present, and I'm broadly comfortable saying that what we call "the flow of time" is a feature of our representation rather than of the world. My supervisor is a presentist who thinks I've moved too fast, and a friend in the linguistics department keeps pressing me with the claim that the tense apparatus of natural language encodes a real asymmetry between past, present and future which the block universe can only accommodate error-theoretically. I want to know what would actually count against my view, not just what critics have said about it.

So please give me a map of eternalism's exposure, as it were. Which observations, results or arguments would be decisive, and which merely awkward? I'm particularly interested in whether I should be worried by possibilities on the physics side -- say, a mature theory of quantum gravity that restored an objective foliation, or the kind of trouble for temporal ordering that arises when causal structure becomes ill-behaved at the Planck scale -- and in whether there are purely philosophical defeaters: consequences the block universe is committed to that might turn out unacceptable, cases it struggles to describe, or results suggesting it quietly collapses into a view that is indistinguishable from its rivals (my supervisor claims that eternalism's treatment of passage as indexical leaves it with no explanation of why experience has the phenomenal character of flowing, only a diagnosis of why we mistakenly think it does, and that this is a cost rather than a bargain). It would help me to know which of these defeaters a committed eternalist would concede as genuine exposure and which they would dismiss as a confusion -- and crucially whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and if so whether that matters for a metaphysical claim of this kind.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, and be honest about where my position is well insulated and whether that insulation is a vice. At the end, tell me what would change your own verdict: which argument or consideration, if it went the other way or if some development actually materialised, should lead a rational eternalist to abandon the block, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. Please write in British English, and don't confine yourself to the objections I've listed -- if there is an exposure route I haven't mentioned, I want to hear about it.
