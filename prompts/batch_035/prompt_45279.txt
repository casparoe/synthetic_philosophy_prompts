I teach philosophy of probability and our little reading group has been stuck on Bertrand's paradox for what feels like forever. Half the group thinks the chord case is a clean kill of the principle of indifference: the same problem, three descriptions, three different probabilities, so "equipossible alternatives" can't do the work it was supposed to. The other half is basically on team Jaynes: the paradox just shows indifference needs an invariance constraint -- your assignment shouldn't change under the symmetries of the problem -- and once you demand that you get a unique answer, plus supposedly the rotation-invariant one even matches the frequency you get from physically throwing straws at a circle. Then there's my colleague who keeps saying everyone is just smuggling in a description of the physical randomizing mechanism and calling it a prior.

Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the same thing. One is answering a question about what a rational agent's credence should be before they know anything, the other a question about what a well-posed problem even is, and neither notices because both use the word "probability." Is that diagnosis right, or is there a real substantive disagreement hiding in there -- maybe about whether invariance is doing any normative work or is just another way of picking your favorite parameter? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, and what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a proper account of the straw-throwing experiments, or an argument that this one should just stay unresolved and why. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the three solutions for the umpteenth time.
