I chair the publication review committee at a mid-sized AI research organization, and I want to stop making these decisions the way we currently do, which is badly. A colleague with a decision theory background diagnosed our recurring failure as an instance of what she called the unilateralist's curse, and while I think she's right, the diagnosis hasn't so far helped me run a meeting. The setup, as she explained it: when several people each independently judge whether some action does more good than harm, and the action goes ahead if even one person judges positively, then even if the group's average judgment is clearly negative, the probability that at least one person's estimate lands on the positive side grows with the number of judges — so the action happens almost no matter what the group collectively believes. That is more or less our situation. We are a committee of six with mixed expertise (two safety researchers, a lawyer, a communications lead, me, and a rotating engineer), we handle roughly one release decision a month, and our de facto rule is that anything goes out unless someone is willing to make a scene about it. The predicted result: releases go out that a straw poll would have rejected, because the person who was least worried just proceeded while everyone else was planning to raise concerns at the meeting that got canceled. The theory says the fix is some principle of conformity — that each member should defer to the group in some way — but that's where the theory ends and my job begins, because "defer to the group" is not a procedure.

What I need is a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a concrete release question within our two-week turnaround, with no dedicated staff and no ability to require quantitative estimates people can't honestly produce. Please specify: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a no without further debate; and where it correctly hands the question to a human judgment call that no rule can absorb — I'd rather know where the irreducible judgment lives than pretend the procedure covers it. Two complications the procedure must handle rather than ignore. First, the curse assumes each member's error is independent noise, but our members aren't coins: our safety researchers are professionally risk-averse and the rotating engineer's view depends on who built the thing. So the procedure needs some way to distinguish a dissent that is information (an independent estimate landing negative) from a dissent that is a known bias, without just letting me discount whoever I find tiresome. Second, the mirror-image failure is real: a naive anti-unilateralist procedure that lets any single member block everything will strangle releases that a straw poll would have approved, and that is also a form of the committee not doing its job. If the procedure involves something like a vote threshold or a requirement to state reasons before dissenting, say why that threshold and not a lower or higher one.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our last year, and include at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, since I need to know that before I adopt it. Case one: a large dataset scraped from public web pages, containing personal information people posted casually years ago, which a partner lab wants for training and which two committee members say is fine because it's public. Case two: a paper demonstrating a new and effective prompt-based jailbreak against current models, where the authors argue publication creates pressure for fixes faster than it spreads misuse. Case three: the borderline one — internal evaluation results showing a capability jump that we would normally keep private, where the communications lead wants to publish a summary for reputational reasons and everyone else is only mildly against it, exactly the low-drama case where our current process let things slip through. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: the situations where conformity gets you the wrong answer on purpose, such as when one member actually has private information rather than a noisy estimate, and how much of the cure's cost — delay, friction, releases that die in committee — I should be signing up for.
