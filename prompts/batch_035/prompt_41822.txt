I've been cataloguing my gut verdicts about when two populations count as the same species, and I've come to suspect they don't add up to any one principle — but before I resign myself to pluralism, I want someone to tell me precisely what they do add up to, and which of them has to go. Here is the list.

First, polar bears and brown bears: they interbreed where their ranges meet, the hybrids are fertile, and there has been real gene flow between them in both directions over evolutionary time. Yet I am entirely confident they are two species, and I don't feel any pressure at all from the interbreeding facts. Second, dogs and wolves: same species, obviously, despite a phenotypic gap larger than the one separating many uncontroversial species pairs. Third, coyotes and wolves: two species, even though hybrids are fertile and increasingly common in the wild — so mere fertile interbreeding does nothing for coyotes that it doesn't do for polar bears, and yet I count the bears as split and the dog-wolf pair as lumped, with no obvious rationale for the asymmetry.

Fourth, the Ensatina salamanders ringing the Central Valley of California: each population interbreeds freely with its neighbors going around the ring, but where the ring closes in the south, the two end forms meet and do not interbreed. My gut verdict is that the two end forms are different species and that each is the same species as its neighbors on either side around the ring. I recognize this is intransitive and therefore cannot be delivered by anything that behaves like identity, but it remains my verdict, and I'd like to know whether any concept of species — as opposed to a mere relation — can honor it.

Fifth, bdelloid rotifers: a lineage that appears to have gone without sex for tens of millions of years. Nonetheless I think it is right, and not merely a bookkeeping convenience, to speak of many distinct bdelloid species. Sixth, the sunflower Helianthus anomalus, which arose historically as a hybrid between H. annuus and H. petiolaris: I count it as a genuine third species, distinct from both of its parents, even though its existence is a standing counterexample to any rule on which species are groups between which breeding doesn't happen. Seventh, sibling species of Drosophila that cannot be told apart by any morphological character yet never interbreed: different species, no hesitation.

My own tentative principle was something like "a species is a population or set of populations with a distinct evolutionary role and trajectory, with interbreeding as the default evidence but overridable by ecology and morphology." I can already see that this is less a principle than a pile of escape hatches, and I would rather have it tested and repaired than admired.

So, please: state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precisely enough that it yields determinate verdicts; say which clause is doing the work in each of my cases; and tell me what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have deliberately left off the list — one, two populations of fish separated by a newly formed canyon for a few thousand years, with no evolved reproductive isolation, that would interbreed freely if restored to contact; and two, bacterial lineages that exchange genes laterally at high rates without any reproduction at all. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your preferred account cannot save, and whether the cost of saving it by complicating the account is worth paying.
