Please argue that Hofstadter-style superrationality can be extended coherently from the fully symmetric prisoner's dilemma to populations in which only some fraction of the players are superrational reasoners, and that the resulting view still prescribes cooperation whenever that fraction exceeds a determinable threshold — the players knowing the fraction but not each other's identities. Your argument should confront the standard objection head-on: superrationality is normally motivated by an identity or perfect-symmetry assumption — the other player is, in effect, running a copy of your reasoning — and once the opponent pool includes non-superrational defectors, that motivation seems to collapse, since the average defector is not a copy of you and your deliberation seems to give you no evidence about their move. Explain what, if anything, replaces the symmetry assumption: e.g., whether the prescription should be derived as a conditional policy (cooperate just in case the fraction of superrational cooperators, discounted by the payoff structure, clears a threshold), rather than as a bare appeal to a shared decision rule that many players simply don't share. You may grant that the perfectly symmetric case remains the motivating special case; the burden of the argument is to show precisely what survives — and what must be re-derived — when symmetry is weakened to partial overlap in reasoning dispositions.
