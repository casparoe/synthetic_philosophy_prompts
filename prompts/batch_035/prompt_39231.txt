I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk of a weekly magazine, and alongside a feature I'm writing on brain size and moral status — covering the old craniometric literature, the awkward fact that dolphin and whale brains are larger than ours while ours appear to have shrunk over the past few tens of thousands of years, the brain-size reduction from wolf to dog under domestication, and the modern habit of gauging an animal's moral weight by counting cortical neurons — we want to run one of our interactive reader quizzes, the kind where you answer questions about concrete cases and are told at the end what you actually believe. The quiz should sort readers on where they stand, not on which label they like: the live questions are whether raw cognitive capacity or brain scale matters morally in itself or only insofar as it subserves sentience; whether moral status comes in continuously with capacity or jumps at a threshold; whether what matters is a property of the individual or species membership and typical case; and whether relations and recognisability to us do any independent work. So the questions must be cases, not self-descriptions: I want you to use at least the following among your eight to ten items — a person with severe microcephaly who functions ordinarily; a patient who has had a hemisphere removed in adulthood; a hypothetical gene therapy that doubles a chimpanzee's cortical neuron count with no measurable behavioural change; a corvid with a walnut-sized brain solving a novel planning task; a hypothetical artifact with a human-scale brain-affecting substrate but no evidence of feeling anything; and a line of selectively bred pigs with substantially enlarged brains and apparently unchanged temperaments. Two or three answer options per question, each phrased as a plain verdict on the case, never as the name of a doctrine. No question should be answerable by consulting the reader's preferred theory; the point is to catch people whose stated theory and case judgements come apart.

Build the scoring key from the logical space first: define your axes, work out which combinations of answers the questions permit, and only then locate the named positions in that space — capacity-scaling views on which status rises with brainpower; threshold views on which a floor of sentience or personhood carries everything above it; species-egalitarian views on which all humans matter equally regardless of properties; relational or recognition-based views; and the deflationary option for readers who reject the whole framing. Where a combination is unoccupied, say in one sentence whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and where two of your leaves cannot be separated by any case you've written, flag it rather than pretending the quiz distinguishes them.

Please return the whole thing as structured data in British English: a JSON object with fields `axes` (array of objects, each with `name` and `description`), `questions` (array of objects, each with `id`, `case`, `options` as an array of objects with `label` and `axis_scores` showing how that option moves each axis), and `scoring_key` (array of objects with `position_name`, `answer_pattern`, `one_line_description`, and `occupancy` taking the values occupied, unstable, or unoccupied with the one-sentence diagnosis), plus a final field `confidence_note` in which you state how confident you are in each mapping and single out the one case or mapping you are least sure genuinely discriminates between the positions it is meant to separate — I need to know where the quiz will mis-sort readers before our developers build it.
