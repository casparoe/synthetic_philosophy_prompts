Last night a friend and I had a three-hour argument that began with philosophical zombies and ended, oddly, in the seventeenth century. I'm a software engineer with a couple of philosophy electives behind me; she's a first-year PhD student in philosophy. She claimed that if a zombie world is coherently conceivable, that's evidence it's possible, and I pushed back the way I'd been taught: before chemistry, it seemed perfectly conceivable that water might turn out to be something other than H2O, yet nothing like that is possible, so conceivability seems like a broken compass. Rather than answering me on modern terms, she said my objection was already anticipated and answered by Descartes in the Fifth Meditation, and that if I wanted to meet her argument at full strength I had to read the bit about triangles first. I did, and now I'm stuck in a way I'd like help with.

The passage, as I understand it, goes roughly like this. Descartes notices that the ideas of things he can invent at will — a winged horse, a siren — feel voluntary and revisable: he can tack on or strip off features without any resistance from the idea itself. But when he considers a triangle, even if he grants that no triangle has ever existed anywhere outside his thought, the idea behaves differently: its three angles must equal two right angles, its sides satisfy certain relations, and he finds himself unable to think it otherwise, however much he tries to pretend. He calls such ideas true and immutable natures and takes them to be things rather than mere fictions — so that, for instance, various properties of the triangle are not invented by him but, in his words as my friend paraphrased them to me, discovered in it, in something like the way an unfamiliar figure drawn in a design hidden in a piece of marble is discovered rather than created by the sculptor. What I cannot figure out is how these pieces are supposed to fit together: how can a nature be immutable if it exists only in my mind; why the point about discovering rather than inventing properties doesn't quietly smuggle in the existence of triangles in some realm; and above all, why any of this licenses an inference from what I cannot conceive otherwise to what could not have been otherwise.

What I'd like is a careful, patient walkthrough of this stretch of the Fifth Meditation and the reasoning behind it: the contrast between fictions and true and immutable natures, why Descartes is careful to say that thinking of the triangle truly or clearly need not be thinking that it exists, and how the passage is supposed to do epistemological work rather than merely describe a psychological difference between kinds of imagining. Then, since my friend's real point was about our zombie argument, I'd want you to address whether her retro framing is fair: is the water case — where pre-chemical thinkers seemed to coherently conceive something that turned out impossible — supposed to be analogous to the siren rather than the triangle, with the conceiving being somehow defective or merely apparent, and if so, what would decide in advance which sort of conceiving a given case involves, rather than deciding only after the chemistry is in? Please explain in plain but precise prose, working from the argument itself rather than from labels, and without pointing me to further reading — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.
