so last summer my family went to Japan and before we left my mom made us all sit through this whole lecture about local customs — don't tip (in japan tipping can straight up be seen as an insult, which is the exact opposite of the US where not tipping makes you basically a thief, since waiters get paid like $3 an hour on the assumption everyone tips), don't eat while walking, stand on one side of the escalator, don't jaywalk even if the street is totally empty at 2am. and it's not just my mom, every travel guide and website says the same thing: when in rome, do as the romans do. and they don't say it like a tip for blending in, they say it like you're a bad person if you don't. like jaywalking in germany is treated as some moral failure even though it's completely normal in a lot of other countries, and cutting a queue in the UK apparently makes people actually hate you.

here's the part that confuses me. in my social studies class we touched on this idea that conventions are basically solutions to coordination problems — like which side of the road to drive on doesn't matter at all, what matters is that everyone expects everyone else to keep doing it, and that's what keeps it stable. nobody ever signed anything and the convention doesn't care whether you personally endorse it. so what is the whole travel-advice industry quietly presupposing about where the obligation comes from? that conventions become morally binding because people rely on them (the waiter's entire wage system is built around the tipping convention, so defecting really does hurt someone)? that you owe the locals some kind of respect by not wrecking their expectations? that it's just fairness — you happily enjoy the smooth queue system when it benefits you, so you can't defect the one time it costs you something? or is it all just about avoiding weird looks and nothing more?

and is the advice even internally consistent? because the same people who say follow the customs will also tell you you're NOT obligated to follow customs that are actually wrong — like nobody today would tell a visitor to the 1950s american south that they should comply with segregation norms out of respect for local expectations, that would be horrifying. so somewhere there's a line between conventions you must follow as a visitor and conventions you're allowed to blow off, and I can't tell whether the underlying view actually generates that line or whether it's just vibes. so basically: reconstruct the implicit theory of what makes an everyday convention a moral obligation for a visitor, tell me which parts of the practice are actually forced by that theory and which parts would still make sense under a bunch of rival theories, and then tell me what the guidebooks would have to say if we swapped the presupposed view for a strict you're-only-bound-by-what-you-agreed-to view. would the advice collapse into pure self-interest (avoid getting yelled at), or would something still be left over? sorry this is long, I got into an argument with my friend about the tipping thing on the plane home and it's been bugging me since
