I am currently taking a philosophy of law course in which we have just finished the Hart–Devlin exchange on legal moralism, and almost every seminar discussion ended up turning on a single analogy. Our professor presented Devlin's position roughly as follows: a society's shared morality holds it together the way threads hold a fabric together. The norms against cruelty, dishonesty, and sexual immorality are the individual threads; a society that tolerates visible departures from any of them weakens the neighboring strands, and if enough threads go, the whole textile comes apart. On this picture, the criminal law may legitimately be used to shore up the moral fabric, much as it may be used to defend a society's government against subversion. Our professor then suggested, provocatively, that the analogy is doing nearly all of the work in Devlin's move from the premise that societies need a shared morality to the conclusion that the state may punish private immorality.

I would like to go further than we managed in seminar, and I also want to test the analogy against a contemporary use of it. At a guest talk last week, a speaker applied essentially the same fabric reasoning to platform governance: a community's standards are the threads, quiet violations still fray the surrounding weave, and therefore strict moderation is justified in the same way Devlin thought criminal enforcement was. I came away suspecting that in both settings the analogy carries more weight than it can bear, but I cannot tell which of its failures actually matter and which are merely picturesque.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Specifically:

1. Give the correspondence table for Devlin's own use: what plays the role of the fabric, of individual threads, of the unraveling, and of the needlework (i.e., legal enforcement)? Make explicit any elements of the source that are quietly doing argumentative work without appearing in the table.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks. I know Hart objected that Devlin slid from the claim that societies need a shared morality to the claim that they need a monolithic and unchanging one, and I gather Hart also thought something had gone wrong in treating moral revulsion as if it were the kind of thing a society could disintegrate over. But I am unclear on which disanalogies between a textile and a moral community genuinely threaten the enforcement conclusion and which ones, however obvious, leave it untouched.

3. Say whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support — that the state may criminalize private immorality — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether Devlin's position was carrying the analogy rather than the other way around. If it does not survive in full, does any restricted version of it survive, and at what cost?

4. Finally, tell me whether your verdict transfers to the content-moderation case. One wrinkle I keep circling: on a real textile, structural integrity plausibly requires that all threads be load-bearing and alike, but some moral communities seem more like woven rope, which holds precisely because its strands are under tension and not uniform. If that rival analogy is better, does it support lax moderation, strict moderation, or neither? And is there a third comparison that outperforms both?

Please order the disanalogies by how much they actually bite, rather than listing every difference between textiles and societies, and be explicit about what each failure of the analogy would and would not show.
