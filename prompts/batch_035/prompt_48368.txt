My housemate Priya (final-year neuroscience) and her boyfriend Dan (philosophy MA) had a massive row in our kitchen on Sunday, and I got the whole thing because I was stuck making tea the entire time. They'd been to a talk that morning on methods in consciousness science. The speaker ran the old colour phi demo — two spots flashed up on a screen in succession, first red then green, and when the timing's right most people report seeing one single spot move across and change colour somewhere in the middle. A volunteer from the audience insisted, under gentle questioning, that she really did see it turn orange-ish about halfway along, and that she'd happily say so under oath.

The row went like this (I wrote it down after, because they both want me to say who won, and I couldn't):

(1) Dan said the demo shows the method is broken: if the science of consciousness is meant to take first-person reports seriously, then the volunteer's report settles it — she had an experience of the colour changing at a particular mid-point, and a science that won't grant that isn't studying consciousness at all, it's studying verbal behaviour.

(2) Priya said he'd missed the whole point of the method: you grant the subject everything she's inclined to report — in her world, as told by her, the dot really did turn orange halfway across — but you grant it as her story, which is the maximal way of taking her seriously, not the minimal. Nothing in the method says her story is false, she said; it says the story, not her brain, is the thing you've been handed.

(3) Dan said that was sleight of hand: taking her report maximally seriously as a fiction is not taking it seriously, it's filing it under literature. If experience is real, there's a fact about whether the change was really experienced at that mid-point, and her report is our best evidence about that fact — better than anything Priya's instruments will give us for decades.

(4) Priya said the fact Dan wants may not exist: if the brain writes the narrative after the fact, there's often no answer to 'when did she experience it?', and insisting there must be one is demanding a Cartesian theatre — a place where the show gets re-run for an inner audience and timed with a stopwatch.

(5) Dan said that concession was fatal to her own position: if there's sometimes no fact about what she experienced, then granting her 'her story' grants her nothing, and the method quietly turns into behaviourism with nicer manners — it explains experience away by never having to look it in the eye.

(6) Priya's reply, which I honestly didn't follow, was that the alternative is worse: every other science gets by on third-person data, and consciousness science is only odd because it says this out loud; and anyway, if Dan got his timed inner show, he'd then need someone inside watching it, and that someone's experience would need another show, and so on all the way down.

They've now agreed to let me arbitrate, which is rediculous, because i do history of art. Can you go through the six moves one at a time and say who's right at each — and then, separately, tell me whether Priya's method really is the respectful option, as she kept claiming, or whether it's just a polite way of patting the volunteer on the head?
