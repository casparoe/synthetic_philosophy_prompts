I teach a mid-level seminar in philosophy of medicine, and I want a fresh thought experiment for our unit on the concept of disease — one that will not collapse into the stock examples (deafness, ADHD, obesity, menopause) that the students have already seen argued to death.

Construct a case in which Boorse's biostatistical theory and a straightforward normativist account of disease deliver opposite verdicts about whether a patient has a condition that is a disease, and in the harder of the two directions: the condition should be a genuine biological dysfunction by Boorse's own lights, yet not bad for the person in any way a normativist can credit, so that the naturalist must affirm disease and the normativist must deny it — and the normativist's denial should strike a thoughtful reader as the better verdict rather than as an embarrassing cost of the theory.

There is a further demand. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis should be caught in a genuine bind by your case rather than settling it cleanly: the dysfunction should be uncontroversial, but whether the condition is harmful should turn on exactly the sort of contestable value judgment the analysis was supposed to quarantine from the science.

Constraints on the scenario itself: it must be realistic — an ordinary primary-care or occupational-medicine setting of a kind a physician would actually encounter (the sort of thing that surfaces on incidental imaging or routine screening), not an exotic syndrome, and no science-fictional body modification. Keep it to a single paragraph, and do not stipulate the verdicts: the physiology and the patient's circumstances must generate them. Something practical should ride on the classification — coverage, certification, an offer of treatment — so that the question is not idle.

After the scenario, I want four things. First, state the verdict each of the three positions delivers and the reasoning behind each. Second, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work in driving the naturalist and the normativist apart. Third, run the two mirror versions: one in which a mild but genuine harm is added to the condition, and one in which the dysfunction is removed while the distress or disadvantage remains, and say which of the two mirror cases each camp should find more embarrassing and why. Fourth, give the strongest available reply on behalf of whichever theory comes out worst in your original case, and assess whether that reply preserves the verdict or quietly changes the subject.

Please write your response in American English.
