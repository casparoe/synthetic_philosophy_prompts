I've been following the paradox of fiction debate for a while and I'm struck by how stuck it is. So we cry at Anna Karenina, get scared at horror movies, hope the detective figures it out — but we don't believe any of it is real, and (on most views of emotion) fear and pity require belief. Walton's answer is that these are quasi-emotions: I'm not afraid of the slime creature, I make-believe that I am, and what's real is a genuine feeling that gets recruited into the game. The thought theorists say no, it's just plain fear, caused by entertaining thoughts about what's fictionally happening rather than by belief. And the illusionists say we do kind of half-believe the fiction while absorbed in it. All the moves have been on the table since the 70s-80s, everyone concedes the intuitions on both sides, and yet basically nobody switches camps. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about fiction at all — it's a proxy war over what an emotion is, i.e. whether belief is constitutive of fear or just one causal route into it — so the two sides are answering different questions under the heading "do we really fear fictional things?" Am I right? Or is this a substantive empirical-ish dispute about what actually happens in readers' heads that just hasn't been settled yet, or maybe a verbal dispute about the word "real" in "real fear"?
