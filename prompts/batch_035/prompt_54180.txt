I'm a graduate student in cognitive science, and a large part of my work is running few-shot evaluations of vision-language models using Bongard problems — the puzzle format invented by the Soviet computer scientist Mikhail Bongard and later popularized by Douglas Hofstadter. In case the format is unfamiliar: each problem is a card carrying six figures on the left and six on the right; the six on the left all share some property that none of the six on the right has, and the solver's task is to name that property. One card might show a single large shape on each left figure and clusters of small shapes on each right figure, so the intended rule is roughly one-thing-versus-several. What strikes me, and what my thesis depends on, is that human solvers and the models I test almost always converge on the intended rule rather than on any of the endless alternatives that also fit the card.

A philosophy postdoc I drink coffee with thinks this convergence is a mirage, and she has an argument I cannot refute but cannot believe. (1) An answer to a Bongard problem earns its correctness only through the twelve figures on the card; the solver has nothing else to go on. (2) For any rule that correctly sorts the twelve figures, there is a rival rule that sorts them identically but sorts every figure not on the card differently — for instance, the rule that classifies a figure as left-type just in case it is one of those very six figures, or a duplicate of one of them; this fits the card perfectly (the right six are not among the left six) and disagrees with the intended rule about nearly every figure anywhere else. Or again: a lookup table recording my arbitrary verdicts for every figure in some large finite atlas of figures. So the twelve figures are compatible with endlessly many mutually incompatible rules. (3) Rules that agree on all the available evidence and disagree elsewhere cannot be discriminated by that evidence. (4) The solver has no further resources — no more figures, no access to the author's intentions — so nothing in the problem favors one rival over another. (5) A choice favored by nothing in the problem is arbitrary, and an arbitrary answer is at best lucky. (6) Nothing in the argument used anything special about Bongard cards, only that the examples are finite; so the same holds whenever anyone learns a concept from examples. The child does not learn triangle from examples of triangles, and the model does not learn one-versus-several from six figures — at most each memorizes and is lucky when the world happens to cooperate. Her conclusion: nobody ever learns a concept from examples, and grading Bongard answers, telling a student she got it, or reporting that a model induced a concept is a fiction we maintain only because the luck usually holds.

If she is right, my evaluation regime measures memorization and luck, and so does all of language learning. I do not believe the conclusion, but every premise looked unassailable as she gave it. Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, including why the step looked fine. My own suspicion is that something slips between (3) and (4) — she moves from the examples do not settle it to nothing settles it, as if the only guidance available had to come from the card itself — but when I pushed, she replied that anything I appeal to instead, such as simplicity or naturalness or the rules humans happen to like, is either fashion or a fact about solvers rather than about the problem, and that a correctness which depends on the solver's tastes is not correctness. I had no comeback. So also tell me how to answer that objection, and what an adequate diagnosis of her argument should say about the datum I began with: humans and models, with very different histories, landing on the same rules and classifying novel figures with high agreement. And if you think the argument is valid and I should simply accept her conclusion, say so plainly, and explain what that view makes of that agreement.
