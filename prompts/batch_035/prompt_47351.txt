I've been collecting my gut verdicts on the classic decision theory cases and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them, because they feel inconsistent. In Newcomb I one-box. In transparent Newcomb, where I can see the box is full, I still take just the one box — though if I saw it was empty I'd obviously take both. Counterfactual mugging: I go back and forth, but when I imagine having decided in advance, I want to be the kind of person who pays the $100 after the coin comes up tails. XOR blackmail: I refuse to pay, even though I know refusing does nothing for me now and the payer-version of me would never have gotten the letter in the first place. The obvious tension: refusing the blackmailer and paying the mugger look structurally the same — in both cases paying is worthless in the actual world but matters in counterfactual ones. And there's a hybrid case I genuinely can't place: Omega flips a coin, and if it had come up tails, Omega would have blackmailed me — threatened to expose something embarrassing unless I paid $1000 — but since it came up heads, it just asks for $100, and it only shows up at all because it predicted I'd pay. Is there one principle that gets all my verdicts, or do I have to give some up? If so, which ones? And what does the surviving principle say about the hybrid — is it a mugging I should pay or a blackmail I should refuse?
