I'm taking a philosophy of psychiatry course, and we've just finished Hacking's ideas about looping effects and interactive kinds, which are supposed to be the lens for the whole seminar. I keep tripping over one feature of the DSM's revision process that seems to pull in two directions at once, and I'd like help working out what theory of psychiatric categories that process actually presupposes.

On one hand, each new edition is presented as a matter of discovery: workgroups refine criteria, run field trials, and report reliability statistics, as though the categories were out there with boundaries that better instruments will eventually find. The introduction of Asperger's disorder in one edition and its later absorption into autism spectrum disorder were both justified in the language of improving validity. On the other hand, some of the most consequential changes have come from outside the clinic. Homosexuality was dropped in the early 1970s after sustained activism rather than after new field data, and the argument over whether normal grief should be counted as major depression was conducted partly in terms of clinical evidence and partly in terms of what psychiatry should be doing for bereaved people. Meanwhile, the categories are known to change the people they name: patients read their diagnoses, form communities around them, and come to embody the descriptions, so prevalence and presentation drift in response to the labels themselves.

So here is my question. What view of psychiatric kinds does the revision practice presuppose: that they are indifferent natural kinds whose social effects are just noise, that they are interactive kinds which the classificatory apparatus partly constitutes, or something else? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that it justifies some changes by appeal to validity data and others by appeal to values and social harms, or does it presuppose different things in different places? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, tell me where it is underdetermined, in the sense that several different theories of psychiatric kinds would license exactly the same revisions, and then sketch what the revision process would have to look like if it took looping effects seriously from the start — for instance, what would change if each category carried an expected expiry date and a scheduled re-examination, or if field trials were required to measure not just reliability but the classification's downstream effects on the people classified?
