I've been reading Lakatos for a philosophy of science seminar and have ended up with a set of commitments I'd like audited for consistency before I present them. Here is where I stand: (a) the hard core of a research programme is held by methodological fiat — no observation can count directly against it, and that immunity is what the negative heuristic buys you, so there is an irreducible element of convention in what any programme takes as non-negotiable; (b) scientific rationality is real and historically efficacious — rational reconstruction, with appraisal of programmes doing most of the explanatory work, should carry the weight in explaining how science actually developed, with sociology and psychology mopping up the residue; (c) a programme that has been degenerating for a long stretch ought to be abandoned — at some point persistence in a degenerating programme is irrational; (d) it was nonetheless rational for physicists to keep working within Newtonian gravitational theory right up to 1915, despite decades of unsolved anomalies like the perihelion of Mercury; (e) whether a programme is progressive or degenerating at a time is an objective fact, fixed by the track record of novel predictions up to that point, not by whether the programme later staged a comeback; and (f) I'm a scientific realist — the only decent explanation of a programme's string of successful novel predictions is that its hard core is at least approximately true. Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what the cheapest single revision would be — be specific about which commitment has to give, and rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally.
