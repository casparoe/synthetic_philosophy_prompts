Two postdocs in my lab, whom I'll call Marta and Jonas, had a long-running disagreement at our methods reading group about what, if anything, the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute has to do with the replication crisis. I was in the room and took notes, and since they've now asked me to referee, I'd rather not tip my hand. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Marta opened by claiming that the replication crisis is a crisis of incentives and sloppy practice, not of frequentist statistics. p-hacking, outcome switching, publication bias, and small samples would corrode any framework: swap p-values for Bayes factors tomorrow and you would soon see B-hacking — peeking at accumulating data until the factor crosses a threshold, trying multiple specifications until one clears a "decisive" band, and settling on whatever prior makes a weak result look strong. The garden of forking paths, she said, is planted in human psychology, not in Neyman-Pearson theory, and neither likelihoods nor priors weed it.

(2) Jonas granted all of that but said it misses the one place where the formalism itself makes a difference: optional stopping. If a researcher monitors the data as it arrives and stops as soon as p < .05, the actual long-run false-positive rate of that procedure is far above 5% and can be driven arbitrarily close to 1, so a frequentist who peeks is doing something the theory itself condemns. A Bayesian who updates continuously commits no analogous sin: the posterior, and the Bayes factor, depend only on the likelihood of the data actually observed, not on the sampling plan that might have produced other data. So at least one documented amplifier of the crisis — undisclosed sequential peeking — is a specifically frequentist vulnerability, and switching frameworks would eliminate it even if nothing else changed.

(3) Marta replied that this "virtue" is bought at a price Jonas wasn't mentioning: the likelihood principle that makes inference immune to stopping rules also makes it immune to things we have reason to care about. It severs inference from anything involving the sample space, which is exactly where error control lives — and error control, she argued, is what a scientific field is for. Replication just is a long-run frequency property; a community whose individual members produce perfectly coherent posteriors can still be collectively miscalibrated, and Jonas's own criterion of success for reform — "will the findings replicate?" — is itself a frequency, so he was judging frequentist machinery by a frequentist standard while rejecting the machinery. She also noted that the newest Bayesian proposals in the methods literature keep importing frequentist ideas, like guarantees of calibrated intervals, which she took as a concession that coherence alone is not enough for science.

(4) Jonas's final word was that Marta had quietly shifted the target. His claim in step (2) was not that Bayes fixes science, only that one specific crisis amplifier is framework-relative, and that claim survives everything she said; that the field's overall health is a long-run property is no argument that an individual researcher's evidential summary should be constructed from hypothetical unobserved data. He added that if she really believed error rates were the whole story, she should explain preregistration to herself: the field's flagship reform is a bureaucratic procedure for constraining the sample space of analyst behavior, which suggests the profession has concluded that the mathematical framework, whichever it is, cannot save it from itself.

They then looked at me, and I discovered I could see both sides of nearly every move. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating; identify any points where one of them is arguing against a claim the other never actually made; and, if there is anything substantive both of them are missing about the relation between the replication crisis and the statistical framework debate, say what it is. Conclude with an overall verdict on who came out ahead, if either did.
