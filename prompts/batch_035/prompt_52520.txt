I cover science and medicine for a general readership, and over several years of reporting on end-of-life issues — intensive care, the hospice movement, the assisted-dying debates — I've accumulated a set of convictions about what death takes from the person who dies. I'm now drafting a long essay on the question, and my editor has asked me to set out the argument's premises explicitly so the piece doesn't smuggle anything in. Laid out in a row, I can no longer tell whether these add up to a single position or to two positions quietly at war. Here is what I actually believe:

(1) Death is bad for the person who dies, when it is bad at all, because of what it deprives them of: the goods the remainder of their life would have contained.

(2) How bad a particular death is, for the one who dies, is a purely comparative matter — the gap between the life the person actually led and the somewhat longer life they would otherwise have led. Nothing else enters into it: not how the person felt about dying, and not the character of the death itself.

(3) No event occurring after a person dies can be bad for them. Breaking a promise made to the dying, defaming the dead, desecrating a grave — these wrong the living, not the dead, who no longer exist to be wronged.

(4) Lucretius's symmetry argument fails: our posthumous nonexistence can be bad for us in a way our prenatal nonexistence is not. I'm confident of the asymmetry — no one grieves that they were born in March rather than January — though I confess I can't quite say what grounds it.

(5) What makes killing wrong, when it is wrong, is that death is bad for the victim. This is why I'm confident that murdering a healthy ninety-five-year-old wrongs him gravely, whatever the actuarial tables say about the goods his remaining years would have contained.

(6) I've never found the Epicurean argument persuasive — the one contending that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because by the time death arrives there is no subject left to undergo the bad — but I'd rather know exactly where it goes wrong than simply wave it away.

Audit these for me. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each conflict? For every real tension, tell me the cheapest way to restore consistency and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set; for every merely apparent one, show me why it dissolves. Rank the findings by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave, be specific about which thesis has to bend, and conclude by telling me whether any single revision would clean up the most — or whether I'm actually holding two views and should admit it.
