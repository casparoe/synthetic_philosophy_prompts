I work on the ops side of an ML lab, and lately I've been getting into arguments with a freind of mine who actually runs RL experiments, about whether any of these agents can suffer. I've been keeping a notes file of my gut verdicts: a drone controller trained with big negative rewards for crashing — no, obviously no, even though the training signal is in some sense 'aversive' to it. A simulated worm with a few hundred neurons, trained with a penalty signal for touching virtual hot zones — still no, though I feel the pull of the other answer, since the penalty is doing behaviorally almost exactly what pain does. A rat in a box getting shocks — yes, of course. An RLHF'd language model getting negative reward for rude outputs — no, though I notice I can't actually say why any faster than I could for the worm. A model-based agent with a decent learned world model that predicts penalties a few seconds out and visibly reorganizes everything it's doing around avoiding them — no from me, but my friend leans yes there, and honestly I can feel myself drifting toward yes as the agents get more capable. And the case that's really bugging me: I give a fish the benefit of the doubt — a perch being chased by a bigger fish, I think there's something it's like to be that perch — while I say no to a model-based RL agent that has strictly more predictive machinery than the fish does. So either I'm a carbon chauvinist, or the fish verdict is tracking something real that my cleverer-agent verdicts are missing.

My tentative principle, which I'd like you to stress-test and repair, is roughly: a negative reward is a candidate for suffering only when it plays a certain functional role — it has to persist across time, generalize across contexts, grab control of planning away from whatever else the agent is doing, and re-rank the agent's other goals, rather than just being a scalar that nudges a policy in a training loop. That feels like it gets me the drone no, the worm a weak no, and the model-based agent a genuine maybe. But it also seems to hand the fish a no or at best a shrug, since I doubt perch negative valence does all that persistence-and-re-ranking stuff, and I'm not willing to give up the fish. Meanwhile my friend's line is the blunt one: reward is just a number, and its sign isn't valence any more than a thermostat's setpoint is comfort, so the whole question is confused until we know what makes any state valenced in the first place — which I half accept and half think is a cop-out, since a similar move would have said 'no' about animal pain right up until the evidence got embarrassing.

So, what I want from you: is there a principle that captures all my verdicts, or close to it, and if not, which verdicts have to go and why? If something has to be overturned, I'd rather know whether it should be the fish (because the functional-role story is right and fish just don't do the relevant thing) or my model-based-agent no (because I'm just being substrate-biased and the drift I feel as agents get more capable is my intuition correcting itself). I'd also want whatever principle you land on to make actual predictions — e.g., what it says about an agent given a constant low-grade negative baseline reward for its entire training run versus sharp episodic spikes, or two agents with identical reward channels where one can't represent the reward channel's current state inside its own world model. And please be honest about which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot save.
