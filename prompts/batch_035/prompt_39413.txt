I teach a second-year ethics module, and every year population ethics turns up in the last fortnight and every year I basically hand-wave it, because my own verdicts on the cases are a mess and I'd rather not be caught out by a student who's actually read Parfit. So let me just put my cards on the table, gut reactions and all, and see if there's a principle lurking in there that gets most of them, and which ones it can't get.

The cases, with my verdicts:

World A: ten billion people, each with a fantastic life, call it welfare +100. World Z: a mind-bogglingly larger population, each living a life barely worth living, +0.001, mud and potatoes and mild contentment forever. Verdict: A is better. Clearly. I'm not giving that up.

World A again, and World A+: A plus, on a distant planet nobody on either side ever contacts, another ten billion people each at +50. Verdict: A+ is not worse than A, and I actually lean towards it being better. Adding happy people doesn't hurt anybody and it adds good lives.

Yes, I know you can get from A to Z in a chain of mere-addition steps where each step looks like my A+ verdict. I'm not stupid, I've read the book. I hold both verdicts anyway. This is the thing I most want you to not let me get away with.

The sadistic conclusion case: World C is a million people at +90. World D is those same million plus one extra person whose life is barely not worth living, at −0.001. World E is those same million plus ten billion new people at +0.0001. Verdict: E is better than C (obviously — you added a lot of happy people!), and D is worse than C (you added a life that went badly). My understanding is that the views which escape the repugnant conclusion by discounting or threshold tricks tend to say E is worse than C, and can even prefer adding the one miserable person over the ten billion happy ones. If a principle forces that on me, the principle has lost.

The average-view check: World F is one solitary person at +200. World G is a trillion people at +190. Verdict: G is better. So averaging is out, unless it's averaging of some very gerrymandered kind.

The depletion case: our society's wasteful policy leads to a future whose people all have lives worth living, but rather worse than the ones the people who would exist under a sustainable policy would have — and crucially the two populations are different people, so nobody in the depleted future is worse off than they would otherwise have been, since under the other policy they'd never exist. Verdict: the depleted future is still worse. So I officially do NOT believe that a world can only be worse if it's worse for someone. Fine.

But now the one that keeps me up at night: you can either improve one existing person's life from +0.001 to +100, or create a brand new person at +100. Equal aggregate gain. Verdict: improving the existing person seems to me slightly better, or at least the two aren't exactly on a par, in a way that smells like existing people count for more, or count differently, than merely possible ones. And that smells like a person-affecting view, which I just claimed to reject three sentences ago.

So that's my mess: total-ish about adding happy people (case 2), anti-total about Z (case 1), anti-average (case F/G), anti-person-affecting about depletion, vaguely person-affecting about improve-vs-create, and unwilling to budge on the sadistic conclusion cases.

The only patch I can see for myself is some kind of threshold: lives above some decent level of flourishing can't be outweighed by any number of barely-positive lives, so Z never beats A, but adding genuinely happy people still counts as a plus, so A+ beats A. But I suspect this patch is exactly what generates the sadistic conclusion, i.e. the patch trades case 1 for case E, and I've told you I'd sooner give up the patch.

What I want from you: tell me whether any actual principle — totalism, averaging, person-affecting views, critical levels, lexically ordered values, whatever you can construct, including hybrids and weirdos — captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it without knowing which case I'm in. Say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and make the case that those are the right ones to sacrifice (I want argument, not just a report). Then tell me what it says about two cases I haven't decided yet, and be honest if the outputs are ugly: (i) one person living for a billion years at a steady moderate +10 versus a billion people each living eighty years at +10 — same total, wildly different population size; and (ii) a world of two billion people at +60 versus a world of three billion where two billion are at +60 and the extra billion are at +59, versus a world of two billion at +60 where we've somehow raised the original population's welfare by 0.5 each — my patch handles the second comparison fine, I think, but I genuinely can't predict what it says about the third.

Also, at the end, please tell me if my threshold idea is a real position with a name or just an ad hoc contraption, and if contraption, what the closest respectable neighbour is. I'd like to know how much shame to feel when I teach it in week nine.
