I'm TA-ing an upper-level seminar on consequentialism and its critics — 14 students, 80-minute section. Next week's topic is Williams's integrity objection, together with the one thought too many point about the rescuer who can save either his drowning wife or a stranger. We've already been through the Smart and Williams For and Against book, and the students just read Railton's 1984 alienation paper where he splits objective from subjective consequentialism, so they will absolutely call foul if I run an activity that only shows that a dumb, maximally direct form of utilitarian deliberation feels bad from the inside. Whatever you design has to survive the student who says "the sophisticated consequentialist doesn't deliberate like that."

So here's what I want: an in-class activity where they run into the problem themselves instead of hearing me describe it. Rules, roles, materials, some kind of payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (we can use points that feed into their participation grade, or just tokens and a running scoreboard on the board), and a timeline that fits 80 minutes. The rough shape I've been imagining — feel free to improve or discard it — is that each participant gets a character sheet with one or two "ground projects" written on it, and there's a rotating auditor role whose job is to accept or reject action justifications, where the rules say impersonal, agent-neutral reasons count at full value and personal ones have to be converted into some common currency first. I want to see whether the felt friction comes from the maximizing as such, or specifically from being forced to translate attachment into the ledger. And I want at least one beat where the same act gets done for the same outcome but the participants themselves judge one stated rationale as somehow worse than the other — the thing about the ordering of thoughts, not the act. That beat is the one I most don't want to fumble.

Give me the full design, including the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the objection, a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing: auditors playing it as caricature villains, and someone recognizing the drowning wife scenario and pre-editing their reactions), and an honest note on which feature of the actual view a classroom game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. One more thing, and I care about this: in the debrief material, please keep apart what's genuinely settled among people who work on Williams from what's contested — e.g. whether the integrity objection is fundamentally a complaint about aggregation or about how agents must deliberate, whether the one thought too many point is a piece of the integrity objection or a seperate argument, and who the argument is even aimed at once you allow indirect or sophisticated consequentialism. I've seen teachers present one contested reading of Williams as just "what Williams argued," and I don't want to do that to my students. And don't bother with a reading list, I've got the syllabus covered — its the activity I need.
