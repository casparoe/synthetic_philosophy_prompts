Two people I know got into it at Thanksgiving over whether machines could ever really mean anything, and I want you to adjudicate it move by move. One of them, a big Dennett fan, brought up Dennett's two-bitser: a vending machine's coin detector that goes into an internal state whenever something with the right shape and weight goes through, and which we'd normally call a mindless discriminator with zero intentionality. Then he leaned on the twist where the very same machine, same wiring, gets shipped to a country whose local currency is coins physically indistinguishable from US quarters — now, he said, that same internal state means something different (it's now in the business of accepting the local currency), so representational content depends on history and environment, not on anything intrinsic to the device. So Searle's original-vs-derived split is a scam, he concluded: our own content is derived too, just from natural selection instead of a human designer, and Searle never says what makes ours original beyond insisting that we just have it.

The other guy, who's read a lot of Searle, pushed back in steps. First: the two-bitser has no intentionality at all, not even derived — a sentence in a book has derived meaning because somebody means something by it, but nobody means anything by the coin detector's state; it just discriminates, like a termostat. Second: the shipped-abroad twist only shows the state's function shifts with the population it's embedded in, and that's a fact about us interpreters, not the machine — it's the intentional stance all the way down, and Dennett himself admits the stance is just a predictive tactic, so calling the state a belief was smuggling in the conclusion. Third: even if our intentionality is derived from evolution, Searle can live with that — the evolutionary story explains how the states got there, but the states themselves are still original in us because they're conscious, and that's the difference that matters; a theory that can't capture it is changing the subject.

The Dennett guy replied that this is exactly the move he was complaining about: saying and that's supposed to end the argument — if being original can't be tested by anything the device does, it looks like a label with no explanatory work to do. The Searle guy's last word was that not every real difference has to earn its keep as a predictor; some things are known from the inside. Then they stopped and nobody convinced anybody, and honestly I couldn't follow all of it.

Please act as a professional philosopher who's fair to both sides, think carefully before answering (maybe sketch the positions first), and then go through the exchange rating each move from clearly right to clearly mistaken, with a short justification for each rating. In particular I want to know where the two-bitser example genuinely does the work my friend wants, where it doesn't, and whether both of them are missing something — like whether "derived from evolution" even means the same thing both of them think it means. Wich of them, if either, comes out more right overall?
