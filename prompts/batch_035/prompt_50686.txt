A friend of mine and I spent most of Friday evening arguing about Sleeping Beauty and got stuck somewhere neither of us expected. The setup we used is the standard one: Beauty knows a fair coin will be flipped after she's put to sleep; if Heads she's woken once (Monday), if Tails she's woken twice (Monday and Tuesday), her memory is erased between wakings, and each waking is subjectively indistinguishable. When she wakes and is asked her credence that the coin landed Heads, my friend — a committed thirder — defended the position with what he called the indifference argument, and I couldn't find anything wrong with it until I noticed it seems to prove far too much. The argument: (1) When Beauty wakes, her total evidence is exhausted by the fact that she is awake in this experiment; she cannot tell whether this is Heads-Monday, Tails-Monday, or Tails-Tuesday. (2) Rational credence requires that possibilities not distinguished by one's evidence get equal weight. (3) So her credence is 1/3 each in the three centered possibilities, hence 1/3 in Heads. So far, so good — or at least so it seemed. But then I pushed the same principle where my friend didn't want it to go. (4) Nothing in the argument is specific to repeated wakings of one person; it is an instance of a general rule that says: treat yourself as a random sample from the possibilities consistent with your evidence, weighted by how many "slots" like you each hypothesis contains. (5) Now consider two cosmological theories that are otherwise equally well supported by all physical evidence: one says the universe contains a trillion trillion observers, the other only a handful. (6) By the rule in (4), the fact that I exist, in an epistemic situation like mine, is astronomically more probable under the many-observer theory — there are vastly more slots I could occupy there. (7) So I should be close to certain that the many-observer theory is true, on the basis of my mere existence and without any new empirical data. (8) But confidently settling cosmology from an armchair, on the grounds that bigger universes give me more chances to exist, is absurd — it means the principle can manufacture evidence for any theory just by inflating its population of observers.

My friend's first reaction was to deny that (4) follows — that Beauty's case is somehow special, maybe because it involves one person woken repeatedly rather than many observers existing once. I pushed back that this can't be right, since the classic variants blur exactly that line: if we replace the memory erasure with a thousand temporary copies of Beauty, one of which is you-now, the indifference argument still seems to deliver thirding, and now there really are many observers. He then said maybe the problem is my step (6) — that existence isn't evidence at all — but I suspect that costs him (3), since Beauty's awakening seems to be evidence of exactly the same kind ("here I am, in a slot compatible with my evidence"). I left suspecting the real flaw is a subtle slide between indifference among centered possibilities that all exist under a single hypothesis and the quite different move of comparing hypotheses by how many such possibilities they contain, but I can't make that distinction precise without it either collapsing into (3) or into an ad hoc exception, and I can't tell whether the two of us equivocated somewhere between what "a possibility" ranges over in (2) versus (6).

Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it doesn't, which premise is least costly to abandon. I'd like to stay a thirder about Beauty if I can, but not if the price is the armchair cosmology of (7), and I'd like a real diagnosis rather than a refusal to generalize — if the reasoning in (3) really does transfer to the copies variant and from there to cosmology, I want to see exactly where the transfer breaks, and if it doesn't break anywhere, I want to know which of my friend's premises he should have refused to buy in the first place and what his halfing costs him in the betting cases, where he insists Beauty should be indifferent between a bet paying out on Heads and one paying out on Tails at two-to-one odds.
