My roommate (works in ML) and his old college friend (a serious amateur Go player) got into it over dinner about whether being careful is a competitive handicap, and I want an outside opinion on who was right where.

(1) The ML friend claimed caution costs you nothing, technique-wise, because of what he called strategy stealing: if a reckless competitor finds some trick that works, you can just copy it a month later — the useful part of the trick didn't require recklessness, so nothing is ever closed off to you forever.

(2) The Go friend said he'd heard a version of that before — it's the old argument that in a symmetric game the second player can't have a winning strategy: if they did, the first player could just make a random opening move (an extra stone can't hurt) and then pretend to be the second player and follow that winning strategy, contradiction. And he said that argument has a built-in limit: it proves at most the first player never loses, never that they win, and if draws break in favor of whoever's ahead on tempo, that's cold comfort.

(3) The ML friend said fine, but not losing is exactly what you want when the downside is catastrophic, which was his whole point.

(4) The Go friend said the analogy quietly swaps a simultaneous game for a sequential one: in the real world the other guy's gains compound while you watch — users, data, money, trust — so by the time you steal the strategy, the position you're stealing it into is no longer equivalent, and the proof only ever worked for equivalent positions.

Neither would give an inch and honestly i couldn't follow all of it. Can you go through the exchange move by move, say who's right at each step, and note anything both of them are missing?
