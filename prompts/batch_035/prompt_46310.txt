So I've been going down a rabbit hole on algorithmic information theory ever since a YouTube video on Occam's razor, and my cousin (CS grad student, loves this stuff) walked me into an argument I can't get out of. Here it is, numbered the way he wrote it to me:

(1) Occam's razor says: if two hypotheses fit all the observed data equally well, believe the simpler one. (2) If simple is going to mean anything precise, it has to be something like shortest description length — the length of the shortest program, on some fixed universal machine, that specifies the hypothesis. (3) But description length is always relative to which machine you picked; there is no machine-neutral length. (4) And here's the kicker: for any two hypotheses H1 and H2, you can build a perfectly legitimate universal machine with H1 hard-coded as a built-in shortcut, so H1 gets a one-bit description while H2 needs a long one — and you can just as easily build the mirror-image machine that does the reverse. (5) So H1 being simpler than H2 is true in some languages and false in others, which means there's no fact of the matter about which is simpler. (6) So Occam's razor, once made precise, never actually favors one hypothesis over another — simplicity is just an artifact of our choice of notation. (7) Therefore when two hypotheses fit the data equally well there is literally no objective ground for prefering one, and everyone who invokes the razor is just smuggling in their favorite notation.

He also has a comeback for the standard objection, which I half-remembered from the video: someone told him that switching machines can only change description lengths by a bounded amount, because you can always bolt an interpreter for one machine's language onto the other, so the differences can't run away to infinity. His reply is that the bound only shows up in the limit — for the actual finite hypotheses and finite data we humans deal with, the interpreter overhead can dwarf everything, so any real preference between two real hypotheses could still get reversed by a legitimate machine. I have a feeling that comeback is where the action is, but I can't tell if it rescues his argument or proves too much. Like, wouldn't his version also make it notation-relative whether the string 0101010101... repeating forever is compressible at all? That feels obviously wrong but I can't articulate why without just pointing at how natural binary is, and he'll say naturalness is exactly the hand-waving he's trying to eliminate.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If it helps, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) — I like the razor and I like the idea of making it precise — but I want to know what I'm paying for that, and whether his claim that the constant doesn't help in finite cases is actually sound or whether it quietly changes the subject somewhere. Lead with your verdict in the very first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no throat-clearing and no restating the whole argument back at me.
