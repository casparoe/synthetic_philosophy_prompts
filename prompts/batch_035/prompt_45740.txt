I'm preparing a seminar session on axiomatic bargaining theory, and I want a worked case that isolates the one structural difference between the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: the latter is sensitive to each party's best feasible outcome (the ideal or utopia point), while the former, once the disagreement point and the frontier are fixed, is indifferent to it. The case is meant to let the class argue about whether that sensitivity is a bug or a feature, so it has to earn its verdicts rather than stipulate them.

Construct the scenario to these specifications. It should be a realistic two-party negotiation — siblings dividing an estate, a founder and an early employee negotiating equity and salary, a city and its transit union, whatever you judge best — with a natural disagreement point (what happens if talks collapse) and a Pareto frontier that is genuinely curved, where the curvature arises from the situation itself rather than by fiat: at least two issues on which the parties have different exchange rates, diminishing-returns valuations, or a randomized clause in the agreement (a coin flip between two packages is a real bargaining tactic; flag it if you use one). Each party's best case should be visible in the story — one of them should have a plausible "best day" package the reader can actually see — because that is what the two solutions will fight over. The target: once you translate the scenario into numbers, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution must give party A strictly more of what A cares about than the Nash solution gives A, by a margin of at least a few percent rather than a rounding error. And the verdicts must be derived, not stipulated: fix explicit utility scales, state the disagreement point and each party's ideal point, give the frontier as a formula or as enough points to pin it down, and then compute both solutions with the arithmetic shown — Nash as the point maximizing the product of the gains over the disagreement point, Kalai–Smorodinsky as the point on the frontier where each party's gain is the same fraction of that party's ideal gain.

After the computation, do three things. First, verify the specification openly: show the check that the gap is real and runs in the intended direction. Second, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — my guess is it will be something about how the two parties' best cases enter the normalization, but be honest if the real driver is the curvature of the frontier — and then say what minimal change to the case would collapse the two solutions into agreement, and why that change works. Third, close with the exchange a partisan of each solution would have about this very case: the Nash defender objecting that aspirations are cheap talk and only feasible gains over the disagreement point should matter, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky defender replying that a split is fair only when the parties have made equal relative concessions, measured against what each could reasonably have hoped for. If you think the case ends up making one of the two solutions look bad, say so plainly — that judgment is what I will open the seminar with.
