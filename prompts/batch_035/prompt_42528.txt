I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences were being posted, and the recurring arguments about acausal trade never converge, because people who say "yes, I'd cooperate" turn out to mean quite different things by it. For my blog I'd like a decision tree titled "Which acausal trade view do you actually hold?": it should take a reader from a root question to a leaf naming their position, with every internal node phrased as a concrete case that no one could answer by guessing which camp it sorts them into.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of schools. The splits I want, reworded as needed but kept case-based:

- Root: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger, would you cooperate if told the stranger had already locked in its move — cooperate if and only if it predicted you would, defect otherwise?
- Whether a common cause suffices: if the correlation between your choices comes from both of you having been shaped by the same circumstances, rather than from each side predicting the other and conditioning on that prediction, does cooperation still make sense?
- How much mutual modeling the "trade" requires: does each side need to rely on the other reading it accurately, or is a rough shared expectation enough for the exchange to be real?
- Distance and time: does it still count as trade if the counterpart can never causally reach you — a civilization beyond your horizon, or one that will not exist for a million years — so that you would be carrying out your side purely in the expectation that somewhere, they are carrying out theirs?

Expected leaves include the causal decision theorist, who declines everything at the root; the "near-copies only" view, which cooperates with close replicas of oneself but not with alien optimizers; the full acausal trader, for whom any sufficiently good predictor is a trading partner regardless of distance or era; and the deflationist, who holds that what survives analysis is ordinary trade under uncertainty about the other's disposition, with "acausal" doing no real work. Include any unoccupied combinations the tree generates, stating for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Give every leaf one line on its most awkward commitment, and flag honestly any two leaves the questions cannot separate — I would rather print an admission than a fake distinction. Skip citations and the history of the literature; the audience is smart but untrained in decision theory, so nothing should presuppose a term like "functional decision theory."
