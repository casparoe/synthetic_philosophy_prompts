I am taking a graduate seminar on decision theory and games with imperfect information, and we have spent two weeks on Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver and the literature it provoked. I now want to test a suspicion of mine by having a case built to order, and I would appreciate it if you built it carefully and then showed your work, because I intend to present it in section.

First, so we are on the same page about the baseline: in the original problem, a driver passes through one or two identical intersections that he cannot tell apart, because he is absent-minded and has imperfect recall of his own past actions. At each intersection he can Continue or Exit. If he exits at the first intersection his payoff is 1; if he exits at the second his payoff is 4; if he never exits, driving on forever, his payoff is 0. Since the intersections are subjectively indistinguishable, he must pick a single policy that applies at both. At the planning stage, before starting the drive, the optimal mixed policy is to Continue with probability 3/8, which yields an expected payoff of 25/16. But once he is actually at a wheel, reasoning ex ante about that very choice (taking himself to be, with probability 3/8, at the first intersection) makes Continue at that moment look like part of an overall Continue probability of about 1/2, and the planning-stage recommendation destabilizes itself. The literature disagrees about whether this is a genuine inconsistency in the theory of decision problems with imperfect recall or an artifact of how the modeller sets up the agent's information sets; I want to probe that with a construction.

My suspicion is that all of the celebrated weirdness is generated by three background assumptions working together — that the problem is a one-shot episode, that the driver has no way to tell the intersections apart other than through his own (faulty) memory, and that the payoffs are rigged to make memory failure costly — and that if you relax all three at once, in an ordinary-life setting, the planning-stage and action-stage perspectives coincide and nothing about the driver's absent-mindedness matters at all. If that is right, it would suggest that the absent-mindedness only does visible work when the environment is otherwise engineered against the agent, which is a conclusion I would find interesting but do not trust myself to reach without a worked case.

So here is what I would like you to construct. The scenario should be one paragraph, realistic (a commuter, not an abstract agent; road signs and route choices, not chance mechanisms or demons), and should satisfy all three of the following specifications simultaneously:

1. Repeated rather than one-shot: the driver faces the same kind of decision nightly over many independent drives, so that he could in principle learn from experience how the route works.

2. Plausible epistemic access to the other instance's conduct: the two intersections should differ observably in some mundane way, so that a driver with normal awareness could tell which intersection he is at without relying on memory — but the driver remains absent-minded in the sense that he never connects tonight's observations to his own past conduct.

3. A payoff regime in which the agent's difficulties are not what make the problem interesting: design the payoffs (the delays, the destinations, the costs) so that a driver who could tell the intersections apart would simply carry out the same action at both, and so that no mixed strategy is ever optimal for anyone.

After the scenario, I need you to demonstrate that it meets each specification, not just assert it. Then:

(a) Briefly reconstruct how the standard one-shot case delivers the planning-versus-action instability, so the contrast is explicit.

(b) Walk through your scenario and show that the ex ante perspective and the at-the-wheel perspective now issue the same recommendation, and explain what your design choices did to the original case to bring this about.

(c) Tell me honestly which of the three relaxed assumptions is doing most of the work. My guess is that it is specification 2, because once the intersections are observably distinguishable the problem quietly becomes a standard perfect-information problem and the imperfect recall is idle; if you agree, say whether the other two specifications are doing anything at all, or whether I have included them needlessly.

(d) Finally, anticipate the objection on behalf of the original framework: a defender of the Piccione–Rubinstein setup might complain that the construction cheats by changing the modeller's stipulation of the information sets rather than by showing anything about decision theory with imperfect recall itself. State the strongest version of that objection and assess whether it succeeds — in particular, whether my case, if it works, shows that the famous instability arises only in an environment where every natural route to coordination has been stripped away, or whether it shows something weaker.
