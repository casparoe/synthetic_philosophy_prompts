Next week my fourteen-student seminar on free will and moral responsibility reaches what Strawson calls the impossibilist view, and I have one eighty-minute session for it. Design an in-class activity in which the students run into the structure of the Basic Argument themselves, before anyone names it. The structure I want them to feel is roughly this: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly responsible for what you do in the deep sense at issue, you would have to be responsible for the way you are in a way that requires having made yourself that way; but any self you might claim to have made, you made using materials and capacities you did not choose, so the demand just regresses; and nothing short of being causa sui — a self that created itself from nothing, which is incoherent — could satisfy it. They should hit the regress as a feature of their own attempts to answer for themselves, not hear it described.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine or replace outright if you can improve on it: a responsibility audit, run in pairs. Each claimant selects a real, moderately significant choice from their own life and tries to earn moral credit for it before an auditor. The audit rule is the heart of it: credit for a choice requires credit for the character that produced it, and credit for character can be established either by citing its sources, which the auditor will reject as inherited and unearned, or by pointing to some earlier choice through which you made yourself that way, which simply reopens the audit at that earlier choice. Auditors should be rewarded for successful challenges and claimants for progress, so that both sides are motivated to keep digging and the session ends with the clock, not the instructor, defeating everyone. The debrief then maps what actually happened, stage by stage, onto the argument's premises.

Give me the full mechanics: materials, how pairs are formed, the timing of each phase, how the audit record is kept and who holds it, what counts as a successful challenge and who adjudicates disputes about that, and a payoff scheme that does not involve real money — an exemption from next week's reading response, or something comparable, distributed by a rule rather than by my discretion. Anticipate what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the claimant who insists there was a genuine turning point at which they remade themselves; the one who refuses the game's premise on the grounds that ordinary, comparatively shallow responsibility is all anyone ever meant; the pair that plays it for laughs; and the sharp student who notices that the audit makes the impossibility look like a merely causal, empirical fact about how humans happen to be formed, when Strawson's claim is that it is conceptual and would apply even to a being that somehow chose its own character, since that choice would itself need explaining by a prior self. Tell me how to handle each of these in the moment.

Say frankly where the design falls short of the view itself and I will have to supply the missing part myself — in particular whether anything in the activity can convey Strawson's point that the determinism question is strictly speaking beside the point, since the Basic Argument swamps it either way. Do not name Strawson or the argument until the debrief, and keep all lecture content out of the room before that point. Please write the whole design as continuous prose, the way you would write a long, careful email to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
