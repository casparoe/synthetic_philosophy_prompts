I'm a doctoral student in environmental policy, and two members of my committee have been at cross-purposes for the better part of a decade: one is a Hardinian, convinced that commons dilemmas only ever get solved by imposing external limits on self-interested users, and one is an Ostromian, convinced that locally crafted common-pool institutions can govern themselves when their rules are well designed. They have finally agreed to a structured adversarial collaboration, and I've been assigned to draft the joint document before our fieldwork begins. The case we'll be studying is a valley whose small farmers have informally governed a shared shallow aquifer for four generations—customary pumping rotations, informal social sanctions, no formal title to water—now hit simultaneously by three shocks: a run of drought years that has cut recharge well below historical levels, the arrival of cheap deep-well pumps that make extraction easier and cheating cheaper to hide, and the appearance of two outside powers—a regional urban authority quietly buying farmland for its water rights, and a national water ministry proposing a uniform permitting scheme that would replace the customary regime outright. The Hardinian, having conceded that privatization is politically dead in this valley, holds that when monitoring costs are high, the median user's planning horizon is shortening, and the resource itself is changing faster than any local rule-set can track, appeals to self-governance are sentimental; only uniform, state-enforced limits will hold. The Ostromian holds that the customary regime has adaptively weathered shocks before, that ministry permits are exactly the kind of one-size-fits-all imposition that erodes local compliance, and that the valley's real problem is that its boundary rules were never built for land-sale newcomers, not that self-governance fails.

Draft the joint statement both could sign. It should contain: (1) the claims they genuinely agree on, including empirical claims about the aquifer, the shocks, and what each kind of institution can and cannot do; (2) the precise statements of their remaining disagreements, each worded so that its own side would accept it as fair to their view; (3) the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, such as whether customary sanctions can be made to bind non-cooperating newcomers, or whether the ministry can enforce limits that it, too, lacks the capacity to monitor; and (4) for each crux, a concrete observable test our study could actually run, with each side committing in writing, before fieldwork, to what it expects the test to show. Please also tell me which parts of their dispute strike you as merely verbal—for instance, if the Hardinian will accept that self-governance works when the state credibly backs it in the background, and the Ostromian will accept that state rules work only where locals supply the monitoring, is that an agreement, or is it the entire disagreement wearing a disguise? I want formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, because both of them will read my draft very carefully.
