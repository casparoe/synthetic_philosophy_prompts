For six years I've been an active member of my city's chapter of an environmental party, and at our next monthly meeting I'm presenting a position on the deal our national leadership just made: to stay in government, they dropped our flagship promise and publicly defended a highway project they had campaigned against. My view, which I've worked out on my own over the years without any philosophical training, is this: what they did was probably the right thing to do — the alternative was handing power to a coalition worse on everything we care about — but it was also genuinely wrong, and the guilt is real and does not cancel out. And I want to press further with something I haven't heard anyone say: that this guilt should be treated less like a stain (something you're stuck with until time washes it away) and more like a debt (something you can only work down, by openly admitting the wrong, giving up some of the power it bought you, and accepting constraints the next time a similar bargain appears). A leader who breaks the promise, keeps all the power, and shrugs that this is just realpolitik hasn't got dirty hands — they've defaulted.

The audience is about twenty chapter members, none of them philosophers: several longtime pacifists who believe there is always a clean option if you're honest and creative enough; a few hard-boiled realists who think guilt-talk in politics is self-indulgent and that governing is simply arithmetic for adults; two law students who will object that an act cannot coherently be both the right thing to do and wrong; and one former civil servant who has seen compromise up close and mostly wants to know what my view prescribes in practice.
