I don't have any background in philosophy or law — I work in logistics — but I've been thinking a lot lately about what we owe to people who aren't born yet, and a podcast I listen to covered a German court case that I keep turning over. In March 2021, Germany's constitutional court ruled that the country's climate law was partly unconstitutional. The law had set emission targets up to 2030 and left the steep cuts for afterwards, and the court said, roughly, that this postponed the hardest choices onto the young: once a large share of the total remaining carbon budget has been used up, people who are young today will face drastic restrictions on their freedom later in life that they can never get back, because emissions are effectively irreversible. The court gave the government a deadline to fix the law, and within days the government had tightened its targets for 2030 and brought forward its net-zero date. So far as I understand it, no court anywhere had framed climate inaction as a transfer of restrictions on freedom from one generation to another like that before.

Here's what I want analysed. I've come across a puzzle philosophers apparently call the non-identity problem, and I want this case run through it, carefully. The court spoke as if future people have something like rights — a claim not to inherit a degraded world — but the complaint was actually brought by young people who already exist, complaining about their own later decades. So who exactly is the wronged party: the young claimants, the not-yet-born, or both? Does the non-identity problem even bite here, since aggressive climate policy would also change who exists in the future, or does it dissolve because the court's reasoning was about distributing burdens among the living rather than duties to hypothetical people? I also want the case examined through the older debate about discounting — the economists' idea that harms far in the future simply count for less — because the court's ruling reads almost like the mirror image of a discount rate: instead of counting future welfare as worth less, it treated future losses of freedom as worth full weight, and even as compounding.

Please be disciplined about the mapping: state plainly what the court actually decided and what it is fair to read into it, and flag wherever the philosophical theory demands something the ruling simply doesn't address (for instance, whether it says anything at all about people not yet born, or about people outside Germany). Then run it the other way: what does this episode do to the theories? Does a court enforcing "fair distribution of freedom across time" count as evidence that rights of future generations can be made workable in practice, or does the fact that it had to be framed around currently living complainants suggest the theory quietly only works for people who can sue? And one thing I'd especially like your view on: I've been playing with a picture of my own, that the carbon budget is like a cake at a party where the first guests to arrive eat most of it and then apologise to those arriving later by leaving them the recipe. Does that picture capture the court's reasoning, or does it distort it, and if it distorts it, where?
