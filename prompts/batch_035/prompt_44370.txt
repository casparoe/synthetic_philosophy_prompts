I've read enough about the black-and-white room to know that I have reactions rather than a view, and I'd like your help extracting the view. Assume the standard setup: a neuroscientist confined from birth to an environment without color, in possession of every physical fact about color vision, then released. Call her Mary. Below are my verdicts on six variants. What I want is the principle behind them, if there is one.

My verdicts:

1. The classic release. Mary steps out and sees red for the first time. She learns something. I am not giving this verdict up, and I am aware the physicalist has more than one way of honoring it; part of what I want from you is to be told which way my overall pattern of verdicts forces me to take.

2. The forgotten glimpse. During her captivity Mary is briefly shown a red patch, and the episode is then wiped from her memory completely. At release, red is as unfamiliar to her as to any Mary. My verdict: the glimpse left her with nothing; she learns at release exactly what the classic Mary learns. So whatever the gain is, a past experience does not supply it unless something of it persists.

3. The dream. Mary once, during captivity, had a vivid dream of a red apple, and she never suspected it was anything but a dream. At release, seeing red startles her with its total familiarity: she recognizes it, can imagine it afterward, could sort red from orange blindfolded. My verdict: at release she learns nothing; the dream already gave her whatever the classic Mary gains.

4. The blind colleague. A congenitally blind neuroscientist knows every physical fact Mary knows, plus every verbal report of color experience anyone has ever written. My verdict: there is something she lacks that even the pre-release Mary has. But I hesitate to call it a piece of knowledge — she is not ignorant of any fact she could be told.

5. The arbitrary pill. Mary takes a drug that produces in her a phenomenally perfect experience of red, while she correctly believes the pill's effect was tuned by chance and that there need be no red thing anywhere near her. My verdict: after the pill she knows what red is like — at release she feels nothing new — but she has learned nothing about red, or about red things, or about their properties.

6. The sighted student. Frank has ordinary color vision and no scientific training; he then learns all the physical facts Mary knew. My verdict: Frank gains nothing that parallels Mary's gain. The two trades do not mirror each other.

You will see the trouble I am in. Cases 3 and 5 together suggest that whatever Mary gains has nothing essentially to do with red things — a dream of red and a causelessly generated red-experience both suffice — while case 1, as I want to read it, says she learns something about the world. Worse, 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions on what matters: in 2 a veridical experience plus amnesia leaves Mary empty-handed, while in 3 a dreamed experience plus intact memory gives her everything. If memory of the episode matters more than the episode's reality, that seems to say something strange, and I would like it said clearly rather than smoothed over.

Please reply in the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "The principle, stated" (at most 150 words). State your best candidate as numbered clauses, each traceable to specific verdicts above. If you need a technical notion (a mode of presentation, an ability, a relation of acquaintance, or something else), define it in the statement itself.

2. "Verdict by verdict" (around 400 words). Run the principle through all six cases, using my numbering, and flag every mismatch between what it delivers and what I claimed.

3. "The verdict that goes" (around 200 words). Name the one of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, explain why it is the right sacrifice rather than one of the others, and describe what the repaired set looks like. If you can save all six at the price of real complexity, say so and show the price.

4. "Predictions" (around 300 words). Construct three cases I have not considered — I would welcome things like gradually fading memory, partial color experience, attention failures, or anything else that stresses your principle — and state the principle's verdict on each. Mark any verdict you are not confident in; an honest shrug here is worth more than a forced call.

5. "Where this sits" (around 150 words). Place the resulting view relative to the familiar options in this literature — the ability answer, the old-facts-under-a-new-guise answer, the new-facts answer, and the denial that Mary learns anything at all — and identify the single point of sharpest divergence from whichever one it is closest to.

Please do not open with a tour of the standard dialectic; the verdicts are given, and the deliverable is the principle.
