I'm a novelist and I've been in a slow-burning argument with my editor about it. My current book has a thread based loosely on a family story my grandmother told, and I went down a rabbit hole checking what parts could actually have happened. When I brought my findings to the editor she waved it off with "the truth is what works" — her point being that if a scene rings true to readers, it *is* true enough, and my fact-checking is basically a category error, like weighing a poem in kilograms. Then at a festival last month I heard another writer use the exact same line to mean something much stronger: that a belief his character clings to is "true for her" because it's what keeps her functional, no external facts involved. And I have a vague sense the line descends from real philosophy — pragmatism, Peirce and James — but that the two of them meant quite different things by the underlying idea, and that at least one of them would have hated the pop version my editor is running with. I'd rather not be confidently wrong about this at my next dinner party.

So can you take "the truth is what works" apart for me? Lay out the distinct precise claims that one little sentence could be making, from the nearly trivial (beliefs that never cash out in anything tend not to survive?) to the genuinely radical (truth is somehow *constituted* by working, such that a belief that serves you couldn't be false), and for each reading tell me whether it's actually defensible and, crucially, whether it would support the use my editor put it to — dismissing the whole question of what historically happened — or the stronger use the other writer wanted. If some readings collapse into something nobody would deny while others make truth depend on whoever's convenienced, I want to see exactly where that line is. A menu format I can keep in my notes would be perfect, with a verdict per item, not just one overall take.

One thing I'd especially love: since people apparently toss Peirce and James around interchangeably, flag which of your readings are closer to Peirce's picture — something about truth being whatever inquiry would settle on in the long run, which sounds like it makes "what works" answerable to how the world is — versus James's picture, where being true seems to partly *happen to* a belief as it gets assimilated and used. If my editor's line is really a mangled hybrid of the two, I'd like to be able to say precisely which half she's borrowing from whom. Sorry if this is a lot, you're the only person I know who won't glaze over.
