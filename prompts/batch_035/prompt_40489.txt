I'm giving the opening remarks at dinner the night before a small two-day workshop I helped organize. Theme of the workshop: whether Sen's liberal paradox, and the whole "meddlesome preferences" literature around it, has anything useful to say about content moderation and recommender feeds. The hook we put in the call for papers is that platforms basically industrialized meddlesome preferences — millions of strangers having intense preferences about what you get to see and post — so maybe the impossibility result bites harder at scale rather than softer. Who knows if that's true, that's what the workshop is for.

The room is about thirty people. Roughly half are social choice theorists who can recite Sen's proof from memory and will wince audibly if I garble the setup or confuse a preference ordering with a choice function. The other half are trust & safety practitioners and a couple of regulators who have never encountered a Pareto condition in their lives and will tune out the moment the talk gets notation-y. There are no slides, it's dinner, I want five-ish minutes.

Write the remarks for me. What I want:

1. Actually walk through the classic prude-and-lewd-reader Lady Chatterley style example so the second half of the room gets the mechanism — the pairwise decisiveness, and how the Pareto condition then forces a cycle. But don't just do the textbook version: recast it with a modern cast, like a user and one of her followers disagreeing about whether a post of hers stays up, where the social states are the versions of that one post. Keep it honest to the structure of the argument though — the Sen people in the room will be checking.

2. Make the paradox do real structural work rather than being a party trick. The speech's job is to set up the workshop's actual question: does "each person is decisive over their own sphere" even mean anything when the spheres are entangled through a shared feed, where what you see depends on what others do and vice versa? Don't resolve it. Opening remarks should open, not settle. But it's fine — good, even — if the speech ends on the sharpest version of the tension you can construct.

3. It's a dinner speech, so it should have some warmth and maybe one laugh, not read like an abstract. Someone will definately bring up Gibbard's extension in the Q&A but I don't need you to cover that, just don't say anything that makes it sound like Sen's result is the last word.

After the speech text, add a short note to me (not part of the speech) saying how confident you are in the technical claims you made about the paradox, and flagging the single point where your rendering is most likely to be an oversimplification or a contested interpretation — I'd rather correct it myself than get corrected by the front row.
