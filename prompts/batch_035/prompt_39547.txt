Hey, so I'm a novelist -- four books, all set in the same fading river town in southeast Ohio -- and my editor talked me into writing a personal essay to run when the paperback of my latest comes out. I've never written a nonfiction thing longer than a dedication, so I'd really value comments on the draft below. Fair warning: it's half draft, half notes, and I already suspect the ending is doing something sneaky that I can't see clearly myself.

The working title is "Staying as a Way of Knowing," which I'm not sure I even believe, so feel free to go after that too.

Here's the draft:

---

When my mother's roof started leaking in the spring of 2019, three men from her church were up on it by Saturday without being asked, and not one of them called first. The way my editor tells this story, it's evidence that small-town life is warm and networked and the city is lonely. But the part I actually find interesting is that two of those men hadn't spoken to my mother in eleven years -- not from estrangement, but because nothing had happened that needed speaking about. The debt was still on the books. It had never once come up for renewal.

I want to say something about the difference between how the people in my novels keep accounts and how the people in my publisher's office keep them. The working-class morality I grew up inside is nominative: your obligations attach to named people, and they do not expire. You help because it's Dale, and Dale resurfaced your grandmother's porch in 1988, and Dale will be at the funeral whether or not anyone remembers to call him. The professional morality I've spent my adult life learning is anonymous and role-indexed: you owe courtesy and real effort to whoever currently occupies the role -- the colleague, the barista, the subcontractor -- and the debt clears the moment they exit the role, which is also the moment they can no longer do anything for you.

Notice that both of these are genuinely moral systems, and I'm not interested in ranking them, though I keep trying to anyway. The nominative one keeps faith across decades and can't handle a stranger to save its life -- the new family at the end of the lane is a rumor for two years before they're people. The anonymous one extends decency to absolutely everyone, on the beautiful theory that it's owed to anyone in the role, and then quietly discontinues it the day you're laid off, because you've left the role and the debt left with you.

[NB -- a friend who read an early version said I'm reinventing some old economics book about "exit" versus something. If that's a real thing I should atleast gesture at it, but honestly I like not knowing.]

---

And then the ending, which is the part I'm least sure of. Right now it goes roughly: the reader assumes the whole essay is a defense of staying, but the last move is a turn on myself -- that my sentimental defense of rootedness is itself the professional-class gesture. I turned my mother's roof into content. Dale never once needed his loyalty aestheticized in order to have it. The people who actually stay don't write essays about the value of staying; the person writing the essay is the one who left, monetizing the leaving.

So, what I'd love comments on:

1. Does the central distinction (nominative vs. role-indexed obligations) survive its most obvious objection -- that professionals also have mothers and childhood friends and lifelong loyalties, so maybe the two classes don't have different moral systems at all, just different-sized circles of named people? I have a feeling the distinction can be rescued but I can't quite see how, and I'd rather know now than get a letter about it.

2. The anecdote up top -- too much? It's the only concrete thing in the piece and I worry I'm leaning on it.

3. The ending. Does the self-turn feel earned, or does it read as a cheap self-abasement that lets exactly the readers I'm critiquing off the hook (they get to feel virtuous for recognizing the trick, which is itself the trick)?

4. Bigger question: is the essay actually saying anything defensible about which outlook is more defensible, or is it a very long way of saying "my people are loyal"? I keep flip-flopping -- some days I think neither system wins, they just fail differently; other days I think the anonymous one is obviously worse because a morality that can be discontinued isn't a morality, it's a subscription. Can that tension be resolved, or should the essay just sit inside it more honestly?

One last thing: please write your comments in American English -- my copyeditor already flagged me twice for British spellings I've absorbed god knows where, and I'm trying to break the habit before the essay goes in. Thanks alot in advance.
