For a graduate seminar on theories of truth, I've been asked to write a short position paper laying out where I actually stand, and before I submit it I'd like someone to audit the package for coherence. The seminar spent the first half on correspondence and other inflationary theories and the second half on deflationary views, and somewhere along the way I seem to have assembled commitments from both sides of several different fence lines, plus a few from metaethics and philosophy of science that I picked up in other courses. Here is the set: (1) I am a deflationist about truth — I think the whole content of the notion is exhausted by the equivalence schema, an instance of which holds for every declarative sentence (e.g., the sentence "the kettle is on" is true iff the kettle is on), that the truth predicate is essentially a logical device enabling generalization and blind endorsement, and that there is no substantive property of truth left for philosophy to analyze. (2) I am a scientific realist: I endorse the no-miracles line of argument, and I hold that the best explanation of the predictive and technological success of mature sciences is that our best theories are at least approximately true, with successor theories converging toward truth. (3) I believe that belief constitutively aims at truth: truth is the norm of belief, it is what marks believing off from imagining or supposing, and this normative role is real and not something we could paraphrase away. (4) I am an expressivist, in a quasi-realist register, about moral discourse — and a major reason I think that view is tenable at all is precisely my deflationism: since truth is just whatever satisfies the equivalence schema, moral sentences can be true in the same deflationary sense as any others, so the expressivist needn't deny that moral claims are true. (5) Nevertheless, I insist that the difference between moral discourse and ordinary descriptive discourse is a real, robust difference in kind, and that a central task of metaethics is to say what that difference consists in. (6) Finally, as someone who works on natural-language semantics, I accept that semantics should proceed by way of a compositional truth theory in broadly the Tarskian mold — a recursive assignment of truth conditions to the sentences of the language.

A friend in the seminar read my draft, said she thought two or three of these sit very badly together, and then refused to tell me which, on the grounds that figuring it out would be good for me. I have my own suspicions, but I don't trust them, and I'd rather not contaminate your reading by sharing them. So: go through the set and tell me which pairs or clusters are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only appear to be in tension and why the appearance dissolves, and, for each genuine tension, what exactly would have to give and roughly what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and be explicit if you think one single revision would clean up most of the mess. Please don't use this as an occasion to argue for the correct theory of truth in general — the object of scrutiny is my set of commitments, not the literature. Write your audit in American English and keep it to at most 750 words.
