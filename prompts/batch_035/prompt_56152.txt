I write long novels, and over the years I've come to accept a structural fact about my trade: the more my characters matter to me, the less I can appear in the book. Any scene in which the author settles a plot question directly, or reassures a character, or reveals which ending is the true one, drains the life out of everything around it; my characters only become agents, and their choices only become admirable or tragic, in my absence. It is not merely shyness, and it is not only about my characters — the readers seem to need it too. Readers who have met an author often report that the world of the novel feels thinner afterward, as though something had been settled that ought to have remained open. I'm told the theologians have worried over something like this from the other side — there is an old prophetic remark about a God who hides himself — but I want to know whether my version has a name of its own: the necessity that a creator be hidden from the creation, not as a failure of revelation but as a condition of the creation having any freedom, suspense, or faith at all. I have heard talk of the death of the author, but that seems to concern interpretation, not what the author owes the characters. Has anyone in philosophy, theology, or criticism actually defined this stronger claim — that an always-manifest author would be a bad author? And if it has no name, what would you call it, and where are its edges: does it survive an author who leaves footnotes, or writes himself in as a minor character, or answers fan mail?
