I keep meeting people who claim we live at the hinge of history: a small window whose choices shape more of the future than any other period's ever did or will. Before I argue about whether it's true, I want the spec for what would make the claim evaluable at all. Write the desiderata that any adequate formulation of the hinge hypothesis must meet. Include at least: how it must fix the comparison class and the measure of influence, so that the verdict doesn't just follow from whichever definition its proponents happen to find convenient; which trivializations it must rule out — for instance, that the present automatically wins any contest over who affects the future most, since later people by definition act on a world the present has already shaped; how it should handle the evidence asymmetry between the past, which has records, and the future, which has none; and whether the claim must be detectable from inside the hinge or whether that demand is incoherent, because no one could tell a hinge was a hinge until it had closed. Also say how the hypothesis avoids collapsing into the truism that every generation believes itself decisive. For each requirement, give the case behind it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference specific to longtermists, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the whole list turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so plainly, since that would itself be the finding. Close with a few sentences each on how the standard-issue version (we have nuclear weapons, AI, and climate change, therefore now) and the deflationary version (every era thinks it is the hinge, and the thought is mostly self-flattery) fare against the list.
