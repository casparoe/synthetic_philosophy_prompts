Me and my flatmate Maya spent the whole of last night going round and round on Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma — we argued through the washing up and then for another hour after — and we've reached the point where I can't even tell what the actual disagreement is, just that she thinks she's obviously right. So I want to practise against her side, played properly. Quick setup in case you need it (it's from his Scientific American column, reprinted in Metamagical Themas): an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people, forbids any communication between them, and announces that if exactly one of them sends him a reverse-charges telegram by noon the next day, that one person gets a billion dollars; if two or more telegram, or nobody does, no one gets anything. Hofstadter's superrational answer is that each of the twenty should privately simulate a roll of a fair twenty-sided die and send a telegram only if it lands on 1 — decent chance of exactly one telegram, everyone gets an equal shot at the billion.

So: play Maya. A committed superrationalist, sharp, absolutely not a strawman, allowed to concede small points to me but never the core claim, stay in character till I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. Here's my opening move: it seems to me the die roll gives your entire game away. The pitch for superrationality was always that perfectly rational thinkers facing the same problem will converge on the same one right answer — that's why you co-operate in the prisoner's dilemma, it's not a choice at all, it's just the answer. But Platonia has no such answer: all twenty people reasoning identically either all telegram or all don't, and both are disasters. So the best you can do is inject randomness and hope the dice decorrelate you — but that's just a mixed-strategy equilibrium wearing a fancy hat, no? You haven't replaced game theory, you've conceded that in the one scenario Hofstadter himself designed to showcase superrationality, it delivers no determinate advice beyond "roll dice and pray". And before you lean on the prisoner's dilemma again: it only comes out clean there because the symmetric answer happens to be deterministic, which strikes me as luck rather than theory.
