I spent forty years as an experimental physical chemist before retiring, and in the last few years I have been working my way through epistemology as an enthusiastic amateur. I have settled on a position I'd like stress-tested. In the lab, higher-order evidence was simply part of due diligence: if a colleague told me the NMR instrument had been miscalibrated overnight, it would be madness to run with yesterday's pristine spectrum because "it still looks like good data to me." Translating that into the philosophers' terms, I hold that epistemic akrasia is always irrational: whenever your evidence that your first-order evidence is misleading or unreliable (in that instance) is itself any good, you are obliged to revise, even when you can see, from the inside, that the first-order evidence still favors the original conclusion. What would count against this view? I want more than the familiar finger-wagging that you can't both believe p and believe your belief in p is unreliably formed — I want to know what it is actually committed to that might be unacceptable: cases it cannot describe (e.g., a weather forecaster with impeccable calibration data who is told, on the basis of evidence she knows to be weaker than her own models, that she got this one wrong), consequences about self-trust and epistemic humility it must swallow, and results in neighboring areas it stands or falls with. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them committed defenders of the view would concede as counting against it and which they would explain away, and tell me honestly whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that would be a defect in a view of this kind or just what doing epistemology looks like.
