I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a feature about the regress problem — the question of what, if anything, halts a chain of justifications from running on forever — and why it has taken on new life in an unexpected place: long reasoning chains produced by AI systems. When a model justifies a conclusion by citing intermediate steps, those steps were themselves produced by exactly the sort of process we are trying to validate, and the audit trail never bottoms out in anything a human has independently checked. For my piece, I'd like you to assess the three classical responses — foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism — as candidate accounts of what a genuinely sound machine-generated argument would have to look like: whether it must terminate in bedrock premises we verify by other means, whether the steps can instead stand by mutual support, or whether an endless-but-well-behaved descent is somehow tolerable. Rate each of the three on a 1–10 scale for promise in this role, with a few sentences of reasoning per rating, and identify which of the three you think its critics most underestimate.
