My closest friend from university now writes speeches for a mid-sized political party, and last night, over a long and eventually heated dinner, we argued about whether plain-language reform is a genuine lever against political deception or merely cosmetic. She has grown contemptuous of the current fashion for plain-language style guides, drafting rules, and editors imported to de-jargon official prose. Her clinching move was to quote Orwell: "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity. When there is a gap between one's real and one's declared aims, one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms, like a cuttlefish spurting out ink." Her point, as I understood it, was that the jargon is a symptom: the gap between real and declared aims is the disease, and as long as that gap exists, the cloudy prose will simply regenerate — so the style guides treat symptoms, and worse, they let the insincerity hide behind clean sentences. I had no equally quotable counter, but I left with the itch that the sentence was being made to carry more weight than it can bear.

Here is what bothers me. In the same essay, Orwell writes that "In our time, political speech and writing are largely the defence of the indefensible," which makes the insincerity sound structural rather than a matter of any individual's bad faith — the euphemisms exist because the facts cannot be faced, and nobody's personal honesty is going to change that. Yet he also says, early in the essay, that "the point is that the process is reversible," and later that "if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought" — which is the exact premise underwriting the style guides my friend despises: work on the language end and you work, a little, on the thought end. So the insincerity slogan sits inside an essay that appears to hold both that prose goes bad because politics is dishonest and that cleaning up one's prose is a real, non-frivolous step toward political regeneration. Whether my friend wins the argument depends entirely on which of these the sentence is actually claiming, and I could not sort it out on the spot.

What I would like, then, is a disambiguation of that one sentence laid out as a menu of distinct precise claims, with a verdict on each. At minimum, I want to see separated: readings on which it is a causal claim about individual psychology (a writer's bad faith produces the cloudy prose); readings on which it is a structural claim (an institution whose real aims diverge from its declared ones will produce evasive prose no matter who writes for it); readings on which it is merely diagnostic (bad prose is evidence of insincerity, committing us to nothing about what causes what); and any stronger readings on which insincerity is necessary, or sufficient, for the corruption of political prose. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether the rest of the essay supports it, and — this is the part that matters most to me — whether it actually delivers my friend her conclusion, that plain-language reform is useless or a cover for dishonesty, or whether it instead collapses into the reversible-process reading she was arguing against. Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — one sentence saying, in effect, which reading is doing the work in her argument and whether it survives — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
