At a dinner a fortnight ago I watched two friends argue about a puzzle, and I have not been able to settle in my own mind who was right. The arguers were Daniel, who works as a statistician, and Priya, who is doing a doctorate in philosophy of science; I was a bystander throughout and will try to report their exchange without tilting it either way.

The puzzle was Priya's. A hiker, Lena, is separated from her group at dusk in an unfamiliar valley, without a map. She does remember one thing the guide said before they set out: by the end of the route the group would be north of the ridge rather than south of it, with odds of about four to one. Lena accordingly takes herself to have credence 0.8 that she is north of the ridge and 0.2 that she is south. From a high point she gets a signal to a warden, who tells her that if she is on the northern side, the nearest roof is three times as likely to be the mill at Harper's Cross as the hikers' hostel. For the sake of the puzzle, two further facts: the hostel is run by the southern district but stands just north of the ridge, so if Lena is north she could plausibly be near either landmark; and if she is south, the warden's remark tells her nothing — nor did her prior, which was never divided at that grain within the southern mass. The question Priya put to the table was what credence Lena should now give to being near the hostel.

Daniel went first and said this was routine. The warden's remark is evidence, and evidence is a proposition, so one conditions on it. He proposed reading the remark as the disjunction that either Lena is south of the ridge or she is north and near the mill, worked through the arithmetic as a one-liner, and concluded that her credence in being south should rise from 0.2 to 0.25, since on his reading the remark is automatically satisfied in the southern case and is therefore, strictly, confirming evidence for the south. Priya replied that this was a reductio of the reading rather than a solution: a material conditional is true whenever its antecedent is false, so a remark addressed entirely to the northern contingency ends up raising Lena's credence that she is in the south, which no one who actually heard the warden would take it to do. Her own view was that conditionals are not propositions with truth conditions that one can conditionalise on; the remark works instead as a constraint on the posterior — after the call, Lena's conditional credence that she is near the mill, given that she is north, should be three quarters — and the southern 0.2 should be left untouched, since the remark spoke only to the northern contingency, with all the revision happening inside the northern 0.8, which the remark now splits three to one.

Daniel's rejoinder had two parts. First, a constraint is not yet a rule: infinitely many posterior distributions satisfy Priya's constraint, and her appeal to revising minimally is empty unless she says what measure of distance between prior and posterior is to be minimised. Second, her own account leaves Lena's credence in being near the hostel anywhere between a fifth and two-fifths, depending on how the untouched southern mass is subdivided, and a rule of rationality that answers a question with a range is not an answer but an admission that the theory has nothing to say. Priya's closing reply was that this admission is the finding: the warden's remark is genuinely incomplete evidence, which fails to determine a unique rational posterior, and a theory that manufactures uniqueness — as Daniel's conditioning does, at the price of the absurd conclusion that the remark confirms the south — is worse than one that locates the indeterminacy honestly. She mentioned in passing that the case is a cousin of the Judy Benjamin problem posed by van Fraassen, and generalised the moral: not every input to belief revision is a proposition to be conditioned on; some are constraints that leave rational belief underdetermined. Daniel's last word, half in jest, was that an epistemology with that property holds whatever one pours into it.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. Say who is right at each point and why — about the material-conditional reading and the vacuous-truth effect that produces the confirmation of the south, about Priya's claim that the remark is a constraint rather than evidence and her treatment of the southern mass, about whether her position is too underdetermined to count as a rival rule, and about Daniel's rejoinder that it holds whatever one pours into it. Then give an overall verdict: is Priya's concluding claim that rational credence after such an input need not be uniquely determined, and that this reflects the situation rather than a failure of the theory, actually right, or does it give up too much? And if she is right, say what a theory of rational belief change should do with inputs of this kind — not just what it should refrain from doing. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a long letter, and please use British English throughout.
