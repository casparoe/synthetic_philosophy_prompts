I'm a science and ideas writer, and for the past few years a lot of my beat has been climate economics, which means I keep ending up in conversations about discount rates and eventually had to admit I don't actually know what I think about discounting the future. So I did what I do with climate contrarians: I sat down and wrote out my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, hoping a pattern would fall out. It half did, and now I need someone to actually do the work of extracting the principle, because I can't get there myself. Here are the verdicts, honestly held: (1) Me taking $100 today over a guaranteed, inflation-adjusted $115 a year from now feels fine — not maximally smart maybe, but not irrational and not immoral, it's my money and my life. (2) My friend who smokes because he discounts his own future self: also his business, no objection from me. (3) A government deciding between a pandemic-preparedness program that saves 100 lives this decade and an investment fund that would save 250 lives in the year 2100 — my gut says the first option wins, those near-term lives feel more pressing, but. (4) If you ask me point blank whether a child born in 2100 matters less than a child born next year, I say absolutely not, and I'd be embarrassed to write anything else. (5) Choosing a tax cut now over preventing a famine in 150 years to benefit people who don't exist yet feels close to monstrous, worse than ordinary fiscal irresponsance.

So there's the obvious split — I discount for myself, I refuse to discount for others — but it keeps fraying at the edges. Case 3 bugs me the most: I judged the near-term lives more urgent even though I deny, in case 4, that the later people matter less, and I can't tell if that's a real moral asymmetry or just that identifiable near-term victims are more vivid, which would be a bias and not a reason. There's also the flip side I worry about: a colleague told me that if you really refuse to discount the future at all, you're commited to a near-total savings duty — this generation scrimping so that the (probably much richer) people of 2300 can be even richer — and honestly that sounds wrong too, which maybe means my no-discounting conviction in case 4 isn't as solid as I claimed.

What I want from you is a principle, or a couple of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it in a story: something that says when discounting is legitimate and when it isn't, and why the self/other split (if that's even the right split) isn't just arbitrary. Please tell me which of my verdicts your favored principle can capture and which it has to overturn — I suspect case 3 goes, but maybe you can save it with the thought that the far-future benefit is less *certain* rather than worth less, and I'd want to know whether that rescue is principled or a dodge. Then run the principle on cases I haven't listed: my colleague's scrimping-for-2300 scenario, and one more I'll leave you to construct — something that would test the self/other split at the boundary, like a person making an irrevocable choice on behalf of their own much older future self, or a small community deciding for its own descendants. If two principles tie on my cases, tell me which extra consideration should break the tie, and don't be shy about telling me a verdict of mine is just wrong.
