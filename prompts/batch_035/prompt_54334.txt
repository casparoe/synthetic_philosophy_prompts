I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about whether game characters matter morally, going off my gut on cases. Mowing down pedestrians in GTA for fun: feels fine, basically the point of the game. My horse in RDR2 dying because I rode it off a cliff: felt bad for days, way out of proportion to any real-world cost. Torturing an NPC who's scripted to beg, just to see what the devs put in there: feels wrong in a way the GTA stuff doesn't — not wrong to the character exactly, more like I'm doing something creepy to myself. Deleting Animal Crossing villagers I've talked to every day for a year: sad but not wrong. Drowning a Sim: classic, fine. A game that tells you up front its NPCs are conscious and suffer, with no other gameplay difference: I genuinely don't want to play it, though I can't tell if that's the fiction working on me or actual moral concern. And reloading a save after a companion dies: completly dissolves the guilt, even though "just undo it" is obviously not a response to real harm. Is there one principle that captures all this or close to it? It can't be sentience, since none of these differ on that. My best guess is something about what my actions express about me versus what they do to the character, but then the conscious-NPC game is a problem — by that logic the fiction of suffering shouldn't say anything about me that GTA doesn't already. Tell me what principle fits, which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, a mod that adds the same begging-and-pleading reactions to enemies in a shooter where they normally just grunt.
