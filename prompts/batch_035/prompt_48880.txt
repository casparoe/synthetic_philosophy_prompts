Quick bit of background so you know who you're dealing with: I'm a retired physical chemist -- thirty-odd years in catalysis and surface science -- and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of science and, more recently, the AI safety literature, which is honestly the most fun I've had since grad school. I've ended up holding a bunch of views about what the interpretability people call "mechanistic anomaly detection," and I want to know wether they actually fit together before I embarrass myself at my reading group (an online AI-safety group that tolerates old farts). The question lurking under all of it, I suspect, is what counts as "the same mechanism" -- which I gather is also an old fight in philosophy of science, so you get to do both jobs at once.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. You can never certify a system safe from input-output behavior alone. Two networks can match outputs on every test set you've got while doing it by different internal means, and the difference can matter -- one of them can be one weird input away from doing something awful. Safety evaluation has to look inside the box. This feels basic to me from catalysis: two catalysts with the same measured conversion today can diverge badly the day you feed them an impurity.

2. "Same mechanism" can't mean same weights or same neurons. Permute the hidden units and it's obviously the same network doing the same thing. Fine-tune it, distill it into a smaller net, run it on different hardware -- I want to say it largely keeps its mechanisms. Mechanisms are individuated by causal role, by what they contribute to the overall behavior. (Multiple realizability, if I'm using that term right.)

3. The definition of anomaly I've settled on: an input is in-distribution if and only if it's processed by the mechanisms that produced good performance on the training distribution; it's anomalous if it recruits something that wasn't selected for during training. That's my gloss of what the ML people mean, and I find it pretty natural.

4. I'm a hard reductionist about the network itself: everything true of it is fixed by the actual parameter values and the actual forward pass. There is no further fact about "which mechanism is running." Whenever interpretability talk drifts too far from the weights I get itchy -- I've seen where loose higher-level talk leads in my own field.

5. In practice the detector would be a trained probe: a small network reading the big network's activations, trained to seperate in-distribution examples from whatever anomalies you can cook up. I'm betting this counts as a genuinely mechanistic detector and not just another behavioral gadget.

6. But I also think a deployed model can legitimately pick up a new trick mid-stream -- in-context learning, some reasoning pattern it settles into on a class of problems it never saw in training -- and that's normal operation, not an alarm. The detector can't cry wolf every time the model does something novel.

So: is this a consistent package? I have a feeling something's off but I honestly can't locate it. My instinct is that (4) is the embarrassment -- that it's flat-footed of me, and I should make peace with some more abstraction and keep the rest -- but I'd rather have a real audit than my own hunch.
