In a philosophy of history seminar we were arguing about whether historical explanation needs laws. Someone sketched the covering-law picture — to explain an event is to subsume it under a general law plus initial conditions — and another student waved it off with "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes," as though that settled it: no laws in history, only loose patterns, so historians explain by exhibiting the rhyme. It struck me afterward that the sentence could be doing several quite different jobs, and I can't tell which one is load-bearing. Could you lay out the distinct claims the slogan might be expressing — from the trivially true (no event recurs in every particular) through the substantive (recurrence at the level of structures or configurations, statistical regularities without strict laws, historians' practice being analogy-laden narrative rather than derivation) to the contentious and possibly false — and for each reading say whether it's plausible and whether it actually undermines the covering-law demand or supports explanation-by-analogy? I'd especially like to know which reading is doing the work when the same student, ten minutes later, explained the outbreak of a war by comparing it to an earlier one: does the slogan, on any of its readings, license *explaining* by rhyme, or only describing? Please lay the readings out as an ordered menu I can bring back to class.
