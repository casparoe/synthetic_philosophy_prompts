I've been reading the rationalist corner of the internet for over a decade now — the blogs, the forums, the endless comment threads about how to resolve apparent disagreements — and the house style there, when a dispute starts to smell verbal, is to taboo the contested word: stop using it, spell out what you actually mean in more concrete terms, and watch the disagreement evaporate. I've recommended that move myself more times than I can count. But I keep running into a class of disputes where it fails in an interesting way, and I'd like to know whether anyone has properly named and studied the class.

Here are two instances. The first: I recently watched two chemists argue over whether heavy water — the stuff where the hydrogens are replaced by deuterium — should count as water. Both knew every relevant microphysical fact; there was no experiment either could cite. When they tried to taboo the word, the disagreement didn't dissolve; it reappeared one level down as a fight about whether the ordinary word had, all along, been pinned to the molecular structure or to the surface role — the clear liquid that fills the lakes and falls as rain. Each could point to real usage on his side. It seemed to be a factual question about the history of a word's links to the world, fixed long before anyone knew about isotopes, and unanswerable from inside either speaker's head. The second instance is smaller but stranger: a patient tells her doctor she has arthritis in her thigh, and she is wrong — not because of anything about her leg, but because of a fact about how her linguistic community uses the word, a fact she never learned and had no way of knowing. Her sentence is false in a way no amount of introspection on her part could ever reveal.

The general pattern, as best I can state it: whether an utterance is true can depend on facts about the world and about the speaker's community that the speaker has no epistemic access to, and this generates disputes that are neither empirical disagreements (no new observation would settle them) nor merely verbal ones (the parties share usage; neither is free to stipulate). The rationalist toolbox — taboo, operationalise, dissolve — presupposes that once the facts are shared and the words unpacked, nothing is left over. Here something is left over, but it isn't a fact about physics; it's a fact about which chunks of the world our words have been pinned to all along.

Does this configuration have a name? I'd also like to know how it relates to the things it is nearest to without being identical to: the ordinary merely-verbal dispute; the case where the parties are really making a disguised normative proposal about how a word ought to be used (I half-remember some philosophy-of-language term for that, though I can't recall it); and whatever the older literature says about words attaching to things through causal chains rather than through descriptions held in the head. I don't want just a label; I want the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, which neighbouring concept, if any, it collapses into, where the boundary runs, and what happens at the edges (for instance, if the two disputants come from communities whose usage has always diverged, does the disagreement become merely verbal again?).
