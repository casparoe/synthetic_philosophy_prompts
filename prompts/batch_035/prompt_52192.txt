You are a philosopher of language with a working knowledge of computational linguistics. Please think carefully, and begin your answer with a short brainstorm of candidate interpretations before settling on your reconstruction.

Here is the practice I want you to analyze. For roughly two decades, the field of machine translation evaluated its systems primarily with automatic metrics like BLEU: compare a system's output to one or more reference translations written by humans, and score the output by how many word sequences of length one to four it shares with the references, with a penalty for outputs that are too short. A system that produced a beautiful, accurate paraphrase would score lower than one that reproduced the reference's exact phrasing. Meanwhile, human evaluation — used less often because it is expensive — typically asked bilingual raters to judge "adequacy" (is the meaning preserved?) and "fluency" (is it good prose?), and translation departments and literary translators insisted that translation quality simply cannot be separated from questions of intent, register, and what a sentence is doing to its reader. The gap was easy to see in practice: systems that topped the leaderboards still garbled idioms, defaulted to stereotyped gender assignments when the source language left gender unmarked, and produced technically word-accurate renderings of poetry that lost everything that made the poem a poem — a point Douglas Hofstadter pressed forcefully in his criticisms of statistical and neural translation.

What theory of meaning does automatic metric-based evaluation of translation presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit view. Candidates you might consider, refine, or reject: that meaning is exhaustively determined by distributional similarity to attested usage; that translation quality supervenes on surface string overlap given a fixed source; a behaviorist or verificationist view on which understanding is nothing over and above matching observable input-output correspondences; or the view that evaluation metrics don't presuppose any theory of meaning at all, because they are merely proxies correlated with quality, in the way a thermometer's mercury column need not presuppose a theory of heat. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: would, say, a truth-conditional, a use-theoretic, and a purely instrumentalist stance on meaning all license the practice equally? Also address the tension internal to the field: practitioners acknowledged that the metric diverged from human judgment at the margins, yet publication, hiring, and funding decisions were driven by the metric anyway. Does that pattern presuppose that translation quality has a dominant scalar component that the metric captures, or does it presuppose something about institutions rather than about meaning — and can those two readings be kept apart? Finally, say what would have to change in evaluation practice if the field instead committed to the view that understanding is required for genuine translation, and whether the recent shift toward human preference judgments and LLM-as-judge evaluation actually amounts to that change or just relocates the same proxy problem.
