My epistemology reading group has spent a whole semester on the fight between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment (the interest-relative invariantist kind), and the two most committed members have finally agreed to write an adversarial collaboration document together instead of talking past each other every week. Draft that joint statement for them.

The doc should have a numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign — about the bank-style and airport-style cases, about lottery cases, about the fact that practical stakes seem to matter to knowledge attributions somehow, about what a plain invariantist still owes us. But nothing vacuous: each claim should be one a hardline classical invariantist would refuse to sign, so we can tell the agreement is real.

Then a disagreements table, with each remaining disagreement phrased so its own side accepts the wording. In particular I want them to state clearly that the contextualist's shift is supposed to happen in the semantics of "knows," as a function of the attributor's conversational context, while the encroacher's shift happens in the knowledge relation itself, as a function of the subject's practical environment — and that these two things come apart when the attributor and the subject are in different practical situations. So the doc has to include our own test case, which we're weirdly attached to: a notary in a low-stakes office processing a stack of routine affidavits signs one attesting that a client's lottery ticket is not a winner, while that client is sitting across the desk deciding whether to drive four hours to claim a prize. Same ticket, same evidence available to both people, low-stakes notary, high-stakes client. Both sides have to write down, on paper, whether the notary knows the ticket lost and whether the client does — and, more importantly, whether their own theory's verdict depends on whose mouth the word "knows" is coming out of and what conversation it's being said in.

Then the cruxes. I suspect one is whether the contextualist can stretch "attributor's context" far enough to include the subject's practical environment that contextualism and encroachment become indistinguishable — i.e. whether part of this fight is verbal — and if so the doc should say exactly which part is verbal and which part isn't. My bet is the verbal part hides a real fight about whether knowledge, as a state of a person, is interest-dependent, which is not a semantic question at all, but let them figure that out. Another crux might be the worry that contextualism only delivers shifts for contents someone actually bothers to attribute, and whether encroachment faces an analogue of that problem or dodges it.

For each crux, name a concrete case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance — we're running the cases next semester and I want it on record so nobody can weasel out afterward. If you think a chunk of their dispute is just about who gets to keep the word "contextualism," say so plainly.
