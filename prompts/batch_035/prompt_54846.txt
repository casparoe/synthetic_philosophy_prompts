Exam question:

The paradox of fiction is standardly presented as resting on three jointly inconsistent claims: (1) readers and spectators sometimes have genuine, full-fledged emotions toward fictional characters and events; (2) having an emotion toward something requires believing that the object of the emotion exists (or existed, or will exist); and (3) readers and spectators do not believe that fictional characters exist. Explain why these three claims appear inconsistent. Then evaluate the make-believe theorist's resolution, on which fiction-directed affective states are mere quasi-emotions: what the audience member imagines (for instance, that a monster is advancing) makes it fictional, in the game of make-believe surrounding the film, that she fears the monster, but she does not genuinely fear it. In your evaluation, be sure to (a) explain how the make-believe theorist distinguishes an adult who is gripped by a monster film while knowing full well that nothing on screen can harm her from a young child who watches the same film while believing the monster is actually in the room, and (b) present what you take to be the strongest objection to the make-believe view and assess whether it succeeds.

Student's answer:

The claims are inconsistent because (2) and (3) jointly imply that no one ever has emotions toward fiction, which contradicts (1): if emotion requires existence belief and we lack the belief, the emotion cannot occur. So at least one claim has to go, and the main resolutions correspond to rejecting each: rejecting (1) yields the make-believe or counterfeit view, rejecting (2) yields thought theory and similar views, and rejecting (3) yields illusionism, on which we briefly lapse into believing in the fiction while absorbed in it.

On the make-believe view, the adult watching the monster film imagines the monster pursuing her, and this imagining makes it true in the game of make-believe that she fears the monster, though she does not actually fear it. The distinction between adult and child is then sharp: the child believes the monster is present and so genuinely fears it, while the adult merely quasi-fears it, since only belief can generate genuine fear.

The strongest objection to the make-believe view is physiological. The adult's heart races, she grips the armrest, she may even leap from her seat. If her fear is only quasi-fear, the make-believe theorist is committed to saying that these bodily responses are only quasi-real as well, which is plainly false — a racing heart is a real racing heart. Moreover, if the adult genuinely felt nothing, we could not explain why she sometimes has to remind herself that it is only a movie: a person with no real fear would not need such reassurance. So the make-believe view overreaches, and the adult's fear must be genuine after all.

Thought theory avoids this by rejecting (2). Lamarque accepts the make-believe theorist's point that the child's fear is full-fledged while the adult's is not quite full-fledged fear — he too calls it a quasi-fear — but he insists that the quasi-fear is still a real affective state, because its object is not the nonexistent monster but the thought of the monster's approach, and thoughts are real. Thought theory also explains away the appearance that (2) holds elsewhere. We grieve for the dead even though we believe they no longer exist, yet our grief is real; so emotions plainly do not require belief in present existence, and pitying a fictional heroine is no different in kind from grieving for a departed friend. Finally, one might object that a mere thought is not a fitting object of fear or pity, but we do fear thoughts — for instance, the thought of failing an exam — so thoughts can be objects of emotion, and the objection fails. Hence the paradox is dissolved by rejecting (2), and the emotions we feel for fiction are fully real.
