Okay so I'm writing a term essay and I've gotten myself stuck, and I think the only way out is through, so let me lay it out.

Everyone who writes about Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day treats Stevens as a case study in self-deception or repressed love or English repression, take your pick. But rereading it this fall what jumped out at me is that the book is basically one long arguement about where reasons for action come from. So that's what I want to do: read the novel through the internal vs. external reasons debate -- Williams on the one side, McDowell and Korsgaard pushing back on the other -- and see whether the novel actually scores any points, or just muddies the water.

Here's the thing that's bugging me. Williams' story is that a reason has to connect to something already in your motivational set via sound deliberation. Fine. But Stevens' motivational set isn't just given, the way the papers tend to treat it -- it was manufactured, over decades, by his father, by the profession, by the whole ideal of the great English butler serving a great man. And the novel shows the set being maintained in real time: the two Jewish housemaids get dismissed, Miss Kenton is ready to resign over it, and Stevens does nothing and later can barely admit he regretted it. So was that because he genuinely had no reason to act, or because the reasons that would have moved anyone else were never allowed into the set in the first place? It looks like a hole in internalism that the novel dramatizes better than any article does: the theory seems to need the motivational set to be a fixed point that deliberation draws reasons out of, but in Stevens' case the set is something that gets built and policed, partly by the very habits of deliberation that are supposed to be neutral. Is that circular? Is it just adaptive preference in fancy dress? I'd like you to actually take a position on that, not gesture at it.

And then the ending, which I can't crack. The bit on the seafront where the stranger basically tells him to quit brooding over the past, since the evening's the best part of the day. People read that either as quiet consolation or as one final evasion. If external reasons exist -- reasons there for Stevens whether or not anything left in him can respond, like reasons to have gone to Miss Kenton while there was still time -- then the ending is him putting a kind face on a wasted life. If internalism is right, maybe the ending is genuinely the best available move for the man he now is, and there's literally nothing more to be said. I honestly can't tell which one the book wants, or whether it wants either. Take a shot at it.

For the scenes that do the work, I'd point at the 1923 conference with the American senator warning the assembled amateurs that they're dabbling in things beyond them, Stevens' father dying upstairs while Stevens stays at his post, and the parting at the bus stop in the rain. But you tell me which scenes carry the argument, and where the book resists the theory -- and where the theory, if we're honest, distorts the book. Also, don't let Stevens end up as just an illustration of Williams. If the novel adds something to the debate that the journal literature doesn't have, name it precisely; and if it doesn't, say that too. That's the part my supervisor will actually grade me on.

(There's also the 1993 film with Hopkins and Thompson but work from the novel, it's got way more room inside Stevens' head.) Sorry this got so long, I've clearly been living with this book too long.
