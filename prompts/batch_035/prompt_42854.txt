I lead the incident-response team for a large distributed online service — databases, caching layers, dozens of microservices, deployments several times a day. A few times a quarter we have a significant outage, and afterward I run a blameless postmortem whose central deliverable is a root-cause statement that engineers, my VP, and occasionally auditors will read, and on which engineering time gets allocated. The recurring problem is not that we lack candidate explanations; it is that we have no principled way to rank them. The meetings devolve into a standoff between people who want the one clean cause and people who want the full causal web, and the writeup ends up encoding whichever side was loudest.

What I want is a decision procedure that operationalizes Occam's razor in its formal version: the idea, from Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction, that a hypothesis's prior probability should fall with the length of its shortest description, together with whatever else from that literature is actually usable — for instance the minimum-description-length thought that comparing hypotheses is comparing the total length of the hypothesis plus whatever data it leaves unexplained. My engineers have no background in any of this, and the postmortem is a one-hour meeting within a day or two of the incident, when everyone is tired. So the conceptual work is the translation, and I want you to be explicit about it: which parts of the formal theory survive into a procedure (what the description length of a root-cause hypothesis is supposed to measure, and why the prior should fall with it), which parts must be dropped as uncomputable or undefined in practice, and how to fix the description language so that one cause versus two interacting causes is a measured difference rather than a rhetorical one. Say what the invariance theorem — the guarantee that the choice of language matters only up to an additive constant — actually licenses here, and where that consolation runs out, given that our hypotheses are short enough for the constant to dwarf them.

Then give me the procedure itself: the questions in order, in plain language; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a stopping rule for when to close the investigation and write down the ranking; and the points where the procedure hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or judgment rather than measurement. It must handle the standard traps: an explanation that is short in English only because it names a familiar villain; two candidates that are rewordings of each other; the pressure to flatten a ranked list into the single root cause the postmortem template demands.

Run the procedure on three incidents, with these facts supplied. First: at 2 a.m., an automated change raised a cache's eviction threshold within a minute of a database primary failing over to a replica; the site went down for forty minutes. Candidate A says the eviction change flooded the database with cache misses and the failover was the database's routine response to load. Candidate B says the failover stemmed from a replication bug unrelated to load, and the eviction change only slowed recovery. A is shorter to state. Does the procedure prefer it, and should it? Second: a single node returned corrupted data exactly once. The shortest explanation is that a cosmic ray flipped a bit — two words, no moving parts. The rival is a long chain: a garbage-collection pause let a health check expire, which forced a restart, which truncated a write ahead of its flush. The scoring here is the interesting part: description length in whose language, and what do we do about the fact that the bit-flip hypothesis compresses to two words only for people who have never had to verify one? Third: an intermittent failure that has now recurred three times, where the first two postmortems each closed with a simple, confirmed cause — a race condition, then a misconfigured timeout — and yet the same symptom returned. Should the procedure treat those past confirmations as data about how simple the true causes of our incidents tend to be, and hence as a reason to adjust the simplicity prior itself, or only rank hypotheses within the current incident?

Finally, be honest about the failure modes: where the procedure will misfire — I suspect it will be too kind to explanations with precedent in our own incident history, because those are cheap for us to describe — what it cannot do even in principle, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as measurement. At least one of the three cases above must come out with an answer I will not like; tell me which one, and why the procedure is still right to give it.
