I am a junior at a public high school, and I serve as one of two student members on a five-person conduct committee that hears disciplinary cases: cheating, plagiarism, harassment, misuse of school accounts. We meet roughly twice a month. Each hearing gets about thirty minutes of deliberation, the evidence is mostly testimony — statements from students and teachers, screenshots, sometimes nothing but flatly conflicting accounts — and every finding goes to the dean with a written rationale, with sanctions running from a reprimand to a recommendation of expulsion. I have a vote but not a veto, and the adults on the committee tend to defer to whichever student speaks with more confidence, which worries me.

In economics we just covered Adam Smith, and my teacher mentioned that before the book about markets he wrote a 1759 book about how people judge conduct, The Theory of Moral Sentiments. The ideas I took from it, and want to put to use, are these: that we approve or disapprove of someone by sympathy, by coming to feel some part of what they seemed to feel, and that we can only go along with a feeling when we can share it; that when I judge my own past conduct I split in two, the agent I was and the spectator I now am, and try to see what I did as a fair, well-informed stranger would see it; that this internal judge can be corrupted, because we crave the approval of the actual people around us and can confuse the desire to be praised with the desire to be worthy of praise; and that punishment in particular is justified only at the level an impartial bystander could endorse, since spectators sympathize with an offender's suffering only up to a point.

Please write a procedure the committee can actually run in a hearing. It should give the questions to ask, in order; what counts as enough of an answer to move on; the points at which the procedure must stop and say that this one is a judgment a human has to make and own, rather than pretending to compute it; and a short set of self-checks a member can use to tell whether they are consulting the impartial spectator or just the room — the parents waiting outside, the dean, the loudest person at the table. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a senior with a scholarship at stake who copied two paragraphs into a paper and, when first asked, said the source was common knowledge; (2) a ninth grader, disliked by half the school, who sent one cruel message about a classmate to a group chat; and (3) a case with no witnesses where two students give opposite accounts of who shoved whom, one of them a friend of mine. I want at least one of the worked cases to come out somewhere I won't like, and I want you to be honest about what the procedure cannot do — especially whether Smith's apparatus gives any answer at all when sympathy itself is the problem, as when the whole committee feels warmly toward a student who did something serious.
