My philosophy-of-mind seminar has been arguing about pain asymbolia, and one classmate keeps leaning on an analogy I want to see audited. He says pain is essentially a smoke detector: the tissue damage is the fire, nociception is the sensor, the unpleasantness is the alarm's blare, and avoidance behavior is the evacuation. A patient with pain asymbolia, on his telling, has a detector that still senses and still blares, but the blare has been disconnected from the evacuation — and since nobody would say an unheeded smoke detector is bad for the building's occupants, the felt sensation of pain is not itself bad for you; all the badness lives in the distress and the flight it normally produces, which would be welcome news for the view that pain's phenomenal quality is a mere signal with no intrinsic disvalue. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than the argument can carry. Please lay out what maps onto what, identify where the mapping breaks down (think about whether the blare of an alarm is bad for someone forced to listen to it for hours even if they can't leave, whether a signal we can't swap for a different one is like a detector at all, and what the analogy silently assumes about who or what the badness is bad for), and then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the disanalogies actually support the opposite view that the sensation itself is what's bad. If there's a better analogy for the asymbolia cases, say what it is.
