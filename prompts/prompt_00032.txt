I'm working through a philosophy of science seminar unit on laws of nature, and I keep getting tangled up in a distinction that my professor treats as obvious but which I don't fully grasp. On one hand, there's the Humean "best systems" approach (à la Lewis) where laws are just the axioms/theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in summarizing the Humean mosaic of particular matters of fact. On the other hand, there's a broader category of anti-Humean or "governing" conceptions of laws, but within that category, people seem to lump together some pretty different views: strong necessitarian accounts where laws are relations between universals (Armstrong, Dretske, Tooley), dispositional essentialist accounts where laws are grounded in the causal powers of properties themselves (Bird, Ellis), and even primitivist accounts where lawhood is just a further irreducible fact tacked onto the mosaic (Maudlin).

What I want is a comparison that goes beyond the standard "Humean vs. anti-Humean" scoreboard of objections (the problem of induction, the "unconnected necessitation" worry, etc.). Specifically: is dispositional essentialism actually a distinct third option, or is it best understood as a species of anti-Humeanism that collapses into either necessitarianism or something surprisingly close to Humeanism once you press on what "grounding" laws in dispositions really amounts to? And relatedly, does Humeanism about laws necessarily commit you to Humeanism about causation and counterfactuals, or can you be a Humean about laws while being a non-Humean elsewhere in your metaphysics? I'd like to understand where the real dividing lines are, rather than just the textbook contrast between "laws that describe" versus "laws that govern."
