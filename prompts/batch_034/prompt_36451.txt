I teach a mid-size seminar on the ethics of communication (18 students, one 75-minute session, no budget for real stakes). Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the distinction between lying and misleading before we name it — ideally the game's rules should pressure them into choosing between an outright lie and a carefully engineered misleading-but-true statement, so that whatever pattern of choices emerges becomes the data for the debrief. Something with asymmetric information and a modest payoff would work: paired transactions where one side knows a defect the other side would pay to know, and the payoff scheme rewards successful deception without distinguishing the two routes to it. The debrief should then map moments in the game onto the philosophical question: whether the felt difference between lying and misleading tracks a morally real difference or merely a convention we exploit to keep our hands clean, and whether the hearer who declines to ask the obvious follow-up question thereby assumes some of the responsibility for being misled. Please include rules, materials, a timeline, the payoff scheme, a facilitator script for the debrief, an honest note on what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to handle it, and a frank statement of which feature of the lying/misleading distinction the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture. Do not mention Kant, or the liar paradox, or any literature by name — students should arrive at the positions themselves and only get the labels afterwards.
