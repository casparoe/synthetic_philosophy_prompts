So here's my situation: I teach ancient philosophy and ethics at a large state university, and like everyone else on staff I'm required to attend the annual "resilience training" HR puts on. If you haven't sat through one, the format is pretty standard by now: there's a facilitator, a slide deck, a "circle of control" worksheet where you sort your stressors into things you control, things you merely influence, and things you don't, and a lot of talk about how the event isn't the problem, your interpretation of the event is. Ours ended with a breathing exercise. I obviously have a professional interest here, since the whole thing is basically Stoicism 101 stripped of the physics and the ethics — the dichotomy of control plus the idea that disturbing emotions are judgments we're making about "externals" rather than features of the externals themselves.

I want to assign my students a paper on this, but I need to work out the question myself first, and I keep going back and forth. What I'd love your help with: what theory of the emotions, and of control, does this corporate practice actually presuppose? Is it the full Stoic line that stress is a judgment we could in principle revise, or something weaker? Which rival views does the workshop quietly rule out — for instance, the view that moods are more like perceptions, tracking devices that are responding to real features of the environment, in which case "your stress is your interpretation" is a bit like telling someone with a smoke alarm that the noise is the problem? And here's the wrinkle I find most interesting: the same institution that runs these workshops also adjusts workloads and staffing when burnout shows up in attrition data, which looks like it treats stress as evidence about conditions rather than as a malfunction in the employee's interpretive apparatus. So do the two practices presuppose inconsistent things, or is the workshop somehow compatible with both views?

Also, if you could say something about the middle ring of the worksheet — "things I can influence" — that's not in the original dichotomy, it's a later popularizer's amendment turning it into a trichotomy, and I suspect the vagueness of "influence" is doing real work, since whether your workload counts as influenceable is exactly the question the employer would rather leave unsettled. And then to close the loop: if we stipulated that the emotions-are-perceptions view is correct, what would actually have to change in the workshop — the content, who gets trained, what the intervention targets, or does nothing much change because the practice was never really relying on the Stoic theory in the first place? I'd like a proper reconstruction of the implicit commitments, an honest account of where the practice is underdetermined so that several theories of emotion could license it, and a verdict on the internal consistency question. Please answer in American English, and please don't bother with citations or a reading list — its for my own working notes, not for publication.
