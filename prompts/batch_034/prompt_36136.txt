I'm the research director for a constitutional reform commission in a mid-sized parliamentary democracy. The commission's mandate is to draft, within two years, a proposal for adding a sortition-based chamber to the legislature — a few hundred citizens selected by lot, most likely serving a single non-renewable term — alongside the existing elected chamber. We have money to commission roughly a dozen academic studies over the next eighteen months. My question is not whether sortition is a good idea, but which open questions we most need answered to produce a workable design, and in what order.

Please rank the research questions you would have us fund, and defend the ordering. The open questions I'm already aware of include the following, but you should add others if you think they matter more:

1. Self-selection. A lottery only delivers descriptive representativeness if the people drawn actually serve. If acceptance is voluntary and correlates with education, income, and civic engagement, the chamber is biased before deliberation begins. What compensation levels, duty framing (jury-style compulsion versus invitation), and opt-out designs best preserve the representativeness that is sortition's main selling point?

2. Facilitation and agenda capture. Members serve briefly and cannot accumulate expertise, so professional staff, facilitators, and expert briefers may end up exercising the real power. Is there any institutional arrangement that secures informed judgment without a permanent unelected elite effectively doing the thinking for a rotating amateur membership?

3. Mandate design. The chamber could hold binding legislative power, agenda-setting power only, or a veto over the elected chamber. How does each allocation make the other problems — capture, legitimacy, self-selection — better or worse?

4. Term limits and professionalization. The anti-career-politician case for sortition assumes the one-term lottery member is a virtue. But does a chamber of perpetual amateurs merely relocate career-politician pathologies to whichever actors are permanent: staff, parties, the civil service?

5. Legitimacy. Does a body nobody chose have the standing to make binding law, and does the answer change once citizens have lived experience with its output?

For each item you would fund, explain what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is within eighteen months (e.g., through mini-public pilots, survey experiments, or natural experiments such as jury service), and what evidence would move it up or down your list. Equally important, tell me which of these you would deliberately refuse to fund, and why — either because the answer would not change the design, or because it is a question for political philosophers to argue about for the next thirty years rather than one the commission's timeline can resolve.
