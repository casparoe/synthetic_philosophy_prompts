My closest friend from graduate school has, over the past decade, become a serious student of early Confucian thought, and she now does ethics entirely in role terms: for her, what a person owes is fixed by the relations that person stands in — the classical five of parent and child, husband and wife, elder and younger sibling, friend and friend, and ruler and minister — and by how those relations are actually lived. I have watched her hand down verdicts on little dilemmas for years, and I now have two new ones to put to her. Before I do, I want to know what to expect, and I would like your help reasoning it through. Here is her track record.

First, the settled verdicts. When her widowed mother's standing Sunday dinner conflicted with helping a good friend move apartments, she kept the dinner and held that the friend should understand; her mother is the person she is closest to in the world. When two friends sought comparable favors the same weekend — one a friend of twenty years with whom she speaks daily, the other a pleasant acquaintance of two years — she chose the older friend without hesitation. When her spouse's weekend claim competed with a comparable request from that same twenty-year friend, she met the spouse's claim and said the friend would understand; marriage, she remarked, is a different order of thing from friendship. When her mother's modest claim on an afternoon competed with her young son's equally modest claim — the boy wanted her at a school event, the mother wanted company at an appointment — she went with her mother and told her son that grandmothers come first on such afternoons. And when her younger brother, whom she talks with daily, had a claim on a holiday that collided with one from a friend she is fond of but sees only a few times a year, she chose the brother and thought the friend was asking a bit much.

Now the new cases, which I will put to her next week. In the first, her department head — someone she has worked under for a year, respects, but is not close to and does not much like — has asked her to stay late that evening on a matter of genuine institutional importance, though one that a colleague could cover with some effort. The same evening, her closest friend, the twenty-year one, is in real crisis and has asked her to come. In the second, her sister, with whom she has been estranged for a decade through no wish of her own, has let it be known that she needs help of a kind my friend could easily provide, and the same week her closest friend has asked for comparable help; both requests cannot be met.

I am less interested in her answers than in the rule she is implicitly running. So please tell me: what do you predict she will say about each of the two new cases; what rules fit her earlier verdicts equally well, and what each of those rules predicts about the new cases; which rule you would bet on and why; and — since the two new cases may not settle it — what further case, described concretely, would best separate the remaining candidate rules from one another.
