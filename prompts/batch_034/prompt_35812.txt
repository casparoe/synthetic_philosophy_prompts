I'm auditing an epistemology course, and after the seminar on the Meno two fellow students carried their argument out into the hallway, with me as a reluctant witness. The topic was why knowledge should be more valuable than mere true belief — Socrates's old observation that a traveler who merely happens to hold a true belief about the road to Larissa will get there just as surely as one who actually knows the way, so the extra ingredient in knowledge had better earn its keep. Maya opened by defending the value of knowledge along stability lines: a mere true belief is fragile, apt to be abandoned under pressure or to mislead on nearby questions, whereas knowledge, being anchored in understanding why, stays put and generalizes to related cases — which, she said, is exactly what Socrates was getting at with his image of true beliefs needing to be tied down lest they wander off. Jonas replied with what he called the swamping problem: suppose knowledge is true belief plus some further condition, whatever it turns out to be — justification, reliability, safety. For someone who cares about the truth of p, the value of a belief that p is exhausted by its being true; so the extra condition's value can only be instrumental, in getting you to true rather than false beliefs, and once the belief is already true that value is swamped. On any given occasion, he said, knowing that p is worth exactly as much as truly believing that p — like coffee from a reliably good machine tasting no better than the same coffee from a machine that got lucky. Maya's stability point, he added, collapses into the same trap: stability matters only because it keeps delivering truth over time, and in a one-shot case an unstable true belief does the job just as well.

Maya answered that Jonas was assuming the value of a whole state is the sum of the values of its parts, which she denied: knowledge is a kind of cognitive achievement — getting it right because of ability rather than luck — and achievements are not valued merely for their products. Someone airlifted onto a summit ends up in exactly the place a climber does, yet we don't conclude that the climb added nothing of value; the manner of arrival matters. Jonas had two replies ready. First, the achievement story seems to overgenerate: in fake-barn country, the traveler who photographs the one real barn among all the facades gets things right through perfectly good perceptual abilities, yet by intuition doesn't know — so knowledge needs an anti-luck condition after all, and it's the value of that condition, not of achievement, that's on trial, and the swamping objection applies to it with full force. Second, even if the achievement analogy succeeds, it shows at most that we value knowledge for reasons that have nothing to do with being a good guide to truth — which he called a concession that the original question, why knowledge is better than true belief for the agent who wants to get things right, has no answer. Maya's final word was that Jonas was running together practical value with epistemic value, and that the Meno problem is precisely the demand to show there is a distinctively epistemic axis of value in the first place; Jonas retorted that naming the problem is not solving it, and that a value no one can connect to anything anyone wants is a suspicious kind of value.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each substantive move — Maya's stability opening, Jonas's swamping objection including his treatment of stability, Maya's achievement reply, Jonas's two-pronged response about fake barns and practical value, and Maya's final appeal to a distinct kind of epistemic value — say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating. Please also indicate who comes out more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed that would move the debate forward.
