I work as the sole human-consent reviewer for a mid-sized university's research ethics board — mostly psychology experiments, some medical studies, maybe 30 protocols a month, and I have maybe 20 minutes per protocol before I have to flag it, pass it, or kick it to the full board. The board's written guidance basically just says consent must be "voluntary, informed, and competent," which is fine as a principle but useless to me on a Tuesday afternoon when I'm staring at a stack of applications. What I want is an actual procedure I can run: the checks in order, what a passing answer looks like for each, where the procedure ends in an automatic flag to the full board, and — this is the part our guidance never admits — where a check can't be settled by procedure at all and I just have to make a judgment call and say so.

The philosophical stuff I half-remember from a bioethics course years ago (validity conditions like adequate disclosure, competence, absence of coercion or manipulation, and whatever the difference is supposed to be between invalidating a consent and merely making it regrettable) should be in there, but translated into questions a non-philosopher like me can ask of a protocol. I especially want the procedure to handle the fuzzy middle cases, because those are exactly the ones I keep agonizing over: a psychology study that uses deception and only reveals it in a debrief afterward (can consent given in advance cover being lied to?); a study paying $200 for a few hours of mildly unpleasant tasks, which I suspect is fine for students but maybe coercive for people in real financial need; and a surgeon recruiting her own patients into her clinical trial, where the person obtaining consent has authority over the participant's future care. If your procedure could also tell me what to do about an elderly participant whose daughter answers all the screening questions "for" her, that would help, because right now I have no idea whether that's a competence problem or a coercion problem or neither.

Please run the finished procedure on those cases and show me the output — including at least one where it gives me an answer I won't like (I'm thinking of the surgeon one, since we've never rejected anything from that department and I doubt the board will thank me). And be blunt about where the procedure will misfire or where two reasonable reviewers following it could come out differently, since the whole point is that I stop pretending my gut instinct is a checklist.
