I teach philosophy of mind at a university in Manchester, and every year I end the module on levels of explanation with Hofstadter's careenium analogy from I Am a Strange Loop — you know the one, where the cranium becomes a giant frictionless billiard table with myriads of tiny marbles (simms? symms? I can never remember how he spells it) careening around, and the "simmballs" are the stable clumps those marbles keep forming and dissolving, which, when you zoom out, dance around like symbols that mirror the world outside the table. The whole point of the setup is his question about which level actually does the pushing inside the thing: the marbles are just obeying classical mechanics, and yet from the zoomed-out view it genuinely looks like the big patterns are calling the shots, steering their own substrate around. My students always nod along to this in the seminar and then, on the exam, write something bland about the whole being greater then the sum of its parts — which tells me the analogy isn't realy landing. So this year I want to try something different: instead of another lecture, I'd like to hand them a short story.

Could you write it for me? Roughly 2,500 to 3,000 words. The setting I have in mind: a retired physicist — or maybe a PhD student who's lost her funding, your call — builds a working careenium as a physical installation, actual magnetic marbles on an enormous frictionless table, with the walls' bounciness wired up to sensors in the world outside, and puts it in a gallery or an old snooker hall somewhere in the north. A sceptical visitor (a neuroscientist? the builder's old supervisor?) keeps insisting that all this talk of simmballs "doing" anything is just a convenient shorthand for very complicated marble trajectories, nothing more — and the story has to make that objection feel both technically correct and somehow completely beside the point. That tension is what I want my students to feel in their bones. Hard constraint: the marbles must never violate Newton — no cheating with quantum woo — so if there's any downward influence it has to be shown through the mechanics, not announced. And if you can earn it, I'd love a simmball that ends up representing the careenium itself, a kind of "I" of the table — but only if it's earned; I'd rather have no self than a mystical one.

One more bit of seeding, which I'm stealing and adapting rather freely: in my version of the setup, a marble's magnetism changes slightly each time it collides, so the table acquires a history — grooves, of a sort, that the patterns happened to throw off and that the marbles themselves then have to live with. There's something haunting about the substrate ending up constrained by the patterns it randomly produced, and if the story can dramatise that — long stretches where one family of simmballs dominates and reshapes the others — even better. Don't worry about being faithful to Hofstadter's own details, I'm not after a dramatisation of his book so much as a story that stands on its own feet but would send a student running back to it. British English throughout please (colour, realise, kerb, that lot). And if you could resist the temptation to explain the philosophy in a closing paragraph, I'd be grateful — let the table do the talking.
