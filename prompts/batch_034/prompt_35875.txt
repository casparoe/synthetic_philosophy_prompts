Next month our clinical ethics committee takes up a case that none of our precedents, as I read them, settles on its own. Here is our track record in the cases that seem closest:

First, we honored a written directive refusing hospitalization and antibiotics for a woman with advanced dementia; she had signed it after discussion with her physician, and by the time the decision arose she was calm, did not resist care, took pleasure in music and small attentions, but could no longer engage with a question about treatment.

Second, we declined to honor a man's single remark at a family dinner years earlier, to the effect that he didn't want to be kept alive in a helpless state; by the time the question arose he was similarly past engaging with it, and we judged the remark too casual and isolated to count as a considered refusal, even though the family insisted he had meant it.

Third, we honored a directive from a woman whose refusal of further life-prolonging treatment matched an abhorrence of dependence she had expressed her whole life; her family reported that she had grown gentler and seemed like a different person, but we treated the directive as speaking for the person whose life it was rather than deferring to the family's sense of who she had become.

Fourth, we set aside a blanket refusal of all treatment that had been signed as part of admission paperwork with no sign of reflection, and we honored it only after finding independent evidence that refusing treatment accorded with the patient's settled values.

The new case: a woman in her eighties with moderate dementia signed, some years ago, a specific and much-discussed written directive refusing a particular surgery should she ever develop the condition she has now developed. When the question is put to her directly today, she answers, clearly and repeatedly, that she wants the operation, and she appears to understand what is being asked in the moment; but an hour later she has no memory of the conversation, no memory of signing anything, and cannot say why she signed it. Her present assent is lucid, yet it floats free of any story she can tell about herself.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I'd like you to identify the rules that actually fit our four precedents — I can see at least three candidates (a rule on which a settled earlier judgment binds whatever the later self now wants; a rule on which an earlier refusal counts only as evidence of what she would choose now, rebuttable by her present expressions; and a rule on which her present wishes govern whenever she can form and express a preference, with the earlier self speaking only when that gate is closed) — and say what each predicts here and which precedent is the closest analog. Then commit to a recommendation: I want a definite decision from you, with your reasons, not a menu left for us to choose from. Finally, describe the further case — one a committee like ours might realistically face — that would most sharply divide the rules that fit our record, and say what each rule would yield on it.
