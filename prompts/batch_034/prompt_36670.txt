I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of art seminar this term, and our running dispute is what, if anything, makes something an artwork. The problem is that everyone in the seminar self-labels in ways that collapse the moment a real case is put on the table: the people who call themselves formalists in the abstract turn out to be artworld contextualists about readymades, and the self-described anti-definitionalists keep smuggling in a hidden "it has to mean something" condition. For the seminar's public-facing blog I've been asked to build a short diagnostic quiz titled, roughly, "which definition of art do you actually hold?" — one that sorts readers by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which theory they like the name of.

Please design the quiz as follows. First construct the answer space from the distinctions that matter for my purpose, and only then locate the named positions inside it: (a) whether something must be a product of an agent at all; (b) whether it must be intended to communicate or mean something; (c) whether it must have aesthetic properties in the object itself; and (d) whether the artworld's treatment of a thing is constitutive of its being art, or merely fallible evidence that it is. The quiz itself should have eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three plain-language answer options, and each phrased so a reader can answer without knowing which theory the answer favors — no question may just ask "is Duchamp's Fountain art?", since everyone has memorized the liturgy about Fountain; where you use the classic cases, twist them out of their canonical textbook presentation. The cases must include: an undetectable forgery that experts and laypeople find aesthetically identical to the original it copies; a natural object — say, a weathered stone that looks exactly like a Brancusi — shaped by no one and selected by no one; a chimpanzee's painting that a museum wants to acquire; an image produced by an AI system with no human selection at any stage, which hangs in a gallery; an object deliberately made by an artist and displayed in a gallery that has neither aesthetic merit nor discernible meaning; a painter's canvas buried in a crate at the artist's death and seen by no one for two centuries; and at least one case of your own invention that you think separates positions the classic cases leave fused. For every item, give three possible verdicts — art, art badly made, or not art at all — since that three-way split is where the seminar's fights actually live.

Then give a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves: the strong institutional view, the historical/narrative view, the aesthetic-functional view, the expression/communication view, the cluster account, and the anti-definitional option that no unified definition is available. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your key cannot really separate (I suspect the cluster account and a weak institutional view will both get scored from the same answers, since both want to say "yes, somewhat" to everything), and note any combination of answers that nobody on record defends, with a sentence on whether that gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or territory nobody has happened to explore. The entire deliverable — questions, options, and scoring key — must come in under 850 words, so keep the case descriptions to a sentence or two each.
