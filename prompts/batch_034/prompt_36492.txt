A couple years back I watched the footage of the Colston statue coming down in Bristol — Edward Colston, the slave trader whose statue went up in 1895, well over 150 years after he died, and protesters pulled it down in June 2020 and rolled it into the harbour. I've been mulling it over ever since, and recently stumbled into Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life (via youtube and a cheap paperback, I'm not studying this anywhere, so go easy). The three kinds of history he talks about clicked for me stright away: monumental (hold up great men to inspire the present), antiquarian (preserve and revere your past), and critical (drag the past to the bar of judgement and sometimes condemn it to destruction). It feels like the whole Colston saga is those three modes fighting each other — the Victorians who put the statue up doing a kind of monumental/antiquarian PR job, the defenders in 2020 saying heritage matters, the protesters doing critical history with a rope. But I want to know if that mapping actually holds up or whether I'm forcing it, because honestly I can't tell anymore.

Some specific things I keep going back and forth on. As I understand him Nietzsche cares mostly about whether history serves life — the health of individuals and cultures — so who exactly is the "we" whose life a statue in a city centre is supposed to serve? And his critical history seems to be about turning the knife on your own past that you can't live with, but he also warns that judging the past too harshly is dangerous because you're sitting in judgement on your own origins. Does it change the picture that the statue was a late Victorian political project rather than anything from Colston's own time? Then there's everything after — the empty plinth, the statue fished out and displayed at the M Shed museum lying down with the protest damage left visible, the arguments about what if anything goes up next, and that sculpture of the protester with her raised fist that appeared on the plinth for about a day before the council took it away. Is the damaged-statue-in-a-museum solution an attempt to hold all three of Nietzsche's modes at once, and would he call that health or just a new kind of clutter? And a weird reflexive thing I can't shake: I've read Nietzsche himself got monumentally used by the Nazis, so if his own thinking can be hijacked like a statue, what does that say about the safeguards he expects "life" to provide?

Answer in British English please, and don't just summarise the essay — I can get that anywhere. What I want is the actual mapping done carefully, then an honest account of where Nietzsche's framework goes quiet on the Colston case (collective memory, who gets a say, all that), and whether some other idea you'd bring in, doesn't have to be from Nietzsche, explains the bits he can't. Also be frank if you think I've misread him somewhere — my only source is me reading him on the bus.
