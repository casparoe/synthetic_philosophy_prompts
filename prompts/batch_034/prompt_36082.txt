I'm a novelist drafting a book whose central character is a household robot that has to make a small number of morally loaded decisions, and I want to get its reasoning right rather than import genre shortcuts. Before I invent anything, I'd like a rigorous reading of Asimov's I, Robot through the question of whether an AI system should reason as a utilitarian or as a deontologist. The Three Laws are a deontologist's architecture: fixed lexical constraints, no weighing. But the collection seems to know this — "Runaround" turns on priority conflicts among the Laws themselves, "Liar!" turns on what counts as harm to a human, and "The Evitable Conflict" ends with the Machines quietly abandoning rule-following for the utilitarian management of humanity's long-run welfare. Please tell me whether Asimov's stories, taken together, constitute an argument that deontological AI fails and fails instructively, or whether the stories quietly rely on deontological scaffolding the utilitarian ending never earns. I'd also like to know what the strongest deontologist's reply to the Machines' final position would be, and where in the collection the stories resist being read as philosophy at all. Be candid about the reading that doesn't work: I'd rather know which scenes are just puzzle-boxes than build my novel on a misreading of them.
