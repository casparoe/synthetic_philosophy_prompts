I run a small foundation that distributes performance-based grants to schools, and after a run of episodes in which our metrics were quietly defeated — attendance figures that spiked without any change in learning, dropout rates that "improved" through administrative reclassification — I have become something of a committed Goodhartian: whenever a measure becomes a target, sustained optimization pressure applied to it reliably degrades the correlation between the measure and the thing we actually care about. I hold this not merely as an empirical regularity about institutions (I have friends who quote Campbell's law and the McNamara fallacy at me as if they settled it) but as something closer to a structural truth about proxy optimization. Friends in AI safety have pushed me further, telling me the effect applies with special force to powerful learned optimizers, and they walk me through a rough fourfold taxonomy: regressional Goodhart, where the measure degrades even with no one intending to cheat, simply because selection on a noisy proxy favors lucky over truly good; extremal Goodhart, where the correlation that holds in the ordinary range breaks down in the tails the optimizer pushes into; causal Goodhart, where the act of optimizing changes the relationship between measure and goal; and adversarial Goodhart, where an agent deliberately exploits the gap between them. I have also absorbed the companion pessimism that replacing a gamed metric with a richer composite, or a rotating battery of metrics, merely buys time.

Recently, though, I have begun to worry that I have insulated the view. When a target does not get gamed, I tell myself the optimization pressure was too weak, or the proxy was close enough to the true goal, or the gaming simply had not started yet — and I notice I can never say in advance which excuse I will need, which is a bad sign. So here is my request: tell me what would actually count against Goodhart's law, understood as the general claim above. I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward: real or plausible cases where a hard, single-number target survived intense and prolonged optimization pressure without corrupting; structural reasons why some domains might be immune (measures that are causally upstream of the goal, or gaming costs that reliably exceed gaming gains, or goals so simple that the proxy simply is the goal); and the murkier cases where "gaming" and "genuine improvement" come apart less cleanly than my slogan assumes — a school that drills to the test and thereby really does teach more of what the test measures strikes me as a case where it is not obvious we have a metric failure at all. Please also keep separate what counts against the law as a universal, almost conceptual claim about proxies and what counts against it as an empirical generalization about when and how fast degradation occurs, since I suspect the view survives one kind of attack much better than the other. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the standard retreat — from "the measure will fail" to "the measure will fail given sufficient optimization pressure" — has converted my position into something unfalsifiable, and if it has, whether that is a defect in a law of this kind or an acceptable feature, as it arguably is for, say, the second law of thermodynamics.
