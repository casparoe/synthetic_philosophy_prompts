For the last two years I've shared a flat with Sam and Priya, and I chair our monthly flat meeting. Six months ago we got into a tangle over house rules that I still can't untangle, and I'd like help thinking it through from first principles rather than being handed a slogan.

The facts are these. Priya's boyfriend Dev stays over most weekends. He is quiet, tidy, never touches our things, and barely crosses my path. Sam nonetheless dislikes it intensely, and his objection is pointedly not about noise or mess — he concedes all that is fine. It's that he chose two flatmates, not three, and he says he cannot relax in a home where someone he never agreed to is regularly down the hall. Sam, for his part, keeps a tarantula in a terrarium in his own room. It has never left that room and never will; Priya could go years without seeing it. But she is genuinely frightened of spiders, and she says she sleeps badly just knowing it is on the other side of the wall.

At the meeting, both of them brought motions: Sam proposed capping overnight guests at four nights a month, and Priya proposed that the tarantula be rehomed. Each said, in effect, that the house should decide. Feeling unable to rank one grievance above the other, I took "everyone affected gets a vote" to be the fair procedure, and rather than side with either of them I voted yes on both motions. Both passed, two votes to one each time.

Here is what bothers me. Sam is devoted to that spider — he has kept tarantulas since he was a teenager — and is only moderately bothered by Dev. Priya treasures the weekend visits and is only mildly bothered by the spider. So the outcome we voted into effect leaves both of them strictly worse off than the status quo, and I cast the deciding vote both times. Worse, the obvious repair is blocked: I suggested a package deal — guests uncapped, tarantula restored, wipe the slate — and both refused. Sam says the meeting already ruled that the guest situation was the problem; he shouldn't have to surrender his pet to enforce a house decision. Priya says the same, with the roles reversed. The vote seems to have created a kind of entitlement that now stands in the way of undoing a decision that hurt its own winners.

I've since considered adopting a standing rule that nothing confined to a person's own room is ever votable — your room, your business. But I've noticed this wouldn't have addressed anything, because each grievance is precisely about what goes on in the other person's room, behind a closed door, with no effect anyone can point to. And Sam's retort is that such a rule hands permanent victory to whoever happens to be less bothered: nothing he cares about would ever come to a vote again, since Dev stays entirely within Priya's room. On the other hand, "put it to the house" seems to mean that a majority can always reach into someone's room, and in our case it did so to the benefit of no one at all.

So my questions are: Was the vote itself a mistake, or did I just chair it badly? Is there any procedure that would have handled both grievances fairly without producing an outcome both grievants prefer to escape? Or is there something genuinely broken in the combination — that each person should be sovereign over their own room, and that the house should decide things that affect its members — such that whenever someone's discomfort is triggered entirely by another's private doings, one of those two principles has to give? I don't know whether this situation has a name; if it does, I'd rather you work it out through our circumstances than label it and move on.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order: "What is actually going on here" (about 250 words), laying bare the structure of the situation in plain terms; "The case for the vote" (about 300 words), the strongest defence of putting both matters to the house; "The case for the closed-door rule" (about 300 words), the strongest defence of room sovereignty, including where it fails Sam; and "What we should do now" (about 200 words), your concrete recommendation for the flat, including whether anything should be done about my having cast the deciding votes.
