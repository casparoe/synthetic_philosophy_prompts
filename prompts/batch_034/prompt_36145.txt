My metaphysics reading group has been stuck on the fink/mask stuff for two terms and I want a diagnosis of why, not another survey.

The situation as I see it: on one side you have people who want to keep some version of the conditional analysis — to be fragile is, roughly, to break if struck, all else equal. Then along come the counterexamples everyone knows: Martin's electro-fink (the safety device that would cut the current before the wire electrocutes anyone, so the wire is live but wouldn't shock), and the mask cases — the carefully packed vase that survives being dropped, the poison that kills but whose visible effect is masked by a sedative. The pro-conditional crowd responds by fattening the ceteris paribus clause ("if struck, and nothing interferes, and nothing masks..."), and the anti-conditional crowd says at that point the analysis has become vacuous — you've just written "it would break if it would break" in fancier letters — and offers instead that dispositions are intrinsic properties, maybe structural properties of the object, that don't entail any particular conditional at all. And then a third group, the powers ontology people, cheer the breakdown: they never wanted conditionals in the first place, and they take finks and masks as evidence for a whole metaphysics where dispositionality is built into properties themselves.

And nobody has moved in months. My working hypothesis — present it as a hypothesis, feel free to demolish it — is that the first two camps aren't actually disagreeing about anything: one is doing semantics of disposition ascriptions (what ordinary speakers commit to when they say the vase is fragile) and the other is doing ontology of properties (what the fragility consists in), and they keep talking past each other because both use the sentence "the conditional analysis is refuted." Or maybe the real fight is hidden inside the third camp's position, and it's a substantive one about whether intrinsicness is even up for grabs. I genuinely can't tell.

So: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, or two questions under one heading, or a real metaphysical dispute in disguise? Diagnose it using what the sides actually say, not textbook summaries. And then tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress here — a distinction, a worked case, a way of framing the analysis that makes the ceteris paribus clause non-circular — or whether our reading group should just accept this one's not going anywhere and move on.

Format: lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
