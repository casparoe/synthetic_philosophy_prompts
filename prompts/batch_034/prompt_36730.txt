I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and in three weeks I'm giving a job talk defending the view that rational credence need not always be sharp: an agent can be fully rational while her credence in some proposition is representable only by a set of probability functions, or an interval, rather than a single number. My positive line is that some evidence is not merely incomplete but symmetrical in a way that rationally forbids any particular sharp assignment — Ellsberg-style cases, where the unknown composition of an urn leaves the agent with no basis for privileging one prior over a whole range of them — and that responding with a precise number in such cases reflects an artificial confidence rather than epistemic virtue. I also want to say a bit about how updating works on my picture, though this is the part of the talk I'm least confident about.

The department is dominated by orthodox Bayesians: confirmation theorists who treat probability theory plus conditionalisation as more or less the whole story about epistemic rationality, and who will be instinctively hostile to anything that smells like epistemic unclarity being excused rather than diagnosed. There is one decision theorist who works on dynamic choice and knows the literature on decision-making with sets of probabilities inside out — he is the one I'm genuinely frightened of. The nearest thing to an ally is a formal epistemologist who is sympathetic but leans precise, largely because she thinks indeterminate credences make action impossible to model cleanly.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this audience — not the ones I'd get at a workshop of fellow travellers — ordered by how much damage each would do to my candidacy, each phrased the way a confirmation theorist (or the decision theorist, where it's his) would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Be sure to cover at least these: the reductionist objection that anything my set of probabilities can do is better modelled as a single precise prior plus higher-order uncertainty about it; the worry that conditionalising a set of priors makes evidence wash out too slowly, or never, so that my agent is dogmatic in a way a precise Bayesian never is; the self-undermining point that my agent's very belief in her own credal set pushes her toward sharp assignments; and, from the decision theorist, the charge that once I allow choices merely permitted by some priors in the set, my agent can be led into choosing a dominated sequence of options over time or otherwise separated from her money. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's the plainest one: what does my agent actually believe when the interval is [0.3, 0.7] and she must bet tomorrow — and note which points I should concede up front rather than have extracted from me, since in a job talk a concession freely offered reads as sophistication and one dragged out reads as a wound.
