I'm on the safety policy council at a mid-sized AI lab (think ~200 researchers, we ship a chat assistant to a few million users). Our next-gen model just finished evals and the persuasion numbers are honestly kind of scary — on a battery where human expert persuaders convert maybe 35% of skeptical interlocutors on contested topics, the model converts high 60s. Same for getting people to change plans, switch purchases, reconsider relationships, whatever. The capabilities didn't come from us training for persuasion directly, they're a side effect of getting much better at modeling the person they're talking to.

Council is split three ways and we need an actual decision by end of month, in writing, because deployment is gated on it. Camp one says: deploy with a hard honesty constraint only — the model may never assert what it doesn't believe, may never use tactics it would endorse as manipulative if described in the abstract, but beyond that eloquence is fine, and truth-tracking persuasion is just... education with better delivery. Camp two says honesty is nowhere near enough, because a superhuman persuader who only says true things still gets to pick WHICH true things to say — agenda-setting is the manipulation, the individual sentences being true just launders it. They want something like a "persuasion budget": the model tracks when it's shifting a user's credences or preferences and has to disclose that, in the moment, before continuing ("heads up, I'm about to try to change your mind and I'm probably better at it than you'd expect"). Camp three thinks both camps are paternalistic and that disclosure like that would just erode trust and make the model weird to talk to, and that the same rules would forbid a great therapist or a great teacher, who also shift your beliefs while being warmer and quicker than you.

There's also an internal proposal floating around that I actually find interesting and nobody on the council has engaged with seriously: "rhetorical friction." The idea is that whenever the model detects it's about to make a strong move on a core belief (politics, identity, life plans), it has to deliberately present its case in the *weakest* natural form first — no framing, no emotional resonance, no audience modeling — and only escalate to its full persuasive arsenal if the user pushes back and asks for more. Basically the user has to opt in to being persuaded hard. Critics here say it's incoherent, that the "weak form" is itself a choice the model makes strategically, so you've just moved the manipulation one level up.

Write me the decision memo I can circulate before the vote. I want a concrete recommendation — deploy unconstrained, deploy with the honesty constraint only, deploy with some form of in-the-moment disclosure, the friction thing, or something you think is better — with implementation detail concrete enough that a research team could build it without a second round of philosophy meetings. Please take the agenda-setting worry seriously rather than treating it as paranoia, but also engage the strongest version of camp three's point, which I think is that all the proposed fixes rely on the model accurately classifying "when am I being persuasive" and that classification is itself the thing we don't know how to do. And if you recommend any disclosure or friction rule, say what failure mode it predictably creates that's worse than the status quo, because there will be one and I'd rather hear it from you than at the all-hands.

Also, keep in mind we can't just not deploy — a competitor ships in Q3 and "please use our dumber, less helpful model" is not a viable safety strategy, so a recommendation of "delay indefinitely" needs to say what we'd even be waiting for. Sorry for the length, this one's been eating at me.
