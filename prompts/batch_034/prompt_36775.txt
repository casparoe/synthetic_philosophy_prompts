I've been invited to give the closing address at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on Boltzmann brains. The first day and a half belongs to the cosmologists and formal epistemologists — the measure problem, whether fluctuation-dominated universes undermine the standard cosmological model, the usual anthropic machinery — and the organizers have asked me, as the lone ethicist on the program, to close with the "so what": what, if anything, follows for practical and moral reasoning if we take Boltzmann-brain scenarios seriously. The audience will be mostly physicists who will punish hand-waving, a scattering of decision theorists, and a few philosophers who have heard versions of this worry since Descartes, so whatever I build the talk around has to survive both a skeptical and a technically demanding room.

The trouble is that the literature gestures at implications without ever sorting out which of them actually carry weight. As I've collected them, the candidates are roughly these. First, there is the disquieting thought about self-interest: if observer-moments like mine are overwhelmingly outnumbered by randomly fluctuated ones that merely happen to carry copies of my memories, then egoistic planning looks confused — the "next" moment of experience is statistically far more likely to belong to some incoherent descendant than to anyone continuing my projects, and it's unclear whether decision theory, or self-locating probability in particular, vindicates this worry or defuses it. Second, there is the self-undermining objection: Sean Carroll has argued that theories predicting fluctuation-born observers are "cognitively unstable," in his words unable to be simultaneously true and justifiably believed, and some people think this dissolves every downstream implication at a stroke — though others suspect it only quarantines the epistemology and leaves the practical questions wide open. Third, there is a point I almost never see developed: if a cosmology fluctuates observers into existence, it presumably fluctuates suffering ones too, and even if we can never interact with them, their existence might bear on how we should value or judge such a universe — whether that thought belongs in a talk like mine or is simply too speculative to carry any weight is exactly what I can't decide. Fourth, there is the deflationary view that all of this is just the Cartesian demon or the brain in a vat dressed in thermal clothing, so nothing follows that skepticism has not already taught us to live with.

I have forty-five minutes and can develop at most two of these threads properly. Please rank the four and defend the ordering with explicit criteria — does the implication really follow from Boltzmann-brain scenarios specifically rather than from generic skepticism; does it survive the cognitive-instability objection or get swept away by it; and would accepting it actually change anything for someone deciding how to live, plan, or theorize, or is it a curiosity with no downstream purchase. Then tell me which two to build the talk around, what to compress into a single closing remark, and what development — conceptual, formal, or empirical — would most change your ranking if it arrived in the next few years.
