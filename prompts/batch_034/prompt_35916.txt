Quick one from grading hell — I teach a course on early modern philosophy, and I just finished a stack of papers on Berkeley that left me with a weird question I can't fully untangle myself. One of my strongest students wrote that Berkeley's master argument demonstrates that physical objects are literally made of ideas, so tables, mountains, and Higgs bosons are all just private mental pictures, basically a step up from hallucinations. She then pushed it further: since hallucinations are by definition subjective and idiosyncratic, the fact that physics makes absurdly precise predictions about stuff nobody has ever perceived (like gravitational waves before detectors caught them) should have been the end of Berkeley's whole system the moment it happened. If the world is just made of mind-stuff, how could a theory about it be right to fifteen decimal places?

I have a feeling she's flattening some distinction Berkeley works hard to maintain, but honestly I've reread the relevant passages so many times this week that I've lost the thread myself. Is she right that idealism collapses once you notice that scientific predictions about unperceived entities keep coming true, or is she smuggling in something about the regularity of nature that Berkeley thinks he has an account of? And relatedly, I keep seeing the claim that on Berkeley's view everything is constantly perceived by God — is that actually load-bearing for answering her, or is it more of a side remark that got famous? If you can show me exactly where the slide from ideas to hallucinations happens (or argue it doesn't happen and she's onto something), I'd be grateful.
