At a dinner party last week, a friend who has been reading about the four-day working week triumphantly declared that hunter-gatherers were "the original affluent society", citing Marshall Sahlins's famous essay, and concluded that foragers worked only a few hours a day and that the agricultural revolution made humanity worse off. It struck me that the slogan was being made to do several different jobs at once, and I would like to know which of them it can actually bear. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that the phrase could be expressing — from the modest claim that foragers' material wants were few and easily satisfied, to the empirical claim about hours spent in subsistence work, to the stronger normative or historical claims about progress and the transition to agriculture — and, for each, whether it is true, defensible, or an overreach. Please present the readings as a menu in a table with the following columns: 'Reading' (a one-sentence statement of the thesis), 'Sense of affluence' (what "affluent" means on this reading — e.g. preference satisfaction, leisure, security, or something else), 'Verdict' (plausible / contentious / false, with one line of reasoning), 'Supports the few-hours-a-day claim?' (yes, no, or only with extra premises, and what the extra premises would be), and 'Supports the claim that agriculture was a mistake?' (same treatment). Below the table, add a brief paragraph saying which reading my friend most likely intended, and whether the full package he was arguing for survives once the readings are prised apart.
