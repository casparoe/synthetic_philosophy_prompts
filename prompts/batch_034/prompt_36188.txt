My study group in philosophy of economics has an argument that seems to show not just that most traders lose money, but that all speculative trading is irrational and that markets cannot perform their supposed epistemic function. It runs: (1) No rational agent accepts a trade she expects to lose by; acceptance signals expected gain. (2) In a purely speculative trade, one side's gain is the other side's loss, net of transaction costs. (3) So each party to such a trade must believe she has an edge — private information or superior judgment. (4) If the parties are rational, know this of each other, and began from common priors, then each one's willingness to trade is itself evidence of an edge on the other side, and once both absorb that evidence their beliefs must converge — they cannot agree to disagree. (5) With the disagreement gone, it is common knowledge that the trade has zero expected value for each party, which no rational agent accepts; so speculative trade between such agents is impossible, and all real speculative volume must involve irrationality somewhere. (6) Moreover, since a rational informed party's willingness to trade would reveal her information and erase her edge, no one ever trades on an informational advantage; so prices can never come to reflect dispersed information, and the market's information-aggregating role is a mirage. The conclusion seems to prove too much: it does not merely explain why day traders lose, it implies the price system cannot work at all. Which step should I reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid given its premises, say which premise is cheapest to give up — though I would much rather keep (4), since it looks like nothing more than the agreement theorem plus the no-trade theorem.
