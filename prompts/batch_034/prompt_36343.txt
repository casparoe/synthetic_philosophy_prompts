I work at a development policy think tank, and a recurring argument between two of my colleagues has convinced me that I need to assign an actual number to my position instead of gesturing at it. The question: can the Nordic model be exported? Let me make the proposition precise enough to have a truth value. Take a country that today has a tax-to-GDP ratio around 20 percent, GDP per capita around the level of present-day Poland, a corruption perceptions score somewhere in the 40s, and no strong tradition of centralized wage bargaining. Suppose it attempts, over the next thirty years, to move to a genuinely Nordic configuration — universal rather than means-tested welfare programs, tax-to-GDP above 45 percent, and enough state capacity to collect and disburse honestly — and count the attempt a success only if the programs survive at least two changes of government and per-capita growth over the period stays at or above, say, 80 percent of its pre-reform trend. My colleague A, who believes social trust is the deep cause and the welfare state merely its surface, gives this around 0.25. My colleague B, who is taken with the Swedish political-science argument that universal programs are causally productive of the very solidarity they are usually said to presuppose — so that the causation runs partly in reverse — gives it around 0.7.

What I want from you is your own number, but mostly the decomposition that produces it. Some terms I can already see: whether high trust is a precondition of the model or a product of it (or whether both are downstream of something else — low inequality plus competent administration — in which case the export question is really a bootstrapping question about getting the flywheel turning); whether ethnic and linguistic heterogeneity genuinely erodes the willingness to pay high taxes for strangers, or whether that finding is an artifact of comparing countries with very different histories; whether the small-open-economy features are load-bearing or incidental — these states tax capital heavily and get away with it partly because they are small, credible, and export-dependent; whether high marginal rates actually cost much growth once you condition on what the revenue buys; and whether long-run political survival is best understood as a lock-in effect, where the hard part is only the first decade, after which the middle class defends programs it depends on. Assign a rough probability, or at least a clear conditional direction, to each term, give one line of justification for each, and then however roughly, combine them into your overall credence.

Then two further things. First, tell me which term you are least confident in and what specific evidence — a natural experiment, a historical episode, a cross-national pattern — would move it most; I want to know where your number is doing its shakiest work. Second, tell me where you think the disagreement between A at 0.25 and B at 0.7 actually lives: is it genuinely about the direction of causation between trust and universalism, or is one of them implicitly pessimistic about political feasibility in a way that has nothing to do with the causal structure at all? I would much rather have an explicitly hedged number with its weak points marked than a confident one resting on a term you privately regard as a coin flip.
