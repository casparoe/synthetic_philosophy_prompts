I came to philosophy of mathematics from logic, and one dispute in the field has been running in place for a century: what Skolem's paradox actually shows. The relativist party—Skolem himself drew the moral, and Putnam later made it the engine of his model-theoretic argument—holds that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem exposes talk of absolute, uncountable totalities as a kind of illusion: any first-order axiomatization of set theory, ZFC included, has countable models, so "all subsets" never has model-independent content, and our sense of speaking about one intended universe is either wishful thinking or fixed by something outside mathematics. The categoricity party answers that we only have to take second-order logic seriously: on its standard semantics, second-order ZFC is quasi-categorical and second-order arithmetic categorical, so determinate reference is restored—the line running from Zermelo through Shapiro and McGee. The relativists' stock reply, that this move presupposes just the determinate grasp of "all subsets" that is in dispute, so the categoricity result relocates the problem rather than solving it, has now been on the table for something like forty years, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed sides in decades. I don't want a survey; I want a diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute about "determinate," a substantive disagreement about whether there is more to the power set of the reals than any model can see, or two questions under one name—a mathematical question about categoricity that has arguably been answered, and a philosophical question about whether mathematical practice needs model-independent content at all? And on your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a new formal result, the right distinction, or good grounds for expecting none.
