Every year I teach the aggregation unit in my intro ethics course, and every year I get tripped up by my own list of commitments, so before this term starts I want an audit of it. Here's roughly what I actually believe: (1) the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism is right — one person's loss can't be made up for by a benefit to someone else, so there's no interpersonal summing of welfare into one total; (2) I'm a prioritarian about policy: benefits to the worse off count for more, and I take this to be a deep fact about what institutions owe people, not a rule of thumb; (3) I'm fine with safety regulators doing cost-benefit analysis — imposing small risks on a few, or letting a statistical few die, to benefit the greater number is legitimate, so long as the risk is spread fairly; (4) if I can save either five strangers or one stranger, I should save the five, and I insist this is a matter of fairness, not of adding up welfare; (5) the transplant case is straightforwardly impermissible — you may not kill one patient to harvest organs for five — and I want to ground that in separateness of persons, not in slippery-slope or bad-incentive stories; (6) within a single life, aggregation is obviously fine — a life goes better when its earlier suffering is outweighed by later goods, thats just prudence. Which of these actually clash, which only look like they clash, and what's the cheapest repair? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and dont restate my question back at me.
