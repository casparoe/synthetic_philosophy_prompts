Give an example of a historical case — technological, institutional, or infrastructural — in which the outward signs all point to path dependence (an early standards battle, a founding accident, heavy switching costs, network effects) but where, on careful inspection, the better explanation is convergence: had the accident gone the other way, something close to the same outcome would have arrived anyway by a different route. The deep question here is the human-history version of the old Gould–Conway Morris dispute about whether evolutionary outcomes are contingent or convergent, and I want a case that actually does the work of separating the two hypotheses rather than merely gesturing at the idea that history matters.

Make it a case you can defend in detail. Explain what the lock-in reading would say about it, then what the convergence reading says, and identify the specific features that decide between the two — for instance, how many independent near-equivalent alternatives were live at the critical juncture, whether the winning design's advantages were visible in advance or only retrospectively manufactured, and whether the supposed switching costs were genuinely unrecoverable or merely politically painful. Then give the single strongest reason to think you have made the wrong call.

Do not cite literature or point me to reading; argue entirely from what you actually know about the case.
