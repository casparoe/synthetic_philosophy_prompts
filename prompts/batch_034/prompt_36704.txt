Write me a parody of those glossy corporate "your year in review" emails — the Spotify Wrapped kind — put out by a fictional data-analytics company whose whole product is reconstructing people's preferences from their behavior. Call it something like Preflytics and have them send users their annual "True Self Report." Around 650 words, formatted like a real marketing email: subject line, hero stat, a couple of widgets, closing CTA, plus a short FAQ at the bottom.

The satire has to come from actually taking revealed preference theory seriously as a business model, so a behavioral economist laughs instead of wincing. The company never asks users what they want, because asking would be methodologically suspect — every claim about the user is inferred from choices only, and the email should wear that as a virtue ("You told three friends you were trying to cut back on drinking. You ordered 74 drinks. We know which of those is data."). Work in the real trouble spots of the theory as product features: sunk-cost hours logged as enjoyment ("you finished a 60-hour game you described in your notes app as 'a chore' — engagement through the roof"), the no-distinction between wanting something and choosing it, the way the report literally cannot express that anything is bad for you because utility is only ever measured relative to observed behavior, and an adaptive-preference badge congratulating users for gracefully ceasing to want things that became unavailable or unaffordable ("Cheapest contentment we've ever measured!").

Include at least one widget where the same set of choices gets spun two ways depending on which framing is more flattering to the company, without ever acknowledging that the two framings conflict — that's menu-dependence laundered as a feature, and I'd like it to be visible to a careful reader but never flagged.

And the FAQ at the end should handle objections the way the theory historically did: someone writes in "what if I chose it because I had no better option," and the answer cheerfully explains that choosing among available options is all choosing ever is. A question about whether people sometimes choose against their own interests should get a response that quietly redefines "interests" as whatever shows up in the data. Maybe a final FAQ item about the unsubscribe link, noting that unsubscribing will be recorded as a preference for less transparency, which the algorithm will factor into next year's report.

Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking narrator, the horror should be entirely structural. And please make sure the legal footer does some real work: something like a clause where the user's self-description, once contradicted by enough behavioral data, gets automatically updated in the company's records, with a friendly note that this is for the user's own consistency.
