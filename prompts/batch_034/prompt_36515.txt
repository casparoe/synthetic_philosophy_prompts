I teach an introductory seminar in philosophy of action — 18 students, one 75-minute session, no prior exposure to the literature. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the specific friction in Kavka's toxin puzzle: a case in which a substantial prize is offered for intending, now, to do something mildly unpleasant later, where the prize is paid out regardless of whether the unpleasant act is ever performed, and where nothing external stops the winner from pocketing the prize and skipping the unpleasantness. The students should run into the gap between deciding, predicting, and intending before any of these notions is named for them.

Constraints and requests:

- The unpleasant act must be real, harmless, and ethical — a sip of something bitter, a minute of an awkward pose, something of that order — and the payoff scheme must not use real money; invent a course-adjacent currency (bonus points, tokens redeemable for something small).
- Address the verification problem head-on. The puzzle conditions the prize on a mental state, and a facilitator cannot observe mental states. Decide explicitly what your game actually conditions the prize on — a sealed written declaration, a witnessed commitment, a self-report under an honor code — and then say in the debrief precisely how that substitution changes what the students experienced. This is the honest note on which feature of the puzzle the activity cannot reproduce and which I must supply myself.
- Give me rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the distinction between intending to do something and predicting that one will do it, and onto the question whether intention can be produced at will.
- Build in at least one designed complication that generates discussion: for instance a second round in which participants advise a classmate facing the same choice, or a comparison arm in which the prize is paid for the declaration alone, with no unpleasant act attached.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students who perform the unpleasant act without any visible deliberation, students who insist they have gamed the setup, a dominant strategy emerging that flattens the phenomenon — and how I should handle each case in the moment.

No citations or literature survey is needed; the design should stand on its own.
