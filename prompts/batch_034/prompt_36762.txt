My two friends Maya and Dev got into it after we saw a news story that a painting at our local museum had been reattributed — the master apparently only painted the faces, his workshop did everything else, and the museum had known for a decade without saying anything. The argument started about whether we'd been ripped off but quickly got more general.

Maya's position was that the value of an original is basically causal: what we pay for, when we crowd around an original, is standing in front of the actual object the artist's hand produced, and a duplicate — even a molecule-for-molecule duplicate — just doesn't have that history. Dev said that's relic worship, no different from venerating a saint's fingerbone: everything we actually love about the painting is fully present in a good reproduction or a perfect copy, so the extra value has to be something we bring to it, not something in the picture. Maya replied that "something we bring to it" isn't a debunking — people also care whether they're hearing a live performance vs. a recording, and caring about the actual historical object isn't obviously irrational, just not aesthetic in the narrow sense. Dev then fired back with a swap case: suppose the museum secretly hung a perfect duplicate, kept the original in the vault, and nobody could tell even on close inspection. The visitors would be getting the full experience Maya says matters — same room, same light, paint strokes indistinguishable — so on her own account they lose nothing, yet everyone agrees they'd be wronged if the swap came out. Maya's answer was that they're wronged because they're deceived about history: what the object is connected to, not what it looks like, and the fact that people feel betrayed and no one thinks that feeling is silly is actually evidence for her side, not Dev's. Dev said that just shows provenance functions like a guarantee of authenticity — an invisible certificate — which is fine for the art market but can't be why the original matters to people who just want to look.
