I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've agreed to give the closing talk at a student conference on longtermism — against the grain of the room. My thesis: taking Bostrom's simulation argument seriously dampens fanaticism about the far future. The idea is that once a non-trivial slice of one's credence goes on our being simulated, the expected-value case for treating the far future as overwhelmingly morally dominant loses its grip: our evidence about the base-level world is screened off, the distribution over possible futures that the hypothesis injects competes with the default story in which our descendants colonise the stars, and the moral weight of the far future becomes radically uncertain rather than astronomically large — which, I want to argue, is a reason to moderate, not multiply, the trade-offs we are willing to accept against the present. The audience is a few hundred effective altruists, mostly committed longtermists who know the standard arguments cold, plus a handful of moral philosophers and a couple of people who do forecasting professionally. Please prepare my question-period sheet: the seven or eight objections this particular room will actually raise, phrased the way they would phrase them, ordered by how much damage each could do; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft; the one objection I am quietly hoping nobody asks; and your judgement on where I should concede up front. Bear in mind that I want to stay neutral between the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, and tell me whether you think that neutrality is tenable under questioning. Please write your answer in British English.
