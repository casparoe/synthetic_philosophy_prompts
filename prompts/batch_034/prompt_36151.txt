I serve as the attending veterinarian and chair of the animal-care committee at a biomedical research institute. Our regulatory obligations extend to mammals, birds, and fish, but a growing share of our work involves invertebrates: electrophysiology on locusts, behavioral assays on crabs and shrimp, and from next year a small cephalopod program. The regulations say nothing about which of these animals require anesthesia, humane endpoints, or post-procedural monitoring, so in practice each laboratory improvises, and the improvisation tracks little more than intuitions about brain size — the crustacean labs have begun using analgesia on their own initiative, while the locust preparations are still performed on fully awake animals, and nobody on the committee can articulate a principled reason why the line falls where it does. The honest situation is that committee members reach for brain size and encephalization because those are measurable, even though everyone vaguely agrees that what matters morally is presumably not the size of the organ but the capacity for suffering that the organ subserves — and nobody knows how to move from the one to the other under time pressure.

Please convert that ragged reasoning into a decision procedure my committee can actually run. The parameters are as follows: roughly a dozen new invertebrate protocols per year; the review must be completed in about an hour per protocol by people who are not comparative neuroscientists; the output is a written determination signed off by me, so it must be auditable by an outside inspector later. The procedure should be a sequence of questions asked in order, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer; it should specify where the procedure terminates in a determination of no protection required, and where it hands the question back to the committee because the issue is one of moral values rather than empirical fact, so that the value judgment is at least visible and consistently located rather than smuggled into a factual-looking step. It must also tell the committee what to do when the evidence needed to answer a question simply does not exist, which for most invertebrate groups is the normal case rather than the exception. Finally, it should contain an explicit warning section on where a brain-size proxy misfires — both directions — since comparative cognition is full of cases in which a small or anatomically unconventional nervous system supports more than its size suggests, and cases in which a large brain coexists with capacities that seem irrelevant to welfare.

Once the procedure is written, apply it to three protocols. First, an electrophysiology preparation on adult locusts that has historically been carried out without anesthesia, where the interesting complication is that anesthesia would destroy the very signal the experiment exists to record. Second, a shell-crushing noxious assay on shore crabs, a protocol the proposing laboratory has already volunteered to run under analgesia. Third, a learning and memory study on cuttlefish, animals whose nervous system is organized so differently from ours that committee members cannot agree whether their intuitions about them are worth anything. At least one of the three applications must return a determination the committee will find unwelcome, and you should say which one and why the procedure nonetheless returns it. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: in particular, whether it can settle the underlying moral question at all, or only render our existing moral uncertainty consistent, documented, and honestly assigned to the right people.
