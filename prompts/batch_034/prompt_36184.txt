Over the past few months I've been keeping a running list of situations where one person's small contribution to some large shared harm strikes me as wrong, and situations with what looks like the exact same structure that strike me as fine, and I've come to suspect my own judgments don't add up. I want to know whether there is a principle behind them or whether my gut is just favoring people I imagine as friends and disliking people I imagine as strangers. Here are my cases with my honest reactions to each.

1. A man eats meat at every meal and, when challenged, says his individual purchases can't affect the number of animals raised, because farms adjust production in batches and one customer more or less changes nothing. I'm confident he's doing something wrong, and I'm confident the excuse is bogus — even though I suspect it's arithmetically true.
2. A woman flies overseas for her best friend's wedding, emitting more carbon in one trip than the meat-eater produces in a year of meals. I think she did nothing wrong, or almost nothing. But I notice I can't say why the "my one flight changes nothing" excuse works for her when the parallel excuse failed in case 1.
3. An election is decided by thousands of votes, so any one ballot has essentially no chance of being decisive. I still think a citizen who stays home out of plain indifference (not protest, not illness, not a scheduling accident) has done something wrong — a small wrong, but a wrong, not merely a missed opportunity.
4. A park is already carpeted with litter, and one more dropped cup would make no visible difference to it. My gut says the person who drops the cup is doing something wrong, full stop, and the "it's already trashed" excuse gets no traction with me whatsoever.
5. The same park has a corner of lawn worn completely bald by everyone cutting across it, and one more footstep makes no difference to the bare patch. My gut says walking across is fine — it barely registers as wrong at all. Yet structurally I can't see how it differs from the cup.
6. A thousand people each throw one stone and a shop window shatters; no single stone was necessary, and any hundred of them would have sufficed. I think every thrower bears part of the responsibility for the window. I'd hold to that even if we learned the window had been cracked already.
7. Someone buys the cheapest jeans from a company that runs dreadful factories. I think this is regrettable but not wrong, or at most negligibly wrong — though I'd be hard pressed to explain what separates it from the meat case, which I called wrong without hesitation.
8. A town's reservoir is low, and one household's lawn watering makes no measurable difference to the level. Here I genuinely flip back and forth: some days the watering seems clearly wrong, other days it seems no worse than the jeans.

I've tried to build a principle myself, and I have two candidates, both of which keep failing on me. The first: it's wrong when your bit is part of something a group is doing together and you'd be coasting on other people's restraint. That one fits the voter, the cup, and the stone, and it explains why the wedding flyer is off the hook (nobody was relying on her restraint). But then it also condemns the lawn-crosser, whom I want to let off, and I can't see what it says about the meat or the jeans. The second: it's wrong when the practice would be collectively disastrous if everyone did it. That gets the meat-eater, and maybe the lawn-watering. But then it swallows the wedding flight too, since universal tourism would be as bad as universal meat, and I refuse to treat that woman as a wrongdoer.

So my questions are these. Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, with each clause motivated by a specific case above? Please test any principle you propose not only against my eight cases but against ones I haven't listed — for instance, a stadium wave that only works if enough people stand, a workplace where everyone takes a pen from the supply cupboard, and a country where so many people under-report small amounts of tax that the treasury has stopped auditing them. And if my verdicts can't all be saved, tell me plainly which ones have to go: I'd rather overturn two of my own reactions than keep them all at the price of a principle with so many epicycles that it can't tell me anything about the ninth case. I'd especially like to hear where you land on cases 4 and 5, and on my wavering case 8, since those seem to be where my intuitions are pulling in opposite directions.
