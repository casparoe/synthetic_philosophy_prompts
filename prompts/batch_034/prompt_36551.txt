I am a third-year philosophy student currently enrolled in an upper-level metaphysics course on identity and persistence over time. For our term project, each of us has to extend a classic puzzle into domains the literature has not worn smooth, elicit verdicts from a non-philosopher subject across a battery of cases, and then analyze what rule or rules best explain the subject's track record — including an honest assessment of whether the verdict set is genuinely informative or just noisy. My subject is my roommate, who has now ruled on six cases, and before I formally test her on the new cases I want a prediction on record so that I cannot retrofit my analysis after seeing her answers. I would be grateful if you could produce that prediction.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A warship has every plank replaced one at a time over a decade while it remains in continuous service; the old planks are carted off and stored. Same ship.
2. A trawler sinks; a year later the wreck is raised, and the salvaged planks are used to rebuild it at a different shipyard, replacing nothing that survived. The maritime registry re-lists it under its old name and number. Same ship.
3. During case 1's decade of replacement, a shipwright assembles the discarded planks into a second ship in the vessel's original home port. She rules that the ship in continuous service is the original and the plank-built one is not, even though the plank-built one contains more of the original wood.
4. Her uncle's jazz quartet has replaced its members one at a time over forty years; no founding member remains, but each new member learned the repertoire from a sitting member. Same band.
5. A restaurant in her hometown kept its name, recipes, and building, but the founding family sold it to new owners who replaced the entire staff at once. Not the same restaurant.
6. A canvas is 90 percent repainted by conservators after a flood, working from photographs and the surviving fragments. Still the same painting.

The new cases I intend to put to her:

A. Our university's oldest drama society folded in 1989 when its last members graduated. In 2015, students found its constitution and minute books in the university archive, revived it under the same name, resumed its traditional annual pantomime, and alumni from the 1980s recognize it as their society. Same society?

B. Decades after case 3, the continuously repaired original burns to the waterline with nothing salvageable, while the shipwright's plank-built ship is still afloat. Does the plank-built ship thereby become the original — or was it never the original, and has the original now simply ceased to exist?

What I need from you:

First, identify the candidate rules that fit all six of her verdicts. I want at least two genuine competitors, not one rule plus a strawman. For what it is worth, my own half-formed suspicion is that she is not tracking material composition or functional continuity at all, but something like continuity of communal record-keeping — what the registry, the fans, the locals, or the museum treat as one thing — though I am not confident this survives case 5, since the locals in that town apparently do say the restaurant kept on as before and she denies it anyway. Please do not feel bound by my hypothesis; kind-relative or disjunctive rules are on the table if you can state them cleanly.

Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about cases A and B, and where a rule gives no determinate prediction, say what the rule would need added to yield one.

Third, give me your best guess at her actual verdicts, with the specific considerations that make you prefer one rule over its rivals as the explanation.

Fourth, design the further case that would best discriminate between the two most plausible candidate rules — specify it concretely, the way I have above — so I can add it to my instrument before the final round.

Finally, flag any verdict among the six that you suspect is noise, or that should make me doubt whether my roommate is running a rule at all rather than answering case by case; our professor has warned us that subjects often run defeasible, kind-sensitive rules loose enough to rationalize anything, and I need to know whether my data is underdetermined in that way or merely messy.
