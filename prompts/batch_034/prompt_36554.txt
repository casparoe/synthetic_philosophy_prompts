I've been part of a reading group that spent last term working through Keynes's Treatise on Probability alongside Ramsey's response to it and Ellsberg's ambiguity experiments, and we've been asked to submit short written profiles of where we've landed for the others to poke at. I've drafted mine, but before I circulate it I'd like an outside check on whether the commitments I've listed actually fit together, since I suspect at least one pair doesn't, and I honestly can't tell which. Here is what I've committed to, roughly in the order I came to hold them. First, I follow the Treatise in taking probability to be an objective logical relation between a hypothesis and the evidence for it: given a fixed body of evidence, there is a fact of the matter about how strongly it supports a conclusion, and two fully rational agents weighing the same evidence cannot reasonably disagree. Second, I accept Keynes's claim that many of these relations are non-numerical, and that in some cases two probabilities are simply not comparable — there is no fact of the matter about which of two conclusions the evidence favors more. Third, I believe this is best understood as an early form of the modern theory of imprecise probabilities, on which a rational state of belief is represented by a whole set of probability functions rather than a single one, and I think that reading captures what Keynes was getting at. Fourth, I hold that a rational agent's credences must always be sharp and fully comparable: the Dutch book and scoring-rule arguments show that degrees of belief ought to form a single probability function, and I regard Ellsberg-type preferences — favoring bets with known odds over bets whose odds are unknown — as a mistake on a par with leaving yourself open to a sure-loss book. Fifth, I take Keynes's later remarks about radical uncertainty in his economics, where he motivates liquidity preference by saying that about certain long-horizon questions there is no basis on which to form any calculable probability at all, to be continuous with the Treatise's non-numerical probabilities: the same doctrine, transplanted from induction to investment.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which only look like they are? Please rank whatever tensions you find by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me — I want to know the price of the repair, not just the diagnosis. Finally, tell me whether there is some single revision that would clean up the whole package, or whether the problems are independent enough that I have to give something up in more than one place.
