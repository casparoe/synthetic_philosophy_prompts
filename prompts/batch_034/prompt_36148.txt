I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and one of the durable lessons I took from that corpus is that most verbal disputes dissolve once you force people to state their claims without the contested abstractions. I now run an upper-level seminar on metaethics — 12 students, one 90-minute session — and I want to stage the Frege–Geach problem as a live game before the students ever hear its name.

The problem, as my students will meet it on paper: expressivism in metaethics holds that a claim like "lying is wrong" doesn't describe a fact but expresses an attitude — roughly, booing. That story is comfortable as long as moral claims occur only asserted and on their own. It strains when the same content shows up embedded: "If lying is wrong, getting your little brother to lie is wrong"; "Either lying is wrong or breaking promises is"; "It's not the case that lying is wrong." In an embedded position the claim isn't being asserted, so nothing is being booed, and yet the argument's validity seems to turn on the embedded clause meaning the same thing there as it does when asserted outright. I want the students to discover this by *needing* the embeddings, not by being told about them.

Design the session. What I have in mind, though feel free to improve on it: the students are split into groups speaking a deliberately restricted toy language for part of the session — one sub-vocabulary purely attitudinal (a small closed stock of praise/blame signals, standing to it as the naive expressivist thinks moral talk stands to the world), the rest ordinary descriptive language. The groups then have to conduct real business under uncertainty — ratifying standing policies of the form "if such-and-such turns out to be the case, we do X," with points at stake, and later settling disputes about what was agreed once events resolve. The intended arc: atomic attitudinal claims work fine (phase 1); then conditionals, negations, and contested embeddings arrive and the attitudinal vocabulary can't state what the group settled (phase 2); finally a disagreement about a conditional between two players who agreed on every atomic claim forces the issue (phase 3).

Please provide:

- Materials, group setup, rules, and the exact sequence of phases, with a payoff scheme in points (no real money) that makes the embeddings genuinely necessary rather than decorative;
- the specific moments where the toy language is supposed to hit its limits, and how I should time them;
- a debrief that names the Frege–Geach problem only at the end, and maps each phase onto its components: the same content asserted versus embedded, modus ponens relying on stable content across those positions, and what the expressivist owes an account of when a conditional is uttered;
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students smuggling descriptive language into the attitudinal vocabulary, phase 2 collapsing into ordinary negotiation, the dispute stage running long — and how to handle each;
- and, frankly, which feature of the actual philosophical problem the game cannot reproduce. My guess is that any staged failure only convicts a naive boo/hurrah language, while the live dialectic is about whether more sophisticated expressivist machinery — attitudes toward attitudes, norms of conversation — can carry the embeddings; the facilitator will have to supply that distinction, and I want you to be explicit about where the game's outcome would amount to a strawman if presented as a verdict on expressivism itself.
