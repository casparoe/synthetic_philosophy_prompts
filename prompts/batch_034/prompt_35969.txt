I've come to hold that AI safety over the next decade or so should be organized around control rather than alignment, and I want to know what would count against the position. By control I mean the family of measures that assume the model in front of you may be misaligned and try to make that survivable: monitoring of outputs and chains of action with separate, less capable models checking the work; strict trust boundaries so that untrusted systems can't take irreversible actions without human or more-trusted approval; deployments designed so that the model can't reliably tell when it is being tested versus used for real; staged capability rollouts with the ability to shut things down; keeping risky tasks on single-purpose, internet-isolated instances. The underlying thought is that alignment, if it works, is invisible: nothing observable distinguishes a lab that has made its model genuinely want the right things from a lab that has merely stopped finding evidence to the contrary, so a stated belief in alignment does no load-bearing work. Control measures, whatever their other faults, can be red-teamed against deliberately misaligned models, and their failures are the kind that show up in testing rather than in the world.

I can see several lines of attack myself, and I want them weighed rather than enumerated. There's the relocation-of-trust argument: the monitoring layer and the approval layer are themselves models or humans that need to be aligned or trusted, so control just changes which part of the system you're taking on faith. There's the human-factors one: keeping a person in the loop means the person is the failure point, and the person will approve the anomalous action a thousand times under time pressure and cost. There's the complacency worry: a control regime lets labs deploy systems they would never otherwise have deployed, so the net increases the frequency of falls even while decreasing the damage of each one. And there's the measurement problem: we validate control measures against today's deliberately misaligned models, but the entire situation presupposes that the models we'll face may be better at evading detection than the ones we tested against — the safeguards are calibrated to a threat model that the threat itself can outgrow.

Tell me what would have to be true for the control-first position to be wrong — observations, results, arguments, including ones arguably already in hand. Say which of these a committed defender of control would concede as counting against it and which they would wave off, and whether the waving-off shows the position has been qualified into something that can't lose. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want a definite verdict: is this position actually exposed to evidence, or only to retrospective regret? Take a committed stance throughout — if you think, say, that the relocation-of-trust objection is fatal while the complacency worry is a management problem, say so and defend it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my view. If it helps you calibrate: the argument that would move me most is one showing a structural feature of control that makes it unable to deliver the one thing it's advertised as delivering — verifiable safety without a prior solution to alignment — but I'm aware that's the objection I already half believe, so don't inflate it beyond what it can carry.
