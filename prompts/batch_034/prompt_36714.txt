I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been commissioned for a long essay on the ethics of lying — not the psychology of deception, which is what my editors usually get, but the normative question the lab studies never quite reach. Rather than another piece about how often people lie per day, I want to hang the essay on Ricky Gervais's 2009 comedy The Invention of Lying and on Augustine of Hippo, whose two treatises on lying, written decades apart, remain the most uncompromosing things ever said on the subject.

In the film's world nobody can lie — and, crucially, nobody can withhold. People narrate every passing thought: Anna tells Mark at the start of their date that she isn't attracted to him and is only there as a favor to a friend. Fiction doesn't exist, so cinema consists of readings of historical fact. Mark, broke and desperate, discovers he can say things he doesn't believe — and everyone must believe him. The pivot is the deathbed scene: his terrified mother, fearing eternal nothingness, and Mark inventing an afterlife on the spot so she can die happy. The hospital staff overhear, the invention hardens into doctrine, and Mark becomes a reluctant prophet announcing the wishes of a Man in the Sky — a religion built entirely from one charitable lie.

Read the film through Augustine, taking both sides seriously. The threads I want pursued:

- The bishop's hardest case. Augustine refuses permission even for the merciful lie — the one told to the sick or dying to ease their terror, the one told to save a life. Mark's scene with his mother is exactly that case, staged first as tragedy, then as farce, then as church. Make the argument Augustine would actually make against Mark.
- The definitional point. For Augustine a lie is an assertion contrary to one's mind, made with the will to deceive; someone who says something false while believing it true does not lie, and he explicitly sets jokes aside for the same reason — the joker signals no intent to deceive. The film's premise, that a world without lying would be a world without fiction or imagination, seems to beg the question against that distinction. Is Gervais's world Augustine's world at all, or a screenwriting convenience that quietly collapses deception and imagination?
- Involuntary truthfulness. No one in the film has the virtue of truthfulness; they have only the mechanism of truth-telling. The casual cruelty of total candor — above all Anna's genetic frankness about Mark's prospects as a mate — suggests a world where truth without charity is its own kind of wound. Would Augustine count that world as innocent?
- The ending's split. Mark declines to lie to Anna — it wouldn't count, he says — and confesses his power, yet the Man in the Sky religion is left standing. Augustine's second treatise was written against a sect that had made lying in the service of religion into doctrine. The film ends by renouncing the lie of love while keeping the lie of God. Coherent, satirical, or cop-out?

Please structure the response in these sections, in this order: "The premise, briefly" (around 120 words); "The deathbed scene as Augustine's hardest case" (around 400 words); "Where the film and the bishop part ways" (around 400 words); "What the comedy adds that the treatises can't" (around 300 words); and "A note for my editor" (around 100 words) telling me what to cut if the essay runs long and what the single takeaway for readers should be.
