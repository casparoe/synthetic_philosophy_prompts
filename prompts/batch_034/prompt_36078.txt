I'm putting together a handout for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar (about 15 students, mostly seniors who've done some metaphysics but not much phil mind beyond intro). The unit is on whether "qualia" even picks out anything coherent, so I want a decision tree that starts them off from something they can't deny -- say, the claim "I'm having a reddish experience of this apple right now" -- and walks them down to a position by way of diagnostic cases, not by way of labels.

The tree's branch points should be concrete commitments about cases, phrased so a student can answer them without knowing which position they're steering toward. At minimum I want the tree to route through: (1) the inverted spectrum thought experiment (is it genuinely possible that tomatoes look to you the way grass looks to me, with everything else about us functionally identical?), (2) the acquired-taste case -- when you loved coffee at 25 but can barely stand it at 45, is introspection even capable of telling apart "the taste-experience itself changed" from "the experience is the same but my appreciation/judgment of it shifted"? -- and (3) the question of whether experiences have intrinsic properties over and above what they represent and their causal/dispositional role.

The leaves should include at least: old-fashioned qualia realism (intrinsic, non-functional, non-representational properties), naive or relational realism, representationalism/intentionalism, the functionalist who just identifies the qualia with the role, illusionism (phenomenal properties don't exist but the seemings do, and the seemings are functionally analyzable), and the flat-out dissolutionist line that the inherited introspective question is confused in a way that shows the posit was incoherent from the start rather than merely false. If your logical space naturally generates an unoccupied combination, keep it as a leaf marked "unnamed" with a one-line guess at why nobody lives there -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

One thing I care about: the acquired-taste case is doing real work in the literature here, because if introspection genuinely can't sort change-in-experience from change-in-attitude, the supposedly direct warrant for qualia gets shaky. So build it in as a branch node, not just flavor text, and be explicit about which leaves survive if the student answers "introspection can't tell those apart."

Also flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate given your branch questions -- I'd rather know where the handout is lying to my students than discover it mid-class.
