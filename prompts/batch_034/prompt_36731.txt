I am a novelist at work on a long essay, provisionally titled "The Entropy of Endings," commissioned by a literary review whose editors have asked for something "between fiction and physics." Before I embarrass myself before fact-checkers, I would be grateful for a rigorous reading of the draft condensed below. My training is in narrative, not statistical mechanics, so I want comments on two fronts: where my physics and philosophy go wrong or slide by on imprecision, and whether the central conceit is genuinely illuminating or merely decorative. Please do not flatter me; a novelist gets enough of that.

The argument, as it stands in the draft:

1. Story-time is parasitic on thermodynamic time. We can only narrate forward because we can only remember backward, and memory is thermodynamically grounded: a record of the past is a low-entropy structure, and creating one is paid for by dissipating heat elsewhere. Every recollection, the draft claims, is a small local confirmation of the second law. If this is right, the arrow of narrative is not a convention of the craft but a thermodynamic necessity.

2. Foreshadowing is the Past Hypothesis of fiction. Cosmologists and philosophers postulate that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate — I believe the term "past hypothesis" was coined by the philosopher David Albert, in a book called Time and Chance — and that this boundary condition, plus time-reversal-invariant microscopic dynamics, is why the universe has a direction at all. A first sentence, I argue, does the same work: it fixes a low-information state from which everything downstream flows, and the reader experiences entropy as the accumulation of the unpredictable. Endings feel inevitable in retrospect for the same reason the past feels explained while the future feels open.

3. The reversible novel is impossible, and here is the crux of the essay. I have been attempting, for three years, to write a novel that can be read equally well in either direction — last page first, with the opening chapter functioning as the ending — a narrative palindrome, each scene as coherent an effect of what follows it as a cause of what precedes it. I now believe the project fails for a principled reason: Loschmidt's reversal objection. The microscopic laws of physics run just as well backward as forward; a film of colliding billiard balls run in reverse breaks no law. Yet no such reversal of the whole universe happens, and I claim a novel readable in both directions fails in the same way: not because any local scene resists reversal, but because the global macrostate — coherence itself — is the kind of thing that can only be built up from a special boundary. The book's first sentence is my Big Bang.

4. Anticipating an objection. I am aware of a line of thought, associated with Huw Price's book Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point, that our sense of the arrow may be perspectival — something about how we creatures are embedded in the world, rather than a feature of the world itself. The draft waves this away in a sentence, on the grounds that whatever the metaphysics, the phenomenology is fixed: readers do experience one direction. I confess I am not sure the dismissal holds, since if the arrow is in the reader rather than the universe, perhaps my analogy has the dependence backward.

Editors will want roughly two thousand words of finished essay, so accuracy matters as much as elegance. I would like your comments delivered as a JSON object with the following schema:

- "claim_by_claim": an array of objects, one for each of my four numbered claims, each with fields "claim" (a short paraphrase of the claim), "verdict" (one of "correct", "imprecise", or "mistaken"), and "explanation" (two to four sentences on what is right and what is wrong or overstated; I am especially anxious about whether memory formation, as opposed to erasure, really must dissipate heat, and about whether my use of Loschmidt's objection gets its point right — I suspect I have converted an improbability into an impossibility).
- "central_thesis": an object with fields "statement" (the strongest defensible version of the essay's thesis in one sentence), "defensible" ("yes", "partly", or "no"), and "main_weak_point" (the single most serious objection it cannot easily survive).
- "price_objection": a paragraph on whether the perspectival view of the arrow, if correct, would genuinely undermine the essay, and whether my one-sentence dismissal is salvageable.
- "interest": an object with fields "illuminating_or_decorative" (which, and why, in two or three sentences) and "best_idea_in_the_draft" (the element a physicist would most regret losing).
- "top_revisions": an array of the three most important changes, in priority order, each one sentence.
- "overall": a single paragraph appraising the essay as a whole.

Please write the JSON fields in plain prose — no headers, no commentary outside the object. If a claim of mine is so garbled that you must reconstruct what I probably meant before evaluating it, say so within the relevant explanation rather than leaving it blank.
