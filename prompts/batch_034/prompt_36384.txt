I've recently joined the trust-and-safety policy team at a company that builds a general-purpose voice assistant, and my first assignment is to draft the guidelines governing when the assistant may refuse a request, question it, quietly reframe it, or simply comply. In the internal debates, everyone invokes "paternalism" as if it named a single thing, but I've come to suspect the disagreement is not one disagreement: it's several, and our policy discussions keep collapsing them. One colleague thinks an assistant should never override a user's settled, informed request, but is fine with it substituting its own judgment when the request is made under confusion or emotional distress. Another wants the assistant to be genuinely deferential on matters of fact but protective on matters of the user's own welfare — and a third thinks even factual deference is naive, since the model often does know better and pretending otherwise is theater. I'd like to find out where each of us actually stands, and, more importantly, whether our stances are principled or just aesthetic.

So: please write a "which stance on AI paternalism do you actually hold?" quiz — eight to ten questions, each a concrete scenario our team genuinely argues about, with two or three answer options apiece and a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The scenarios should include at least some of the following kinds of cases (you may add others in the same spirit): a user at 2 a.m. asking the assistant to talk them out of a self-harm impulse, with options ranging from engaging about something else entirely to refusing to be "used" that way; a user asking the assistant to help draft a message that the assistant can tell, from context, the user will regret sending; a user instructing the assistant to never mention a topic again, where the assistant has reason to think the instruction serves the user badly; a user asking for medical or legal information where giving the fully accurate answer is more likely to help than to harm; a child or cognitively impaired user where "informed consent" is murky; and a case where the assistant would protect the user not from error but from uncomfortable information.

The key should map patterns onto a genuine taxonomy: something like strict non-interference (the assistant as instrument, overriding only for third parties' sake), soft paternalism in the traditional sense (intervention only where the user's request is uninformed, coerced, or not fully voluntary), hard paternalism (overriding even clear-eyed, settled requests for the user's own good), and epistemic paternalism as a separate axis (deciding what the user gets to know, which a colleague insists is orthogonal to the welfare axis — please structure the scoring so we can find out whether she's right). Every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard the term "paternalism," and no question may just ask "should the assistant override the user?" — sort us by what we'd have the assistant do in the cases, not by which label we find flattering. Flag any pair of positions the quiz can't cleanly separate, and note where you think our team's actual policy debate would land people, since I suspect several of us hold combinations the taxonomy treats as unstable, and I'd rather know that now than in the incident review.
