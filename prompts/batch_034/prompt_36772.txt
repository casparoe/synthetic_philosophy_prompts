Context for the writeup: a prompt on the Judy Benjamin problem and updating on conditionals, in the "argument that seems to prove too much" format.

---

A guy on my team (ex-physicist, loves probability, argues about everything at lunch) has cooked up an argument that he says shows Bayesian updating "refutes itself" when the evidence is a conditional, and I've stared at it for two evenings and can't find the exit. It runs on the old Judy Benjamin puzzle, which he told me like this: a soldier parachutes into territory split into three equal thirds — Blue, Red, Yellow — with her landing spot uniformly random over the whole map. She radios HQ to ask where she is. The operator can't (or won't) tell her which territory she's in, but does say: "if you're in Blue territory, you're twice as likely to be in the rear half as in the front half." Nothing else. Blue's rear half is a sixth of the whole map, front half a sixth, and she goes in with P(Blue) = P(Red) = P(Yellow) = 1/3.

Now his argument:

1. Anything that counts as evidence is a proposition — a set of possible worlds, with the true one inside it. Learning is ruling worlds out; that's just what evidence is.
2. The rational way to update on a learned proposition is conditioning: zero out the worlds where it's false, renormalise.
3. The operator's statement, if it's evidence at all, is the proposition it expresses: "if you're in Blue, you're in the rear half" — i.e., not-Blue-or-rear-half, the material conditional.
4. Conditioning on that kills the front-half-of-Blue worlds and leaves everything else alone, so Judy's new P(Blue) works out to 1/4, down from 1/3. But the operator explicitly refused to tell her anything about which territory she's in — her credence about Blue vs Red vs Yellow should be untouched. (The intuitive answer keeps P(Blue) = 1/3 and just moves weight within Blue, 2/9 on the rear, 1/9 on the front.)
5. He claims he's checked the other candidate propositions too: any single proposition you condition on either shifts the territory odds (like the material conditional does) or fails to pin down the 2:1 ratio within Blue, because what Judy actually got told is a constraint on her posterior conditional, and a constraint isn't a set of worlds.
6. So no proposition captures Judy's evidence.
7. So by (1) and (2), the operator's statement is not something Judy can rationally update on at all — it's not evidence.
8. And here's the "proves too much" part he's so pleased about: by the same reasoning, no conditional statement is ever evidence. The doctor who says "if it's bacterial, this antibiotic will clear it," the on-call engineer who says "if the deploy failed, it's almost certainly the migration" — none of it counts, because there's no proposition to condition on. He says the moral is that Bayes works fine for outcomes but has no story at all for conditional testimony, which is most of what actual experts ever tell you.

Something feels off to me somewhere around (3) to (5) — the bit where a sentence somebody says gets turned into exactly one proposition — but I can't turn the feeling into a diagnosis, and he's thought about my hand-waving replies already (he just says "fine, then find me the proposition"). I'd genuinely like to keep (2), I use conditioning-style reasoning in my job basically every week and it'd take more than one lunch argument to give it up.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fix is that evidence isn't always a proposition to condition on but sometimes a constraint (or something about the speaker's state of knowledge), I want to see how that's more than a dodge — what's the actual updating rule, and does it keep the good cases of conditioning while handling Judy's? And is there a principled way to say why the operator's sentence is NOT equivalent to the material conditional, given that in plenty of everyday cases we do seem to update on sentences like that? Please answer in British English.
