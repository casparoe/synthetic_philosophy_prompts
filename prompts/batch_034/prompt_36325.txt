I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for the better part of two decades, and last week a thread I was following produced the sharpest exchange I have seen in years. It began under a post on population ethics, spilled over into the monthly open thread, and by the end I could not tell who had won. I would be grateful for a careful, point-by-point adjudication, and I should say up front that I have no stake in either side winning.

The exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage (the wording is close to verbatim, with the snark trimmed):

(1) One commenter, whom I shall call P, opened with the money pump. If 'better than' is not transitive, there can be options A, B and C such that A is better than B, B is better than C, and C is better than A. Anyone who acts on all three rankings can be cycled through them indefinitely, paying a small fee at each step to swap for something she herself ranks higher, and ending up holding what she started with, minus the fees. Since being exploitable in this way is a paradigm case of irrationality, P concluded that the better-than relation had better be transitive — and, since it should also be asymmetric and roughly complete, that it ought to behave like the ordering induced by real-valued utilities.

(2) The reply, from a commenter I shall call Q, was that money pumps threaten choices, not comparisons: they show that you cannot act on an intransitive ranking, not that the ranking itself is incoherent. She then ran a spectrum argument. Line up a long sequence of worlds. At one end sits a short life of extraordinarily high quality. Each subsequent world extends the life by a tiny sliver while degrading its quality by a degree too small to register as a loss. Reasonable judges take each world to be at least as good as its predecessor; indifference, like 'better than', would then seem to inherit transitivity; yet at the far end sits a very long life of unremitting drudgery, obviously worse than the life we started from. Either every imperceptible difference is decisive, which nobody accepts, or these comparative judgements fail to chain — which is to deny transitivity somewhere.

(3) P came back with cardinality. Real-valued utilities exist: however small the degradation, some negative quantity is attached to it, and there is no smallest difference between two real numbers. The 'too small to register' move is therefore a fact about the grain of human discrimination, not about the better-than relation. The spectrum merely exploits the mismatch between a coarse perceptual grid and a fine-grained scale of value, just as a series of colour patches can make a scarlet patch look 'the same colour' as an orange one without 'same colour as' failing to be transitive at the level of reflectance.

(4) Q answered that the analogy misfires, because population ethics supplies spectra in which every difference is fully visible and the chaining still looks bad. Take Parfit's mere addition: begin with a world containing a few million lives of very high quality; at each step add a sizeable group whose lives are somewhat less good, with nobody's existing life touched in any way, until you arrive at a vast population whose lives are barely worth living — the repugnant conclusion. Each step looks like an improvement, or at least no loss; the terminus looks clearly worse than the start. She added that if P wants the utility function simply to declare the end state worse than the middle, he owes us the point in the sequence at which further additions stop being welcome — and there appears to be no non-arbitrary candidate.

(5) P's final message split the relation: 'better for a given person' is transitive, and money pumps plus cardinal utilities secure that; 'better' tout court, as an impersonal ranking of worlds, may fail to be transitive, and population ethics is precisely where it fails. Q's rejoinder was that this concedes the original claim, since the thesis under dispute was the transitivity of 'better than', not of 'better for'; P had saved transitivity by amputating the interesting half of the relation. She also wondered aloud whether the personal/impersonal split can do any work at all once you notice that additions change the very population whose welfare is up for comparison, so that it is unclear 'for whom' the comparison is even being made.

That is where I got lost. P seems right that the sliver-spectrum merely exposes the grain of our discriminations; Q seems right that the mere-addition spectrum does not trade on imperceptibility in the same way. But I cannot see what, if anything, in P's final move survives Q's reply, nor whether Q's own position amounts to anything more than 'transitivity fails somewhere, somehow'.

Please adjudicate each of the five numbered moves, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating briefly; say who is more right overall; and identify anything both of them are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
