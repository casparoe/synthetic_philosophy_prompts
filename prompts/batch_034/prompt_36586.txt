I teach moral philosophy, and for the last three years I have served as faculty adviser to a student giving circle that allocates roughly $20,000 a year among charities working on global health, farmed-animal welfare, and disaster relief. Over that time the group has drifted, informally and without ever voting on it, toward a suffering-focused outlook: they weight the prevention of intense suffering far more heavily than the creation of happiness or the extension of painless lives, and several members are sympathetic to a broadly negative-utilitarian position. They have asked me to help them make this explicit — to turn a mood into a method — and I have concluded that I am the wrong person to do it, since my job in the room is to argue with them, not to systematize them. So I am outsourcing it.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the group can actually run: a sequence of questions, applied to a shortlist of eight to twelve candidate organizations, that produces a ranking the group can then vote on, within the constraints of a single two-hour meeting each quarter. The members are intelligent undergraduates with no philosophical training, so the procedure must be executable without theory: each question should be one a student can answer from a charity's published material and one page of notes, and each should have a stated threshold at which the answer is sufficient, insufficient (charity deprioritized), or indeterminate (flagged for the final discussion). The conceptual work is in the translation, and I want you to be explicit about how you did it:

1. Which distinctions internal to suffering-focused ethics must survive the simplification, and which you deliberately dropped. The candidates I see are: intensity versus duration of suffering; suffering that ends in death versus suffering that persists; preventing future suffering versus relieving present suffering; the suffering of presently existing beings versus beings who will exist only if the intervention succeeds or fails; and quick deaths that preempt suffering versus deaths preceded by it. Some of these can be collapsed; some cannot without distorting the position. Say which is which and why.

2. At least one worked application in which the procedure yields a result the group will not like. My candidates, though you may choose others: ranking palliative analgesia for a small number of people in severe pain above preventing many quick deaths; or declining to fund a life-saving program because the deaths it averts are, by the available accounts, largely painless. Run the procedure on whichever case you pick, step by step, and show where the uncomfortable verdict is generated rather than stipulated.

3. A guard-rail section. You will know, as I do, that strict negative utilitarianism is committed to views the group would find monstrous if stated plainly — that a world without suffering is preferable even if it contains no one, and that eliminating life could therefore be a solution. The procedure must never output anything that treats the elimination of capacity for suffering as a terminus rather than a tragedy, and I want you to say where the guard-rail is doing real work and where it is merely papering over a commitment the group has not examined.

4. An honest accounting of where the procedure will misfire. In particular: the places where a numeric-looking step (weighting intensity against numbers of sufferers, trading chronic mild suffering against acute severe suffering) is a judgment call dressed up as arithmetic; the points at which reasonable suffering-focused theorists disagree with one another, so the procedure is choosing a convention, not applying the theory; and the point at which the group should stop and vote, because the remaining question is one of values that no procedure can settle.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do at all. I want a short closing statement of the indeterminacies in negative utilitarianism itself — how to compare aggregate against intensity, whether and how death is bad for the one who dies painlessly, whether small harms to many can ever outweigh agony in a few — such that any ranking produced under this heading is a convention the group has adopted, and should be presented to donors as such. If it works, the group gets a method it can defend; if it fails, I would rather it fail visibly, in the places you flag, than invisibly, in the arithmetic.
