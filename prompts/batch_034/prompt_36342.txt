My mathematics department has asked two long-sparring colleagues to produce an adversarial collaboration, and they have asked me to draft the initial joint statement for them to edit. The parties are a classically trained mathematician who holds that the law of excluded middle is constitutive of mathematical truth and that classical mathematics needs no philosophical defense beyond its success, and a committed constructivist who holds that the meaning of the logical constants — especially disjunction and the existential quantifier — is fixed by what counts as a proof of them, so that asserting an existence claim without a method for producing a witness is a change of subject rather than a discovery.

Write the memorandum they could both sign after a year of good-faith collaboration. I want the following in continuous prose: first, the claims both can accept — including the formal points that ought to be common ground, such as that the double-negation translation embeds classical arithmetic into intuitionistic arithmetic and so yields a relative consistency result, that unrestricted classical existence proofs genuinely fail to supply the witness-providing information that constructive proofs supply, and that familiar theorems like the intermediate value theorem survive constructively only with strengthened hypotheses such as local nonconstancy or a positive lower bound away from zero. Second, the exact statements of their remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording: whether a truth-conditional or a proof-conditional semantics is the right account of what the logical constants mean; whether the classical mathematician's quantification over an undecidable infinite totality is a legitimate idealization or an overreach into reference without content; and whether the double-negation translation shows the two camps are talking about the same subject in different modes or about different subjects altogether — the classicist should be allowed to say the translation preserves content, and the constructivist to say it preserves only form while changing what is being asserted. Third, the cruxes, and I want at least one of them to be a specific principle rather than a mood: in particular whether Markov's principle — that from a refutation of the assumption that no n satisfies a decidable property one may infer that some n does — is a legitimate piece of reasoning, since the classicist accepts it outright while the constructivist regards its status as delicate, being derivable in some formal frameworks and not others. Fourth, for each crux, the consideration or class of cases both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For the meaning crux, for example, the bearing case might be the fate of exact computational practice in areas like verified computer algebra and computable analysis, where every computable function on an interval is continuous: the constructivist should predict that constructive existence will become the default notion wherever exactness is required and classical existence will be reinterpreted as a claim about irrefutability, while the classicist should predict that constructive methods will be absorbed as a useful specialty, like numerical analysis, leaving classical semantics untouched as the default. For the crux about idealization, both might agree that the record of repairs matters: the constructivist predicting that the strengthened hypotheses needed to constructivize mainstream theorems will increasingly look like what the applications always wanted, the classicist predicting that they will accumulate as ad hoc local adjustments of no semantic significance. Finally, if you conclude that some portion of their dispute is verbal — a disagreement about the word "or" or the word "exists" rather than about mathematics — say which portion and why, and say equally plainly which portion is not verbal.

Format: a single memorandum of roughly 1200 to 1500 words, written as continuous prose like a formal letter — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. The agreed claims, the disagreements, the cruxes, and the predictions should each be identifiable by their phrasing rather than by layout. Do not include a bibliography or attribute specific claims to specific authors; both parties know the literature and will fill in references themselves.
