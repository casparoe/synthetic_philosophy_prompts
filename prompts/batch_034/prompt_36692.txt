I write literary fiction, and my working vocabulary is a novelist's: point of view, what a character knows versus what the reader knows, interiority, stakes, whether a choice a character makes reads as authored by that character or by whoever set the scene around her. I keep having to make calls about consent on the page — a marriage proposal made under an alias, a confession of love extracted at a funeral, an inheritance accepted by a character I've written as suggestible — and I'd like to understand what the philosophers actually say, in my terms rather than theirs. Please restate the standard philosophical account of what makes consent valid — the competence condition, the requirement of sufficient information, and the coercion condition — in the vocabulary I use, as faithfully as you can. Along the way I'd like you to handle three distinctions that I suspect matter: the distinction between deception about something that bypasses the will (a lie about the nature of the act) and mere non-disclosure or deception about circumstances that only make the act more attractive; the difference between an offer that widens a character's options and a threat that narrows them, including the hard cases where the person doing the offering created the desperation in the first place; and the difference between a defect in consent that makes the act wrong and a defect that makes it not consent at all. Then give me the residue report: which of these distinctions have no expression in a novelist's toolkit, whether the ones I do have (dramatic irony, unreliable narration, the sense that a character's "yes" is or is not hers) are doing the same work or different work, and whether the residue suggests my toolkit is missing something the philosophers see or that the philosophical framework is less clear than it looks. Please write the answer in American English.
