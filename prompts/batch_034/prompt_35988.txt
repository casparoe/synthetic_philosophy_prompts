At my probability reading group this week we got badly stuck on Bertrand's chord paradox, and two members, Elena and Marcus, argued past each other for the better part of an hour. The set-up: inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle, draw a chord of the circle 'at random', and ask for the probability that the chord is longer than the triangle's side. Everyone quickly found the three classical constructions: pick two points independently at random on the circumference and join them (answer 1/3); pick a radius at random and a point at random along it, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius (answer 1/2); or pick a point uniformly at random in the disk and take the chord having that point as its midpoint (answer 1/4). What they then disagreed about was what this shows.

Marcus claimed the question has a single right answer, 1/2, on empirical grounds: if you physically realize 'a random chord' by tossing long straws onto a floor with a painted circle, keeping the chords where a straw crosses the circle, the long-run frequency of chords beating the triangle's side approaches 1/2, and that is what 'a random chord' ought to mean. Elena replied that all three constructions are equally legitimate applications of the principle of indifference — the principle just gets applied over different underlying variables, position on the circumference, distance along a radius, area of the disk — so the principle licenses contradictory answers to the same question and is thereby refuted. Marcus answered that nothing is refuted: the principle attaches to the experiment as actually performed, not to a vaguely worded description, and once you fix the generating procedure, indifference over that procedure's outcomes gives one determinate answer, with the straw-tossing fixing the canonical procedure. Elena pushed back that on his account the principle does no work at all: it only tells you to be uniform over a parameterization you have already chosen, and choosing the parameterization was precisely what the principle was supposed to settle. His straws, she added, don't discover which randomness is 'the' randomness; they merely stipulate one symmetry and ignore others — his procedure makes the midpoints of the chords cluster toward the centre (the distance of the midpoint from the centre comes out uniform along each radius, not uniform over area), which is exactly why it yields 1/2 rather than 1/4. Marcus closed by saying that even granting all that, her conclusion doesn't follow: she'd have shown at most that 'picked at random' is ambiguous, and ambiguity in a question is not a contradiction in a method — a fair question can admit different answers under different precisifications without any precisification being wrong.
