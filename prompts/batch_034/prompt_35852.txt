I'm a novelist and I've decided -- fairly firmly, though my editor keeps lobbying -- to kill off the protagonist of my series at the end of book four. She's been with readers through three books now, and I'm telling myself that a real death, on the page, unflinching, is worth more than a soft fade-out. But before I commit, I want to understand what actually happens in a reader when a fictional person dies, and the best raw data we have for that is the big one: Conan Doyle pushing Sherlock Holmes off the Reichenbach Falls in "The Final Problem," published in the Strand in December 1893.

So here's my ask: take the Holmes episode and run it through the philosophy of the paradox of fiction. As I understand it, Colin Radford set out the puzzle: we genuinely grieve for characters, we genuinely believe they don't exist, and genuine emotion seems to require belief in the existence of its object -- and the three claims can't all be true. I know the standard escape routes at least by name: Kendall Walton's make-believe picture where the reader's state is only a "quasi-emotion" (you feel quasi-fear for the slime in the horror movie, not fear), Lamarque's thought-based account where thinking hard about something can move you without belief, and maybe Gendler's alief if you want to bring in the idea of belief-like attitudes that run alongside belief.

Now map the episode. What exactly were those Victorian readers feeling when they read the last page and found Holmes gone with no body recovered? By Walton's lights it wasn't grief at all -- so what do we call the thing that made people furious at Doyle personally, kept the legend going that readers wore black armbands in mourning (I've read that historians doubt the armbands, and I'd want you to be careful about which parts of the 1893 legend are documented and which are retellings that grew in the telling)? By Lamarque's lights it was grief, but grief caused by vivid thought rather than belief -- and the Holmes case seems like a stress test for that, because readers were angry at a real man, Doyle, over an event that never happened to anyone. Where does the emotion "land," if you like -- on Holmes, on Doyle, on themselves? And the detail I keep chewing on as a novelist: the bodies were never recovered. Doyle left himself the loophole and the readers knew it -- hope never fully died, and Doyle eventually caved and brought him back (first sideways with The Hound of the Baskervilles being set earlier, then properly in "The Empty House"). Does the paradox-of-fiction literature have anything to say about hope for the non-existent? That seems to me like it might be where the theory is silent rather than merely contested.

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theories? A reader who spends a decade agitating for a fictional man's return, and who feels real vindication when he steps back into the fiction -- that looks like more than quasi-grief and more than thought-caused distress, though maybe I'm romanticizing. I'd also like your honest read on what this means for my own decision: if Walton is right, my readers' grief will be a kind of playacting and I can go ahead with a clean conscience; if the realist or thought-theory side is right, I'm doing something to them, and the fact that they'll pay me for the sequel to whatever they're feeling doesn't settle anything.

Two requests about honesty. First, please keep the settled and the contested apart -- both in the philosophy (e.g., where the Walton vs. Lamarque vs. Currie debate actually stands, and which positions are minority views rather than live contenders) and in the history (the documentary record of 1893 versus the folklore). Don't hand me a contested reading as the consensus, and if something I've said above is wrong or contested, flag it. Second, if you can't find anything in the literature that addresses something I've asked about -- the hope question especially -- just say so plainly instead of papering over the gap. I'd rather know the theory breaks there than get a confident paragraph of filler.
