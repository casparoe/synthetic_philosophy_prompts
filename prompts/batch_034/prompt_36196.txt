Last semester my school's philosophy club spent a month on Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment, and it ended in the closest thing to a real falling-out I've seen there: two of our senior members, Elena and Marcus, argued about it across four meetings and never resolved anything, so I'm trying to figure out who was right about what. To set the stage: the club had broadly agreed on Parfit's framework that there's a difference between numerical identity and psychological continuity, and that what we care about when we care about "surviving" might be the latter. The disagreement started when Elena proposed a modified case, which she called the staggered transporter: instead of scanning and destroying you all at once, the machine knocks you out and copies your memories, character, and beliefs onto a duplicate on Mars over the course of an hour, and then destroys the original body. Because the original is asleep the whole time, Elena argued the case makes the identity question unavoidable: if the duplicate wakes up on Mars feeling exactly like you, feels certain he is you, and continues your relationships and projects seamlessly, then ordinary people who say they'd step into this machine are already showing that psychological continuity, not bodily or numerical identity, is what matters to them. She added that calling this "death" would make death meaningless, since nothing of practical or emotional importance would have been lost.

Marcus objected at each step, and honestly I couldn't tell which of his points were strong. (1) He said Elena's staggered version is rigged: the one-hour unconsciousness and the sleeping original are designed to hide the destruction from the person undergoing it, so the comfort of potential users proves nothing except that people are comforted when they can't watch the bad thing happen -- a sleeping person also wouldn't mind being quietly killed, but that's not evidence that quiet death isn't death. (2) He also argued that the reason people would step in isn't that they've secretly solved the metaphysics, but that they care about effects on other people: the duplicate will comfort their parents and finish their novel, and it's those forward-looking effects, not "survival" per se, that make the choice rational -- so Elena's inference from rational stepping-in to "continuity is what matters" conflates prudence with personal identity. (3) Elena countered that Marcus's distinction proves her point rather than his: if all the things that make survival worth wanting -- your projects continuing, your relationships persisting, your future experiences happening, even if had by someone else -- are preserved, then Parfit was right that identity is not what matters, and Marcus is just refusing to say the word "survival" while conceding everything the word was for. (4) Marcus replied that this makes the view self-undermining: if what matters can be had by "someone else," then something must already matter that isn't reducible to the duplicate's psychological states, since a third party on Earth could also continue your novel and comfort your parents, and no one thinks that's survival; so the special thing about the duplicate must be the very identity relation Elena claims is dispensable. (5) Elena's last word was that Marcus was smuggling in a metaphysical relation he could never specify, and that "the very identity relation" was a placeholder for a feeling, not a fact.

I've written up all five moves in my notes, and I've been asked to present a verdict at the next meeting, but I genuinely can't tell who's right at each step -- some of the moves look strong to me and then a later move seems to undercut them. Could you adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the five moves, say whether the argument works, works only partially, or fails, and briefly justify each rating? Then, if you think both of them are making a shared mistake somewhere -- some assumption about what the staggered transporter case is supposed to show, or about what "matters" means -- please identify that too, since I suspect that's where the real action is. I'd also like to know who comes out more right overall, if only by a little, because I have to write a conclusion and the club hates it when I refuse to pick a side.
