After a career in observational astronomy, spent largely correcting survey data for selection effects, I have come to hold a fairly strong version of the thesis that history is written by the victors, and I would like to know what would count against it. In my working life I never trusted a luminosity distribution until the selection function had been modeled: Malmquist bias taught us that a biased sample does not merely omit galaxies, it manufactures a distorted structure that no amount of careful measurement within the sample can undo. My view now has four parts. (1) The surviving documentary record is a heavily filtered sample of what was once produced. (2) The filtering is not random but directional: the same concentrations of power that win wars also decide which archives are kept, copied, and catalogued, and which are burned or left to rot. (3) The resulting distortion is structural rather than local — not like one missing papyrus but such that major interpretive questions would likely come out differently had the filtering been even. (4) The standard correctives historians use — source criticism, triangulation, attention to silences — cannot fully compensate, because they estimate the selection function from the very data the selection function produced. In epidemiology we would call that circular; I gather historians call it the discipline. What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure: what observations, arguments, or findings would count against it, ranked by how decisive they would be, with special attention to (3) and (4), since (1) strikes me as nearly a truism. What does the strong view actually predict that its weaker rivals do not — where, concretely, would a fair sample tell a recognizably different story — and are there cases a committed holder of the view ought to concede as counting against it rather than explain away? I have in mind things like acerbic histories written under the very emperors they criticized, or defeated parties successfully shaping a later generation's memory. Finally, the question I find hardest, and the reason I am writing to you rather than to a historian: my instinct as a scientist is that a view insulated from empirical defeat is defective, yet I suspect this one has been qualified by its defenders until almost nothing could refute it — the loser's words surviving are read as confirming how deep the filter runs, and the victor's self-criticism as proof that dissent is permitted only when it no longer matters. Is there a principled version of the view that remains genuinely exposed, and if not, is unfalsifiability here a defect of the view, or simply what we should expect of a thesis about the state of our evidence rather than about the past itself?
