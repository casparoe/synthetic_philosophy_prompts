so heres a thing I keep bumping into. at readings and in my writers group, whenever the problem of other minds comes up (which is more often than youd think when you write first person), someone reaches for the same image: another person's mind is like a house you are permanently locked out of. you stand outside, you watch the lights go on and off, the curtains twitch, you hear someone moving around upstairs, and from that you guess at the layout, who lives there, whether theyre happy. you never get inside. your own mind is the one house you actually live in, so you know its rooms directly. and the conclusion my friends draw from this -- sometimes gloomy, sometimes kind of smug -- is that knowledge of other minds is forever indirect, guesswork really, second-rate compared to the direct view you get of your own. my editor even turned it on me personally: she said all my characters are just me with the lights arranged differently, because a novelist has never been inside anyone else's house and never will be. so, audit the analogy for me. lay out what maps to what -- what plays the role of the lights, the inhabitants, being locked out, the one house you live in -- then where the picture breaks. in particular i want to know whether the lights (behavior, facial expressions, things people say, i guess?) really are just external signs of a hidden interior the way twitching curtains are signs of someone unseen, or whether something is already off about that. and what does the analogy quietly import that the target doesnt have -- like, houses have insides that exist whether or not anyone sees the lights, and maybe that assumption is doing more work than the gloomy people admit? then tell me whether their conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the picture the whole time. and since you're at it: is the novelist version in better shape or worse shape than the general one? my editor's claim is that i literally cannot know anyone's inner life but my own, so all characterization is self-portrait in costume -- does that follow from anything defensible in the analogy, or is it a stronger claim than even the gloomy friends would sign off on?
