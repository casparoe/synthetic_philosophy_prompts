I'm a journalist — I write longread features about science and ideas for a magazine — and my next piece is about how ugly practices die. Not the big moral questions, the mechanics of it: the strange fact that something can run along for centuries, everyone half-knowing it's brutal, and then collapse in a generation or two once it starts to go. The case I want to build the piece around is the end of footbinding in China. As I understand it, the practice went from near-universal among elite families to broadly finished over roughly 1890–1910, and the thing that did it wasn't imperial edict or decades of missionaries complaining (both had been tried, the Qing even attempted bans back in the 1600s that just got quietly walked back, or so the standard histories say) — it was anti-footbinding societies whose members took a public pledge: don't bind your daughters' feet, and don't let your sons marry a woman with bound feet, with fines if you broke it.

I want to run this case through Appiah's account of moral revolutions — his line that dueling, slavery and footbinding ended when the honor code flipped, i.e. when the people doing the thing stopped feeling dignified by it and started feeling embarrassed by it, with arguments and interests trailing along behind doing cleanup. But here's the thing that bugs me about applying it here: the pledge setup looks like its solving a coordination problem more than its flipping anyone's honor. Nobody family could safely quit alone, because an unbound daughter was unmarriageable — so it was an equilibrium, not a value — and the pledges shifted a whole marriage cohort's expectations at once. On that story honor didn't lead the change, it followed the market: once a critical mass of respectable families pledged out, having bound feet quietly went from asset to liability, and the shame is downstream of the marriage maths. My editor likes this framing — "dueling was the easy case for Appiah because the duelist's own honor was on the line in the act itself, whereas footbinding put family honor on the daughter's body, and that's why it needed the pledge machinery dueling didn't" — but I don't want to file a neat thesis that a philosopher could take apart in a paragraph.

So: analyse the episode through the honor thesis as it stands. Say what it predicts for this case, what it genuinely explains (why the Qing bans and the missionary scolding did nothing, if that's the right reading), and where its silent or has to stretch. Then run it back the other way and tell me what footbinding does to the thesis — is the coordination explanation a real rival that squeezes honor out of the story, or is 'being the sort of family that doesn't do that to its daughters' itself an honor fact, in which case my market-first framing is doing alot less work than I think it is? And give me an honest verdict on my dueling-is-the-special-case line: does it survive contact with the actual mechanisms, or do I need to cut it before filing?
