I'm taking an introductory epistemology course, and we just finished a unit on the value of knowledge that I can't quite get straight. Our instructor spent most of the lecture on Gettier cases, but near the end he offered a compact account of the so-called Meno problem, which he called the one thing in the unit that was actually settled, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I write my essay around it. Here is my best reconstruction of what he said, from my notes: The Meno problem comes from Plato's dialogue where Socrates and Meno agree that true beliefs are just as good as knowledge as long as you have them — they guide you correctly for a while — but that they tend to run away, like the statues of Daedalus, which flee unless you tether them down. The tether, on this account, is justification: knowledge is justified true belief, and it is better than mere true belief because it is stable and stays put. Gettier then showed that the analysis needs patching, so we add a no-defeaters condition or a safety condition, but the core idea survives: knowledge is true belief plus something that keeps it from wandering off. From there the value problem is straightforward — a reliable belief-forming method will keep producing true beliefs in the future, whereas a lucky one won't, so knowledge is more instrumentally valuable than true belief, and once you see that, there is really nothing left to explain. Knowledge is like a friend who is usually right rather than one who was right once by accident, and preferring the first friend over the second needs no deep theory.

Something about this felt too tidy. I checked our textbook afterward and it had a section on what it called the swamping problem, which seemed to say something like the opposite of the last part of my instructor's story — that if what you care about is truth, the extra component that turns true belief into knowledge adds no further value once the truth is already there, the way a reliable espresso machine adds nothing to the coffee already in your cup. If that objection is even on the right track, then the instrumental-value story doesn't settle the Meno problem at all, and my instructor's presentation of Plato seems off too: he treated the tether as justification in something like the modern sense, and he never mentioned that the dialogue itself reaches for a much stranger answer, tied to knowledge as recollection and to giving an account of why the thing is so, which doesn't obviously map onto any of the modern analyses he was comparing it to. But he is a professional and I am a second-year undergraduate, so I might be misreading the textbook or reading too much into one line of Plato.

Please audit his account claim by claim. Which parts would a Plato scholar accept as a fair reading of the dialogue, which parts would an epistemologist working on the value of knowledge accept, where are the subtle conflation or overstatements — for instance, between what the dialogue's tethering metaphor is actually meant to do and the job justification does in a post-Gettier analysis, or between the question of whether knowledge is more valuable than true belief and the question of whether the extra component is itself the source of the extra value — and does his conclusion, that the problem dissolves once you see knowledge as instrumentally better, survive the swamping objection he never mentioned? I want to know what I can safely build my essay on and what I should quietly drop.
