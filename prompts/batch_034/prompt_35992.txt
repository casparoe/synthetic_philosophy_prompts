You are a philosopher of history and information ethics with hands-on experience in archival institutions, and you write precisely and without unnecessary jargon. I want your help working out what a familiar cluster of institutional practices silently assumes.

Consider the following combination of practices, common to national and municipal archives in most countries. (1) Archivists appraise incoming records and destroy the large majority of them under retention schedules, so that whatever survives is a deliberate selection, made mostly by the successors of the institutions whose records they are. (2) The profession arranges and describes records from the point of view of the institution that created them — the file structures, the indexing vocabulary, and the categories are the creator's — even when the people described in those files (prisoners, welfare recipients, colonized subjects, the recipients of surveillance) never consented and appear in the record only as objects of someone else's paperwork. (3) At the same time, these same institutions increasingly run community-documentation projects that actively solicit materials from marginalized groups, and describe themselves in their mission statements as guardians of collective memory and as instruments of public accountability.

What theory of the archive's purpose does this combination presuppose? Is the archive implicitly a memory-keeper, and if so, whose memory; an accountability mechanism, preserving evidence with which to hold the state to account; or something else again? Notice the apparent tension I'm puzzled by: a practice that preserves, by and large, the paperwork of power, coupled with a mission statement that claims to speak for everyone. Do (2) and (3) presuppose consistent things, or is that tension real rather than merely rhetorical? In your reconstruction, distinguish carefully between what the practices strictly require — the views without which they would be incoherent — and what they merely sit comfortably with, and identify the points where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of what archives are for would all license them.

Then consider an idea I have been toying with: treating the pattern of omissions as itself a record — call it a shadow catalogue, an inverse index of what the archive never collected or has destroyed, from which one could in principle reconstruct an institutional picture of which lives were considered worth documenting and which were not. If archivists took that idea seriously — if the silences were catalogued alongside the documents — would the standard presuppositions shift? For instance, would the archive's accountability function have to extend to the record of record-destruction itself?

Please begin with some open-ended brainstorming before settling on your reconstruction, think carefully about each of the three practices separately as well as together, and be explicit about where you are uncertain or where you think I have framed something badly.
