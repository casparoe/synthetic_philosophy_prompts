I write a column on science and ideas, and a longtime source of mine — call him Daniel, a mathematician who thinks a great deal about Hofstadter's old superrationality pieces, the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery — has a track record on cooperation cases that I have never managed to pin to a single principle. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a total stranger about whom he knows nothing, he defects. In a one-shot dilemma with a close friend, after the two of them have talked the problem through and converged on a view, he cooperates. He votes in national elections even when the chance of his vote being pivotal is negligible, and he calls the expected-value argument for abstaining a mistake. And in a lottery he actually plays with nineteen equally reflective friends — each privately names a positive integer, and if all twenty name the same number, each receives a million dollars divided by that number, while if even one person diverges nobody gets anything — he names 1 without hesitation. Next week we meet to discuss two new cases, and I would like to walk in with a prediction. Case one: a one-shot dilemma with a stranger who, he happens to know, has read all the same rationality literature he has, though he has no idea whether she acts on it. Case two: an election in which, by every poll, the voters whose reasoning most closely mirrors Daniel's overwhelmingly support the other candidate, while the people who share his values but reason in a completely different style support his. What will he say about each? I am less interested in the answers than in the principle he seems to be running — superrationality, an evidential treatment of his own choices, team-reasoning keyed to shared values, or something else — and in which rival principles fit his record just as well. Please take a definite position: name the principle you would bet on, explain why it beats the alternatives on his record, and tell me what further case would best discriminate between the contenders.
