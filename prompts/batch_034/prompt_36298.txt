I spent the last thirty-six years of my working life in low-temperature experimental physics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in earnest. I have drafted an essay for a small blog I keep, aimed at former colleagues and other scientifically literate laypeople, and I would be grateful for a careful critique before I publish. Rather than asking for general impressions, let me state my thesis, reproduce a condensed version of the draft, and then list the specific respects in which I want to be corrected or pressed.

The thesis: thought experiments in physics are best understood as consistency audits of our idealizations. A good thought experiment brings two models, or two applications of one model, into contact at a limiting case and asks the theory to assign that case to both; the contradiction exposes a seam between idealizations that we had been papering over. Because the audit concerns our conceptual arrangements rather than the world, a thought experiment can never confer empirical support by itself; the world gets its say only when the repaired theory is tested afterward. My difficulty is Maxwell's demon, which appears to violate this thesis, and I cannot decide whether it defeats me or confirms me.

Condensed draft:

Physicists speak of thought experiments with reverence, as if Galileo settled the question of falling bodies from an armchair. In the Two New Sciences, Salviati indeed needs no tower: he takes a heavy stone and a light one, joins them with a cord, and asks whether the compound falls faster or slower than the heavy stone alone. As a single body it is heavier than the heavy stone, so by the received doctrine it should fall faster; yet the light stone, dragging behind, should retard the heavy one, so the compound should fall slower. The doctrine assigns the case two contradictory verdicts because weight serves it in two roles it cannot distinguish — as the cause of natural motion and as its measure — and only one of those roles survives the audit.

Einstein's elevator works the same way. Newton's mechanics accepts the exact equality of inertial and gravitational mass as a brute coincidence, and the observer sealed in a cabin, unable to tell free fall from inertial motion or a gravitational field from uniform acceleration, dramatizes that coincidence until the seam becomes unbearable. Notice where thought experiments are drawn: not to the center of a theory, where everything fits, but to these unexplained equalities and limit cases where two of our models meet uneasily.

The schema explains something otherwise puzzling — why thought experiments feel like a priori revelation. They are, I claim, coherence restoration. The knowledge gained is knowledge about the arrangement of our concepts; the world contributes nothing at the moment of the thought and everything at the moment of the later experiment. A thought experiment can refute, or force a restructuring, but it cannot support.

Here is my problem case. Maxwell asks mechanics and thermodynamics to jointly describe a tiny being who sorts fast molecules from slow, and each theory is individually content while the pair licenses a perpetual motion machine of the second kind. Leo Szilard's analysis of a one-molecule engine, and the later recognition that the demon's memory must be erased at thermodynamic cost, located the expense of measurement in information itself. But this did not merely restore consistency between two models of ours; it seems to have discovered something about the world — the trade between information and entropy. I confess I do not know whether the demon is the counterexample that sinks my thesis or an especially dramatic instance of it.

I deliberately excluded Schrödinger's cat, on the ground that it is not an argument exposing a seam but an illustration of a predicament a theory already admits; it startles only if we smuggle in classical expectations.

Now my requests:

1. Accuracy. Have I rendered the Galileo argument, the elevator equivalence argument, and the demon-to-Szilard-to-erasure story correctly, both as physics and as history? I would rather be told bluntly now than corrected in my comment section.

2. The demon. As I see it, either the demon was a disguised argument with a false premise — that measurement and memory are thermodynamically free — which Szilard and his successors refuted, in which case my schema holds; or the information-entropy connection was genuinely discovered by imagining a being, in which case my thesis that thought experiments never confer empirical support is false. Is there a third option? Which of the two is right?

3. Reinvention. At a seminar I attended, one participant maintained that thought experiments are nothing but arguments wearing vivid clothing, and another that in a successful one we somehow perceive the laws of nature directly — a view that struck me at the time as mysticism, though I may have done it an injustice. Are my schema and thesis distinguishable from the first of these, and have I been fair to the second? I want to know whether my essay is a contribution or a retired man's rediscovery of a well-worn wheel, and, if the latter, in what direction I should push the thesis so that it becomes genuinely mine.

4. The cat. Does my exclusion of Schrödinger's cat hold up, or does it show that my criterion — that a genuine thought experiment must probe a seam between idealizations — is doing too much work, quietly ruling out whatever it cannot accommodate?

5. Interest. Would this essay hold the attention of my intended readership, or should the autobiographical framing and the somewhat scholastic apparatus be cut? Please comment as freely on the presentation as on the argument.

I should add that I have no access to a research library, so please confine your comments to what you can state from your own knowledge.
