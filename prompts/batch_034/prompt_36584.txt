A friend from my evolutionary-biology reading group — the sort of person who explains altruism at parties in terms of inclusive fitness — has been pressing an argument on me that he says settles the levels-of-selection debate once and for all, in favour of the gene. The trouble is that when I ran the same reasoning one level *down*, it seemed to demolish his own position, and neither of us can locate the mistake. The upward run goes like this. (1) A unit of selection must be a replicator: an entity that persists across generations by making copies of itself, and whose copies are what selection sorts among. (2) Genes pass this test: they persist for many generations, in many bodies, via faithful copying. (3) Organisms fail it: an offspring is a scrambled half-and-half mixture of two parents rather than a copy of either, and the organism itself is gone within a generation — a temporary vehicle, in Dawkins' terminology. (4) Groups fail it even more spectacularly: their membership churns continuously, and no group ever produces a copy of itself. (5) Therefore all selection is ultimately selection among genes, and talk of selection at higher levels is mere bookkeeping — a coarser description of gene selection that happens to be easier for big-brained primates to follow. This is roughly the line Gould complained about somewhere when he said the gene is a bookkeeper, not a beneficiary, and my friend is proud of it.

Now the downward run, which is where I got stuck. (6) Take any gene and split it in half: whenever the gene is copied, each half is copied, and each half persists across generations exactly as long as the gene does. The halves are even safer from recombination than the whole gene, since crossing over splits long sequences far more often than short ones — so by the persistence criterion, the fragment is a *better* replicator than the gene, and the single nucleotide better still, all the way down to individual base pairs. (7) A base pair at a conserved position, identical in every member of a species for tens of millions of years, has been copied with perfect fidelity an enormous number of times; by (1) it is the paradigm of a replicator. (8) So by the same argument that dismissed organisms, gene-level selection is dismissed: all selection is really selection among nucleotides, and gene talk is bookkeeping. My friend says this is where he knew I was being silly — a nucleotide at a fixed locus isn't competing with anything, nothing is being sorted among nucleotides at invariant positions, junk DNA included; down there there are no winners and losers, just uniform copying. But when I asked him to point to the step that fails, he started talking about "variation" and "competition," which sounded like a second criterion entirely — and when I noted that his original dismissal of organisms and groups had used only the copying criterion, he accused me of sophistry and went to make coffee.

So here is what I want from you. Name the precise step that should give, explain why that step looked fine when my friend ran the argument upward, and price the options honestly. I can see at least three exits and can't evaluate them. One: the argument equivocates between mere copying and differential copying with consequences, and the repair that saves gene selection has side effects — it would demote genes at fixed loci from being units of selection, and it would quietly readmit some organisms, since a parthenogenetic aphid produces offspring that really are copies of herself, which my friend will hate. Two: bite the nucleotide bullet and accept that gene selection just is nucleotide selection described coarsely — but then the friend's dismissal of group selection collapses into the same symmetry, and everything becomes bookkeeping all the way down, which seems to satisfy no one and to make his original conclusion vanish rather than follow. Three: reject the idea that there is a single privileged level at all, and say the question was misposed from the start — though I'd want to know what that costs, since the gene's-eye picture does seem to explain a lot. If any of these corresponds to a standard position in the literature — the replicator/vehicle distinction, Williams' choice of currency, the selection-for versus selection-of distinction — tell me which option it maps onto and what it pays for the mapping. For what it's worth, I would most like to keep (2), genes as the paradigm replicators, but if keeping it means swallowing (7) I will pay; and both my friend and I keep having the vague sense that the trick is that one criterion is applied going up, to exclude organisms, and a different one going down, to exclude nucleotides — so if that is in fact the flaw, please make it precise, because neither of us has managed to.
