I am a novelist, and my husband died last spring. Somewhere in the weeks after the funeral I built an argument out of Hofstadter's idea that the self is a pattern — a loop of symbols modelling itself — rather than the grey matter carrying it, so that a person can, in some diminished sense, live on in the brains of those who knew them. It consoled me for about a month. Now it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the faulty joint.

(1) My "I" is not the organism but the pattern it runs: a repertoire of memories, turns of phrase, tastes, reactions, and the habit of referring all of these back to itself.
(2) A pattern is not tied to one substrate; it can be instantiated, wholly or partially, elsewhere.
(3) Those who have known me intimately have, over decades, built running models of me that partially instantiate that pattern.
(4) A partial instantiation of my pattern in another brain is a part of me located in that brain; so I am already distributed, mostly but not entirely, inside my skull.
(5) When the organism dies, the external instantiations persist; so, in the same diminished sense Hofstadter intends, I survive my death.

The trouble is what else these steps license. By the same reasoning, a novelist whose prose style thousands of readers have absorbed is more thoroughly survived than a mother whom her child only half remembers; the critic who has made a career of mocking my work has built a more detailed model of me than my sister ever needed, so I will apparently persist most vigorously in ridicule; and since I spent four years inside my last narrator, with readers spending longer still, there is by now more of her pattern running in other brains than there ever was of mine — and nothing in the argument, as far as I can see, keeps her from counting as the one who survives, rather than me.

Each step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down. Which one do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost — including, I would like to know, whether it costs me the consolation with which all this began? I would rather keep the survival claim than most of the rest, if a choice must be made. Please state how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the step or claim about which you feel least sure.
