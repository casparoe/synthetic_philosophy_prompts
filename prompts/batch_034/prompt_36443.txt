I am a novelist, and my editor — meaning well — pressed on me a chapter from a book on the craft of writing that leans on the philosophy of hard choices. I won't name the book, since its author deserves better than my paraphrase, but the relevant passage runs roughly like this:

"What every serious writer eventually faces is what the philosophers call incommensurability. When two options are incommensurable, they cannot be measured against each other on any common scale, so there is no fact of the matter about which one is better. Think of the choice between the novel you need to write and the stable teaching post that would pay the mortgage. Philosophers such as Ruth Chang call these hard choices, and the lesson of her work is a liberating one: since reason runs out in such cases, the choice between them is arbitrary, and we are free to simply pick. The mark of the artist is picking without apology."

I have two reactions I cannot reconcile. On the one hand, the claim that there is literally no fact of the matter about whether finishing the novel is better than taking the post seems too strong — surely, in some ordinary sense, facts obtain about which life is better for me. On the other hand, the conclusion that one may simply pick feels right, and it is reassuring, because I have picked, repeatedly, and largely without apology.

So my question: is this a fair rendering of what philosophers mean by incommensurability and hard choices? Please go claim by claim. Which parts would a philosopher working on value accept, which are subtle distortions, and which are simply wrong? A few things I would especially like sorted out. First, is incommensurability the same as indifference between options, or does the view carefully keep those apart — and if it keeps them apart, where in the passage does the conflation do its work? Second, does 'cannot be measured on a common scale' amount to 'no fact of the matter about which is better,' or is something smuggled in by that step? Third, does the view of hard choices actually license simply picking, or does the popular gloss invert the point the view was making about what agents do when reasons run out? I'm inclined to trust the reassurance, so I'd also like you to be blunt about whether that trust is earned. Finally: does the author's advice to the artist — pick, and pick without apology — survive the corrected version of the view, or was it resting on the distorted parts all along?
