I'd like a genuine credence, with its derivation, on the statue-and-clay problem. The claim: in the standard case where a statue sits on a shelf and the piece of clay constituting it occupies exactly the same region and shares all of its non-modal properties at every moment, the statue and the clay are nonetheless numerically distinct objects. My credence in distinctness is about 0.6; a friend who agrees with me on nearly everything else in metaphysics sits at 0.2. Please give your own number, and decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on: the probability that the indiscernibility of identicals forces distinctness once we grant that the clay, but not the statue, could survive being squashed into a ball (i.e., whether modal properties count as genuine properties); the probability that constitution-is-not-identity is a coherent middle position rather than a contradiction with too many coincident objects; the probability, conditional on coincidence being intolerable, that the cheapest escape actually works, whether that is denying that statues exist at all, relative identity, or a four-dimensionalist account on which the two share their current temporal parts but differ in extent; and any further terms you think belong, each with a number and a one-line justification. Then combine them, tell me whether my friend and I most likely disagree about the status of modal properties or about the cost of the alternatives, and identify the single argument that would move your number the most.
