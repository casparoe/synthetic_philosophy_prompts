So heres my situation. I'm a novelist -- fifteen years in, mostly literary fiction -- and I've become slightly obsessed with a question from a completely different world: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Apparently there's this whole research tradition around it (Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project and cousins of it) about how a letterform is somehow both a category and a style at once -- every 'a' ever drawn, in every font, every person's handwriting, is recognizably the *same thing*, and yet there's no fixed set of features they share, and the skill of *recognizing* an 'a' and the skill of *creating* a new one that still reads as 'a' seem to be two faces of one ability. Some people talk about a Platonic essence, others about family resemblance, and the whole thing smells faintly familiar to me but I can't pin down why.

I want you to act as a cognitive scientist who moonlights in philosophy of mind and who has actually read novelists. Please think carefully before answering -- start with a little brainstorming on paper about which pieces of my vocabulary could even do the work, then commit to the best translation and be honest about the rest. Here's the vocabulary I actually think in: voice and how it stays constant across every line of dialogue; what happens to a character when an actor is recast or a book is adapted to film; how I can change a character's age, job, gender even, and she survives, but one wrong sentence in her mouth and readers feel the character "break"; the difference between consistency (which is table stakes) and aliveness; how a character can be recognizably herself in another author's fanfic. Restate the letterform debate in those terms as faithfully as you can -- category, style, recognition-as-creation, whatever the Platonic vs family-resemblance dispute amounts to here.
