A philosophy podcast I caught summed up the whole William James vs Clifford debate like this: Clifford held that it's wrong to believe anything without sufficient evidence, so he basically wanted to ban religious faith. James replied that Clifford was being too rigid, because on James's view, whenever a question matters to you personally, you're entitled to just go ahead and believe whichever answer you prefer -- our passional nature has as much say as our intellect. His go-to case is religion: since God's existence can't be settled by evidence, James says believing anyway is justified, and the belief can even help make itself true, the way confidence that you'll win a duel makes you win. So in the end James thinks faith creates its own evidence, and Clifford loses because most of what people believe day to day isn't backed by anything like proof anyway.

Is this a fair reading? It sounded kind of strawman-ish to me, especially the "whenever it matters to you, believe whatever you prefer" bit, but I've only read about the essay secondhand. Which parts would a James scholar nod along to, which are distortions, and does the podcast's final takeaway that faith creates its own evidence actually follow from anything James says? Also curious whether they're being fair to Clifford or if thats a caricature too.
