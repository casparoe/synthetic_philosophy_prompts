In three weeks I present the closing plenary at an interdisciplinary workshop on famine early warning, and I need help preparing for the question period.

My thesis, in brief: famines are political crimes, not natural disasters, and the "natural disaster" framing is not merely a descriptive error but a causally complicit one — it licenses slow responses, spreads responsibility thin, and lets the culpable off the hook. My argument runs as follows. First, Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis in Poverty and Famines (1981): people starve in famines because they lose the legal and economic ability to command food — entitlements collapse — not because food is absent; Sen classified the 1943 Bengal famine as a "boom" famine, in which food availability per head was not exceptionally low and some groups' entitlements rose while others' collapsed. Second, the export pattern: during the Irish famine of the 1840s and the Chinese famine of 1958–61 (the Great Leap Forward), substantial food outflows or requisitions continued while people died — again, availability was not the binding constraint. Third, the predictability point, following Alex de Waal (Famine Crimes, 1997, and Mass Starvation, 2018): if a famine is predictable months out through early warning signals, and if the decision not to act is a political choice, then the resulting deaths are closer to a culpable omission than to a natural catastrophe — hence his case for treating starvation as a matter for international criminal law rather than humanitarian charity.

The audience, unfortunately for me, is precisely the professional culture that speaks the language I want to retire. Roughly half are climate scientists and hazard modelers who think in terms of drought indices, precipitation anomalies, and attribution studies; their working vocabulary treats drought as the famine driver and famine as its downstream impact. About a third are humanitarian logisticians and early-warning practitioners — the people who actually run systems like FEWS NET — who think in terms of phases of acute food insecurity, seasonal calendars, and market functioning, and who are viscerally suspicious of what they will hear as a lawyer's framing of their operational problem. The remainder are a few development economists who know Sen's work well and will not be impressed by a hand-wave in his direction. There is also one international lawyer whose questions about mens rea and command responsibility I cannot answer with my current knowledge.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise — not generic objections from a philosophy seminar — ordered by how much damage each would do to my case if I fumbled it. Phrase each objection the way that segment of the audience would phrase it (the climate scientists in the language of drought and drivers; the logisticians in the language of operations and triage; the economists as pushback on my reading of Sen; the lawyer as pushback on my use of the word "crime"). For each, give me a two-minute reply I can actually deliver, and an honest note on where the reply is soft — I would rather know now than onstage. Pay particular attention to the two objections I can already see coming and cannot currently answer: the climate scientists' point that anthropogenic warming is now measurably worsening droughts, which threatens to re-naturalize the very famines I am trying to de-naturalize; and the lawyers' point that "crime" requires intent or at least knowledge, and that lumping negligent famines with deliberate ones may weaken rather than strengthen the criminal-law framing. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, state in a fixed form at the very end a last line beginning "Verdict:" containing a short phrase naming the single objection, from your ordered list, that is most likely to sink the talk if I do not defuse it.
