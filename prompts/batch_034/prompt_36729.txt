I'm a retired invertebrate zoologist -- thirty-some years mostly on decapod crustaceans, crayfish and shore crabs, with a detour into octopus husbandry late in my career -- and since I stopped going into the lab I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of mind. The reading group I joined (mostly retired academics, one ex-chemist who's now a Buddhist) keeps circling back to which animals are conscious, and I've noticed we all use the word "conscious" to mean about five different things and then talk past each other for an hour. Ever since that whole boiling-lobsters-alive business made the news my grandkids keep asking me if crabs feel pain, and honestly I'd like a better answer than "define feel." So I want to build a decision tree for the group: you start from something everyone in the room will grant (a dog is conscious, a rock isn't) and each branch node is a question about a concrete case or commitment, phrased so people can answer it without already knowing which philosophical camp it funnels them into, and each leaf is a named position on how consciousness is distributed across animals.

Here's the structure I'd like you to work out, and please do some actual thinking before writing it, sketch the logical space first rather than just dredging up a list of famous names: the first split should probably be whether consciousness comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing, then whether it requires a specific kind of neural machinery (my old bias, honestly) or is multiply realizable, then whether reportability or metacognition is essential versus whether a creature can suffer without knowing it suffers, and then whether whatever your favorite complexity measure is, integrated information or whatever, entails experience as such. Test cases have to include at least: fish pain, bee navigation and play-like behavior, lobster nociception, the nematode with its ~300 neurons, and (to make the panpsychists sweat) a thermostat or a running simulation. Leaves must include at least a higher-order-thought style view, a global-workspace style view, a neurobiological-substrate view, a deflationary or behaviorist "there's no fact of the matter" view, and a panpsychist or IIT-style "it's everywhere, in degrees" view. For each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and for any combination of answers nobody seems to occupy, say whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unattractive or unexplored. If two leaves genuinely can't be separated by any question a normal person could answer -- I suspect the substrate view and a conservative version of the degrees view end up twins on crustaceans, for instance -- flag it instead of inventing a fake distinction.

Please write it as if you're a professional philosopher of mind who also knows the ethology literature, and keep the whole thing tight: the tree plus the leaf commentary plus the flags, 700 words max, no separate essay, and don't pad it with a history-of-the-problem intro, my reading group will not read that, they barely read the actual readings. Bonus points if the very first branching question would make my Buddhist ex-chemist friend and me split apart within two moves, since that's the whole point of the exercise. Don't worry about citations either, I just want the tree.
