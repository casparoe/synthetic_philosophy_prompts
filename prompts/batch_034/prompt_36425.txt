I was diagnosed three weeks ago with a cancer that, depending on which study I read, carries a five-year survival rate somewhere between 40% and 85%. The spread is not scientific chaos: it is the reference class problem in its purest form. Whether I am counted among all patients with this diagnosis, or only those under fifty, or only those whose tumors carry my particular mutation, or only those treated since immunotherapy became standard practice, changes the number more than any treatment decision my doctors and I will make. This weekend I am giving a short address at a family dinner — my partner, my siblings, my two adult children, and a niece finishing her PhD in biostatistics — at which I will explain the situation to them for the first time. Please write the speech. It must tell them the truth about the diagnosis, and it must also explain, without condescension, why the statistics they will inevitably look up that same evening do not constitute a verdict on me specifically: which group I belong to is not a fact about the world waiting to be looked up, but a choice — and my doctors, my family, and I disagree about how to make it. Assume the niece will catch any statistical fudging, and assume my youngest will hear every sentence as an answer to a single unasked question. The speech should give her a real answer, not a comforting one.
