I chair the methodology panel of a fourteen-person forecasting unit that supplies numeric probability estimates to our parent organization's risk committee — we forecast operational, market, and reputational events on quarterly horizons. Our output was purely qualitative until two years ago, when I pushed the move to explicit credences, and last quarter's year-end review has landed us in a controversy that my panel must now resolve by written opinion.

The immediate occasion is the case of one of our strongest forecasters. Two facts emerged from the review. First, while each of her individual forecasts was as well-calibrated as anyone's on the panel, taking her forecasts across related questions together reveals incoherence: on several occasions the probabilities she assigned to mutually exclusive outcomes, issued weeks apart, sum to well over one, and no single omniscient probability assignment could rationalize her ledger. Second, in an exit interview she admitted that on one question she deliberately reported a higher credence than she actually held — a supplier insolvency she privately put at perhaps fifteen percent but reported as thirty — because she believes the risk committee systematically underreacts to that class of risk, and she wanted her forecast to do compensatory work as an action rather than a report.

My panel is split three ways. One faction wants formal discipline: adopt a strictly proper scoring rule, evaluate each forecast on its own merits, and treat her strategic misreporting as falsification. A second faction argues that incoherence across separately issued forecasts is a bookkeeping artifact rather than a rational failing — no one is ever asked all questions at once — and that her distortion, whatever its ethics, actually improved the committee's decisions, which is the point of the exercise. A third worries that any scoring regime we adopt will reward calibration and resolution while remaining blind to whether forecasts were reached by rational updating at all, which none of our metrics can see.

Draft the opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete policy — what, if any, scoring rule we adopt; how we treat cross-question incoherence; and what we do about this forecaster specifically — and it should engage the strongest version of the position that loses, not a caricature of it.
