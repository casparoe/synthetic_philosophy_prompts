I teach a small seminar on decision theory for philosophy students — about 14 of them, third year, one 90-minute session. We've just gotten to the absent-minded driver (the Piccione–Rubinstein example from the imperfect-recall literature), and I want them to feel the paradox in their bones before we touch the follow-up papers, because on the page it just looks like algebra.

In case it's not at hand: the driver is heading home along a route with two identical intersections. At each intersection they can exit or continue. Exit at the first intersection and the trip is worth 1 (long way home). Continue past the first, exit at the second: worth 4 (best). Continue past both: 0 (highway forever). The catch is the driver is absent-minded — at an intersection they genuinely don't know whether it's the first one or the second one. If they commit in advance to a policy of continuing with probability p at whichever intersection they find themselves at, the math says p = 3/8 is best, expected value 25/16, which beats the 1 you lock in by always exiting. But from *inside* the information set things fall apart. Sitting at an intersection, exiting guarantees 1, while continuing looks terrible once you notice that whatever you do here, past-you did too — if I'm the kind of driver who continues, I already continued at the first one, so continuing now means the highway and 0. So the policy that's best to commit to is not the action that's best to take once you're there, and no belief about where you are seems to make the 3/8 mix both sensible to plan and sensible to execute. That clash is what I want them to feel, not the formula.

Design the in-class game. My half-formed thought is to make the absent-mindedness physical somehow — e.g. the "driver" is a relay of different students at the wheel, none of whom knows which intersection they're at, operating under a shared policy they agreed on beforehand — but you tell me whether that works or whether there's a better way to wipe memory between intersections (and keep them from just counting off the intersections when I'm not looking — they're sharp and will absolutely try to cheat the information set, so close that loophole). I need: rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (candy or a couple of course points are fine — small stakes but real), a timeline fitting the 90 minutes including the debrief, and the debrief itself, mapping what actually happened onto the planning perspective vs the action perspective, the randomization, and the "whatever I do now, I did then" reasoning. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the paradox the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking afterwards. If you think the relay-of-drivers framing creates a distortion — they'll start treating it as a multi-agent bargaining problem instead of one confused agent — say so and give me the fix. definately don't just give me a lecture outline with a quiz at the end; the problem has to happen to them.
