I teach a second-year bioethics course at a university — 18 students, one 80-minute seminar — and next week's topic is speciesism: the question of whether favouring human interests over the interests of animals, merely because they are human interests, is a prejudice analogous to racism or sexism, or is instead a defensible way of treating species membership as morally significant in its own right. I want an in-class activity in which the students run into the problem themselves rather than hearing it characterised from the front.

The activity I have in mind should work roughly as follows. The class is split into two parallel 'allocation committees' for a fictional charitable trust with a fixed budget that can fund only some of a set of medical cases — but the two committees receive their case files in different forms. One committee sees species labels with the dossiers (a healthy adult chimpanzee, a dog, a human patient in a permanent vegetative state, a severely cognitively impaired human child, a typical adult human); the other committee sees the same cases described only in terms of the patient's capacities and interests — sentience, memory, social bonds, capacity for fear and anticipation — with species deliberately never named, or replaced with a code. The cases should be constructed so that species membership and cognitive capacity cross-cut: some human cases with capacities at or below those of some animal cases. The trust's charter is left deliberately thin — something like 'allocate the funds where they do the most good' — so the committees must decide for themselves whether species is a permissible sorting criterion.

Please design the full activity: the case files themselves (six or so, with enough realistic clinical and behavioural detail to be answerable without the species label), the budget and what the money buys so that trade-offs are forced and real money isn't needed, the committee roles, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and how to run the reveal when the two committees' allocations are compared side by side. Then write the debrief: which moments in the activity map onto which parts of the philosophical debate — the argument from marginal cases, the overlap between the human and animal distributions of capacities, the difference between denying that species matters directly and holding that it matters via relations or potential — and how to get students to articulate whether the anonymised committee's allocations are more principled or merely less informed.

Finally, include two honest notes: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students guessing the species behind the codes and treating the anonymity as a puzzle to be solved, or the discussion collapsing into which cases the students happen to find emotionally vivid) and how to handle each; and which feature of the debate the activity structurally cannot reproduce — I suspect the relational considerations, like what families owe their own members, are hard to stage in a committee about strangers, but you should tell me whether that's right and what else the format distorts, so I can supply it myself in the debrief.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
