I'm in a moral psychology seminar and we have been stuck on basically the same fight for six weeks now and its getting annoying. The setup: moral foundations theory says moral intuitions cluster into a handful of foundations (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, plus the liberty one that got added later), and the well-replicated finding is that American liberals run mostly on care and fairness while conservatives use all of them. Haidt drew a semi-normative moral from this in his big book — his metaphor is taste, like conservatives simply have more receptors and can taste more of the moral menu, and liberals who want to understand or persuade anyone should widen their palate.

Then the question everyone fights about: does any of this tell us who is RIGHT? Half my seminar says obviously not, it's psychology not ethics, and anyway breadth isn't a virtue — someone with rich sanctity and loyalty intuitions can be a superstitious bigot, and the breadth makes them worse off morally, not better. The other half (me, sometimes) pushes back: it seems like a genuine defect if you literally cannot register certain moral considerations, the way we'd say a person with no capacity for gratitude is missing something even before we ask what they do with it.

There's also a third thing that keeps getting dragged in. Some critics claim the fairness foundation is doing a cheat, because it lumps together two very different things — proportionality-based fairness, where rewards track merit, and equality-based fairness — and that the way the theory carves things up already bakes in a conservative-friendly picture where proportionality gets to be the "default" version of fairness. So maybe the whole who-uses-more-foundations question is just an artifact of how you slice the pie.

Here's the thing: no facts are moving anyone. Everyone accepts the survey results, everyone accepts that intuitions don't budge, nobody switches sides. My working theory is that the argument is secretly about whether moral breadth is a virtue, that this is a normative question the psychology can't touch, and the two sides keep talking past each other while pretending to argue about data. But I'm not sure — maybe it's partly a verbal fight about what "moral" even means in "moral foundations," or maybe the is-ought gap can actually be climbed here, e.g. if some foundations demonstrably track real harms better in certain domains. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side is really claiming, and tell me what would even count as progress — a sharper distinction, a particular kind of study, reframing the question, whatever — or whether this is just one of those arguments people have forever and the stall itself is telling us something. Also, if my taste-bud framing is load-bearing in a bad way (like if it already assumes breadth = value), I'd want to know that too.
