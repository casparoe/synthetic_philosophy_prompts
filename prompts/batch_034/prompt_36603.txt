I've been reading about the history of animal minds on my own — no formal training, just evenings and a couple of popular books — and I keep running into Descartes' comparison of animals to machines. As I understand it, he had in mind both the plain clock and the cleverer mechanical statues that stood in the royal gardens of his day, which would move when a visitor stepped on certain tiles. My brother-in-law is a butcher, and when the topic came up at dinner he leaned on exactly this picture: an animal is a clockwork statue, clocks don't suffer, so a squealing pig is just noise from the mechanism, and there's nothing for anyone to feel bad about. I couldn't answer him as well as I wanted, and since then I've noticed two things that make me unsure the picture does the work he thinks it does.

First, the two examples aren't the same. A clock runs one fixed cycle and ignores you entirely; a garden statue responds to which tile you step on. If the argument rests on the clock version, then the moment you point out that animals plainly respond differently to different situations — flee, freeze, approach, learn — the analogy looks broken. But the garden-statue version seems to survive that, since those statues really did react to their surroundings, and Descartes apparently knew both kinds of machine and still put animals on that side of the line. So which version is my brother-in-law's argument actually using, and does it matter?

Second, we now believe human bodies are machines too — chemistry and electrical signals rather than springs and water, but machinery all the way down. If being built of mechanical parts was enough to empty the pig of an inner life, the same reasoning looks like it empties me, and my brother-in-law doesn't accept that conclusion. He says humans have a soul or reason that the machine lacks, but that seems like putting in by hand the very thing the analogy was supposed to establish.

Can you help me get clear? I'd like a straightforward mapping of what is supposed to correspond to what — the spring to the heart, the water to the nerves, or whatever it is — then where the mapping breaks down, and especially which features of clocks and garden statues the picture quietly carries over that animals don't have: being built to a maker's plan, existing to put on a display, working the same when taken apart and reassembled, having no good or bad states of their own. Then tell me whether my brother-in-law's conclusion — nothing suffering, nothing owed — survives the breakdown, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. And if some honest modern version of the picture can be salvaged, what would it look like, and what could it actually show?
