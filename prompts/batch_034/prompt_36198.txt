been reading about the vagueness wars again (epistemicism vs supervaluationism) and honestly the whole thing feels stuck in amber since like the mid-90s. epistemicists say "heap" has an exact cutoff, we just can't know where it is, and that our inability to know it is explained by margin-for-error principles. supervaluationists say a borderline statement is neither true nor false, it's true only if true on all precisifications, so "this is a heap" comes out false and so does its negation. both sides have had their standard moves for decades — the epistemicist's "you cant deny bivalence without contradiction," the supervaluationist's "your unknowable boundary is a fake fact, an epicycle" — and basically nobody switches teams.

and here's the thing that makes me actually care: I keep running into this in AI eval work, where we have to draw lines like "is this model capable enough to be dangerous" or "did this eval demonstrate real scheming" and the capability is a continuum but the policy needs a binary. some people on the team basically go epistemicist by instinct — there's a fact of the matter about when the model crosses the line, we just can't measure it precisely — and others go supervaluationist — the borderline cases just dont have a determinate verdict, so stop demanding one and define a convention instead. watching that argument repeat the philosophy literature in miniature makes me suspect the philosphy debate is not really about heaps at all.

so: what kind of disagreement is the epistemicism vs supervaluationism thing, actually? verbal (two meanings of "true"), substantive-but-empirically-empty (a hidden commitment about the nature of facts), or two different questions — maybe one about the semantics of ordinary speakers and one about the metaphysics of language — smuggled under one word? and given your diagnosis, what would even count as progress here — a formal result, a decision-theoretic application that punishes one side, or is this one of those debates where the impasse is itself the finding and we should just accept the disjunction and move on? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey. my working theory is that the disagreement is really about whether truth is a property of sentences relative to a language or of utterances relative to speakers' commitments, but I half suspect im just restating the debate in fancier words. tell me if I am.
