I'm teaching a course on political corruption and next week we're doing the anthropological side — the gift/bribe distinction, patron-client relations, and the claim (which I half buy) that Western anti-corruption orthodoxy draws a line between a gift and a bribe that lots of honor-based cultures just dont recognize, so that what we call clientelism looks like obligation and mutual aid from the inside. I want to use The Godfather (the first film) as the week's text. The opening scene seems made for this: Bonasera asks the Don for justice, and Vito refuses payment — money would make it a mere transaction — and instead asks for respect and friendship, on the understanding that someday he might call in a favor. And the whole first hour is basically a ledger of favors: the baker Enzo, Johnny Fontane's problem with the studio boss and the horse head as the ugly face of a favor, Luca Brasi coming to pay respects. Read the film through this gift/clientelism lens: does it show that the line between corruption and a legitimate moral economy is drawn from outside, by people like us, or does the film end up drawing that line itself — think of the baptism sequence playing against the hits, the recurring insistence that it's never personal and strictly business, the Don's own sermonizing about family? Where does the film resist the anthropological reading? I don't want to sell my students a movie that agrees with me if it doesn't. Two more asks: be honest about what's actually settled vs genuinely contested among people who study corruption — e.g. don't present the idea that clientelism is just an alternative moral economy as if it were the consensus, I know there are serious objections to it — and flag any part of the reading that leans on a contested interpretation of a particular scene.
