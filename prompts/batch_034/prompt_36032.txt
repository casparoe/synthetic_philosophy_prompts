My friend and I got into a two-hour argument about Molinism over coffee last weekend and it left me wanting to stage the whole thing rather than just talk about it. I run an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar (12 students, 90 minutes) and next week we hit middle knowledge. The core thing I want them to feel, not just recite: God is supposed to pick which world to actualize using a stock of "would-do" facts -- if placed in circumstances C, agent A would freely do X -- and the whole system quietly depends on those facts being true before God decides anything. My friend's take was that this is basically a tabletop game where the DM also invents the results of every die roll in advance, and honestly I think he's onto something with the framing even though he's a compatibilist about it and I'm not sure I am. So I want an actual game, not a lecture.

Here's roughly what I'm imagining, but please rework it as needed: students play something like a world-selection committee ("the divine planner" role), and they get shown a ledger of would-do counterfactuals for a small cast of agents and circumstances -- enough for them to go hunting for a world where everyone gets saved or where the least evil gets actualized. The catch I want baked into the mechanics: someone else, maybe a hidden student or me, is the one who wrote the ledger, and the writers should not coordinate. The students should discover on their own the grounding objection -- who or what makes it true that Peter would betray in circumstance 7 rather than hold fast -- and also the more painful practical problem, that they can't just mix and match: they can choose which circumstances to actualize, but the would-do facts are fixed, so maybe every world on offer with free creatures has some betrayal in it. That's the moment I want: they realize they're stuck choosing among menus someone else authored, and then they find out the menus were authored by a classmate with no better access to the truth than they have. If you can also get the transworld depravity / why-doesn't-God-pick-a-world-where-everyone-freely-accepts issue to emerge from the mechanics rather than from me saying it, that would be great.

Please give me: concrete rules and materials (no real money, points or something else is fine), a rough minute-by-minute for 90 minutes, roles including the hidden ledger-writer thing, a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of Molina's picture (natural vs free vs middle knowledge, the grounding objection, the world-selection problem), and an honest note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it. Also be upfront about what the game can't reproduce -- my worry is that by making God's knowledge look like a lookup table the activity quietly rigs the argument against Molina, and I'd want to know where the activity is doing that so I can say so in the debrief. One more thing: wherever you tell me something is just how Molina meant it, or that some objection "refuted" the view, please separate what's actually settled among philosophers of religion from what's genuinely contested -- I know for a fact my friend and I were both presenting contested readings as the obvious ones and I don't want to hand my students the same move. And if you think the hidden-writer mechanic is too cute and there's a better way to get them to feel the grounding issue, say so and design that instead.
