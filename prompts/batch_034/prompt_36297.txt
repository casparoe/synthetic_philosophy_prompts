so last friday my friend (bayesian stats phd student, almost done) and I got into this hours-long argument that started because I brought up the Pasadena game and she just shrugged and said take the expectation, if it exists. To be clear, she knows the game's expectation doesn't exist in the usual sense -- her actual position was that cases like that are fringe artifacts, and you deal with them by patching things up case by case (extra axioms, special provisos) rather than rethinking the expected-value machinery itself. And I took the opposite line: I argued the Pasadena case isn't a curiosity, it's the visible tip of a general problem. Once you allow tiny-probability, huge-magnitude payoffs into your calculations, you get divergent or order-dependent sums all over the place where people actually use EV -- pascal's mugging style arguments, longtermist cost-benefit reasoning, anthropic stuff, infinite-ethics arguments. So far just a disagreement. But then I floated a fix that she immediately called "decision-theoretic voodoo," and that's where it got heated: I suggested a truncation test where a tail outcome only enters the expectation if its probability is, in some sense, verifiable at the time of choice -- below that threshold, the outcome drops out of the calculation entirely, it doesn't just get discounted. She said this is precisely the move of people who throw out inconvenient data, and that "verifiable at the time of choice" will end up meaning whatever lets the decider keep the conclusion they already wanted. I couldn't answer that cleanly and it bugged me.
