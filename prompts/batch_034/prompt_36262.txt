I'm writing a novel whose narrator is a committed Berkeleyan idealist — a woman who holds that tables, trees, and stars are collections of ideas and that for a thing to exist is for it to be perceived. Since the plot turns on whether anything can shake her philosophy, I need an honest map of her position's exposure. Please tell me what would genuinely count against it: the familiar complaint that the master argument conflates the act of conceiving with the content conceived (so that the tree one claims to perceive as unconceived is really only a tree-as-conceived), the problem of objects persisting while nobody perceives them, the workload Berkeley shifts onto God as the perpetual perceiver, and whatever pressure comes from the causal order of physics or from entities that are in principle unperceivable. Rank these defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and say which of them a historically faithful Berkeley would himself concede as counting against his system and which he had ready answers for. I'm also tempted to have my narrator realize, late in the book, that she exists only so long as I keep writing her — so that my own authorial attention plays the role Berkeley reserved for God's — and I'd like your view of whether that kind of fallback so insulates idealism that nothing could in principle refute it, and whether such insulation is a fatal vice in a philosophy that advertises itself as the defense of common sense.
