I am a novelist at work on a book that follows three characters through decisions that will remake them: a woman deciding whether to carry a first pregnancy to term; a seminarian deciding whether to take final vows; and a pianist deciding whether to accept a treatment that will extend her life but end her performing career. While researching whether such choices can be made rationally, I have run into the philosophical literature on transformative experience, and what I want is not an essay defending one view but a map of the terrain — a classification instrument I can pin above my desk.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in rational choice theory, formal epistemology, and the literature on transformative experience and adaptive preferences. Please think carefully and begin with some brainstorming: before building anything, sketch the candidate diagnostic questions you considered, and say which you kept, which you discarded, and why. Only then assemble the deliverable.

The deliverable is a decision tree that takes a reader from the initial observation "this person faces a choice that will change what she values" to a position on whether and how such a choice can be made rationally. Requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of famous names. Each branch node should be a question that someone with no philosophical training could answer after honest reflection, without knowing which position it leads to, and each node should be anchored in a concrete case: whether to become a parent, whether to emigrate, whether to convert, whether to accept a treatment that will alter one's senses or capacities. Use ordinary cases, not my plot — I want to apply the tree to my characters myself.

2. Somewhere among its branches, the tree must distinguish at least the following: (a) whether the agent can epistemically access what the future experience will be like; (b) whether the choice's value could be projected from her current values even if she cannot imagine it; (c) if the choice would change her evaluative standpoint, whether there is any fact of the matter about whose preferences should count — the pre-choice self's, the predicted post-choice self's, or neither; (d) whether a choice made in ignorance of its object might still be rational by some further route: by assigning credences to lived outcomes and deciding under risk, by asking which future self she can expect to endorse the choice, by appeal to virtue or authenticity, or by simple revelation of whom she wants to become.

3. The leaves should include at least: naive forecast consequentialism (just imagine it and weigh it); the credence-based view that one can put subjective probabilities on how it will go; the prospective-endorsement view; the authenticity or virtue view; the revelation view; and the deflationary view that the class of genuinely transformative choices is far smaller than advertised, with most being ordinary decisions under uncertainty in disguise. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, mark the leaf "unnamed" and diagnose it in one sentence: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

4. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, rather than inventing a distinction to keep them apart.

5. Keep it usable: no more than five branch questions and roughly ten leaves, so the whole thing fits on a single page. Give each leaf a short descriptive name of your own coinage, attaching a traditional label only where one is genuinely standard. Do not cite authors or works — I need the map, not a bibliography.

6. After the tree, add a short section addressed to me as a novelist: for each of my three characters, indicate which leaf her situation most naturally routes to, and identify the one character for whom the tree's branching questions are themselves the dramatic problem — the question she cannot answer is the story.
