I am a recently retired field zoologist who has, these past few years, taken up philosophy of mind with more enthusiasm than expertise. My reading group met last week to discuss the Discourse on Method, and the conversation about Descartes' treatment of animal minds nearly came to blows between two of our members. I will try to reproduce the exchange faithfully, numbered by move, and would then like a careful adjudication.

The disputants: a retired neurologist I will call M., and a philosophy lecturer I will call H. M. began by insisting that the beast-machine doctrine was a scientific achievement, not a scandal. Descartes swept away vital spirits, animal souls, and teleology, and proposed that animal behavior admits of mechanical explanation — which, M. argued, is roughly what behavioral neuroscience now believes, and which cleared the ground for it centuries early. Judging Descartes by the subsequent mistreatment of animals is anachronism.

H. replied that the prescience is incidental, because Descartes' argument was not cautious inference but overreach. The famous test in Part V — that animals never speak, not even to signal distress, while the dullest human can arrange words to make thoughts understood — fails against mute humans, stroke patients, and infants, none of whom Descartes wished to deprive of souls. An inference with that many known counterexamples is not science; it is a conclusion searching for an argument.

M. conceded the counterexamples but defended the form of the inference: in the state of seventeenth-century physiology, observable behavior was the only evidence of interior life, and where the only observable sign is absent, denying the unobservable is ordinary scientific hygiene — the same reasoning that led people to deny consciousness on the Moon.

H. answered that hygiene would counsel agnosticism, and Descartes offered none. Worse, the denial was metaphysically compulsory before any evidence arrived: since mind is thinking substance and matter is pure extension, whatever is organized mechanically can have no interior life, whatever it does. On Descartes' own principles, a dog could never behave its way into feeling anything; the empirical test was theater. And H. added that this is what made the doctrine dangerous — the oft-repeated stories of Cartesians at Port-Royal beating living dogs were only possible because a metaphysics had declared the dogs' cries to be the creaking of machinery.

M.'s last word was a rescue attempt: the doctrine's real heir is modern animal cognition science, which explains the navigation of bees and the tool use of crows in mechanical terms without doubting for a second that the animals experience the world. Descartes was wrong only in his packaging — in assuming mechanism excludes experience — and that assumption is our error to correct, not his, since he could not have anticipated it. H. replied that this makes Descartes right by turning him into someone else, which is no defense at all. Neither of them, notably, ever raised the passages where Descartes himself attributes sensations such as hunger, pain, and fear to animals.

Please structure your adjudication as follows, with these section titles and order: "Summary of My Ruling" (about 100 words); "Adjudication, Move by Move" (roughly 80 to 120 words per move, rating each of the six moves as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with reasons); "What Both Missed" (about 200 words, including whether the concession of sensation to animals undermines either side more than the other); and "Concluding Assessment" (about 100 words, on who is more right overall).
