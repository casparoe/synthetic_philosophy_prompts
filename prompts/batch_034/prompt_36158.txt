I'm an undergraduate currently taking a seminar on astrobiology and the philosophy of biology, and the unit on defining life has ended in a stalemate that I'd like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. Our seminar split into three camps that, as far as I can tell, have been re-fighting the same fight since at least the 1950s. The definers want a single crisp criterion — something in the neighborhood of the working definition used in the origin-of-life community, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of open-ended Darwinian evolution — and they treat "life" as a natural kind term with an essence waiting to be pinned down. The pluralists say that different research programs need different definitions: a virologist, an origin-of-life chemist, and someone designing a biosignature instrument for a Europa probe are not asking the same question, and it's a mistake to force one answer on all three. The skeptics hold that "life" is a family-resemblance leftover from folk biology, with no hidden unity to be discovered. Meanwhile we all agree on the facts: viruses straddle the metabolism criterion, giant viruses with their own translation machinery blur it further, synthetic cells built around minimal genomes have no natural history, and origin-of-life research describes a graded sequence in which self-replicating chemistry shades into cellular life without ever crossing a bright line. Every discovery reopens the debate and moves nobody.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about biology at all but about what a definition is for: the definers take themselves to be discovering a joint in nature, the pluralists take themselves to be solving a coordination problem, and the skeptics think there is no joint and no problem. But I've also noticed an asymmetry I can't fit into that story: in medicine, definitions of death do real binary work because decisions like organ retrieval can't be graded, and nobody thinks that debate is merely verbal. Astrobiology arguably faces the same pressure — a mission either reports life on Enceladus or it doesn't — which makes me wonder whether the pluralists' "different purposes" move just hides a substantive question about where the real-world joints are.

Please respond as a professional philosopher of biology. Think carefully, and start with some brief brainstorming of two or three candidate diagnoses — verbal dispute, hidden empirical question, differing standards for what a definition must accomplish, sides answering different questions under one heading — before committing to the one you find most plausible. Then tell me, under that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new empirical finding, a theoretical framework that is not yet available (perhaps the way stellar physics eventually disciplined the definition of a planet), or a conceptual distinction no one has drawn. And if your honest view is that no progress should be expected and why, I'd rather hear that than a synthetic reconciliation.
