For the past two years I have worked on the analytical staff of a public commission charged with regional adjustment: our unit must recommend how a uniform national benefit -- part cash, part in-kind services -- should be weighted across regions that differ in the price of the local market basket, in climate, in the prevalence of disability and chronic illness, and in the quality of public infrastructure. Every substantive dispute we have eventually reduces to the old question of what two lives should be compared *in* when we ask whether a distributive arrangement treats people as equals. But I do not need another entry in that debate; I need a specification the commission can work from. So write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate metric of interpersonal comparison for a redistributive scheme like ours must meet.

Include, at minimum: the cases the metric must get right, and say what "right" amounts to in each -- expensive tastes as against expensive needs; disabilities that raise the cost of functioning rather than merely lower welfare; preferences adaptively formed under deprivation and then, awkwardly, preferences shaped by genuine knowledge and conviction; chosen disadvantage and responsibility-sensitive cuts; costly locales where the same functioning costs different amounts; and the household-formation choices that make any comparison of individuals underdetermined. State the distinctions the metric must respect -- most importantly between the metric a society uses to measure shortfalls and the currency in which it actually compensates, which for us come apart since we adjust a benefit, not whole lives. Flag any results from economics or psychology that the metric cannot contradict (preference revelation problems, hedonic adaptation, the elasticity of need to price).

Then tell me two things I most want to see. First, which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable for any state that redistributes, and which are instead prejudices of one camp -- for instance, which requirements presuppose a luck-egalitarian standard of responsibility that a Rawlsian or a sufficientarian would reject. Second, whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself forces a choice; I suspect the expensive-taste case and the adaptive-preference case pull in opposite directions, and I would like that confirmed or refuted rather than waved at.

Close with a brief scoring, no more than a paragraph each, of preference-satisfaction welfare, resource-based views of the Dworkin type, and the capability approach against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is the least important part. No citations or reading lists -- name positions, not papers.
