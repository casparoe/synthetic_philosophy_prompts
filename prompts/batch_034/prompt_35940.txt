I'm writing a short piece on imprecise probabilities (credences as sets of probability functions rather than single numbers) and would love comments on my notes below. Aimed at philosophy-adjacent readers, not IP specialists. Mostly I want to know if the central argument actually works, but accuracy nitpicks are welcome too.

The main idea: defenders of imprecise probabilities usually motivate the view with Ellsberg-type urn experiments — people prefer betting on the known 50/50 urn, supposedly revealing credences that aren't any single number. I think that motivation is weaker than advertised, and there's a better one available from what I want to call "slow grind" scenarios. The thought: an imprecise agent is immune to Dutch books in the classic one-shot sense — no finite set of bets, each individually acceptable given her set of probabilities, guarantees a loss. But a bookie who merely knows her credal set can grind her over time: as long as she accepts any bet that at least one probability function in the set prices as fair, the bookie can pick which side to take, and the agent's behavior ends up tracking the worst-placed member of her set. So imprecision buys static safety at the price of dynamic exploitability by anyone who knows your state.

The second half of the essay argues this doesn't count against imprecision, because precise agents have a mirror-image vulnerability: a bookie who knows your exact credence can grind you too, whenever you accept bets that are barely favorable by your own lights. So exploitability-by-the-informed doesn't discriminate between the two views at all; only single-time-slice coherence does, and both kinds of agent can have that. If that's right, the Ellsberg motivation was never doing much work — the real case for imprecision has to come from elsewhere (I gesture at representation: single numbers encode a precision of self-knowledge we mostly don't have, and pretending otherwise makes you confidently wrong in a way being interval-valued avoids).

There's also a sketchier thread I'm attached to but unsure about: dilation, i.e. cases where conditioning on intuitively irrelevant evidence *widens* your credal interval. Critics find this pathological. I want to say it's arguably a feature if you think of the interval as partly a reservation price for future information — a dilated agent is one whose price for updating has gone up in a way that reflects her not knowing which member of her set she'll end up tracking. I have no idea how to make that precise (no pun intended). Cut it or keep it?

Two specific questions: (1) is the slow-grind argument against imprecise agents actually sound, or does some standard update/decision rule (e.g. only betting when all members of the set agree it's favorable) block it — and if it does block it, does that strengthen or weaken my symmetry claim? (2) Am I strawmanning the literature by saying Ellsberg is usually read as motivating imprecision specifically, as opposed to just as a problem for the sure-thing principle? I don't want to fight a strawman but I also don't want the piece to have a reading list in it.
