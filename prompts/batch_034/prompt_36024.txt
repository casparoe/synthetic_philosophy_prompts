I'm taking a survey course on classical Indian philosophy, and we've just finished a unit on the Nyāya school, which our instructor presented mainly through its theory of the pramāṇas. To get a broader picture I bought a short popular book on the history of logic, written by a science journalist, and its chapter on Indian logic compresses the whole Nyāya system into a few sentences. I'm going to set out its summary as I remember it, condensed but close to the original wording, since I'd like to know how much of it I can trust before the final exam. Here is the passage:

"Nyāya is the logic school of classical India, and it recognizes four pramāṇas, or instruments of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and reliable testimony. But on inspection only the first two do any real work. Comparison and testimony reduce to inference in disguise: we recognize the strange gavaya as cow-like by reasoning from its resemblance to the cow, and we accept the Veda because its authors were trustworthy, which is just an inference from their character. Moreover, Nyāya's theory of inference is at bottom a theory of enumerative induction: a valid inference requires the invariable concomitance, or vyāpti, of the middle and major terms, and such a concomitance can be established only by observing it in every case. Since nobody can survey all instances, Nyāya inherits Hume's problem of induction, and its famous five-membered proof schema turns out to be a piece of rhetoric, a sermon with premises. In the end the school amounts to a realism about perception bolted onto a psychology of habit."

Something about this felt both flattering and deflating in a way our instructor's lectures didn't, and I suspect the book is shaving off distinctions to make a punchy chapter. My own inclination is to accept the Hume point, since it matches what I know about the problem of induction generally, and to be suspicious of the claim that testimony is just inference, since our instructor spent a whole lecture defending the independence of śabda without my following why it matters. Please go through the summary roughly clause by clause and sort it into three piles: what a working Nyāya philosopher (say, a later Naiyāyika in the tradition of Udayana) would accept as stated, what they would accept only after a correction, and what is flatly wrong about the school's actual position. For the middle pile, tell me what the correct statement would be — in particular how Nyāya claims to ground vyāpti without an exhaustive survey, and what its actual account of upamāna and śabda as independent pramāṇas amounts to. Finally, evaluate the book's concluding verdict: given only the parts of the summary that survive correction, does the characterization of Nyāya as "a realism about perception bolted onto a psychology of habit" follow, or is it trading on the errors earlier in the paragraph? If the verdict does partially survive, I'd like to know which fragment of truth it's anchored to.
