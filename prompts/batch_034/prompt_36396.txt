I'm teaching a final-year module on Marx and the novel, and next week we're on Melville's Bartleby, the Scrivener — the one where the Wall Street lawyer's new copyist gradually stops doing anything at all, answering every request with "I would prefer not to", until he's living in the office, then haunting the stairs of the building, then dead of starvation in the Tombs. I want to build the seminar around whether Marx gives us the tools to read him, but I want us to be careful and not just wave at the word "alienation" and go home.

Specifically I'd like a reading that keeps two things seperate — my students run them together constantly. On one side there's alienation in the early manuscripts (from the product, from the activity, from species-being, from other workers), and on the other exploitation in Capital, which strictly needs the sale of labour power and the extraction of surplus value. So which is Bartleby? The copying is about as alienated as labour gets — someone else's documents, none of his own purposes in them, and there's the fact that when he stops working he literally spends the day staring at a brick wall. But is he exploited? Here's the wrinkle I want the reading to actually confront rather than dodge: for Marx a lot of clerical and service labour doesn't produce surplus value at all, and the narrator is a lawyer handling rich men's bonds and mortgages, not a factory owner. If the scriveners count as unproductive labourers in Marx's sense, the exploitation lens seems to misfire — so does the story show us alienation without exploitation, is that even a coherent combination within Marx, or does it point at something the two texts cant quite meet on?

And then the refusal itself. Is "I would prefer not to" a withdrawal of labour power, the one thing the worker has to sell — and if so, why doesnt it look anything like a strike or collective action, which is what you'd expect the framework to predict? Turkey and Nippers might matter here: the narrator runs them as a matched pair, one functional in the morning and useless after noon, the other the reverse, two battered bodies covering a single working day between them. Does that pair dramatise something about how capital uses people up that Bartleby's exit doesn't touch? I'd also like you to be honest about where the story resists a Marxian reading — the narrator's bursts of conscience, the way Bartleby refuses even the alternative jobs the narrator offers when he genuinely tries to help, the dead letter office rumour at the end, the final cry of "Ah Bartleby! Ah humanity!" — and then finish by saying whether the story gives the philosophy something argument alone can't, or whether the mismatch is the whole point. Point me to the specific scenes I should put on the handout, and tell me which one to open the seminar with.
