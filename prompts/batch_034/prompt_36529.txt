I've been collecting examples of how different legal systems treat the hours a person works, and the pattern looks philosophically loaded. Japan compensates karoshi — deaths from heart attack, stroke, or suicide following extreme overtime — as occupational injury, with certification tied to the overtime the deceased actually put in during the period before death, and the employer bears responsibility even where the worker chose those hours, sometimes enthusiastically and often without extra pay. The same country's 2019 work-style reform then capped overtime for ordinary workers on the theory that they cannot adequately protect themselves, while carving out an exemption for high-earning professionals, on the apparent theory that those workers' informed consent to open-ended hours is real and sufficient. France pushes in another direction: a statutory 35-hour week with mandatory overtime premiums that an individual employee cannot bargain away, plus, since 2017, a right to disconnect obliging larger firms to define when their workers may be unreachable. Yet these same systems let an adult sign a waiver and step into an amateur boxing ring, run ultramarathons, or take a commercial fishing job whose fatality rate no office would tolerate — treating that consent to bodily risk as fully valid. What theory of voluntariness or consent does this cluster of practices jointly presuppose? Is the thought that economic need vitiates consent to long hours — and if so, why does the high-income exemption, which seems to concede the dependence between bargaining power and valid consent, feel like it gets the ethics backwards? Or that fatigue gradually erodes the very capacity whose exercise consent requires, so long hours are self-undermining in a way boxing is not? Or that the harms of overwork fall on third parties — families, health systems — in a way leisure risks do not? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the parts are consistent or whether karoshi compensation and the hours caps actually presuppose different things, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely fit well with, and tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward revealed-preference picture on which workers' actual hour choices are taken at face value. If you can, also say whether the implicit view, once reconstructed, has anything determinate to say about platform work, where the worker's own free choice of hours is the entire sales pitch.
