And here's the answer my classmate turned in:

"The color phi phenomenon is a visual illusion discovered by psychologists in the 1970s. Two stationary spots of light are flashed in succession at different positions, first a red one and then a green one. Subjects don't see two separate flashes — they see a single dot moving from the first position to the second, and the strange part is that they report the dot changed color somewhere in the middle of its journey. So they experienced the color change partway through the motion, before the green spot had even been presented, which looks like an effect occuring before its cause. The phenomenon is essentially apparent motion, but it only arises when the two stimuli are different colors, which is what makes the color change possible.

There are two competing accounts of what's going on. The Orwellian view, named after Orwell's ministry that rewrote history after the fact, says the subject first consciously experienced just the two flashes with no motion, and then the memory of that experience was overwritten, so that by the time they give a report they sincerely remember having seen the moving, color-changing dot. Consciousness itself was accurate; only the memory record is corrupt. This is basically just the familiar fact that memory is unreliable, so it's the commonsense view. The Stalinesque view says instead that the brain edits before the fact: like a censor holding a story until it's been fixed up, the content is held back and only the final edited version is ever experienced at all. Either way the subject's later report is the same.

Dennett's argument is that no experiment could decide between the two, because any experiment has to go through the subject's report, and the report is produced after the editing in either case, so the two accounts predict identical data every single time. You might think you could catch the pre-edit experience by probing the subject very quickly, but probing is itself an event in the stream that interacts with what gets reported, so it can't settle anything either.

That said, Dennett does think the phi case by itself is a tie, but Libet's experiments on the readiness potential break the tie in favor of the Stalinesque account: since consciousness lags roughly half a second behind the stimulus, the brain has plenty of time to finish the editing before we're aware of anything. So the Stalinesque view is what actually happens and the Orwellian view is refuted empirically.

The conclusion about timing: since we can't say whether content enters consciousness early and gets revised afterward or gets delayed and enters already edited, there's no fact of the matter about the exact moment something becomes conscious. Dennett calls this the Multiple Drafts model — the brain keeps producing drafts of content, and on his view the final draft gets stamped official in the Cartesian Theater just before the report is issued, which is why the timing question never gets a determinate answer."
