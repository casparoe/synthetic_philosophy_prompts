I'm on the committee of a small conservation charity that owns ninety acres of ancient woodland. A project group wants to reintroduce red squirrels to our valley, and they have told us the reintroduction has little chance of success unless grey squirrels are suppressed in and around our wood — culled, in plain language, by paid controllers over several years. The greys have been in this country for a century and a half; they live and breed in our wood now. Our committee is split three ways: some think protecting the reds is the whole point of the charity and we should agree at once; some think paying to kill healthy wild animals that have done nothing wrong is not conservation but gardening with corpses; and I am in the middle, and I cannot settle it. What troubles me is that the reasons on offer seem to point in different directions. The greys would suffer when trapped and shot just as the reds would, and I cannot see what makes one squirrel's pain matter less than another's merely because of which species it belongs to or where its ancestors came from. On the other hand, it does seem to mean something that the reds are the ones who would be here if we had not interfered, and that a whole species can be lost for good while no amount of individual suffering adds up to that. I have no philosophical training; I have just been turning this over on my own, and I need it written down properly.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — agree outright, refuse outright, or agree with conditions, and if conditions, say what they would be — give the reasons, and treat the strongest point on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it. In particular, address the question I keep going in circles about: whether the case for the cull rests on treating the greys' interests as worth less than the reds', or on some other ground I have not managed to name. Keep the memo to a maximum of 450 words, and please write it in British English.
