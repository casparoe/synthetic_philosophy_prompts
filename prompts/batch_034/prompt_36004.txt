I work in nursing-home administration and lately I've been pulled into ethics case reviews about advance directives for residents with advanced dementia. There's this argument that comes up over and over and it never goes anywhere, and I'm starting to wonder if it's even an argument in the normal sense. On one side you have the people who basically follow Dworkin's line: a person can make decisions in advance that bind their later, demented self, because the earlier self is the one with critical interests -- the life-plans, values, projects -- and the demented self has only experiential interests, so if she finds eating pureed food distressing now, that discomfort doesn't override what the competent person wanted. On the other side there's a crowd who insist that the person in front of us is the patient, full stop: she's content in the moment, she doesn't experience her situation as a violation of anything, and forcing tube feeding on a calm, happy woman to honour a signature from fifteen years ago looks like letting a dead person boss around a living one. We've had the same exchange maybe eight times now, with exactly the same points, and nobody has ever switched sides. It's not for lack of information -- we all agree on the facts of the case.

My own hunch, and I don't know if it's any good, is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about what to do so much as answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking something like "whose life is it / what did the person decide" and the other is asking "what does the patient, now, have reason to want" -- and maybe 'the patient' doesn't pick out one thing at all once you have dementia in the picture. But I can also see it being a genuine substantive disagreement about personal identity (is the demented woman the same person who signed the directive, or her survivor in some sense?), or even a clash of bedrock intuitions about autonomy versus welfare that neither side can argue for without circularity.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on the tube feeding. What kind of disagreement is this actually -- verbal, metaphysical, normative at the foundation, different questions smuggled under one label? Explain what each side is really committed to when you push on it, tell me whether something could in principle settle it (a conceptual distinction about identity over time? an empirical finding about how dementia patients experience their earlier values? a legal/policy compromise that's not philosophically satisfying?), or whether it's just permanently hard. And if you think the question is partly ill-posed -- e.g. because 'the patient' stops having a single referent -- say so and say what question we should be asking instead. Please answer in British English.
