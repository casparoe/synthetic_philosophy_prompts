I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of autonomous systems, and my committee has me cornered with a parity argument against the very phenomenon my dissertation is about. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

Here is the argument, in the form my committee's external member put it:

(1) A person is morally responsible for a harm only if they controlled whether it occurred: they could have acted so as to prevent it, and they were in a position to know that what they were doing mattered to that risk.
(2) When a machine-learning system causes a harm, no engineer foresees or intends that specific output; the system's behavior emerges from training rather than from any individual's decision.
(3) Someone who neither foresees nor intends a specific harm does not control it, since control requires knowing the options one is choosing between.
(4) The end-user controls whether the system runs, but not what it does; the system itself is not a responsible agent.
(5) So AI-caused harms fall into responsibility gaps: no human is responsible for them.
(6) But when a trucking firm hires drivers, no manager foresees or intends any particular collision; driving behavior emerges from the driver's training and temperament, not from any manager's decision.
(7) By parity with (2)-(3), the firm does not control its drivers' collisions.
(8) Yet we rightly hold the firm responsible for its drivers' negligence, and nobody thinks that situation is a responsibility gap.
(9) So either responsibility gaps are ubiquitous - which is absurd - or something in (1)-(5) is wrong.

I want to keep (5), since the gap is the phenomenon I set out to characterize, but I confess I can't locate the flaw, and the two cases feel genuinely symmetric to me: in both, the deployer manages a fallible system whose token failures nobody foresees. Presumably I have to reject some step, or find a disanalogy between the cases strong enough to break (6)-(7) while leaving (2)-(3) intact.
