I cover science for a magazine and I keep getting in trouble with the word "code" in my genetics pieces -- readers email me either insisting DNA is literally a code or huffing that it's only a metaphor, and honestly I don't know what I think. So I've started writing down my gut verdicts on things, and now I want to know if theres a principle behind them. Morse code: code, obviously. The codon-to-amino-acid table, the actual "genetic code": also a code, I'd have said that without blinking. But DNA as a whole being a "code" or a "blueprint" -- here I balk, and I can't say why, since the codon table is part of it. A recipe in a cookbook: same family somehow, but I wouldn't call it a code. A footprint in snow that tells a tracker the animal's weight and gait: information, sure, but not a code. A compiler turning Java into bytecode: a code in two senses at once, which is annoying. Basalt cooling into hexagonal columns: pattern, not code. Sheet music: I want to call it a notation but not a code, even though a piano roll, which does almost the same job with holes in paper, feels more code-like to me. My tentative principle is that a code requires a conventional mapping deliberately set up by someone -- a coder intending a decoder -- but that seems to rule out DNA, which I do think of as a code, and it maybe wrongly lets the recipe in, since a recipe is nothing but a deliberate conventional mapping from words to actions. So: is there one principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, stated precisely enough that each clause is doing work for some case? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why those? And tell me what the surviving principle says about two cases I haven't decided: the honeybee waggle dance, and the "histone code" that keeps showing up in the press releases I cover.
