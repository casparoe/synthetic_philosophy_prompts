I write a blog aimed at people with some philosophy background but no formal training, and I want to build a diagnostic quiz titled, roughly, "What do you actually think punishment is for?" Please write it as follows.

Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the standard positions in the philosophy of punishment: retributivism (in both its negative form, as a constraint on punishing the innocent, and its positive form, as a claim that the guilty deserve suffering), forward-looking consequentialist theories split by their good — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation — and, if you can make them come apart in the quiz, expressivist or communicative theories, restorative justice, and outright abolitionism. The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by which label they find congenial, so no question may ask directly whether punishment should give people what they deserve, whether it should deter, and so on; the position labels must appear only in the scoring key.

Include at least these five cases, since much of the literature turns on them: the sheriff who can prevent a riot only by framing an innocent man (which separates side-constraint retributivism from straight deterrence reasoning); the condemned murderer who, unknown to anyone, has a terminal illness and will die before any member of the public learns of his sentence (which separates desert from any forward-looking benefit of punishment); the brilliant, remorseless embezzler who is certain never to be caught again and can never be deterred, but whose last theft is discovered only after the statute of limitations has run (which tests whether desert is independent of all consequences or only of aggregate welfare); the unsuccessful attempt that fails only by luck, versus the completed crime (which probes whether harm done or harm willed is the object of the desert claim); and the civil disobedient who accepts punishment while announcing, as part of the protest, that what she did was fully justified (which separates communicative and restorative views from retributivism). Fill out the remaining questions with cases of your own choosing, but keep them at the same level of specificity.

For the scoring key: group answer patterns into named positions, note honestly which pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate — I suspect expressivism and positive retributivism will collapse together, and restorative justice may be hard to distinguish from rehabilitative consequentialism — and include at least one cell of the logical space that is coherent but unoccupied, with a sentence on why nobody holds it. Finally, tell me which question you would cut first if I need to shorten the quiz, and what is lost by cutting it.
