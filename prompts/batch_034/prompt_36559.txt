I'm presenting at a small Aquinas colloquium in a couple weeks on his views on tyrannicide. The audience is maybe fifteen moral theologians, mostly scholastically trained Dominicans and seminary people who know the texts cold, plus a couple of political theorists who mostly care about the later recpetion (Bellarmine, Suarez, the monarchomachs). My thesis: Aquinas's position on killing a tyrant is neither the quietism people attribute to him nor the right of resistance that later Catholic theorists borrowed him for — it turns entirely on whether the one who kills acts with public authority rather than as a private person, and since Aquinas never really settles where that authority resides when the ruler is a usurper, his view is underdetermined at exactly the point everyone fights about. Thats why all those later camps could each claim him as their own.

The texts I lean on are the De Regno discussion of tyrants, where he says it isn't praiseworthy to kill a tyrant and that doing so usually exposes the community to danger since someone worse often takes over, and the Summa's treatment of killing, where the private-versus-public-person distinction does the real work. I paraphrase on the slides rather than quote, so assume the audience can supply the exact loci from memory.
