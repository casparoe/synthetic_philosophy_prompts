I am a novelist, and I have noticed that my working intuitions about when a text counts as "the same novel" are really the Ship of Theseus in a profession's clothing. I would like to know whether my verdicts on the cases add up to a principle, or whether I am merely rationalizing sentiment. Here are the verdicts. My first novel went through eleven drafts in which every sentence was eventually discarded and replaced, and I never once doubted that I was writing the same novel throughout. I count the French translation of that book as the same novel, although not one word of it is a word I wrote. When my publisher reissued my second book under a new title with the protagonist renamed, I still called it the same novel. Yet when a younger writer produced a book that follows my plot scene for scene in entirely new prose of her own, I was indignant at the suggestion that she had written the same novel — plagiarized, perhaps, but a different book. And when my editor once proposed reordering my fourth novel so that it ran backwards, I felt the restructured version would be a different novel even if nearly every sentence survived untouched. The case I keep going back and forth on is my unfinished last novel, which my estate may one day have another author complete, that author ending up responsible for the final two-thirds. Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts — and if there is, how does it manage to treat the eleven drafts and the backwards restructuring so differently when both keep some thread and replace the rest? Please state the principle precisely, tell me what it predicts for the completed-posthumously case, and be honest about which of my verdicts cannot be saved.
