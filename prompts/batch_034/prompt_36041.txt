I am a professor of philosophy, and this semester I am teaching a graduate seminar on social epistemology that includes, for the first time, a unit on the epistemology of markets — Hayek's picture of prices as aggregators of dispersed knowledge, the literature on prediction markets, and the like. I am not an economist, and I want to be careful about a remark made at a conference last month that has troubled me since. During the discussion, an economist from the audience observed that, by the no-trade theorem, a rational agent will never knowingly trade with another rational agent on the basis of private information; hence, she continued, every trade that actually occurs on a financial market involves at least one party who is irrational or mistaken. It seemed to follow that the price mechanism is powered by error — that markets aggregate not wisdom but noise — and that the entire idea of the market as an epistemic engine is sustained by irrationality rather than intelligence. She delivered this almost as a truism, and the pushback it received was compressed and technical in a way I could not follow.

I would like your help with two worries. First, if the no-trade theorem really establishes that rational agents never trade, then the enormous trading volume we observe would seem to measure how far actual markets fall short of rationality, and I do not see how any Hayek-style epistemic optimism could survive that result. Second, if prediction markets forecast well, does their accuracy derive from the very irrationality the theorem imputes to their traders — in which case the epistemology of markets would be, awkwardly, an epistemology of noise? Something feels off in how I have framed this; the conclusion looks too strong to be what the theorem says. But I cannot locate the seam myself.

Please answer in American English, structured as follows, in this order. First, a section titled Where the premise goes wrong, of roughly 150–250 words, diagnosing the flaw in the inference as I have stated it — including which of my formulations are literally false. Second, a section titled What the no-trade theorem actually says, of roughly 200–300 words, setting out the theorem's assumptions and content in a form accessible to a philosopher with no economics training. Third, a section titled Consequences for the epistemology of markets, of roughly 200–300 words, on what does and does not follow for Hayekian aggregation and for prediction markets. Finally, a section titled Bottom line, under 100 words, giving your verdict on the conference remark that started this.
