I run a small blog that's mostly read by people who think way too much about anthropic reasoning and existence risk. For the past year two of my most prolific commenters have been at war over SSA vs SIA — the self-sampling assumption vs the self-indication assumption, if you know the terms, and if you don't here's the whole background so nobody has to search anything. The SSA person thinks you should reason as if you're a random sample from the observers in your reference class, so a theory's a priori probability isn't boosted just for positing more people. The SIA person thinks finding yourself to exist is itself evidence that favors theories with more observers — you're more likely to find yourself somewhere there are more findable-yous. One is basically a fan of Bostrom's SSA, the other thinks SSA hands you the Doomsday Argument for free and refuses to touch it.

They've gone through the entire spiral: the Doomsday Argument, Sleeping Beauty (thirders vs halfers, which maps onto this fight in a way they argue about too), the presumptuous philosopher (two cosmological theories that both fit the data equally well, but one posits trillions of times more observers — SIA says that theory is now overwhelmingly more likely, SSA says it gets no boost, and most people's intuition is that the SIA move is nuts, but SIA folks bite the bullet), reference classes (SSA has to say *which* observers you're a sample of and every choice looks arbitrary), and big/infinite worlds (SIA seems to go insane or just break when there are so many observers that everything gets dominated by universes full of Boltzmann-brain-ish weirdos). At this point they're posting past each other and everyone else has stopped opening the threads.

So I've bullied them into an adversarial collaboration and they actually agreed, probably because each is convinced they'll obviously win. They asked me to draft the joint statement since I'm "the only neutral-ish one". I am not neutral — I lean SSA — which is exactly why I need you to draft it instead, so it doesnt come out tilted.

The document should have:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things both could genuinely put their name to about what anthropic evidence is and isn't, what's wrong with the naive view that ignores observer-selection effects entirely, and whatever common ground actually exists (e.g. maybe both agree the presumptuous philosopher case is uncomfortable for *someone*, even while disagreeing about who).

2. A disagreements table. Each row is one disagreement, stated twice: once in wording the SSA person would sign, once in wording the SIA person would sign. No weaseling — if a formulation is vague enough that both sides nod along to it, it belongs in section 1, not here.

3. The cruxes. My guesses: whether "I exist" is evidence that favors many-observer theories, whether Doomsday-style conclusions are a bug or a feature of anthropic reasoning, and whether SIA is doing legitimate confirmation or just double-counting (counting the observer once as evidence and once as the one doing the confirming). But find the real cruxes, and for each, state the case or argument both sides would agree bears on it — some concrete scenario, a Sleeping Beauty variant, a simulation-hypothesis case, whatever works — with each side's prediction written down in advance, before the case is "run".

4. If any part of their fight is really verbal — like they're implicitly defining "this evidence" differently, or one means probability-on-waking-up and the other probability-across-the-whole-experiment — flag which part and why.

Two extra requests. First, since I lean SSA: actively watch for places where you've made the SIA side sound dumber than it is, or quietly let SSA off the hook. In particular, do not let the agreed-claims section smuggle in that reference classes are unproblematic, because they're not and both of them know it. Second, I have to stand in front of these two pedants and defend every sentence, so end with a short honest note: how confident you are that each side would actually sign the formulations attributed to them, and the single formulation or crux you're least sure about — the one most likely slanted in a way you didn't notice.

Format: numbered agreed claims, then the table, then cruxes with predictions, then the verbal-dispute bit, then the confidence note at the end.
