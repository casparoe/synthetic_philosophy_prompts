I'm finishing a philosophy of mind unit on the frame problem, which began in the technical literature on automated planning and ended up as a general problem about relevance, and I'd like a poem to close the final session.

Please write a Shakespearean sonnet (abab cdcd efef gg, iambic pentameter with tolerable substitutions) whose structure enacts the history of the problem. The octave should be in the calm voice of a planning system doing its bookkeeping: the robot deduces what follows from its action, writes down what changes, and holds the rest of the world still — the tone of early AI, where the difficulty looks like a mere annoyance about writing too many axioms. The volta at line nine should be the moment the problem turns philosophical. One way in: the robot that pulls a wagon out of a room to retrieve the spare battery on it, without registering that the bomb it was meant to avoid is riding on the same wagon — or, more precisely, the realization that no list of unaffected facts could ever have flagged the bomb, because relevance does not announce itself in advance. The sestet should widen from there: if any fact whatever might bear on the outcome, then holding the world fixed cannot be a matter of enumeration at all, and the poem should feel the flood of unchecked consequences — every ordinary thing a potential player in the plan. The closing couplet should state what has to go, namely the hope that what stays put could ever be written down in a closed list, without pretending the problem has been solved; leave it open, since that is honestly where our discussion ended up.

A few constraints. No proper names — no McCarthy, no Dennett, no Fodor — anywhere in the poem: the content has to do the recognition work, not the name-dropping. The two versions of the problem should each be recognizable to someone who has read the literature, and distinguishable from each other: the octave belongs to the axiomatic problem about representing what an action leaves unchanged, and the sestet to the problem of how any system, biological or artificial, knows what matters without checking everything. And someone who has never heard of the frame problem should still find it a good poem about a mind trying to work out what it is entitled to ignore.
