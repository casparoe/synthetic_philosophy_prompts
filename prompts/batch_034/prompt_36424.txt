Two friends of mine have been at each other's throats for like two years about the leveling-down objection and I've finally convinced them to do a proper adversarial collaboration, with me as scribe. I need you to draft the joint statement they'd both actually sign.

The two camps: one is a telic egalitarian — she thinks equality is intrinsically good, full stop, even between strangers, even in a world where nothing else happens; the other is a prioritarian — he thinks benefits matter more the worse off the recipient is, and that once you have that, equality has no independent value at all. Their entire fight has orbited the thought experiment where you take a two-group world, everyone in one group is better off than everyone in the other, and then you level down: you make the better-off group worse off so the two groups are now equal, nobody gains anything. Is that world better in ANY respect?

Please write the document with these pieces:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. Make these substantive, not vacuous — e.g. they can both agree on what the objection actually targets and what it doesn't (it's not about envy, and it's not a complaint that anyone was made worse off in a way that matters for that person's sake... careful, that second one is where they split, so find the real common ground). They should also be able to agree on things like: the objection does NOT straightforwardly force you to utilitarianism, and there are bad things about inequality that aren't captured by either of their views.

2. A disagreements section — table would be great, one row per disagreement — where each row states the disagreement in wording BOTH would accept as a fair statement of their own view. This is the hard part and I'll know if you phoned it in. If one row is just "is equality intrinsically valuable?" restated three ways, that's phoned in. Dig into the specific moves: does the egalitarian really have to say the leveled-down world is in one respect better, or are there ways to take that back that aren't cheating; can the prioritarian explain why we should care about RELATIVE position at all without smuggling in equality; does prioritarianism quietly collapse into either utilitarianism (if the weight is small) or egalitarianism (if it's large), and would either collapse bother him.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them — and for each, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument they've both agreed bears on it, with each person's prediction written down in advance (what would she say about it, what would he say, so that if the prediction is wrong they'd have to budge). You'll probably need to invent some cases since the classic ones are exhausted — maybe something like an intermediary who is only slightly worse off and a transfer that overshoots, or a case where making the better-off worse off would incidentally help a third party, so they have to untangle the leveling-down component from the helpful component. At least one crux should be whether their dispute is partly verbal — e.g., whether "better in one respect" is a genuine fragment of value or just a dressed-up report that some non-value fact obtains — and if you think part of it IS verbal, say which part and why.

Keep it to a hard max of 1100 words — they're both busy and the last thing we need is another 40-page thing nobody reads. Casual-precise register, no citations or reading lists needed, just the content.

One more thing: don't let the egalitarian come off as the reasonable one just because her position is weirder. Write it so that if they read it, each of them thinks "yes, that's exactly my view and exactly where he/she goes wrong."
