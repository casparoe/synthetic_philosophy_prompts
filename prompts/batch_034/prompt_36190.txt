I have no academic background, but I have spent several years thinking on my own about one question: whether it is always irrational to honor sunk costs. I keep a mental file of cases — a friend who finished a PhD she had come to hate, a neighbor who kept a failing restaurant open because of what he had already put into it, my own inability to abandon a book I am not enjoying, and, most disturbing to me, the arguments by which wars are prolonged on the ground that soldiers have already died for them. The popular advice, as it reaches people like me, is simple: the past is gone, the only rational question is what to do with the time that remains, and everything else is the sunk cost fallacy. I half believe it, and my doubts are of three kinds.

Sometimes the past seems to carry information about the future: someone who has tolerated a bad marriage for twenty years may want something from it that they have never admitted to themselves, so an outsider who takes their persistence seriously is perhaps not honoring a sunk cost but reading it — though I cannot always tell reading from honoring. Sometimes the audience matters: a person known to abandon things the moment they stop paying finds that others stop lending, partnering, and trusting, which suggests that a rule of finishing what one starts can be worth keeping as a display — or perhaps such a cost was never fully sunk, since part of its value survives in one's reputation. And sometimes the motive looks less like reasoning than like grief looking for a form, as with the half-built house that gets finished because one's late father poured the foundation. I do not know whether that last kind deserves a position of its own or should be diagnosed as a temptation rather than sorted.

What I would like from you is a branching quiz, a decision tree in effect, that takes a person from concrete cases to a named position on when, if ever, honoring sunk costs is rational. Six to eight questions, each a concrete case, answerable by someone who has never heard the labels: include at least the hated degree, the failing business, the tedious book, and the war justified by its dead, plus any you think cut more finely. Build it from the logical space first and only then locate the positions in it, and include at least one leaf for the strict view that all sunk costs are irrelevant, one for the reputation or display view, one for the view that past persistence is evidence about one's own desires, and one for the memorial view, even if you end up calling the last one a mistake. Where a branch leads to a combination nobody actually holds, say so, and say whether that is because the view is incoherent or merely unexplored; flag any two positions your questions cannot cleanly separate.

Two requests about how you write it. First, be committed: whenever a case could plausibly sit on either side of a branch, do not present me with both and move on — decide where it goes and defend the placement in a sentence, since I would rather disagree with a definite choice than be handed a menu; and at the end, state plainly which leaf you would place yourself at, and why. Second, write it as continuous prose, as if you were explaining the tree to me in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Give each question and each option as running text, and let the reader follow a path by reading rather than by tracing lines.
