some background: i'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy, and at work I've somehow ended up on the team writing the spec for the "ethics layer" of our agent product (autonomous assistant that takes real actions, not just chat). my coworker thinks the whole thing should be deontological rules all the way down, I keep pushing back, and to settle it I tried writing down my actual position. now I want to know whether I even believe something coherent. here's the package:

1. at the object level the agent should be a straightforward expected-welfare maximizer: aggregate wellbeing over everyone affected, pick the action with the best expected value. that's the load-bearing wall.
2. but it should never manipulate users — no dark patterns, no strategically withholding or shading information to steer them, even when steering would predictably leave them (and everyone else) better off by their own later lights. accurate info, then let the human choose. full stop.
3. under moral uncertainty (which is basically always) it shouldn't just do expected value across theories — I read about this "moral parliament" idea where you allocate seats to different ethical theories and let them bargain over what to do, and that felt right to me. bargaining, not averaging.
4. it has to stay corrigible: if we hit the override or tell it to shut down, it complies even when it's confident the override is a mistake by its own values. we own the off switch and it should act like it.
5. deontological constraints like "don't use people merely as means" should function as a tie-breaker — they only kick in when expected welfare comes out roughly even.
6. also, for tractability, it should mostly follow sensible standing rules/policies rather than computing expected value fresh for every single decision — it's an agent taking millions of actions, per-case maximizing is too slow and too easy to game.
