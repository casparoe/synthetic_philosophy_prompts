My labmate — a virologist by training and very opinionated — has been issuing verdicts all year on what counts as alive, and I've started keeping a ledger. Her rulings so far: bacteria and archaea, alive; humans, mules, and sterile worker bees, alive; viruses of every kind she's been asked about, not alive; Bacillus endospores sitting dry and metabolically inert for decades, alive; desiccated tardigrades, alive; prions, not alive; self-catalyzing clay surfaces of the Cairns-Smith sort, not alive; candle flames and hurricanes, not alive; mitochondria inside a cell, alive as parts of an organism but not organisms themselves — she insists on separating the question of being alive from the question of being an organism, and I think she's right to.

Next month she's sitting in on a talk I have to introduce, so I want to be able to anticipate her objections. Three new cases will come up:

1. Digital organisms — self-replicating programs in an Avida-style environment that mutate, compete for CPU time, and have evolved elaborate foraging behavior, with nothing chemical anywhere in the system.
2. A bottom-up protocell: a lipid vesicle enclosing a replicating RNA genome and a rudimentary light-driven metabolism that repairs the membrane, maintains its own gradients, and grows and divides — but whose replication is so error-prone that there is no stable heredity across generations, hence no cumulative evolution and no lineage at all.
3. A mimivirus-type giant virus carrying metabolic and translation-related genes, which it expresses only after commandeering a host's ribosomes.

Predict her verdict on each, then do the part I actually care about. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well — something autopoiesis-shaped, something like the NASA working definition with its self-sustaining and Darwinian clauses, perhaps something substrate-neutral about evolution or something cladistic about descent, and note honestly which of these her virus verdict already strains. Say which of the three cases does the most to separate the surviving rules, what each rule predicts there, and, where the rules still agree, what further case would force them apart — she would enjoy a hard one, and the gradient from captured endogenous retroviruses to placenta-making genes occurred to me. Give me your best guess at her actual verdicts with your reasons for preferring one rule over its rivals, calibrated to how underdetermined her ledger really leaves them.

And one request in her spirit: if you think the ledger is internally incoherent, or that a single rule can't be extracted from it because "alive" is doing several jobs at once across her rulings — alive versus being an organism versus being a life-form versus matter of degree — say so plainly instead of manufacturing a principle to keep the prediction game going. She'd much rather be told her concept is overloaded than be fitted to a rule she'd reject on sight.
