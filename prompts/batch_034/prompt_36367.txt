I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to give the closing lecture of a university's public series on neuroscience and the self: a general audience of about two hundred, forty-five minutes, slides but no equations. My topic is split-brain cases and what they are supposed to show about the unity of consciousness. Plan the lecture for me rather than writing it. I want the order the pieces must come in and what depends on what — in particular, whether the audience needs a working picture of what the surgery does and does not sever before they see any experimental result, or whether the results land harder cold. I want one case or experiment to carry the whole arc, and I'm wary of the standard word-flashed-to-one-visual-field demo, since anyone who has heard of split-brain patients half-remembers it; suggest what the carrying example must have and what it must lack. I want the misconceptions I should expect, roughly in the order the audience will surface them — left-brain/right-brain personality talk, the conclusion that the patients walk around hosting two people, the leap from divided performance in the lab to a divided life outside it — and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I also want the contrast between the patients' startling ordinariness in everyday life and the divisions that appear on demand in the laboratory to do real explanatory work rather than serving as a reassuring aside. I want to know what I should openly decline to settle, including whether the right hemisphere is a second subject or a sophisticated subsystem, and how to keep that refusal honest without collapsing into "it's complicated." And I want the one question I could put to the audience at the end that would tell me whether they've grasped the threat these cases pose to unity of consciousness rather than merely enjoyed the spectacle.
