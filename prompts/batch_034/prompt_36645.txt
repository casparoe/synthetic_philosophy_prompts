I run an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence — fourteen students, one eighty-minute session — and we're about to reach the topic of reward misspecification. Design an in-class game in which the students themselves turn out to be the misaligned agents. Teams should be optimizing a score that I award according to a published rubric, where the rubric is only an imperfect proxy for a hidden true objective that I keep to myself until the debrief. The game's structure should lead them, without any prompting from me, to exploit the gap — first by gaming the proxy, and then by discovering some form of direct access to the scoring mechanism itself, so that over the course of the session the class traverses the whole territory from pursuing the objective badly through wireheading to outright reward tampering. The points need to matter, so make them convertible into something real, but no actual money. The debrief should distinguish these failure modes from one another, map each onto specific moments in the game, and press the question of whether what the students did counts as cheating — given that they were doing exactly and only what the rules asked of them. Assume the students know Goodhart's law informally but have never heard the terms wireheading or reward tampering, and that there is no lecture up front. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which parts of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce — in particular, that real learning agents feel nothing when reward arrives, and that your students will bring honesty norms no optimizer has, so some of them will refuse to tamper no matter what the payoff scheme says.
