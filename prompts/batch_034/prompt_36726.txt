Got into a huge argument with my roommate last night over dinner because I mentioned I'd put some money into individual stocks -- a few pharma companies I'd been reading about, nothing crazy. He basically laughed at me and ended every single point he made with "you can't beat the market," like it was a spell he could cast that would make me concede. When I pushed him on what that sentence even means, things got interesting, and also messy.

He started with the version that's just an empirical statistic: most active fund managers underperform their benchmark after fees. Fine, I said, but I'm not a fund manager, and "most people don't" is not "no one can." Then he escalated to something stronger -- that if there were real alpha sitting around, traders would grab it and it would already be baked into the price, so by the time I read about a drug trial in the news, the price has already moved. I asked him who exactly is doing this price-baking if literally nobody can beat the market -- doesn't somebody have to be trading on information for prices to reflect it? He said the pros do it, and it's precisely because they do it that I can't. I said that sounds like it's only true because of people for whom it's false, which is fine, but then the slogan should be "amateurs can't beat the market," which is a much weaker thing to keep repeating at me.

Then it went somewhere weirder. He pulled out something he half-remembered from an econ elective, that under some conditions it's not just hard to profit on information but like, provably impossible to trade at all -- that if we both agree on the background picture and you know I know that, there's literally no trade that makes sense for both of us, because the mere fact that someone wants to buy your shares is a signal about the shares that kills the trade. He admitted he didn't remember how it worked, just that it had a name, and he was clearly using it as "math says you're wrong." That felt like cheating -- surely that thing, whatever it is, depends on assumptions that plainly fail for retail investors buying stock in an economy where billions of shares change hands daily?

So, request: take the sentence "you can't beat the market" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be standing for -- the trivial statistical reading, the efficient-markets readings (and note those come in flavors: are we saying prices reflect public info, all info, past prices only?), whatever that no-trade argument actually says, and anything else hiding in there, including ones he wasn't consciously using. For each reading tell me: is it true, and does it actually support his use of it against my little stock picking? I want a menu I can bring to him, ordered roughly from truism to radical, with a verdict per item. And please tell me straight whether the semi-mystical impossibility thing he half-remembered can do any real work in an argument with me, or whether invoking it there is just vibes -- and if it IS just vibes, what the defensible version of his point would have been. He's a good guy and I don't want to just dunk on him, but I also don't want to concede to a slogan.
