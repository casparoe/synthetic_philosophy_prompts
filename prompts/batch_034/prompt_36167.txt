I'm putting together next week's session for my grad seminar on intentionality, and I want the students to get in the weeds with the Dennett/Searle exchange rather than just recite the positions. The centerpiece is Dennett's two-bitser, which you know: a vending machine whose coin mechanism was built in the US to discriminate genuine quarters from slugs, then ends up in Panama, where the quarter-balboa is minted to the same size, weight, and metal as the US quarter. Once the machine is there — and once nobody remembers what it was originally designed for — Dennett claims it's simply indeterminate whether the mechanism is still a quarter-detector or has become a balboa-detector. Nothing about its intrinsic physical nature settles the question; only its selective history does, and that history underdetermines the answer. Then comes the big move: natural selection is our designer in just the same way, so what Searle calls original intentionality in us is derived in exactly the sense Searle means when he calls the machine's discrimination derived. The original/derived distinction doesn't mark a line between minds and machines; there's no bottom layer of intrinsic meaning-makers, just histories all the way down.

One of my students pushed back hard on this in office hours. Her claim was that the analogy is doing sneaky work: the two-bitser only detects quarters *for us*, relative to our purposes and our interpretations, and Searle's whole point is that our case is different in kind — I can ask whether the mechanism detects quarters without asking about anyone's beliefs, but I can't ask whether I believe it's raining in a way that waits on someone else's purposes. And, she says, Dennett's move just applies the word derived at a different level (derivation from natural selection rather than from a conscious designer), which is either a pun or an equivocation on what Searle meant by the term. I told her we'd settle it by audit rather than by dueling intuitions.

So that's what I want from you. Lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the designer, the discriminated object, the design specification, the forgetting of the design intent, and — crucially — what plays the role of the interpreter whose purposes allegedly fix the machine's meaning but allegedly aren't needed for ours. Then spell out where the mapping actually breaks: which disanalogies are real (e.g., that the machine's success conditions are externally stipulated while ours seem self-ascribed, or that selection has no purposes at all while the engineers did), which are just Searle's intuitions restated as facts, and which don't matter either way for the conclusion at stake. Finally, tell me whether Dennett's conclusion — that the original/derived distinction either collapses or survives only as a difference that does no philosophical work — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the conclusion was being carried by the analogy the whole time. I'd rather not get the standard textbook recap of both positions and a shrug; I want the stress test, and I want it honest about the points where Dennett is leaning on the picture as much as Searle is. If it helps, assume we've all read the Chinese room argument and nobody needs it re-explained.
