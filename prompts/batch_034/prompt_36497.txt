My mother died last spring, and I inherited a project she had left half-finished: a family history built around three generations of letters, a set of farm ledgers, and the paperwork from a 1948 lawsuit that split the family into two branches that have barely spoken since. To carry it forward I brought in two people: a retired academic historian I'll call Dr. Rasmussen, whom I engaged to organize and verify the archive, and my cousin Elena, a literary editor who has agreed to help shape the material into something the family will actually read. At our first working meeting they managed to disagree about nearly everything, and since their disagreement is really about what my project should even be, I would be grateful for a considered adjudication.

Rasmussen opened by saying that the first obligation is to the record: before any shaping, the job is to establish what actually happened, in Ranke's sense — the past as it really was, reconstructed from the documents rather than judged from the present. Anything else, he said, is propaganda with footnotes. Elena replied that this is naive about archives: the letters survived because somebody chose to keep them, and what people keep is the dramatic correspondence — the quarrels, the apology, never the dull decade of reconciliation that followed. The surviving record is not a window on the past but an artifact of past selection, so the neutral chronicle he wants is not on offer.

Rasmussen conceded the point about preservation and then denied it mattered: inescapable selection is not arbitrary selection, and the discipline of archival method is precisely what allows some reconstructions to be refuted. The 1948 rift either was about the inheritance or it was not; the documents can settle that, and if Elena emplots the whole saga as a tragedy of doomed sibling love, the ledger can simply prove her wrong. Elena answered that this confuses content with form: of course the events are constrained by evidence, but the *telling* is not, because any arrangement of true events into a story — where to begin it, what counts as its turning point, whether it resolves — is an act of emplotment, in Hayden White's sense, and the same well-attested events sustain a tragedy, a farce, or a redemption narrative without any of them being false. Even Rasmussen's bare chronology, she noted, begins with the family's arrival in America in 1907, which already implies a story about origins.

She then made what she called the Carr point: the questions we bring to the documents come from our present situation, and mine are transparent — I am grieving, and I want the rift explained. Rasmussen should study the questioner before trusting the account. Rasmussen replied that this is where the relativist slide does its work: granting that inquirers are situated does not mean their claims are unfalsifiable, and he warned that once the family is licensed to prefer the consoling version, every branch gets its own truth and the history dissolves into competing myths — which, given the 1948 rift, is a failure mode this family has already demonstrated. Elena's last move was that his ideal of the self-effacing narrator is itself a dated rhetorical pose; the footnotes perform authority just as surely as her chapter titles perform drama, and a family that reads only one version has been told only one story regardless.

I found each of them convincing in turn, which is why I am asking you rather than deciding myself. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific points, where does each overreach, and what are both of them missing? And since I do have practical decisions to make about the manuscript, please close by saying what your verdict implies for how the project should actually be run — where the archival constraints should be treated as non-negotiable, and where, if anywhere, Elena's shaping judgments legitimately take over.
