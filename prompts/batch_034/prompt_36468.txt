You are an expert in anthropic reasoning and the philosophy of probability, with long experience designing classroom demonstrations that let participants stumble into conceptual problems before those problems are named. Please think carefully and in stages: before settling on a final design, brainstorm two or three candidate activities, briefly explain why you prefer the one you choose, and only then give me the full, runnable version.

Some background on me and my situation: I am a retired physicist (statistical mechanics and Monte Carlo methods were my bread and butter), and in retirement I have gotten seriously into philosophy. I run a small adult-education seminar on probability and inference — twelve participants, mostly educated laypeople, one 90-minute session. Next month I want to tackle the doosmsday argument — no, the doomsday argument: the claim that one's birth rank among all humans who will ever live is not a neutral datum, and that finding oneself comparatively early in the sequence should raise one's probability that the total number of humans will be smaller than one might otherwise have thought. I understand the formal core — treat your birth rank as a random draw, condition on it, watch the posterior shift — but I have found that presenting it on the page does almost nothing: people nod, and then dismiss it the moment the name "doomsday" is uttered.

So here is what I need from you:

1. A game or simulation, with concrete materials, rules, a timeline, and a scoring scheme that uses points rather than money, in which participants first make choices and updates using a number they draw themselves, and only afterwards discover what they have been modelling. They should not be told the topic beforehand; ideally the target argument should dawn on them mid-debrief rather than being announced. My own instinct is that some sort of two-urn structure is the right vehicle — one urn holds ten numbered balls, the other a thousand, and participants must bet on which urn they drew from after seeing their number — but I am open to better constructions, including ones with genuinely sequential draws or hidden population sizes.

2. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the argument: the sampled number as birth rank, the prior over urn totals as the prior over humanity's future, the likelihood computation as the engine of the shift. The debrief should then stage the two standard escape routes — the objection that the sample was not random at all, and the rival view on which finding yourself existing at all is itself evidence in favor of hypotheses with more observers — as disagreements the participants must articulate themselves, in the vocabulary the game gave them, before any philosophical labels are attached.

3. A candid prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure without lecturing.

4. An honest note on which feature of the argument no tabletop version can reproduce — my own suspicion is that it is the fact that no one ever gets to look inside the urn, ever, so that the participants' updating can never be vindicated or refuted the way the game's scoring seems to promise — and what I, as the facilitator, must supply in its place.

You may assume participants can handle urn models and Bayes factors if they are walked through them gently; do not assume prior acquaintance with any of the philosophical literature.
