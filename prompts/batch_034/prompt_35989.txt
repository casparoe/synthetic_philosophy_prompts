I run genetics for a mid-size laying-hen operation and we're moving from small cages to bigger mixed-group pens, and suddenly the old way of picking breeders is breaking. Until now we've measured each hen's production and picked the top individual hens, but in the group pens the hens that score best on their own are often the nastiest ones — they eat more because they shove others off the feed. Someone at a conference told me there's a whole literature on this, that a couple decades back some people got calmer, more productive birds by selecting whole groups together instead of individual hens, and that this hooks into an old fight in evolutionary biology about whether selection acts on genes, individuals, or groups. I have a math background but zero evolutionary theory, so translate as you go.

Write me a decision procedure for choosing breeders that my team can run every generation. It should be grounded in what the levels-of-selection debate actually says — when picking on the individual is fine, when you have to pick at the group level, and why (I gather it's something about heritable variance getting partitioned between levels, but explain that in plain terms). The procedure needs: which measurements to take and in what order; a rule for when to rank birds individually vs. rank whole groups; what to do about traits you can't score at the group level (mortality is easy, feather cover is harder, pecking order is a nightmare); and it has to be honest about where the math stops and somebody just has to make a judgement call — mark those places clearly, don't dress them up as steps.

Then run it on three cases that are basically our real decisions: (1) feather pecking, where the damage is done by a few hens but the whole group loses feathers and it's hard to tell who's the culprit; (2) feed efficiency, where our costs are per-pen but every heritability number we have our per-bird; (3) a line where we've selected individually for egg size for ten years and I'm told moving to group selection now would cost us years of progress on the exact trait we sell. If the procedure gives an answer I wont like on case 3, give it anyway. And tell me what the procedure can't do — anywhere the biology genuinely dosn't have a settled answer, flag it instead of inventing a rule. It needs to be runnable by a technician in an afternoon with nothing fancier than a spreadsheet.
