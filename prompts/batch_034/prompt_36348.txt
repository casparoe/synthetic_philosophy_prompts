A philosophy column in a general-interest magazine explained the perdurantism/endurantism debate to me roughly as follows: 'Common sense says that you persist through time: the person who woke up this morning is the very same person who fell asleep last night. Perdurantists deny this. Following the block-universe picture that comes out of relativity, they hold that a persisting thing is really a four-dimensional "worm" stretched along the time axis, and that what exists at any given moment is only a part of it -- a temporal part, or time-slice. So strictly speaking nothing persists: things are frozen into a static spacetime, and the passage of time is an illusion. Endurantists, by contrast, say that a thing is wholly present at every moment at which it exists. But David Lewis showed that this can't be right: change requires having incompatible properties at different times, and nothing can be, say, both bent and straight simpliciter, so endurantism cannot account for change at all. That is why most metaphysicians working today accept the four-dimensional view.' Something about this feels loaded, but I can't put my finger on where, and I have no way to check the closing claims about Lewis or about what most metaphysicians believe. Please go through the passage bit by bit: which parts would a metaphysician accept as stated, where does the phrasing smuggle in something the perdurantist need not be committed to, and is the claim about current opinion in the field accurate? The column also closes by remarking that on the worm view, punishing someone today for a crime committed last year would be punishing the innocent, since what suffers is only a later temporal part rather than the part that committed the crime. Does that conclusion follow from anything a perdurantist actually believes?
