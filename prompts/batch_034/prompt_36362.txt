I teach intro comparative politics and every year I get to the "why are some countries rich and others poor" unit and feel a bit defeated. The debate has been running in basically the same shape for decades now: the institutions crowd pointing to the reversal of fortunes stuff (places that were relatively developed in 1500 being relatively poor today, which they read as institutions being destiny), the geography crowd with disease burdens, latitude, landlocked-ness, agricultural potential, and then the culture people with norms, trust, attitudes toward commerce, Weber's shadow hanging over the whole thing. And it just... doesn't move. Each side has its favorite natural experiments and its favorite counterexamples to the other sides' natural experiments, and every few years someone publishes a big book that restates one position more confidently, and everyone else writes a review saying the book ignored their favorite variable.

My honest guess is that a lot of this is not really one disagreement but several stacked on top of each other. Like, some of it seems to be about proximate vs. ultimate causes — the geography people can say "fine, institutions matter, but why do good institutions cluster where they do? maybe geography all the way down," and the institutions people can flip it the other way, and neither is wrong, they're just answering different questions. Some of it might be about the counterfactual: "would Nigeria be rich with better institutions" vs. "could Nigeria have had better institutions given where and when it is" feel like genuinely different questions that get argued under one heading. And some of it might be a real empirical dispute that's just hard — the identification problems are brutal, the colonial-era data everyone uses as instruments gets picked apart, and there are what, 150 countries, so the effective sample size for testing grand theories is tiny.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which factor is actually most important. Which parts of this fight are substantive empirical disagreements (and if so, is there any realistic evidence that could settle them, or is the data just too thin forever?), which parts are the sides asking different questions while using the same words, and which parts are maybe disagreements about what even counts as an explanation of a one-off historical outcome like the industrial revolution? If some of it is people talking past each other, can you say precisely where the pass happens? And given the diagnosis, what would actual progress look like here — better instruments, panel data over centuries, decomposing "institutions" into pieces, or just everyone admitting the question is ill-posed at the level of grand causes and moving on?
