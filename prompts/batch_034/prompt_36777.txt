I teach an introductory philosophy of mind course for undergraduates — about twenty students, mostly freshmen with no philosophy background — and I have a single 75-minute session coming up on Dennett's short fantasy "Where Am I?" from Brainstorms. In case it's been a while: Dennett imagines being recruited to retrieve a radioactive warhead buried underground near Tulsa, Oklahoma, and to protect him from the radiation his brain is surgically removed and kept alive in a vat back in Houston, his body remaining in radio contact with it. He ends up naming the brain Yorick, the body Hamlet, and later a replacement body Fortinbras, and eventually things get worse: a duplicate of his brain (Hubert) is made, and technicians can switch between the original and the duplicate without him being able to tell. The puzzle is that each candidate answer to "where am I?" — with the body, with the brain, wherever my point of view is, and finally "which of the two am I, anyway?" — seems right until something forces a switch. My students read it last week and got the plot, but I don't think they got the instability: on the page it reads as a cute science-fiction setup, and they are comfortably dividing themselves into camps (brain-locators versus body-locators versus point-of-view-locators) rather than discovering that their own judgments flip under pressure.

Design a 75-minute in-class activity that manufactures that instability instead of merely describing it. My rough idea, which you should improve rather than merely accept: some kind of staged second-person narration ("you are riding in a truck toward the site; your brain is in a vat in Houston...") punctuated by buzzers, at which each student has about five seconds to hold up one of a small fixed set of location cards answering "where are you?" — no deliberation, no talking — with the tally marked on the board. Between buzzers the scenario changes exactly one thing at a time (the senses are rerouted through a remote camera; a transmission lag is introduced; the body is duplicated; the brain is swapped without announcement) so the tally has to move, and the movement itself is the data the debrief works from. Give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact sequence of scenario updates and why that order, and a way of recording responses that makes the flips visible to the students themselves. The students should not be told what the activity is supposed to show until it has run its course.

Then the debrief: map each flip in the tally onto a specific part of Dennett's puzzle — the brain-location answer, the point-of-view answer, and the moment the question quietly stops being about location and becomes about identity — and say what each shift reveals about what the word "I" is doing in "where am I?" I also want an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students treating it as a quiz with a right answer and hunting for it; order effects anchoring the tally; someone answering in the third person about Dennett rather than in the first person about themselves; groups where the tally never moves at all) and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about the limit: no classroom game can reproduce the first-person stakes that make Dennett's fantasy gripping rather than merely clever — nobody's actual self is on the line when the scenario card says your brain has been swapped. Tell me precisely which part of the point the activity cannot deliver, and what I have to supply in the debrief by way of compensation.
