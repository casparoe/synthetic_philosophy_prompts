Can a Moral Theory Be a Theorem?

In 1955, a Hungarian-born economist named John Harsanyi published a paper in the Journal of Political Economy that, on one reading, did something philosophers had been attempting since Bentham: it proved utilitarianism. Harsanyi was not yet famous -- he would eventually share the 1994 Nobel with John Nash -- but the argument he laid out has been a thorn in moral philosophy ever since.

The machinery comes from decision theory. A decade earlier, von Neumann and Morgenstern had shown that if a person's preferences over risky prospects satisfy certain axioms of rationality, then those preferences can be represented by a utility function that is unique up to the choice of zero and unit -- what mathematicians call an affine transformation. This gives each individual a cardinal utility: not just a ranking, but a scale on which differences mean something.

Harsanyi's move was to apply the same machinery to society. Suppose society itself is rational in the von Neumann-Morgenstern sense, and suppose society respects its citizens: whenever everyone prefers one prospect to another, society does too. Then, Harsanyi showed, the social utility function must be a weighted sum of the individual utilities. Add the natural requirement that people's utilities count equally, and the weights come out uniform. Add the utilities of many and you have, precisely, the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Utilitarianism, it seems, follows from rationality plus respect for individuals.

If that sounds too easy, it is. The first problem is that the von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem licenses comparisons only within a single person's head. Your utility scale and mine are each fixed only up to arbitrary choices of zero and unit, and those choices can be made independently. Since the vNM machinery already hands us a cardinal scale for each person, it might seem that the common currency utilitarianism needs is simply there for the taking -- rescale everyone to a common zero and unit, add, and go. But the freedom to rescale each person independently is exactly what the theorem leaves untouched. Harsanyi's formal result is silent on which rescalings are the right ones, and utilitarianism needs an answer. Different answers give different verdicts about whose welfare beats whose.

The second problem came from the economist Peter Diamond in the 1960s, and it is the objection I find hardest to shake. Take two people and a single indivisible benefit. Either person alone could receive it, or you can flip a coin. Suppose the total utility is the same either way -- a utilitarian tied only to sums is indifferent. Yet most of us think giving each person a fifty-fifty shot is better than, say, handing the good to person A with certainty, even if person B is compensated in a way that equalizes the totals. The coin flip matters even when nothing in the ledger changes, because it treats the two as equals rather than as one who matters and one who does not. The utilitarian ledger has no column for that.

There is a third reading, and it is where Harsanyi meets his most famous interlocutor. Harsanyi had earlier offered a separate argument: imagine a single rational agent choosing among social arrangements while ignorant of which person in that society she will turn out to be. Under uncertainty, she maximizes expected utility, and it follows that she endorses average utilitarianism. Rawls, in A Theory of Justice, ran nearly the same thought experiment and got his two principles of justice, with maximin doing the work that expected utility does for Harsanyi. The formalism does not adjudicate between them: it never told anyone how much risk aversion to have behind the veil.

So what did the theorem prove? Here is my answer: not utilitarianism, but something about what any acceptable moral aggregation must look like. If you want a social ranking that is rational under risk and responsive to individual rankings, it must be additive in some weighted way over some welfare-like quantities. The theorem fixes the shape of the container. Everything contested -- interpersonal comparability, equal weights, whether fairness is more than a number -- is a fight about what goes in. That fight is philosophical, and no theorem will end it. Perhaps that is the real lesson of the paper: formal results in ethics rarely settle the question they are famous for, but they tell us, with unusual precision, where the question lives.
