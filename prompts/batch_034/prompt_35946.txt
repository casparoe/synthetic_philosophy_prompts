I teach a small upper-level seminar that crosses philosophy with clinical ethics — sixteen students, mostly nursing and philosophy majors, meeting once a week for ninety minutes. For years I assigned Augustine on lying as a museum piece: extreme, fascinating, safely distant. That changed this spring. My mother's dementia progressed to the point where she moved into memory care, and the staff there are skilled, patient people who practice what the literature blandly calls therapeutic fibbing. They tell her that her late husband is at the store, that this stay is temporary, that the doctor is coming tomorrow — because the plain truth, delivered to her fresh each time as though for the first time, produces a panic and grief that are unbearable to watch and, we are told, genuinely harmful to her. Standing in that hallway, I have found I can no longer treat Augustine as a period curiosity. His claim that every lie is wrong — including one that harms no one, spares terrible suffering, or even saves a life — is now something I carry on every visit, and I want my students to meet it as a live position rather than an artifact.

For next week's session, design a ninety-minute activity with the following features:

1. No lecture up front, and no mention of Augustine's name or conclusions until the reveal at the end.
2. An opening phase in which students play through staged cases under an enumerated set of permitted moves — flatly asserting something, misleading by means of true statements, deflecting, staying silent, refusing to answer — so that they feel the narrowness of the space Augustine actually allows. The cases should be drawn both from his territory and from mine: concealing someone from a persecutor asking directly where they are; a judge pressing a witness; a memory-care corridor at four in the afternoon.
3. A middle phase in which students rank the lies they were tempted to tell, from most to least excusable. Only afterwards should they see Augustine's own eightfold ranking of kinds of lies — including, at the less culpable end, the lie that harms no one and saves a life, and the one told merely to please — alongside his flat refusal to license any of them.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific Augustinian distinctions: his definition of a lie as speaking against one's mind rather than merely saying something false; his distinction between assertion and concealment; his willingness, in the treatise occasioned by the plan to pose as Priscillianist heretics in order to expose them, to condemn even a lie in service of the truth.
5. An account of the predictable failure modes the first time I run this — including, if you think it likely, students refusing to lie at all and thereby gutting the exercise, or treating the dementia cases as so easy that they stop engaging with the harder ones.
6. An honest note on what no classroom activity can reproduce. My own suspicion is that the prohibition's real load-bearing element is theological — the soul injured by falsehood, truth understood as something like the divine nature — and that this is precisely what a game with tokens and role cards cannot manufacture. If you agree, say so and explain what the facilitator must supply instead; if you disagree, show me what could carry that weight.

Please write out the rules, roles, timeline, and debrief in full, use no real money or graded stakes, and answer in American English.
