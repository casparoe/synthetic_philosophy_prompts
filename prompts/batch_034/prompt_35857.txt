I help run an alignment reading group and I want a decision tree handout that starts from the question "what would it take for you to trust an AI system?" and ends at a named position on worst-case vs average-case safety. Each node should be a concrete judgment call the positions actually split on, phrased so someone can answer it without knowing the labels — stuff like: if a system behaves badly on 1 in 10^12 inputs but is great on the distribution it'll actually face, do you care? Is a safety property that holds for 99.9999% of cases under your prior meaningfully different from one that holds for all of them? Whose prior — yours, the designer's, or a adversarially-chosen one? Does it matter whether the failure inputs are there by accident or because something is searching for them? And where do quantilizer-style views ("just don't take extreme actions") fit — are they a third option or just average-case with extra steps?

Leaves should include roughly: hard worst-case verification (the 'I want a proof or nothing' stance), empirical/average-case safety research (run the evals, estimate the tail), the adversarial-robustness view that average-case is fine until it isn't because the world contains optimizers hunting for your failure modes, quantilization-style bounded-impact views, and the deflationary 'guarantees are the wrong frame entirely, manage it like any other risky engineering' leaf. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of camps, and flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate (I suspect average-case-Bayesian and engineering-deflationist end up indistinguishable, but tell me if I'm wrong). Also mark the unoccupied combinations — e.g. someone who demands worst-case guarantees but thinks adversarial pressure is negligible — and say whether nobody holds that because its incoherent or just because its a bad deal. One page, decision nodes as questions, keep the jargon out of the nodes themselves.
