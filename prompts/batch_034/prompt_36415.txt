I run a political philosophy seminar for second-year undergrads, 12 students, 90 minutes, and next week we hit Kant's Perpetual Peace. I don't want to open with a lecture — I want a game they play *first*, so that when we turn to the text they keep having the experience of reading their own game session transcribed into 1795 German. The core thing I want them to feel is Kant's weird claim that peace is not about good intentions or brilliant diplomacy: it's a design problem, where the right constitutional machinery makes war irrational (or at least ruinous) even when every actor is perfectly selfish.

My rough sketch, which you should feel free to rip apart: six or so delegations on a fictional map with asymmetric resources, several rounds in which they can trade, form alliances, and go to war, points as the payoff (no real money). What I'd love to have happen: their first peace treaty collapses — ideally because somebody tucked a secret reservation into it, or quietly rebuilt their army under cover of the truce — and they then have to reinvent, from scratch, something like the preliminarly articles, and eventually something like the federation, on their own. One wrinkle I'm half-attached to: making some delegations autocracies (one student decides everything) and some republics (they must deliberate and vote, with a rule that war costs get itemised to them personally), so Kant's first definitive article shows up in the data rather than in my mouth. But tell me if that's too gimmicky or too heavy for 90 minutes.

What I need from you: complete rules and materials, a payoff scheme where war is tempting early and ruinous late (so a naive player discovers the standing-army trap the hard way), the round structure with rough timings, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific bits of the text — secret reservations, standing armies, the debt-financed war problem, the federation-of-free-states-not-a-world-state point, and hospitality for strangers on your territory.
