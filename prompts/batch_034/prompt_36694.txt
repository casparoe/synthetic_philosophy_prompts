"Lewis wants to explain how conventions like driving on the left or using a particular currency can exist without anyone being in charge. He says a convention is basically a solution to a coordination problem: a situation where everyone has an interest in matching what the others do, so any way of coordinating beats no way at all, and once it's in place nobody gains by going it alone. Take which side of the road we drive on: I don't intrinsically care whether we all drive on the left or the right, I just want us all on the same side. That shows arbitrariness: either convention would do equally well, so the convention is arbitrary in the sense that it could be switched to the alternative at no cost, which is why a government could change the rule overnight if it wanted to.

For it to be a genuine convention there also has to be common knowledge: everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, everyone expects them to expect that, and so on. This unending chain of expectations is what keeps the convention stable.

Where do conventions come from? Lewis's paradigm case is explicit agreement — a convention is like a promise made by a whole population at once, and salience just explains which option they end up agreeing on. Salience itself comes from Schelling's focal points: if two people have to meet somewhere in a city without communicating, the train station is salient, so they'll both head there. Once a first instance of coordination succeeds, precedent makes that option more salient for the future, and so conventions snowball on their own without anyone planning them.

Finally, enforcement. Conventions persist because deviations are punished. If I drive on the wrong side, other drivers honk, I get fined, and I damage my reputation as a cooperator. Lewis's account therefore explains the stability of conventions via the sanctions a group attaches to them, which is also why conventions can solve collective action problems like overfishing, where everyone is tempted to defect."
