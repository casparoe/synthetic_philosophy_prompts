For the past several years I have co-taught a seminar in which half the students end up convinced retributivists and the other half convinced consequentialists about punishment, and I have noticed that by the final session the two groups are mostly talking past each other: the retributivists accuse the others of being willing to punish the innocent whenever the arithmetic works out, while the consequentialists accuse the retributivists of dressing up a desire for vengeance in the vocabulary of desert. I want to try something new next term: instead of a debate, an adversarial collaboration. Before I ask the students to write one, I want a model document to hand out.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed positive retributivist — someone who holds that the state has a duty to give wrongdoers the punishment they deserve, and that this duty holds even when punishing produces no good effects and even when a wrongdoer's suffering makes nobody better off — and a committed consequentialist — someone who holds that punishment is justified only by its effects (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, perhaps norm-expression), and that a punishment producing none of these is gratuitous cruelty — could both sign in good conscience after a year of good-faith collaboration. The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. I expect these to include the obvious ones (that punishment involves the deliberate infliction of suffering and therefore needs justification; that both sides reject torture and punishment grossly disproportionate to the offense) but I especially want the non-obvious ones a good collaboration would surface: for instance, both sides accept some version of negative retributivism — that the undeserving must not be punished — even though the consequentialist regards it as defeasible in principle while the retributivist regards it as absolute; both sides can accept that punishment has an expressive or censuring function, though they disagree about whether the expression is intrinsically fitting or instrumentally useful; and both can accept that the reform of an offender counts in favor of leniency, while disagreeing about why.

2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement must be stated twice — once in wording the retributivist would sign, once in wording the consequentialist would sign — so that neither row is slanted. For example, the retributivist's row on the point of punishment should speak of giving the offender what justice requires, while the consequentialist's row on the same disagreement should speak of reducing future offending, and the table should make clear these are two formulations of one disagreement, not two different topics.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the three or four sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect the candidates include: (a) whether the guilty have, by their wrongdoing, made themselves liable to suffering in a way the innocent have not, or whether liability to hard treatment is solely a function of consequences; (b) whether there is a morally significant difference between harming someone as a means to others' benefit and harming them in response to what they have done — the consequentialist's willingness to trade off, and the retributivist's refusal to; (c) what follows from the case, familiar from the literature, of the sheriff who could prevent a riot that will kill several innocent people by framing and executing one innocent person, and the parallel question of whether the retributivist's absolute refusal there commits them to refusing the punishment of an innocent person even to prevent vastly greater crime; and (d) the standing puzzle for retributivists of the criminal who genuinely reforms, or dies, before sentencing — is there still a reason to punish, and what kind of reason is it?

4. For each crux, a test, case, or argument both parties would agree bears on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance — for example, both should agree that jurisdictions' experiences with celerity-versus-severity tradeoffs bear on whether deterrence reasoning can carry the justificatory weight the consequentialist needs, and both should agree that the retributivist's ability to give a non-circular account of how much punishment an offense deserves bears on whether desert can do the work the retributivist needs. Where the two parties would predict the same outcome but interpret it differently, say so explicitly.

5. A closing section on which parts of the residual dispute are substantive and which are, or risk being, merely verbal — for instance, whether the disagreement over whether punishment is "intrinsically deserved" or "deserved because of what expressing it achieves" is a real metaphysical disagreement or a dispute about whether to treat the same morally loaded facts as basic.

Assume both parties are sophisticated: the consequentialist is aware of and grants the objection from punishing the innocent, the retributivist is aware of and grants the objection that desert yields no determinate sentencing scale and no guidance in cases of legal moralism run amok. Do not have either side concede more than their actual position would allow, and do not soften the disagreements into a bland consensus — the document is a map of the terrain, not a treaty. Format: a preamble of two or three sentences both would sign, then the numbered agreed claims, then the tables and sections as specified. Keep it under about 1,500 words.
