I'm the valedictorian of my graduating class, and I've been asked to deliver a five-minute speech at commencement. For the past semester I've been absorbed in Plato's cave allegory — I wrote my extended essay on Book VII of the Republic, so I know the details that popular summaries tend to drop: the fire behind the prisoners, the puppeteers carrying artifacts along the wall, the freed prisoner's eyes aching so badly in the light that he wants to crawl back down, the contest in which prisoners win honors for predicting which shadow will pass next, and the grim suggestion that if a returned prisoner tried to free the others, they would laugh at him and, if they could, kill him — which Socrates seems to intend as a dark nod to his own fate. My essay supervisor made two further points that have stayed with me: Socrates insists that education is not a matter of putting sight into blind eyes but of turning the whole soul around toward what is real, and he says the philosophers' descent back into the cave is compulsory — the ones who have seen the sun owe it to the people still inside.

Here is my problem: a graduation speech about the cave is, structurally, a returning prisoner's speech, and the allegory is quite clear that those do not go well. If I cast my classmates as prisoners, I insult them on a day meant to honor them, and I imply that I alone have seen the sun, which at eighteen is a difficult claim to make with a straight face. If I cast the teachers as the ones who turned us around, then the diploma becomes a step into the daylight — but the allegory's actual claim is that the ascent takes decades, that the eyes hurt the whole way, and that the first things you see outside are mere shadows and reflections in water before you can bear anything brighter. I also can't deploy the "turning the soul" line as a compliment to my class, because Socrates means it partly as a critique of schools exactly like mine: institutions that fill students with facts while leaving them facing the wall, rewarding whoever is best at naming the passing shadows — which is uncomfortably close to what I just spent four years excelling at.

Please write the speech. It should run about five minutes, roughly 700 words, and the audience is mixed: most of the room is family who have never read a line of Plato, while a handful of teachers, my supervisor among them, know the allegory cold and will catch every liberty I take. I want the cave to do genuine structural work — shaping what I can honestly claim lies ahead of us, and what we owe to the people still down in the dark — rather than being name-dropped in the opening line and abandoned. The speech should face the allegory's hardest edge, that the return is compulsory and unwelcome, without making the room feel insulted or doomed. If you conclude that no honest version of the cave story can survive a commencement, tell me so plainly, and give me the best speech that honesty permits.
