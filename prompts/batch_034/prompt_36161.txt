I am a novelist, and I need help from someone with serious grounding in philosophy of science and psychiatry — treat yourself as a professional philosopher who has worked on classification, and please think carefully and do some private brainstorming before answering, because I want the distinctions to be sharp, not the usual gloss.

Here is my situation. I am writing a novel whose central character is a woman in her forties who, over the course of the book, comes to be described — by a psychiatrist within the novel — as having a dissociative disorder. I have not decided how the diagnosis lands: whether she accepts it and is changed by it, or whether she comes to see it as a frame someone else put around her life. At a recent dinner, a psychiatrist friend of mine leaned across the table and told me that I was, in his words, making up people. He was invoking Ian Hacking's slogan, which he knows from the literature on psychiatric classification and what Hacking called the looping effects of human kinds. From the slogan he drew two conclusions about my novel, and I want to know whether either actually follows.

His first conclusion was ethical: that I bear a special responsibility unusual for a novelist, because fictional depictions of a diagnosis feed back into real diagnostic practice and into the self-understanding of real people who receive that diagnosis — that my character can, in a small way, help constitute the kind of person she is depicted as being. His second conclusion was metaphysical, or at least anti-realist: that since people are in some sense made up rather than discovered, there is no fact of the matter my depiction is tracking — no pre-existing condition there to be described correctly or incorrectly — so my diagnostic choice for the character is, in his phrase, unconstrained, a free act of classification rather than a report.

I am sure the slogan making up people can be read many ways, and that some of those readings would support one of his conclusions, some the other, some neither, and some would contradict him outright. So here is what I want from you: lay out, as a structured list I can work through at my desk, the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing — starting with readings so weak they are near-truisms (classifications influence the people classified), through substantive middle readings (say, that classifications can create the behaviors and self-descriptions they name, or that the kinds are interactive in a way natural kinds are not), up to the radical readings (something like full dynamic nominalism: the kind of person did not exist, in any sense, before the classification). For each reading, tell me: whether it is plausible and defensible, whether it is merely a platitude dressed up, and whether it is actually false. Then, crucially, tell me which readings would genuinely support his ethical conclusion about my novel, which would support his conclusion that my choice of diagnosis is unconstrained, and which would undermine one or both. Please also consider the obvious objection he waved away: that whatever looping effects psychiatric categories have, a novelist's character is not a patient — nothing is being diagnosed in her, only depicted — and assess whether that difference breaks the chain from his slogan to either conclusion, or merely weakens it.

I would like the answer to be a disambiguation with verdicts, in effect a menu of the slogan's readings ranked from weakest to strongest, rather than a defense of one interpretation. Please keep it self-contained: assume I have no access to the literature, explain any terms of art you introduce, and do not point me to books or articles — I want the reasoning itself, not a reading list. Take your time, and where a reading turns on a subtle distinction, spell the distinction out rather than naming it and moving on. The decision I am trying to make — how my character and her diagnosis coexist, and whether I owe her, or people like her, anything — depends on getting these readings apart, so precision matters more than elegance.
