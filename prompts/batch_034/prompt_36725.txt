I direct a small municipal sustainability office, and my staff spend a surprising amount of time arguing about whether our smallest measures are worth doing at all: converting the city fleet to electric over a decade, taking beef off the menu at city-run cafeterias two days a week, a composting program, tree plantings. At every budget meeting someone objects that the city's emissions are a rounding error next to global totals, that no single one of our programs changes the climate by any measurable amount, and that we should either do something big enough to matter or stop performing virtue. My staff, in turn, keep reaching for different and incompatible replies — that each small act contributes to a collective harm or benefit in a way we understand from cases like voting; that there's some small but nonzero probability our program triggers or contributes to a threshold effect; that it would be wrong for a city of our size to free-ride even if the act itself changes nothing; that the point is leadership and signaling. We have no settled framework, and the arguments restart every quarter.

Please turn whatever the philosophy of individual contributions to collective harms actually supports into a decision procedure my staff could run on a proposed measure in a single meeting: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear "not justified" even when the measure feels good, and which questions are not really about the inefficacy worry at all and shouldn't be smuggled in as answers to it. Be explicit about which of the replies my staff currently use the procedure should endorse, which it should reject, and which depend on contested value judgments that have to be handed to the elected council rather than settled by staff. Then run it on two proposals: the beef reduction in city cafeterias, and subsidizing home solar installations that we know will mostly be taken up by households that would have installed them anyway. Include at least one case where the procedure's verdict conflicts with what I'd intuitively want, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
