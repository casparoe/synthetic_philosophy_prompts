I've become fairly convinced that care ethics is not merely a corrective supplement to justice-based morality but a complete foundational framework for ethics: roughly, the view that the primary subject of moral evaluation is not the act, the rule, or the virtue of the agent in isolation, but the quality of caring relations and the responsiveness of carers to the concrete, situated needs of particular others. My reading started with the Kohlberg–Gilligan dispute and moved on through Noddings and Held, and I've come to think that the relational picture does better than any contractarian or Kantian rival at explaining why we owe anything to severely cognitively impaired people, newborns, and others who cannot reciprocate or deliberate with us. Still, I want to stress-test this. What would genuinely count against the view? I don't just mean the standard objections I already know how to parry — the partiality worry, the problem of caring for distant strangers, the charge that 'care' is an oppressive feminine ideal. I want the deeper structural worries: for instance, that the account is circular because identifying which caring dispositions are morally admirable seems to presuppose a standard that care ethics itself cannot supply, or that the view quietly collapses into virtue ethics with 'caring' as a master trait, in which case it isn't a rival framework at all. How serious are these?

Please also tell me which empirical findings would cut against the position. I gather the original case for the ethics of care leaned partly on claimed gender differences in moral reasoning, and I'm aware that some of that psychology has been contested or refined since. If, say, robust cross-cultural evidence showed that 'care reasoning' tracks parochial in-group loyalty far more than its defenders claimed — so that elevating care risks enshrining clan morality — would that be a problem for the normative view, or only for one contingent argument in its favour? And is there an asymmetry here: would evidence that men and women reason identically about care hurt the view more than the corresponding evidence about justice would hurt Kantianism?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, an honest statement of which of them committed defenders of care ethics would themselves concede as real exposure (rather than only what critics allege), and an assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by shifting between the sentiment, the virtue, the relation, and the practice of care as the theory's core — and if so, whether that kind of flexibility is a defect in a moral theory or simply the price of doing ethics at the level of particular relations rather than abstract principles. Please write your answer in British English.
