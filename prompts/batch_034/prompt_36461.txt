I'm the quantitative geneticist at a mid-sized layer-hen breeding company, and I need a standing decision procedure more than I need another argument. Background: most of our history is individual selection on single-hen cage records — egg production to the gram, perfect pedigrees, no interaction between birds. New welfare rules mean our birds and our customers' birds will live in large colonies, and instrumenting colony housing (RFID per bird, individual feed intake) runs about 8x the cost per bird, so we can only instrument a fraction of any flock. Every selection cycle we end up re-litigating the same fight: do we select on the individual records we have on the subflock, on group means over whole colonies, or some blend? The sales team, the welfare consultant, and I all have different gut answers. The breeding director has told me to write down a procedure the junior geneticists can actually run so he stops adjudicating this by vibe. He signs off on whatever the procedure recommends, so the handoff points matter.

Here's my problem though: a philosophy-of-biology friend of mine told me our fight is basically the old units-of-selection debate — genes vs individuals vs groups, Dawkins and Gould, Sober and Wilson, multilevel selection, the Price equation partition, something called contextual analysis where you regress individual fitness on the bird's own trait and separately on its group's mean. I've read enough to follow the shape of it but I'm no philosopher and no theorist; what I'm fluent in is mixed models, BLUP, kinship matrices, and experimental design. So turn whatever that literature actually says into a procedure I can run in an afternoon with data we really have: colony-level production and mortality records, individual records on the instrumented subset, full pedigrees, batch structure.

What I need from the procedure:

- The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure terminates with a clear "select on individuals" or "select on group means" versus where it has to punt to the director because the remaining question is about money or values, not statistics.
- It has to encode the distinction the philosophers actually care about: cases where individual and group selection pull the same direction versus cases where they genuinely conflict. (Feather pecking is our classic conflict trait — individually productive birds can be awful cagemates — but I want the procedure general, not a one-trait hack.)
- Somewhere it has to be honest about the equivalence point: whether picking the individual or the group description ever changes the decision, or whether it's just bookkeeping with different column headers.
- Run it on three cases: (1) feather pecking and mortality in colony housing, where individual selection historically made things worse; (2) feed conversion efficiency, where I suspect individuals and colonies agree; (3) resistance to a challenge disease where birds get dosed in batches, so the only records that exist are batch-level — no individual option at all.
- One of those cases should come out the way I won't like — I have a feeling it's the pecking one, and the right answer is expensive — and I want you to actually bite that bullet rather than soften it.

Also flag what the procedure can't settle, so the juniors know when to escalate instead of trusting it. And please be upfront about confidence throughout: mark which steps are load-bearing theory and which are rules of thumb dressed up as procedure, and single out the one point in the whole thing you're least sure of.
