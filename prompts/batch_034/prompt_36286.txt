A popular book on blame and moral responsibility that I've been reading introduces P. F. Strawson's position, from his 1962 essay "Freedom and Resentment," roughly as follows: "Strawson demonstrated that the free will debate is a pseudo-problem. Our practices of praise and blame are not really responses to a person's freedom at all; they are natural human reactions, on the order of flinching at a loud noise, and we are psychologically incapable of abandoning them. Since we cannot help but blame, blaming stands justified, and the truth or falsity of determinism is simply irrelevant to responsibility. When behavior is explained by mental illness or personality disorder, we withdraw blame by adopting what Strawson called the objective attitude -- the judgment that the person is not responsible -- and this is essentially the same thing as an ordinary excuse, like saying he was pushed." Something about this strikes me as running together distinctions that I've heard Strawson is famous for keeping apart, but the book is by a professional philosopher and I can't locate the errors myself. Could you audit it for me? In particular, I'd like to know: which claims here would a careful Strawsonian accept as a fair paraphrase; which are misrepresentations, and what exactly do they distort; and does the book's further conclusion -- that, on Strawson's view, we ought to regard psychopaths as objects of treatment rather than as blameworthy agents, since the objective attitude is simply the attitude we take toward anyone whose behavior can be explained -- actually follow from anything accurate in the summary, or does it fall with the misrepresentations?
