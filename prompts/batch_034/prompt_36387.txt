My friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about vagueness, and I came away with a page of verdicts I cannot make consistent, so I would like help extracting a principle, or learning that there isn't one. Here is what I find myself confident of, case by case. Ten thousand grains of sand poured into a pile: a heap. The same ten thousand grains scattered evenly across a floor: not a heap — so whatever makes a heap, it is not a grain count, and the sorites question should perhaps have been asked about the pile rather than the grains. A man with sixty thousand hairs arranged as a monk's fringe is bald; a woman with forty-five thousand hairs evenly distributed over a somewhat larger scalp is thin-haired but not bald — arrangement again beating number, which makes me suspect the one-grain-at-a-time tolerance premise was never quite about what it seemed. Asked one conditional at a time — if a pile of n grains is a heap, is a pile of n minus one a heap? — I answered yes at every step without flinching, down to numbers I would never call a heap; yet I flatly reject the conclusion that a single grain is a heap. I think that, even for the nastiest borderline case, it is trivially true that either it is a heap or it is not; but I also think no statement in the borderline zone is true, and I can hear that these two convictions cannot both stand. A contract that stipulates a sharp threshold for what counts as substantial completion seems to me to have genuinely created a fact where before there was none, and I am comfortable with a court's verdict hinging on which side of the line the work falls; but the analogous claim that ordinary words like heap already had sharp cutoffs all along, merely unknowable ones, strikes me as flatly false. And when my friend asked whether the boundary itself is fuzzy — whether it is indeterminate whether it is indeterminate — I said yes, and yes again at the next level up, without believing that the ascent ever terminates in a level where things become determinate. Is there a single account of vagueness — semantic, epistemic, pragmatic, or something else — that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which would you overturn first, and at what cost? I would like the principle stated precisely enough to adjudicate two cases we never got to: whether there is a right answer to write on a police form that demands a yes-or-no about whether a borderline driver is bald, and whether a stipulated legal threshold changes what the words mean or merely changes what happens to you.
