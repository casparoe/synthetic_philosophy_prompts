I'm writing a term paper for a course on classical sociological theory, and I've chosen to read Defoe's Robinson Crusoe against Weber's Protestant ethic thesis — the claim that a this-worldly asceticism, the idea of the calling, and methodical self-examination produced a spirit of capitalism that then outlived its religious roots. On the face of it the novel looks like a dramatization of Weber's ideal type, staged before there is any capitalism to speak of: Crusoe, whose father warns him against wandering out of the middle station of life, comes to read his shipwrecks as Providence; he undergoes an individual conversion alone on the island with nothing but a Bible; and he then rationalizes the place — a calendar kept by notches on a post, a journal whose page of comforts and miseries he lays out like a debtor-and-creditor account, labor deliberately budgeted, tools and goats and grain accumulated rather than enjoyed. Even his attitude to money seems to be precisely the transitional case Weber cares about: when he finds coins on the wrecked ship he reflects at length on how useless they are to a castaway, and then pockets them anyway.

I'm stuck on two problems, and I'd like your help thinking them through. First, the causal problem: Weber's real thesis is that values can be a cause, and the novel cannot straightforwardly show that, because Defoe was a dissenting tradesman in an already commercial England, writing to sell books — so any confirmation of the thesis in the novel may just be a capitalist projecting his own values onto an imagined castaway. Where does the novel support Weber, where does it instead support the materialist reading he was arguing against, and can a fiction ever be evidence about the direction of causation between values and economic conduct? Second, the asceticism problem: Weber insists the Puritan earns wealth methodically and consumes it sparingly, yet Crusoe keeps sailing off on new adventures once he is rich, and he plainly enjoys his island estate, his fortifications, and his command over Friday — so is his conduct ascetic at all, or is it adventure and domination dressed up as providence? Walk me through the scenes you would point to — the two-column journal, the coins on the wreck, his conversion during the illness, the footprint, the making of Friday into servant and convert — and say for each whether it fits Weber's ideal type or strains it. Then tell me honestly: does Crusoe dramatize the Protestant ethic, complicate it, or quietly parody it? I want a reading that takes both the novel and Weber seriously enough to say what each one shows that the other cannot.
