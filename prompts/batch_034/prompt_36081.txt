I run a small chain of three bookshops in a mid-sized city. Over the past eighteen months, independent shops like ours across the city — florists, cafés, other bookstores — have been hit by a wave of ransomware attacks: someone gets into the till and ordering systems, encrypts everything, and demands a few thousand for the decryption key. The sums are deliberately small, sized so that paying feels cheaper than rebuilding. My second shop was hit last month and I paid; last week a café owner two streets over told me she'd paid twice. It's becoming clear that paying once marks you, and everyone like you, as a reliable payer, so the attacks keep coming back — the racket seems to test targets on businesses that resemble ones that paid before.

At a meeting of the independent retailers' association, one member made a proposal most of the room found bizarre. She wants every member to sign a public pledge: if any member is attacked, every member closes for a day in solidarity, and — this is the drastic part — each of us publicly commits in advance to never paying a demand, no matter how small, even if refusing costs more than the ransom would have. Her argument was that what matters is not what any of us does after an attack but what an attacker expects of us before choosing a target: a visible, slightly irrational-looking stubbornness now could make attacking any of us look unprofitable later, and the pledge is meant to work entirely through the attacker's beliefs about how we will behave, not through any enforcement — if we're believed, we never have to act on it at all. The objections came fast: once an attacker has already struck, the pledge just makes us suffer pointlessly, since refusing to pay after the fact can't undo anything; and the pledge is unstable anyway, because whoever is actually attacked will find it rational to pay quietly and dissolve the scheme for everyone else.

I find her argument persuasive and the objections persuasive, and I can't get clear on whether the pledge is sound strategy or theatre. Is the proposal actually good sense, and if so, what exactly makes it work — does the pre-commitment change anything real given that its whole force runs through an attacker's model of us rather than any mechanism that binds us? And is the pledge stable, or does each member's incentive to defect when genuinely attacked guarantee that, one quiet payment at a time, it unravels? Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
