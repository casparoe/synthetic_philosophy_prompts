Some months ago I was prescribed an SSRI for anxiety, and it has worked in a way that unsettles me. The situations that used to trigger me still strike me as bad — my workload still registers as unsustainable, my father's illness still registers as terrible — but the accompanying charge, the dread in the stomach, seems to have been turned down. At my last appointment my psychiatrist asked whether I still feel anxious about things or merely notice that they are bad, and I realized I could not answer, because I do not actually know which of those is the emotion. This has sent me into the old literature-adjacent question of what emotions are: judgments, bodily feelings, or perceptions of some evaluative property. To work out where I stand, I have been collecting cases where the cognitive and the affective components come apart, and I have noticed I give verdicts on them without being able to state the principle behind them. I would like your help extracting that principle, or deciding that there isn't one.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. A friend with a dog phobia knows perfectly well that the neighbor's retriever is harmless and will tell you so, yet is terrified of it: plainly fear, even though the judgment that the dog is dangerous is absent — indeed absent while she sincerely denies it. A firefighter I know will tell you the burning building is dangerous and act accordingly, but says he feels nothing during entry, just runs the checklist; I am inclined to say he is not afraid, that he has a belief about danger without the emotion. My sister's therapist has her reappraise situations, and when her judgment that a colleague's remark was a slight changes to the judgment that it was clumsy, her anger genuinely lifts — which suggests the emotion is the judgment. But a friend on beta-blockers who gives a speech with a calm body still counts as nervous, so bodily arousal cannot be necessary. And someone in the grip of a panic attack feels full-blown terror while sincerely believing nothing is wrong; I want to say that is fear-like but somehow not about anything. Two more: I can be genuinely angry at a villain in a novel, fully felt, and yet it seems not to count as real anger, because I do not judge that any wrong was done; while a colleague admits to being envious of a rival she sincerely believes deserved the promotion, and that envy does seem real to me, which sits oddly with the novel case. Finally my own case: since the medication I keep the appraisals and lose the pang, and I keep going back and forth on whether I still have the emotions or only their cognitive shadows.

So here is what I want. Take the three familiar candidate theories — emotions as evaluative judgments, emotions as feelings or bodily disturbances, and emotions as perceptions of value, the way a perceptual experience can present something as red — and tell me which, if any, delivers all of my verdicts. If none does, say which verdict has to go and why it is that one rather than another; I would rather give up a verdict than accept a theory gerrymandered to save them all, but I want to see the cost stated honestly. Then apply the winning account to my own case: on that account, do I still count as grieving about my father, or has the medication taken the emotions and left the beliefs? And tell me what the account predicts on a case I have not mentioned — someone who is proud of an achievement while sincerely believing it was mostly luck — so I can check the prediction against my own reaction and see if the theory is actually capturing my practice or merely re-describing it.
