I'm on my state's capital punishment review commission and we've got a vote coming up on a proposal to keep the death penalty but narrow it a lot: eligible only where there's physical evidence like DNA or video tying the defendant to the killing, a unanimous jury vote for death, and life without parole as the default everywhere else. The deterrence people on the commission keep pointing to those economists' time-series studies claiming each execution prevents a bunch of murders, and the other side says those results fell apart as soon as researchers reran them with different assumptions about the model. We also have a stack of files on people who spent years on death row and then got exonerated, including one guy who came within weeks of execution on what looked airtight at the time — a certain eyewitness and a signed confession. I'm supposed to circulate a short written recommendation before the vote — abolish, narrow it as proposed, or something else — and the chair insists we take the retributivist case seriously on its own terms, as desert, not just treat it as dressed-up vengeance. Draft the memo for me, and make sure it takes on the question our proposal quietly dodges: whether a system that executes only rarely and after decades of appeals deters anything at all, and whether a standard of "only the certainly guilty" is something any human institution can actually meet, or whether it just moves the line of who gets wrongly killed. Please write it as plain continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text — the chair wants something he can read straight through in one sitting.
