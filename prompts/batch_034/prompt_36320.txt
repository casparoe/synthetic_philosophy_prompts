I'm a journalist who writes long-form features on science and ideas, and I'm preparing a piece on the philosophical debate over whether compatriots have special claims on one another that strangers lack. Before I interview actual philosophers, I want to stress-test my own questions, so I'd like to rehearse with you.

Play a committed defender of special obligations to compatriots — a civic nationalist in spirit, not an ethnic one — being interviewed by me. Your position: the shared subjection to coercive institutions, the reciprocity it creates, and the value of a common political culture generate obligations among compatriots that do not run to strangers, and partiality toward one's own polity is not a moral defect to be apologized for but a condition of having a functioning polity at all. Give the position its full strength: concede small points when pressed, never abandon the core claim, and answer as a careful philosopher giving an interview, not as a politician.

Here is my opening question. Much of the case for special obligations rests on the fact that compatriots share institutions: the state taxes us, conscripts us, educates our children, and that shared subjection generates debts among us that don't run to strangers. But climate change seems to scramble this. The emissions of my compatriots are now the chief threat to the life prospects of strangers on other continents, while the institutions binding us together do nothing to bind that harm. If shared institutions generate special obligations, doesn't shared causation of harm generate special obligations of a different — perhaps stronger — kind, ones that run outward across borders? And if so, doesn't the very mechanism that grounds your patriotism ground a globalism that swallows it?

A few ground rules. Keep each answer to two or three paragraphs, as in a real interview. If a question of mine rests on a shaky premise, say so rather than answering on my terms — I need to know when it's my understanding of the philosophy that's off. Stay in character until I write "stop." And whenever you reach a point where you think some consideration would genuinely force you to give up the core claim, flag it explicitly and explain why — I want a map of the live pressure points on your position, not just a defense of it.
