I am a human-factors engineer who spent two decades working on cockpit and process-control automation and now advises hospital IT, so my native vocabulary is the vocabulary of automation research: levels of automation in the style of Sheridan and Verplank, supervisory control, human-in-the-loop versus human-on-the-loop, out-of-the-loop performance problems, mode confusion, workload management, shared authority, and the ironies of automation in Bainbridge's sense. I have followed the recent debate between "tool AI" and "agent AI" only from the sidelines, and my working suspicion is that my field mapped this terrain forty years ago: a tool is just a system at the low end of the automation hierarchy, where the human retains the decision and much of the execution, while an agent is a high-level delegated system that selects and executes actions under nominal human supervision. Restate the tool-versus-agent debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without flattering my suspicion, covering at least the following positions: that agents are more useful because their value compounds over long task horizons; that tools are safer because errors stay legible, reviewable, and localized, and authority remains with the operator; the worry that agents pursue goals misaligned with the operator's intent over exactly those long horizons; and the argument that the distinction collapses because even a modest tool shapes the operator's choices enough that it effectively wields authority anyway.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Where does my vocabulary handle the debate natively -- for instance, is the compounding-horizon claim just the old point that full automation pays off when the human's monitoring load exceeds the benefit of retained control? Where does it translate only with distortion -- does "supervision" mean the same thing when the delegated system operates over an open-ended task domain rather than a bounded one like managing a throttle, and does "mode confusion" capture what happens when the operator no longer understands what the system is even for? And what has no expression at all in my vocabulary -- I notice we have nothing to say about a system that represents its own goals, negotiates over them, or acts where there is no fixed task specification and no criterion of correct performance that a test engineer could operationalize. Conclude by taking a side: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, something automation research never needed to say and must now learn to say, or a sign that the tool/agent debate is less substantive than it looks -- a dispute my field would dissolve into one continuum with tunable parameters? I want the translation to be honest, so do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present them as the content of the AI-safety people's claims.
