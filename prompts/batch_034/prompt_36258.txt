I'm putting together a philosophy of film unit built around Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, and I want to screen The Truman Show as the main text instead of yet another play. Read the film through the Letter. Rousseau's complaints were that theater gives us pity without action (you cry at the tragedy and then go home having done nothing), that comedy like Molière's Misanthrope teaches us to laugh at sincerity itself, and that his positive alternative for Geneva was public festivals where the spectators are themselves the performers. Now map that onto Seahaven: Truman is the only person in town who isn't acting, which makes him weirdly close to Rousseau's ideal citizen while being its exact inversion — the whole town is a fête staged at him rather than with him, and the audience's sympathy for him is precisely the cheap spectator-pity Rousseau hated. The ending, where the viewers cheer for Truman and then immediately flip the channel to see what else is on — is that Rousseau vindicated about spectators two centuries early? Where does the reading break down, though? Truman is real in a way stage characters aren't, so maybe the audience's pity for him isn't simulated passion the way Rousseau worried about. And does the film's critique of spectacle reach Rousseau's own festivals too, or does it leave them standing? I want a reading that treats the Letter as actual philosophy rather than just grumpy cultural criticism, and that ends by saying whether the film adds anything to Rousseau's argument that argument alone couldn't, or whether its just a glossy illustration of it.
