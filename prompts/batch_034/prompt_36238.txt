Two seniors on my school's debate team, Marcus and Elena, got into a long argument after practice about whether you can ever derive an 'ought' from an 'is,' and since I was the only one who stayed neutral, they asked me to write the exchange down and get an outside verdict. It started because our coach keeps insisting that a good debater can move smoothly from facts to values without any dubious leap. Marcus opened by invoking Hume: no matter how many purely descriptive premises you stack up — facts about suffering, about what people desire, about how cooperative behavior evolved — no normative conclusion follows unless you smuggle in at least one normative premise, and anyone who claims to have grounded ethics in science alone is just hiding their oughts. Elena replied that this is a trivial point about logic, not about ethics in particular: no conclusion about Tuesdays follows from premises that never mention Tuesday either, but that doesn't make Tuesdays mysterious; and bridge principles like 'if an action causes needless suffering and has no countervailing upside, that's a reason not to do it' are as safe to rely on as anything, so demanding a further justification for them is like demanding a justification for logic itself.

Marcus then shifted ground to Moore, saying the deeper problem is that 'good' can't be defined in natural terms at all: for any proposed definition — that good means pleasurable, or that good means whatever evolution happened to select for — it remains an open question whether that thing is really good, which shows the definition failed. Elena answered that the open question argument proves too much: before chemistry worked out the details, it was an open question whether water is H2O, but that didn't mean water and H2O were two different things, so a definition's not being obvious on reflection doesn't show that the property it picks out is non-natural. Marcus's final move was that moral concepts are unlike natural-kind concepts in being essentially action-guiding: 'water' can sit inert in your head as a piece of knowledge, but grasping that something is cruel is supposed to motivate you against it, and no merely descriptive state has that built-in connection to motivation, so the reduction fails after all. Elena shot back that motivation is just another natural fact about desires and brain states, so there's nothing left over for morality to be.

They've now asked me to referee, and I honestly can't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each step, say whether it is basically right, basically mistaken, or right-but-missing-something, and justify each rating. Also flag anything both of them are overlooking — for example, it seems to me they may slide around among different readings of 'you can't get an ought from an is' (a point about logical entailment, about the meaning of moral words, or about what moral properties are made of), and I suspect which reading is in play changes who wins each round. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and whether either of them has a clear edge or whether the dispute mostly dissolves once the readings are separated.
