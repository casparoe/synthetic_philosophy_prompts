I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and I've ended up as an industry representative on the board of trustees of our state's public university system — I got asked after chairing a curriculum advisory panel, mostly because I'd been vocal about tech-hiring pipelines. Our legislature is now debating a bill that would phase out tuition entirely at all public universities over five years, replacing the lost revenue with a mix of general funds and a surtax on high earners, explicitly modeled on how Germany and the Nordic countries handle it. The board has been asked to submit a formal written opinion before the committee markup, and we are badly split. One faction argues that tuition is a barrier at the door and that education, like roads or basic healthcare, is the kind of thing a society should just pay for collectively; a second faction counters that "free" tuition is a regressive transfer, since university graduates skew toward higher-income families, so the janitor subsidizes the future consultant, and that income-contingent loans and targeted debt relief — the direction the US and UK have taken — help the students who actually need it without that problem; a third group mostly worries about what happens to our budget autonomy and to student effort if nobody pays anything upfront.

I'd like you to act as a senior policy advisor with serious grounding in both political philosophy and the economics of education. Before drafting anything, please think through the underlying questions carefully: what education actually is as a good (private investment, public good, some mixture), what the different financing schemes implicitly assume about fairness between students, graduates, and non-graduate taxpayers, and how the German/Nordic model and the tuition-plus-loans model each handle those questions. Please brainstorm the key considerations first and briefly set out that brainstorming in your answer, so I can see the reasoning rather than just a conclusion.

Then draft the memo itself, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, written so a board of non-specialists can follow it — no jargon without a plain-language explanation, and no bibliography or citations, since the trustees will argue with each other, not with sources. It should open with a clear and concrete recommendation (endorse the bill, oppose it, or endorse it with specific conditions), justify that recommendation, and — this matters to me — give an honest and serious treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, not a strawman of it. Assume the board will adopt whatever we submit nearly verbatim, so the reasoning has to hold up under scrutiny from people who disagree with us.
