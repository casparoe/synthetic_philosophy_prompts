Student's answer: "The Dutch book argument begins by interpreting an agent's degree of belief in a proposition as the price at which she would buy or sell a bet that pays one unit if the proposition is true and nothing otherwise; a credence of 0.7 in P means she would pay up to 0.7 units for such a bet. The Dutch book theorem, proved by de Finetti in the 1930s and anticipated by Ramsey, states that an agent's credences violate the probability axioms if and only if a bookie can offer her a finite set of bets, each individually acceptable at her stated prices, whose net payoff is a loss in every possible world; so coherence and immunity to a sure-loss book are two sides of the same coin. The argument for probabilism is then straightforward: a rational agent should not be vulnerable to a guaranteed loss, so her credences must satisfy the axioms. And since Kolmogorov's axioms include countable additivity, the argument likewise rules out merely finitely additive credences. Two assumptions matter here. First, the agent must be willing to accept bets on either side at her fair price, which effectively assumes her utility is linear in the stakes, so the argument is safest when restricted to small stakes. Second, the bookie must know the agent's credences, which is unrealistic, though this does not affect the logic of the theorem, which concerns vulnerability rather than actual exploitation. Some critics object that the argument is merely pragmatic: it shows that incoherent bettors lose money, not that their beliefs are false. But this understates the result — the guaranteed loss shows that an incoherent agent must hold at least one false belief, since a set of bets that loses in every possible world could not be constructed from credences that are all accurate. In any case, the argument is best understood as establishing probabilistic coherence as a requirement of practical rationality, whatever its ultimate status as an epistemic norm."
