I'm taking a decision theory course this semester and we just covered the Ellsberg stuff, and last night two people in my study group got into a long fight about what the "paradox" even is, and I couldnt referee it even though I tried. The setup they were fighting over: one urn has exactly 50 red and 50 blue balls, another has 100 red/blue balls in completely unknown proportions, you draw one ball and win $100 if you called the color right. (1) Dan said subjects who prefer the known urn are just risk averse, like preferring a sure $50 over a coin flip — nothing new for expected utility. Maya said that can't be right: someone who bets on red from the known urn AND on blue from the known urn is acting like each color is less than 50% likely in the mystery urn, and no single set of probabilities says both. (2) Dan said fine, then it's distrust — if whoever filled the mystery urn could have rigged it against you, or even just had the opportunity to, avoiding it is straight expected utility with a suspicious prior, so still no paradox. Maya said people still avoid the mystery urn when it was filled in front of them by a demonstrably fair machine, so distrust can't be the whole story. Dan said nobody fully believes the machine, and in real life there's always some chance of rigging, so the lab result doesn't generalize anyway.

(3) Then it got weird. Dan invented a case: suppose the mystery urn's composition is set by first drawing a slip from a hat containing slips numbered 0 through 100, and that number is how many red balls go in. So you have exact second-order probabilities over every possible composition, and by total probability red comes out to exactly 50% on any coherent calculus. Dan predicted people would STILL prefer the literal 50/50 urn over the hat-determined one, which he said shows people aren't averse to unknown probabilities at all — they're violating the probability calculus itself, treating "50% by construction" and "50% by a chance process over mixtures" as different numbers, which he thought was just a flat-out inconsistency. Maya predicted the opposite: people would be totally fine with the hat-drawn urn, because what they hate is a mix with no story behind it, and she thought that preference was actually defensible — her line was that a probability attached to a described mechanism is worth more to you than a number you're merely entitled to, and expected utility theory just has no room for that distinction, which is the real hole. Neither of them knew of any actual experiment on the hat version, they were just arguing about what people would do and what it would mean.

(4) Dan's last move was that "ambiguity aversion" isn't one phenomenon anyway — the subjects are probably a mix of the risk averse, the paranoid, and Maya's mechanism-lovers, and the paradox only exists if you assume one clean type of reasoner; the theory was never refuted, just applied to noisy people. Maya said no, the paradox was never about actual subjects — it's that the pattern of preferring the known urn at either color is inconsistent with ANY assignment of probabilities (plus a utility for money), even for an ideally coherent agent, so expected utility theory as a normative theory has a genuine hole in it, whatever the undergrads in the lab happen to be doing.
