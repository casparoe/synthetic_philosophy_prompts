I teach a seminar on the historiography of the Holocaust, and I want a hypothetical case study that does something no real archive managed to do for the Browning–Goldhagen debate: actually discriminate between the two explanations. Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) were built largely on the same body of testimony from Reserve Police Battalion 101, and part of why the debate stalled is that the surviving evidence underdetermines the two accounts — situational factors (obedience to authority, conformity, deference, the ratchet of escalating tasks) and ideological ones (eliminationist antisemitism as a deep feature of German political culture) can each be stretched to cover nearly everything the men did.

So construct for me a fictional but realistic case: a German Order Police battalion in an occupied territory, described with the kind of material an archive could plausibly contain, engineered so that the two accounts make different predictions, and the stipulated material splits the difference — some of it favouring Browning, some favouring Goldhagen.

Constraints:

- Realism. Stick to mechanisms that actually operated: conscription of men too old for front-line service, commanders offering exemption from killing duty, the progression from guarding and deportations to massacres, heavy drinking, the use of local auxiliaries and the Hiwi camps, the absence of serious punishment for refusal. No invented psychology experiments embedded in the story, and no omniscient access to the men's motives — the evidence must be behavioural or documentary, not confessional, except where postwar testimony would exist.
- Discriminability. The data you stipulate must be the sort an archive could hold: refusal and exemption rates, who volunteered for killing duties and when, how the men treated Jewish versus non-Jewish victims in otherwise parallel situations, what they wrote home, what they told interrogators decades later. Each fact should follow from how such units actually behaved rather than being reverse-engineered to force a verdict. If any fact you need would strain plausibility, say so rather than smuggling it in.

After the scenario, please do four things. First, walk through what each account predicts and show exactly where the stipulated material supports it and where it cuts against it. Second, identify which single feature of the case is doing the most work in each direction, and say what happens to the discriminating power if that feature is removed. Third, give the strongest attack that a committed Browning-defender and a committed Goldhagen-defender would each mount on your case — that is, how each would explain away the half of the evidence that tells against him. Fourth, tell me what would have to change in the stipulated material for the case to come out cleanly for one side, and whether that change would still be realistic.

The purpose of the exercise is to teach students what genuinely decisive evidence would look like in the situation-versus-culture debate over perpetrators — so a case that stipulates the verdict rather than generating it from the theories fails, and so does one resting on facts no archive could hold. Please write in British English. Finally, be candid about the fragility of your own construction: tell me which stipulated fact a working historian would be most likely to challenge, and what consideration would lead you to redesign the case altogether.
