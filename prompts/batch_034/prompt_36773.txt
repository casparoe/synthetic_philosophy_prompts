Then explain Aquinas's response to the underlying question of whether an omnipotent being can do the logically impossible, and explain George Mavrodes's treatment of the stone paradox. Finally, assess whether these responses succeed in showing that omnipotence is a coherent notion."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The paradox of the stone asks whether an omnipotent being could create a stone so heavy that it could not lift it. Either it can or it cannot. If it can, then there is something it cannot do, namely lift the stone, so it is not omnipotent. If it cannot, then there is something it cannot do, namely create the stone, so it is not omnipotent. Either way, omnipotence is incoherent.

Aquinas responds that when terms are combined contradictorily, as in 'square circle,' they fail to describe any possible object at all. God's not being able to draw a square circle is therefore not a limitation on his power, since there is no genuine task being described. Aquinas, like Descartes, was willing to extend divine power even over the laws of logic itself: since God freely created even the eternal truths of mathematics, he could make two plus two equal five if he so willed. Thus for Aquinas the paradox never gets going, since God could create the unliftable stone and then lift it anyway.

Mavrodes develops this line of response. He argues that the description 'a stone its omnipotent creator cannot lift' is self-contradictory, so creating one is not a genuine task but a pseudo-task, and inability to perform a pseudo-task is no defect in power. But Mavrodes goes further than this: he claims that even if we set pseudo-tasks aside, God could create such a stone and then lift it anyway, on the ground that a being capable of performing one task with a self-contradictory description — creating the problematic stone in the first place — could equally well perform another.

One might worry that restricting omnipotence to the logically possible is a weakening of the concept, but since this restriction is what Aquinas meant all along, the paradox is fully dissolved: omnipotence, understood as the power to do anything at all, has thereby been shown to be coherent. And it is worth noting that even on Aquinas's own terms God cannot lie — Scripture itself says it is impossible for God to lie — so even Aquinas concedes that God has a genuine inability, which is exactly what the atheologian set out to establish."
