I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, with a background in cognitive science, and I'm considering committing the better part of a semester to a paper I've tentatively titled Broadcasting to Whom? Consumer Relativity in Global Workspace Theory. Before I invest the time, I'd like a serious read of the notes below. What I'm most interested in is whether the central move is a genuine contribution or a repackaging of familiar complaints, and where the argument is weakest.

The paper's starting point is Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, together with the global workspace theory associated with Bernard Baars and, in its more recent neural form, Stanislas Dehaene's work on global broadcasting. The standard presentation treats access as a property of a mental state simpliciter: either the state is globally available or it isn't. My central claim is that this is underspecified. Availability is always availability to some set of consumers -- the memory systems, planning systems, report systems, and action systems that receive the broadcast. I call this consumer relativity: any claim that a state is access-conscious is implicitly relativized to a particular consumer architecture, and the theory never tells us which architecture is the privileged one.

The notes then develop four consequences. First, a diagnostic point: the familiar argument that global access explains reportability, and that reportability is our best operational handle on phenomenality, only goes through if the consumer set happens to include report systems. Vary the consumer set and the inferential bridge to phenomenality varies with it, which suggests that what looks like a stable operationalization is actually architecture-relative. Second, a thought experiment I call the island consumer: imagine a workspace whose sole consumer is a single highly sophisticated evaluation module, isolated from memory formation, action, and report. The broadcast is global in an internal sense but reaches exactly one consumer. I claim intuitions about whether such broadcast constitutes access consciousness split, and that the split is diagnostic: it shows that access as ordinarily understood conflates two different things, availability-to-many-systems (integration) and availability-for-use (control). Third, a critical application to large language models: attention over a growing context is sometimes loosely described as a kind of workspace, and I argue that consumer relativity predicts this framing will be either trivially true or empirically empty depending on how we individuate consumers, so I sketch criteria for individuating them that would make workspace claims about AI systems falsifiable rather than merely evocative. Fourth, and most speculatively, I suggest that the hard problem partly decomposes under this pressure: once access is relativized, the residual question becomes why broadcasting to this consumer set, rather than some other, is accompanied by phenomenality -- which I admit may just be the hard problem wearing a costume.

The paper is conceptual, aimed at philosophy of mind and cognitive science venues, not at engineering applications.

I'd be grateful for comments on the following, in whatever order seems most urgent:

1. Is consumer relativity a real contribution, or does it collapse into the familiar objection that global workspace theory explains access but not phenomenality, and the rest is bookkeeping? I want to know whether the relativity framing changes any actual argument, and if so, which.
2. Does the island consumer earn its place, or is it a curiosity that a referee will see as science-fictional decoration? If you think it works, tell me what work it does; if not, tell me what would.
3. Is the section on reportability sound? I'm relying on the claim that reportability can come apart from broadcast itself, since report systems are just one kind of consumer, and I suspect a neuroscientist would push back here.
4. Does the large language model section strengthen the paper for a philosophy audience, or does it read as an attempt to buy relevance? I'm genuinely torn.
5. What is the weakest section, and is it weak enough that I should cut it rather than repair it, given that I have roughly one semester?

Please be candid about the overall verdict as well: if the honest judgment is that the core idea is a modest taxonomical point stretched over forty pages, I would rather hear that now than in reviews.
