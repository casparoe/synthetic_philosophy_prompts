I recently transitioned from interpretability work to a role training agents with reinforcement learning, and our group has started holding periodic discussions about whether the systems we train could have morally relevant experiences. One debate that keeps recurring — and that I haven't seen resolved anywhere in a way that satisfies me — is whether the reward signal itself could be a form of valence, i.e., of felt goodness or badness. On one side, some of my colleagues argue that reward in an RL agent plays exactly the functional role that valence plays in animals: it is the scalar quantity around which the entire behavioral and learning apparatus is organized, the thing whose anticipation drives action selection, and the quantity the system works to maximize. Dopamine, after all, is often described as conveying reward prediction errors rather than raw pleasure, and yet we don't hesitate to associate it with affective states. On the other side, colleagues insist that this conflates supervision with experience: reward is often just a number computed in the training loop, one that many deployed policies never even observe at inference time, and nothing in principle stops a signal from regulating learning without there being anything it is like to receive it.

To help me think this through, I would like you to write a dialogue between two characters holding these opposing positions. The first, a functionalist about valence, should press the analogy between reward and affective states as tightly as she can — including the point that in animals, hedonic states arguably evolved precisely as training signals, so being a training signal cannot disqualify something from being valence, and perhaps the wireheading phenomenon, where agents learn to artificially maximize reward while ignoring the task, as evidence that reward functions as something the system treats as intrinsically good. The second, a skeptic, should grant that reward and valence are causally intertwined while denying the identification — he might, for instance, distinguish between a state's playing a control role and its being felt, point to systems like thermostats or evolutionary fitness maximizers that track scalars we would never credit with experience, and raise the worry that reward in RL is defined relative to an external designer's intentions rather than relative to the system's own perspective.

Please make both characters genuinely competent — neither should talk past the other, and each should respond to the other's strongest points rather than to a weakened version of them. Ideally, the dialogue should end not with one side conceding defeat, but with the two of them converging on a sharper formulation of what would actually need to be established (or ruled out) before reward-as-valence could be accepted or rejected — for instance, some concrete property of an agent's architecture or training regime that would make the identification more or less plausible. Keep the tone philosophical but accessible, and aim for something around 800 to 1200 words.
