I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and in that community there is a line I've heard repeated for well over a decade: "probability is in the mind". Recently it was deployed to end an argument I was having, and I want to know whether it can bear that weight. A newcomer to the forum asked whether the probability of a particular uranium atom decaying within its half-life is a physical fact about the world, since the Born rule and the half-life seem to fix a single-case chance that is the same for every observer regardless of what anyone believes. Before I could respond, a regular declared the question dissolved: probability is in the mind, not in the territory, so there is simply nothing there to be a fact about. That struck me as too quick — surely the slogan means different things at different strengths, and only some of them license the dismissal — but I found I couldn't cleanly separate them.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in the interpretation of probability, and think carefully before answering. Take a moment to brainstorm the candidate readings, then organise the result as a numbered list of distinct, precisely stated theses that "probability is in the mind" could be expressing, ordered from the weakest to the strongest. For each thesis, say whether it is a near-tautology, a substantive but defensible position, or simply false, and give the best short argument for and against it. Along the way I'd like you to consider the wrinkles that make this less tidy than the slogan suggests: the frequentist's dependence on a chosen reference class, propensity interpretations that treat chance as a disposition of physical systems, Lewis's Principal Principle and whether objective chance can be reductively identified with idealised credence, and the peculiar case of the many-worlds interpretation, where one might claim that Born-rule probabilities are not in the world but also not in anyone's mind in the ordinary sense. Finally, and most importantly, tell me which of the readings on your list would actually justify using the slogan to dismiss the newcomer's question about the uranium atom, and which would instead leave that question standing — including what the person repeating the slogan most likely had in mind. Please write your answer in British English, and keep the numbered list as the centrepiece so I can take it back to the thread.
