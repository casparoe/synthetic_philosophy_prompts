Analyze the My Lai massacre of March 1968 as a test case for the Browning--Goldhagen debate about what makes perpetrators kill. I don't want the standard framing where both get summarized and set aside; I want the actual mapping. So: treat Browning's "ordinary men" machinery -- situational pressure, conformity, obedience, the diffusion of responsibility, and his argument from Battalion 101 that conformity trumped ideology -- and Goldhagen's counter, that behind the choices lay a deeply rooted eliminationist culture that made killing seem an acceptable thing to do, and ask which one better explains what Charlie Company did in those few hours. The obvious wrinkle: this is American soldiers in Vietnam, so there's no centuries-old antisemitism to point to -- but there was dehumanizing language for Vietnamese, free-fire zones, the cumulative trauma and grief after the unit took heavy casualties in the weeks before, and a culture that treated Vietnamese lives as cheap. Does that count as Goldhagen-esque eliminationism-lite, or does it show his framework only really works retrofitted onto one history, while Browning's situationist story travels? And what do you make of the fact that some men refused or shot wide without any apparent special virtue -- Calley's own men, and Hugh Thompson's helicopter crew -- does that support Browning's "character matters at the margin" concession, or does it blow up the whole situationist story? Give me a committed verdict at the end: which framework explains My Lai better and why, not a both-sides-have-merits hedge. Also be honest about what the case can't settle -- e.g. what the Peers Commission's cover-up dynamics do or don't tell us about the massacre itself.
