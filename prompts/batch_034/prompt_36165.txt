I'm a novelist at work on a book set at Nuremberg between 1945 and 1946, narrated from the point of view of a German defense counsel — a man who took the job not out of sympathy for the defendants but out of a lawyer's conviction that even the worst men on earth deserve the forms of a trial. My problem is one of interiority: I need to know what it was possible for such a man to think from inside that courtroom, and that turns on a question I can't answer myself as a matter of legal philosophy. Was the law he was practicing law, or was it the victors' will wearing law's costume?

Analyze the trials, Nuremberg above all but with Tokyo in view, through the lens of retroactive law and victors' justice — and run the analysis in both directions, as I set it out below. By way of mapping, the things I take to be established and want the analysis disciplined by: the London Charter of 1945 defined the counts — crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity — after the conduct it prosecuted; the tribunal's own answer to the retroactivity charge was that the Charter did not create the law but recorded it, aggressive war having already been outlawed (the Pact of Paris being the usual exhibit); and the victors declined to prosecute conduct of their own that was continuous with what they tried, unrestricted submarine warfare being the clearest case, with Tokyo adding openly dissenting judges willing to say in opinions what at Nuremberg went unsaid.

First direction, theory to episode. Where does the victors' justice analysis genuinely explain what happened in that courtroom, and where does it overreach? I want a careful accounting: which features of the trial the analysis captures (the asymmetry of who sits in judgment, the law arriving after the fact, the refusal of tu quoque arguments), and which it misdescribes or cannot see at all — the procedural protections that were real rather than decorative, the defendants acquitted on some counts, the defense's genuine, if doomed, legal arguments. Tell me where the theory is simply silent, rather than forcing it to pronounce.

Second direction, episode to theory. What does the conduct of the trials show about the theory of retroactive law? Does Nuremberg confirm the strict view that law defined after the conduct is not law, or does it suggest the strict view was built for cases like parking fines and never anticipated a case where the alternative to retroactive law was no law at all? Where, honestly, does the tribunal's recorded-not-created argument work, and where does it strain credulity?

Then return to my character. Given your analysis, tell me what is available to him from inside: can a defense counsel in 1945-46, arguing in good faith, experience the proceeding as a fair trial rather than as power dressed as law — or is that experience available to him only through an illusion the novelist would be dishonest to author? If the honest answer is that he must feel something in between, tell me what the in-between feels like, because that is the sentence I am actually trying to write. I'd rather have the analysis conclude that the question has no answer than have either side of it settled for me by an argument that doesn't hold.
