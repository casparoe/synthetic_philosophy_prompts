When Franz Kafka died in 1924, he left behind an instruction to his friend and literary executor Max Brod: that his diaries, manuscripts, correspondence, and everything he had written be burned unread. Brod, who had privately told Kafka years earlier that he would never do any such thing, refused. He instead edited the unfinished novels, decided where they broke off, and published The Trial, The Castle, and Amerika over the following three years, thereby making Kafka a central figure of twentieth-century literature. The episode has a further wrinkle: Brod's editorial choices were themselves interpretive, fixing the shape and sometimes the ordering of texts whose author was dead and had wanted them destroyed.

Analyse this episode through the lens of the debate over authorial intention and textual meaning. Does Brod's refusal only make sense on an anti-intentionalist view, on which a work's meaning and value belong to readers and to literary history rather than to the author's wishes — or can an intentionalist defend him, perhaps by arguing that Kafka's request was itself ambivalent (he chose as executor the one man he knew would disobey), so that fidelity to his deeper intentions required disobedience to his stated one? Where does each theory go silent here, and where does it misdescribe what Brod actually faced — an ethical question about a friend's dying request as much as a hermeneutic one about what texts mean? Finally, say what the episode shows about the theories themselves: whether posthumous publication against an author's explicit wishes is a confirmation of the death of the author, an anomaly the theories never anticipated, or simply a case in which questions of meaning and questions of obligation come apart. Answer in at most 450 words.
