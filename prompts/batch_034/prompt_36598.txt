I run a small reading group on experimental philosophy that has spent the better part of a year on the Knobe effect, and we have hit what I can only describe as a wall. Everyone accepts the basic findings: in the harm-help asymmetry, the chairman who knowingly harms the environment for the sake of profit is judged to have harmed it intentionally, while the chairman who knowingly helps the environment is judged not to have helped it intentionally; the asymmetry replicates across cultures, formats, and even young children. What we cannot make progress on is what the finding means. One camp holds that the ordinary concept of intentional action just is normatively loaded, so that moral considerations are partly constitutive of the concept's application conditions. A second camp treats the effect as contamination: blame processing leaks into judgments about intentionality, whether through pragmatic inference, through an implicit assumption that the agent had a justifiable excuse, or through people importing information about the agent's "deep self" and what he genuinely cared about. A third camp, following Machery's skeptical line, doubts there is a single coherent folk concept here at all and thinks the survey method is measuring something other than the concept. Each camp keeps reinterpreting the other side's data rather than moving, and every new variant experiment seems to get absorbed rather than to discriminate.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really a disagreement about intentionality at all. It seems to me that the first camp is answering a question about the semantics and application conditions of the word in ordinary language, the second camp is answering a question about cognitive architecture — which psychological systems generate the verdicts and in what order — and the third camp is raising a methodological worry about what a forced-choice questionnaire response is evidence for. If that is right, the debate is structured more like the kind of case where two disciplines share a vocabulary while asking different questions, and the "stalemate" is an artifact of the shared word 'intentionally' making the positions look like rivals when they are compatible descriptions at different levels. But I am not confident of this, and there is an alternative diagnosis I take seriously: the camps genuinely disagree about one empirical question — whether the norm-sensitivity is part of the competence or a performance effect — and the impasse persists only because nobody has found evidence of the right kind, such as a population or a condition in which blame and intentionality judgments dissociate cleanly.

So here is what I want from you, and I want diagnosis, not adjudication and not a survey of everyone who has written on this. First, assess my hypothesis: is the Knobe-effect dispute best understood as two or three different questions running under one heading, or is it a substantive empirical dispute that has merely resisted the evidence available so far? Be concrete about what each camp actually claims, including how the norm-conceptualists handle the embarrassment that similar norm effects show up in judgments of causation, happiness, and knowledge, which they cite as showing the folk concepts are moralized across the board while their critics call it overgeneralization. Second, tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress — a particular kind of experiment, a conceptual distinction between the concept and the capacity, a stipulative reform of the word for philosophical purposes — and be honest about whether some part of the dispute is likely to remain intractable, and why.
