I've been asked to circulate a position statement on distributive justice ahead of a workshop, and before I do I'd like a consistency audit of what I've ended up committed to. Here is the package as it stands:

(1) I am a luck egalitarian in the strong sense: the master aim of distributive justice is to neutralize unchosen luck, so that whatever inequality remains traces to choices for which the person is genuinely responsible.

(2) I draw the standard line between option luck and brute luck. The outcomes of gambles one freely undertook — including the decision to forgo insurance against known hazards — belong to the gambler. This ambition-sensitivity is not a regrettable concession the view has to make; it is the point of the view.

(3) I hold that the state should compensate people for expensive disabilities but not for expensive tastes the person identifies with. Someone whose life is costlier because of how they were born is owed something; someone whose life is costlier because they developed a consuming passion for mountaineering is owed nothing. That contrast seems to me exactly right.

(4) I also hold, on what I take to be firm empirical grounds, that most adult preferences — tastes, ambitions, and especially attitudes toward risk — are artifacts of socialization and adaptive preference formation. People do not, in the ordinary case, choose what they want or how prudent they are, and in badly ordered societies the preferences of the worst-off are the most distorted of all.

(5) I accept the envy test as the specification of what it means for a distribution of resources to be equal: the distribution is equal when no one prefers anyone else's bundle to their own.

(6) I reject sufficiency as a fundamental distributive value. Justice is comparative; thresholds and floors are at most derivative policies, justified, when they are, by their tendency to produce a more equal distribution.

(7) I think it is a mistake for egalitarians to attack incentive payments to the talented. Within a society governed by something like the difference principle, if the talented will produce the best outcome for the least advantaged only when they are paid more, the extra pay is just.

(8) I do not think justice requires the state to correct for the fact that the choices of adequately informed adults are predictably distorted by present bias, optimism bias, and the like. Requiring genuine voluntariness as a condition for letting outcomes stand is a standard no administrative scheme could apply, and it is the wrong standard anyway.

(9) I believe the fundamental metric of egalitarian justice is resources, not opportunity for welfare, and part of what I like about the resources view is precisely that it refuses to make people's subjective satisfaction the measure of what they are owed.

(10) Finally, I am confident that luck egalitarianism, properly stated, is not refuted by the harshness objection. A society that lets the imprudent face destitution might be failing in some virtue, but the failure is not a failure of distributive justice.

Some of these I have argued for at length; others I inherited and never checked against each other. What I want from you is an audit, not a survey: identify which pairs or larger groupings among these are genuinely inconsistent, which merely look unstable, and which are merely awkward, and rank the problems by severity. For each genuine problem, say exactly which commitment would have to give, what would remain of my position afterward, and what the revision costs in intuitive bite. Where a tension only appears because I have misdescribed a position or drawn a distinction in a nonstandard way, say that instead of manufacturing a crisis. Please answer in American English.
