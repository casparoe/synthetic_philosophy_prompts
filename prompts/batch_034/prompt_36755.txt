I've been working through social choice theory on my own, and I've constructed an argument which, if it worked, would show that open problems in mathematics should be settled by global referendum. I don't believe the conclusion, but every premise still looks fine to me. Please tell me which step to reject, or which inference is invalid, and what rejecting it costs — because, as you'll see, the costs I can see are ones I'm reluctant to pay.

Background: Condorcet's jury theorem says that if a large number of voters each vote sincerely on a binary question, each is more likely to vote correctly than incorrectly (competence, p > 0.5), and their votes are independent, then the probability that the majority is correct approaches certainty as the electorate grows — and approaches it quickly, so that even voters who are barely better than random (p = 0.501) suffice, given a large enough electorate. The theorem demands no understanding, no deliberation, and no expertise: only competence and independence.

The argument:

(1) By the jury theorem, for any binary question whatever, there is a finite electorate size N such that a majority vote among N sincere, independent, individually-better-than-random voters answers that question with probability as close to 1 as we like.

(2) Competence is nothing but a correlation with the truth, and a voter can have it without understanding the question. Someone who always votes for whichever side the mainstream experts endorse is, across the run of questions on which mainstream experts have turned out to be right more often than wrong, right more often than not; so is someone who always votes for the simpler-sounding theory. Each is a tiny-but-positive correlation, which is all the theorem needs.

(3) So for almost any binary question, we can assemble a vast electorate — every adult on Earth, say — each member of which is at least slightly better than random on it, since nearly everyone commands some heuristic with a slightly positive track record.

(4) Independence is a feature of procedure, not of statistics: voters who decide alone, behind a secret ballot, with no campaigning, no deliberation, and no knowledge of how the others are voting, are independent in every sense a fair election requires. A well-run referendum satisfies this as fully as anything ever has.

(5) There are some five billion adults, which is far more voters than the theorem needs, even at p barely above 0.5, to drive the probability of a wrong majority down below one in a billion.

(6) So a global secret referendum, held without campaigning, on the question "Is the Riemann hypothesis true?" would return the correct answer with probability indistinguishable from 1 — more reliably, presumably, than a committee of Fields medalists deliberating for a decade.

(7) Therefore open questions in mathematics — and, by parity of reasoning, in science — should be settled by plebiscite rather than by expert deliberation; the plebiscite is the better instrument, and the deliberation is at best theater.

I can't accept (7). But each escape route is uncomfortable.

The obvious move is to deny independence: five billion people share newspapers, heuristics, and deference to the same experts, so their votes are not independent. But (a) that concedes that procedural independence, as characterized in (4), was never enough, and I'd want to know precisely what would be enough — which exact statistical condition, holding of whom, in which actual elections; and (b) if shared sources and shared heuristics break independence, then no real electorate in history has ever satisfied the jury theorem either, which seems to demolish its standard use as an argument for the epistemic superiority of democratic decision-making over expert rule. I would rather not pay that: I believe the theorem does say something real about why large, messy electorates often outperform small councils of the well-informed. If your repair costs me that, say so plainly — but then explain what, if anything, survives of the theorem's democratic moral.

The other routes look worse. Denying (1) means denying a mathematical result. Denying (2) or (3) by insisting that competence requires understanding seems to purchase competence at the price of the theorem's whole interest: the theorem was supposed to show how the many, individually mediocre, can collectively rival the few experts, and if it only applies to voters who already understand the question, it isn't showing that. And note the argument is symmetric: a population slightly worse than random would be exactly as informative, provided we negate its verdict — so any repair had better not make superstitious majorities look like epistemic gold mines.

So: which step or inference do you reject, what exactly is wrong with it, and what does rejecting it cost? And what, if anything, survives of the idea that the jury theorem explains the epistemic value of democracy?
