As you may know from my complaints at faculty meetings, I teach the department's survey of early modern philosophy, and each spring a handful of students arrive with some version of Leibniz's doctrine of the best of all possible worlds already in their heads, absorbed from popular science writing. This year the source is a book on the history of optimism that a colleague in chemistry has been enthusiastically lending around, and before I decide whether to steer students toward or away from it, I want an independent check on its central summary of Leibniz. The author puts it roughly as follows: "Leibniz argued that because God is omnipotent and perfectly good, God had no choice about which world to create: given the divine nature, this world follows from it as necessarily as the theorems of geometry follow from the axioms. Since God could not have done otherwise, whatever happens is for the best, and so each event taken by itself — the Lisbon earthquake, a lost war, a child's illness — is the best thing that could have occurred at that point in history. It follows that efforts to improve the world are at best futile and at worst presumptuous, a line of thinking that held back European reform for a century until Voltaire's Candide, written after the Lisbon disaster, demolished it." The book also credits Leibniz with distinguishing three kinds of evil (metaphysical, physical, and moral) and with saying that God permits evil only for the sake of a greater good, which sounds right to me.

I would be grateful for a careful audit of this summary, claim by claim. Please sort each assertion into three categories: what a Leibniz scholar would accept as an accurate statement of the doctrine; what a scholar would wince at as a subtle distortion; and what is simply false as an attribution to Leibniz. My own suspicions: the "no choice / as necessarily as the theorems of geometry" line seems to run together two things Leibniz worked hard to keep apart, and the slide from the best world as a whole to each event's being best in itself looks like a conflation the doctrine explicitly rejects — but I have not read the Theodicy closely in years and would rather not lean on my memory in front of students. I am particularly interested in whether the book's quietism claim ("efforts to improve the world are futile") is a fair inference from anything Leibniz actually held, given that the historical Leibniz spent a great deal of energy on practical projects, and in whether describing Candide as having "demolished" the doctrine confuses satire with argument.

One request about the epistemic register of your reply: please distinguish throughout between what is settled among Leibniz scholars and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretive reading as the consensus. For example, my impression is that theses like the absolute-versus-hypothetical necessity distinction are textually secure, whereas questions such as whether the "best" is to be understood primarily in terms of the maximum of perfection and harmony rather than of happiness, or exactly how Leibniz secures the contingency of the actual world, remain matters of live disagreement. If I am wrong about which side of that line any of these falls on, say so plainly, since I would be repeating your classifications to students.
