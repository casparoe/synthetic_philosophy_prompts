I sit on the people & culture committee at a mid-size company (~450 employees, mostly office workers but some lab staff), and we're revisiting parental leave before the June board meeting. Quick history: we currently give 16 weeks paid to whoever gives birth and 4 weeks to everyone else who becomes a parent (partners, adoptive parents, intended parents in surrogacy arrangements). Two proposals are on the table. Proposal A: one flat allocation, say 12 weeks paid for every new parent no matter how the child arrived, PLUS a separate medical-recovery leave for anyone who gives birth, treated like any other medical event. Proposal B: a shared pool, say 24 weeks that a couple can divide any way they like with a small minimum for each parent. There's also a faction pushing us to keep some version of a "primary caregiver" designation, where you declare one parent as the main carer and that person gets the longer leave.

The fight underneath is about what the leave is actually *for*, and nobody will say it out loud. If it's medical recovery, then the birth parent needs weeks and the adoptive father needs almost none, and it's a bit odd to call any of it parental leave. If it's bonding and early child development, then equal leave for all parents makes sense and it's the birth recovery that should be carved out separately, which is Proposal A's logic. If it's about leveling labor-market outcomes so women's careers don't take the hit, then maybe you actually want to *skew* leave toward non-birthing parents or condition benefits on fathers actually taking them, because otherwise the 16/4 split just bakes in the assumption that mom stays home and we're subsidizing one family form. If it's about supporting the dual-earner family as such, Proposal B's shared pool quietly rewards couples where one parent takes almost everything, which cuts against the whole both-parents-work-and-share thing. And the edge cases are where it gets genuinely weird: a surrogate who gives birth but isn't becoming a parent (recovery leave? parental leave? she's our employee either way), my sister and her wife who both work here and where one carried and one didn't, an employee adopting a 7-year-old where "bonding" means something pretty different, twins, etc. The primary-caregiver designation bothers me most, honestly -- it basically forces parents to tell HR which of them is the real parent, but its defenders point out it's the only version that's honest about how couples actually divide things.

Can you draft the memo I'd circulate to the committee before the board meeting? It should recommend a concrete structure (not "form a working group" -- an actual policy, with numbers), and it needs to name the purpose it's betting on and follow through on the edge cases above, since those are exactly what the committee keeps relitigating. Please also engage seriously with the strongest version of the argument for whatever you recommend against -- if you go with the flat structure, steelman the primary-caregator people and the shared-pool people; if you go with a pool, steelman the flat-structure side. My committee chair is an employment lawyer and will pounce on hand-waving, so make the reasoning something she can actually respond to rather than vibes. Two pages or less, and it's fine to admit where the evidence on child outcomes vs. maternal employment effects is thinner than people pretend.
