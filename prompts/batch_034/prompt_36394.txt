I've been covering mind-and-brain research for a general science magazine for some years, and I'm drafting a column that states plainly where I stand on the scientific study of consciousness, since readers keep asking. Before I send it to my editor, I want a consistency check on my own position, because I suspect I'm holding views that quietly undermine one another. Here is the set: (1) I'm a physicalist — consciousness is entirely a product of the brain, with no extra metaphysical ingredients; (2) I think Dennett's heterophenomenology is the right methodological framework for the field: first-person reports are data to be gathered and interpreted neutrally, like an anthropologist's field texts, with no privileged status granted to them; (3) I take the confabulation literature — split-brain patients inventing reasons, choice blindness, post-hoc rationalization — to show that introspective reports are often wrong not just about the causes of our mental states but about the experiences themselves; (4) I think first-person training, as in neurophenomenology's use of experienced meditators, can genuinely correct this, yielding reports that track the experiences better rather than just being more convenient for experimenters; and (5) I nonetheless think the hard problem is real — even a completed neuroscience paired with a completed catalogue of what subjects believe about their experience would leave something unexplained — while also denying that qualia, in the classic sense of intrinsic, ineffable, private properties, exist at all. Which of these commitments are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest way to restore coherence? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, say what each fix would cost me, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most if I had to make one.
