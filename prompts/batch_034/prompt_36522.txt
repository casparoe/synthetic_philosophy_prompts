I'm a phil grad student and im putting together a "which view of death's badness do you actually hold?" quiz for a blog that covers philosophy for a general audience. Please write eight or nine questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions: the strict Epicurean (death is never bad for the one who dies), the deprivationist who thinks death is bad by comparison with continued life but owes us a story about *when* it's bad (this is where they usually stumble), the reader who accepts the old symmetry point about our nonexistence before birth and either bites the bullet or explains the asymmetry via desires about the future, desire-satisfaction views that locate the badness in unfulfilled desires, and narrative views on which a life cut short is bad partly as an incomplete story. The cases should include: prenatal vs. posthumous nonexistence, a sudden death vs. a slow terminal diagnosis you can plan around, someone whose remaining life would have been miserable anyway, an artist who dies with major work unwritten, and a person put in suspended animation for two centuries and revived (is the interval bad for them, and does the answer change if revival fails?). Phrase the options so a reader can answer without knowing any of the labels — sort them by what they say about cases, not by which school they like the name of. And flag honestly any pair of leaves the quiz can't really separate, like strict Epicureans and desire-based theorists who agree death isn't bad but for very different reasons.
