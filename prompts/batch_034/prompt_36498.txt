I head the interpretation team at a regional history museum, and all of our exhibit copy — wall text, object labels, timelines, audio guide scripts — is drafted by curators, edited by generalist writers, and signed off by me before it goes to print. After our most recent exhibit, a historian on our advisory board complained that our labels were Whig history in miniature: the city's past read as a dress rehearsal for its present, every old institution judged by whether it "led to" what we now have, and the losing side of every conflict treated as an obstacle on a road whose destination everyone at the time supposedly knew. My staff split over what to do. One faction thinks the cure would be worse than the disease — visitors want a story with some direction, and a label so hedged that it presents everything as equally contingent is unreadable in the eight seconds a visitor actually spends on it. The other faction suspects that any moral language about the past is Whig, that we should stop calling the 1908 milk-pasteurization campaign a triumph and the child labor in our mills a horror, and I don't think that can be right either: some things really were better and worse than others, and pretending the past is a moral flatline is its own kind of falsification.

What I want is a procedure our copywriters can run on a draft label in the time they actually have — call it ten minutes per label, several hundred labels per exhibit — with no historian in the room. Please translate whatever the historiography genuinely offers (Butterfield's original complaint, the distinction between narrating change and narrating progress, the distorting power of hindsight, the "on the right side of history" rhetoric that even our fundraising copy keeps drifting into) into a sequence of tests a non-specialist can apply: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends with a concrete rewrite, and where it has to hand the label back to a curator because the problem is one of interpretation rather than detectable wording. Be honest about which of your tests are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — my writers can tell the difference, and they'll stop using a checklist that pretends.

Then run the procedure on three real drafts I'm sitting on. First, our label about the textile mills closing: "The industry that built this city could not survive the modern economy, but its legacy lives on in the thriving arts district it made possible." Second, the pasteurization label: "A pioneering step toward the public-health protections we take for granted today." Third, our Then-and-Now timeline wall, which pairs 1890s photographs of the same street corners with 2020s ones. Include at least one case where the procedure returns a verdict I won't like — I am fond of that timeline wall and have defended it publicly — and make sure you say what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of Whig history live at the level of the exhibit as a whole, in which objects get selected and how visitors are routed through the gallery, that no per-label checklist will ever catch.
