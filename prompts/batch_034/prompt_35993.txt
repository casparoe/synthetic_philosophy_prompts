I teach an upper-level seminar on causality and causal inference for a mixed group of philosophy and statistics students — 14 students, one 75-minute session. Most of them already know Simpson's paradox as the Berkeley-admissions chestnut and think the lesson is "disaggregate your data," and I want an in-class activity that breaks that overcorrection while making them feel the paradox's force for real.

Design a game in which the students act as a drug-review board deciding, under time pressure, whether to approve a treatment for a simulated patient population. Set it up so that the aggregate data and the disaggregated data point in opposite directions, and give the decision real stakes within the game — points standing for patient outcomes in a simulated population, committee members' scores tied to how the population fares, no actual money. Materials and handouts should be things I can print in advance (contingency tables, subgroup reports that different "stakeholders" circulate at different moments), and you should give me a timeline: when the aggregate table arrives, when the subgroup analyses leak out, when the vote is forced.

Then build in the twist that carries the real lesson: at the end, reveal a second, structurally identical case — same numbers exactly — in which the partitioning variable is a post-treatment variable rather than a pre-treatment one, so that the correct decision reverses and the aggregation the students had just learned to distrust is the one they should have trusted. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the formal machinery (marginal vs. conditional association, collapsibility, why the data alone cannot tell you which partition to condition on), and should make the point that Simpson's paradox is resolved by causal knowledge imported from outside the tables, not by a blanket disaggregation rule.

Also tell me, concretely: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students treating it as a gotcha about lying with statistics, or just freezing on the arithmetic), how to handle each failure mode, and which feature of the actual paradox no classroom staging can reproduce — what I will have to supply by fiat or lecture, however well the game runs.
