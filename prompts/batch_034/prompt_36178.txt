My tutorial partner Priya and I have spent this term in our Kant course feeding example maxims to the Formula of Universal Law, and I've begun keeping a record of her verdicts because I suspect she isn't running the test Kant actually describes, though I can't yet prove it. Her rulings so far: borrowing money on a promise to repay while secretly intending not to is impermissible; letting one's natural talents rust in favour of a life of pure amusement is impermissible; never helping anyone in need, when helping would cost little, is impermissible; telling a flattering falsehood about a friend's unfortunate new haircut purely to spare her feelings is permissible; and a shopkeeper who, during a shortage, charges desperate customers what the market will bear — with no deception, no broken promise, no coercion — is impermissible. Next week we take up fresh maxims, and I'd like a prediction before she delivers it. On my understanding the leading candidates for the rule she is implicitly running are the genuine Kantian apparatus, on which a maxim fails either through a contradiction in conception or a contradiction in the will; a rule-consequentialist stand-in, on which a maxim is impermissible when its universalisation would leave everyone worse off; and a hybrid that supplements the Kantian apparatus with the humanity formula. The new cases are, first, the maxim of smoking cigarettes purely for pleasure while fully informed of the risks, and second, the maxim of eating meat from animals raised and slaughtered humanely. What will she say about each, and which rule best fits her track record?

I'll grant that the record is not tidy. The white lie and the shopkeeper pull in opposite directions: if she were a strict Kantian about deception, the haircut verdict is surprising, whereas if she were running universalisability alone, the shopkeeper verdict is surprising, since there seems to be nothing self-defeating about a world of universal price-gouging, and something other than the contradiction tests must be doing that work. My own working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that she is running a simple 'would the world be worse if everyone did this' rule in a Kantian costume, with the shopkeeper verdict smuggling in a distaste for exploitation that the rule doesn't strictly deliver. Take that seriously, but don't flatter it. Tell me your prediction for both new cases, the best diagnosis of her implicit rule, the strongest rival diagnosis, and — my real interest — the single further maxim that would most cleanly separate the two hypotheses you find best, with an explanation of which way each would have to rule on it.
