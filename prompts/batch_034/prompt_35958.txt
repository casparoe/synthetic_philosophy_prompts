I do foresight work at a mid-sized consultancy — mostly scenario planning for clients in energy and shipping. A big chunk of my job is building "what if" histories: we tell a client, suppose X hadn't happened in 1973, here's the plausible world that follows, and we use that to stress-test whatever strategy they're about to commit money to. My problem is my colleaugues' counterfactuals keep smuggling in inevitability. Somebody writes "even without the oil shock, the energy picture would look basically the same today," and you can just tell they ran history forward to whatever answer they already believed and called it a counterfactual. The whole point of the exercise is to locate where contingency actually lived, and we're using it as decoration.

I vaguely remember from college that philosophers of history have actual tools for this — something like a "minimal rewrite" rule (change the least you can to get the divergence), and distinguishing causes that were necessary from ones that just happened to be sufficient in that setting. But I only half-remember it and can't reconstruct it. Can you turn whatever is actually usable from that way of thinking into a procedure I can run when vetting a draft scenario? To be concrete about my situation: we produce maybe three or four of these a quarter, each goes into a report with the client's name on it, the partner signs off, and I usually have a day at most to review a draft before it goes up the chain. So what I need is:

- a checklist I can run on a draft in under an hour, with the questions in order
- for each question, what counts as a pass and what counts as a fail
- the places where the checklist has to stop and hand it back to the author, because the question is really about deep structure or values and no procedure can settle it
- an honest note about where the procedure will misfire — I get that historical counterfactuals can't be tested the way they can in physics, so tell me what the procedure is actually doing in that case, even if the answer is "mostly keeping the author honest"

Then run it on three scenarios from recent drafts:

1. "If the shipping container had been invented ten years earlier, global manufacturing would still have ended up concentrated in East Asia." A junior swears by this one. I think it's inevitability in disguise — the second half of the sentence is doing no work, it's just today's world with a different start date — but I can't proove that with anything except my own irritation.
2. "If the 1973 embargo had lasted six more months, Japan would have decarbonized by 1990." Client loves this one, it fits their story about energy transitions being shock-driven.
3. "If Britain had never joined the EEC, its manufacturing decline would have looked the same, because deindustrialization was global." — actually make it two scenarios, one where the counterfactual change is small and one where it's enormous, so I can see whether the procedure treats them differently.

And please be straight with me about at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict I won't like (probably #2, given who's paying us), and about what the whole exercise can't do — if the checklist ends up just laundering my intuitions with extra steps, I'd rather know that now.
