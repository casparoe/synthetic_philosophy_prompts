So I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and once a year my outlet does a little festival thing where readers can sign up for workshops with the writers. I've got 90 minutes and probably around 25 people, mixed crowd, no philosophy background assumed. I want to run a game that makes them feel how hard "just aggregate everyone's values" actually is when you say it in the context of AI alignment — like, whose values is a powerful AI system even supposed to serve?

Here's the setup I'm imagining, but push back on details if they don't work:

- Each participant gets a card assigning them a "constituency" they have to role-play the whole session. Not their own views — a card. So one card is a devout social conservative who thinks kids shouldn't be exposed to certain content, one is a straightforward total utilitarian, one is somebody who mostly cares about their own family and is checked out of politics entirely, one is a liberal-neutralist who thinks the system should refuse to take sides on contested ways of life, and so on. Maybe 6 or 7 card types, several people per type.
- The group has to settle, by some voting procedure, what a hypothetical "advisor AI" is allowed to optimize for — I'm thinking 5 or 6 concrete questions, like "should it tell white lies to protect people's feelings" or "should it flag content it judges misleading."
- The wrinkle I actually care about: about a third of the electorate is absent. Cards for future people, kids, and low-information non-voters, whose ballots have to be filled in by "proxies" — other participants who are just told to guess what those constituents would want. So a chunk of the aggregation is unavoidably indirect, and everyone knows the guesses are doing a lot of work.
- Second wrinkle: some cards contain values that are about how *other* people live. At some point the group has to decide whether those values even go into the aggregate, or whether the exercise has a neutrality constraint that screens them out. What I want is for them to hit the Sen-style tension themselves — enough people believe the system should stay neutral on lifestyles that they end up overriding what a majority of constituencies actually voted for, and then they have to sit with whether that was principled or just rigging the process.

Materials should be printable cards and some kind of tokens, no apps, no real money. Payoffs: I want some symbolic stake so the votes have weight and people actually fight for their card instead of coasting — maybe losing constituencies get visibly marked on a scoreboard, or something, you figure out something that works.

What I want from you: the card texts written out so I could basically print them as-is, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real problems — agenda-setting, cycling majorities, whose proxy guesses stood in for absent people, what neutrality costs and who pays for it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which part of the actual philosophical problem a game with candy and index cards just cannot reproduce. My readers will ask.

One rule: no lecture up front about AI risk or anything else. The point is they run into the aggregation problem themselves before anybody names it.
