I am a novelist. Over the next decade I plan a trilogy of historical novels, each built on a foundational legend of Western culture: the first set during a late Bronze Age siege at Troy, the second in post-Roman Britain around a war leader who may or may not be "Arthur", and the third following a small community departing Egypt into the Sinai. My problem is not a shortage of material but a surplus of undifferentiated material. The legends hand me Bronze Age warriors who behave like Iron Age chieftains, a fifth-century Briton wearing the armour of a high medieval king, and a mass departure of a whole people rendered through the theological lens of communities writing many centuries later. Daily, mid-draft, I face decisions like these: do I treat the Trojan Horse as an event, a memory of siegecraft, or a folkloric motif I must transform? Do I give my British war leader a band of mounted companions and a hall, or strip him down to what a fifth-century reality could support? Do I stage the plagues, or treat them as later liturgical elaboration? I cannot stop drafting to read archaeology reports, and I refuse to outsource the judgement to a single popularising book. What I need is a procedure I can run myself, in twenty minutes, from general historical knowledge.

Please construct that procedure for me. It should be a sequence of questions asked in order — about, for instance, the presence of contemporary attestation, the fit with what is generally known about the period, signs of anachronism, evidence of later political or theological motive, convergence of independent strands of tradition, and the distinction between a legend being untrue of its putative period yet genuinely informative about the period that produced the version we have. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure may stop with a confident "treat as unhistorical", and where it must hand the decision back to me because the question is one of weighting rather than fact. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Crucially, the procedure must keep two outputs separate: the epistemic verdict on an element (how much reason there is to think it happened) and the novelistic use (what I may still do with it, since a novel can knowingly deploy myth as myth — my narrator or a character can believe what I do not). Include a step for that override, with conditions under which it is legitimate rather than self-deception.

Then run the procedure on three specific elements: the Trojan Horse episode; the image of Arthur as a figure with a renowned band of warriors and a capital, rather than a merely local commander; and the scale of the departure from Egypt as a mass movement of an entire people. In at least one case the procedure should be allowed to reach a conclusion I will dislike — I confess I am fond of the horse — and I want to see how the override step handles that honestly.

When you have finished, add a short note on what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of legendary material it will misclassify or leave stranded.
