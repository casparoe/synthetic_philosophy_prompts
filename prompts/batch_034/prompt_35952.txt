I'm preparing to teach a unit that pairs Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide with Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, and I'd like a serious critical reading that takes both texts on their own terms rather than using one as decoration for the other.

On the philosophy side, the relevant Aquinian material is this: he holds that private individuals and citizens generally may not kill tyrants, partly because killing a ruler ordinarily belongs to public authority, and partly on prudential grounds -- in his political writings he argues that attempting to remove a tyrant typically provokes sedition and civil strife that harm the community more than the tyranny did, so that enduring the tyrant is often the better counsel; but he allows that where the multitude has conferred rule on someone, it may also withdraw it, and there is a recognized scholastic tradition after him that builds in more permissive conditions under which tyrannicide becomes licit. On the drama side, the play gives us Brutus: a private citizen with no public mandate, who is convinced by Cassius that Caesar's crowning would enslave Rome, who reasons his way to the deed as a sacrifice rather than a murder, and whose act leads to mob violence, the proscriptions, and civil war.

Read the play through this lens. I want to know where the two texts genuinely converge and where they pull apart. Does the trajectory of the play -- from the assassination through Antony's funeral oration to the chaos at Philippi -- vindicate Aquinas's prudential argument, staging exactly the harms he predicted from a tyrannicide undertaken without public authority? Or does Shakespeare load the dice: is Brutus's failure dramatized as a set of personal errors (his misjudgment of Antony, his naïve faith in the crowd's reasonableness, his preference for honorable abstractions over political reality) rather than as anything inherent in the act itself, so that the play shows us a botched tyrannicide rather than an argument against tyrannicide? Consider especially the orchard scene where Brutus reasons to himself about what Caesar might become, the moment the conspirators debate whether to kill Antony too, the mob's murder of Cinna the poet, and Brutus's later insistence that his cause was just even as everything collapses around it. And think about whether the play quietly addresses the question Aquinas raises but leaves abstract: who or what counts as the public authority competent to judge and remove a tyrant, in a state where the old institutions have already been hollowed out by the very man they would judge?

Please be honest about where the play resists the reading. If Aquinas's framework fits Brutus's case only by stretching, say where it strains. And end with what the play gives the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot -- whether drama, by making us watch a prudential argument play out in a particular man's life, teaches something about tyrannicide that the treatise tradition could not articulate.
