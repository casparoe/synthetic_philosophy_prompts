For a philosophy term project, I'm writing about Leibniz's claim that our world is the best of all possible worlds, and I'd like to try something a bit unusual: reading it against The Giver, the Lois Lowry novel we just finished in my tenth-grade English class. My teacher suggested I get a second perspective on the argument before I commit, so I'd like yours, and I'd like it to actually take a side rather than list the possibilities for me to sort through.

Here is the parallel I want developed. In the novel, the community's founders set out to design a world from the ground up — the way Leibniz's God surveys all possible worlds and selects the best — and their design principle is the elimination of suffering: no war, no hunger, no painful memories, no choice (since choices bring mistakes). They achieve this partly by Sameness, which flattens color, climate, and feeling, and partly by concentrating all the retained memory of human pain and joy in a single Receiver. Meanwhile, Leibniz holds that a world without evil would not be the best world: evil is permitted because a world containing it, on balance, has greater perfection than any evil-free alternative, the way a painting's shadows contribute to its beauty, and because certain goods and certain evils are literally incompossible — you cannot have genuine courage without danger, or forgiveness without injury.

So my core question, and the one I want a definite answer to: does the novel refute Leibniz, illustrate him, or complicate him in some third way? If the community is a genuine attempt to build the best of all possible worlds and it fails because it has to delete love, color, and memory to get there, that looks like a vindication of Leibniz — suffering and its causes turn out to be incompossible with the goods that make a world worth having. But I can also see the opposite reading: the community is what you get when you accept Leibniz's reasoning and hand it to finite planners, and the fact that their "best" world is a horror in disguise shows that the whole optimizing framework is suspect, not just its execution. Pick one of these readings (or a clearly stated third one), defend it against the strongest rival, and commit to it — tell me which interpretation the novel's ending most supports and why, including whether the ending's release of the memories back to everyone counts as evidence for or against the framework.

Two cruxes I'd like you to address in the reading you commit to. First, the Receiver himself: the community does not actually eliminate suffering, it warehouses it in one person, which resembles both the free-will theodicy (someone must bear the cost of the world's perfection) and its sacrificial inversion. Does Jonas's role make Leibniz's picture look more or less defensible? Second, the question of who the designer is: Leibniz's God is supposed to be omniscient, choosing among infinitely many complete worlds, while the community's Elders are not. Does the analogy break here in a way that undermines the whole exercise, or is the difference inessential to what the novel is saying about optimized worlds?

A note on how confident to be: I'm being graded partly on defending a definite thesis, so please take an actual position with reasons, not a survey of interpretations. But I'm also supposed to acknowledge where my argument is weakest, so end with one honest paragraph on where your committed reading strains against the text of the novel or against Leibniz — the place a critic who took the other side would push hardest — rather than presenting the reading as airtight. And since I'm a student and not a scholar of Leibniz, keep any technical terms (incompossibility, theodicy, and so on) explained in plain language as you use them.
