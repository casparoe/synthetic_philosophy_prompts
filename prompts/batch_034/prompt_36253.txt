Is there a name for this? I'm on the board of my condo association, and we just had a genuinely nasty fight about hiring a private security firm to patrol the complex overnight after a string of break ins. What struck me was that the fight was never about cost or whether guards would actually prevent anything — it was about whether paying armed private patrols was "supporting the system" or "admitting the system doesn't work." One guy said we were building a little private state; another said a neighborhood association can't *be* the state no matter what it pays for. The exact same patrol, doing the exact same things, was described as either order or lawlessness depending entirely on which side of that line people filed it under.

Since then I see the pattern everywhere and I can't unsee it. People argue about whether campus security are "real police," whether a militia showing up armed at a protest is self-protection or a rival government, whether contractors and unmarked vans that officials later deny knowing about still count as the state acting, whether a lynch mob back in the day was "private violence" or the community enforcing an order the law quietly approved of. In every case the physical facts aren't even disputed — everyone agrees who did what to whom — and the entire moral argument happens at the level of whether to classify the actor as official or private, state or non-state. And the classification seems to do all the work by itself: file it under state and the violence is presumptively legitimate, file it under private and it's presumptively a threat that the real state ought to crush.

So my question: is there an established concept or literature for disputes where the whole fight lives in the classification of the actor rather than in the conduct — where drawing the state's outer boundary is itself the political act, and where the boundary seems to get drawn *by* the argument rather than settled before it? To be clear I'm not after a bare label, I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is and isn't — how it's different from people just stretching a word to make their side look good, or from an ordinary dispute about where to draw a definitional line. Does anyone who thinks hard about what makes the state the state actually address this, that the monopoly gets contested at the edges and the contest is the point? And if there's no established name, coin one for me and walk me through the boundary cases, including where my condo situation falls.
