I'm a research engineer at a mid-sized AI lab, and a running argument has broken out among my colleagues that I've been asked to settle in written form for an internal seminar series. The trigger was a reading group we started on von Neumann's posthumously published Silliman Lectures, The Computer and the Brain (1958), which he left unfinished at his death. One colleague, broadly a scaling optimist, claims von Neumann would look at large language models and see them as the vindication of his conjectures: proof that a statistical, somewhat imprecise system operating on something like natural language, rather than on the formal languages of mathematics, can exhibit the kinds of behavior we used to reserve for minds. Another colleague, a former neuroscience postdoc, insists this gets the book backwards -- that von Neumann's whole point about the brain being a hybrid of digital and analog, of its code being statistical in character, and of the shallow logical depth forced on it by neurons firing orders of magnitude slower than vacuum tubes, was a claim about how *un*like von Neumann architecture the brain is, and that he would regard a pile of matrix multiplications on his own architecture as precisely the wrong substrate for the kind of computation he thought mattered.

My assignment, due in two weeks, is a careful piece on what von Neumann would actually say if he could be briefed on LLMs, deep learning, GPUs, and the current state of the field. Please write me that analysis. Ground it in his documented views -- the 1958 lectures, but also his earlier automata work, his 1948 Hixon Symposium lecture on building reliable organisms from unreliable components, and anything else in his record that bears on the question. I want you to address at least these specific questions: whether he would treat the success of LLMs at natural-language tasks as confirming or disconfirming his claim that mathematics is not the brain's natural language; whether he would be more struck by what current systems can do or by their sample inefficiency and hunger for energy relative to a brain; whether he would see neuromorphic and analog computing as the eventual necessary path (and whether it would amuse or disturb him that his own architecture became the bottleneck people now name after it); and whether his work on games and strategy would make him more or less worried than the average researcher about losing control of systems that can out-plan us.

Two constraints on the write-up. First, avoid anachronism and hagiography alike: where his writings simply don't determine an answer, say so explicitly and mark the claim as my extrapolation rather than his, since the audience for this piece will include people who have actually read the lectures closely. Second, distinguish the reasoning: for each prediction, tell me which of his commitments does the work, so that a reader can see which predictions would flip if that commitment were interpreted differently. I care less about a verdict on "scaling optimist vs. skeptic" than about a reconstruction of the man's intellectual framework pushed hard against 2020s machine learning, including the places where it would crack.
