Two philosophers are ending a year-long adversarial collaboration on the nonidentity problem, and I need the joint closing statement they should be able to sign. The first defends the narrow person-affecting claim: an act is wrong only if it is bad for, or wrongs, some particular person; since the children born under a risky depletion-style policy could not have existed under the safer alternative, they are not harmed but simply caused to exist, with lives well worth living. The second defends an impersonal, totalist view: the value of an outcome turns on how well lives go, however many lives there are and who lives them, so a policy foreseeably producing worse-off people where better-off ones would otherwise have existed is pro tanto wrong regardless of identity effects. Both accept the problem's setup — that who exists is fixed by the causal origin of the act — and both reject, in advance, any joint formulation containing a hybrid clause that each side would privately read its own way.

Produce the document with: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, covering what the nonidentity cases do and do not show, what both take wrongful-life litigation to get right or wrong, and the neighboring views both reject (e.g., that merely possible people can be victims); (2) the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes; and (4) for each crux, at least one test case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two cases they designed together and have agreed to include are:

- The Compensable Conception case: a fertility clinic chooses an outdated protocol that will produce children whose lives are well worth living but predictably somewhat worse than those of the numerically different children the newer protocol would have produced; the clinic can, at modest cost, fully compensate the resulting children so that their lives go exactly as well as the alternative children's would have. Is anything still wrong?
- The Reversion case: a policy foreseeably degrades living conditions for exactly one generation — the numerically different people born under it — after which conditions and populations revert precisely to what they would otherwise have been, except that the following generation inherits a small but definite permanent burden. Can the wrong to the first generation be tracked through the second generation's complaint, or must the two be treated as entirely separate wrongs?

Finally, include their shared judgment about which residual disagreements are merely verbal (e.g., over whether "harm" means worse-off-than-otherwise or something like counterfactual consent) and which are substantive. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for cruxes and each side's pre-registered predictions.
