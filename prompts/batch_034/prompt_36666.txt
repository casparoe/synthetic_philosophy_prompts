I sit on the allocation committee of a regional health foundation, and I need a decision memo to circulate before our next quarterly meeting. The case on the agenda instantiates, whether or not we name it, a classic problem in decision theory, and I want the memo to face that squarely rather than bury it.

The concrete decision: we must commit this year's surplus between two road-safety programs. Program A is a package of intersection redesigns that, by our analysts' consensus projection, will certainly prevent roughly 400 traffic deaths over the next decade. Program B is a combined enforcement-and-technology initiative with a 10 percent chance of failing outright (preventing nothing, if a planned system-wide rollout collapses) and a 90 percent chance of preventing roughly 600 deaths. So B has the higher expectation — about 540 lives — while A removes the possibility of the worst outcome.

Complicating matters, a colleague on the committee — our resident decision-theory enthusiast — has flagged that next quarter, under a separate earmarked grant, we will face a structurally related choice: Program C, an 11 percent chance of preventing 400 deaths, versus Program D, a 10 percent chance of preventing 600, with no lives saved otherwise. He points out that C and D share the same 89-percent common outcome, so the independence axiom (or, if you prefer, the sure-thing principle) says that anyone who prefers A to B in this quarter's choice should prefer C to D in the next; preferring A now and D later is precisely the preference pattern that generates the Allais paradox, just spread across two meetings instead of one questionnaire. His proposal is that we adopt a standing rule, effective now, committing the committee to rank all life-saving programs solely by expected lives saved (with ties broken by worst-case outcome), which would settle today's vote for B and next quarter's for C in one stroke.

The committee is split three ways. Some members accept the standing rule: they argue that an institution like ours is not a person, feels no dread while the gamble resolves and no regret if it fails, so the familiar psychological defenses of preferring certainty — anticipated regret, the value of living without anxiety — simply do not apply, and stewardship of donor funds demands expected-value maximization. Others counter that the foundation is answerable to a community whose members do bear those outcomes, that our constituents would overwhelmingly prefer A, and that their risk attitudes are not obviously a confusion to be corrected away. A third group holds that each one-off choice should be made case by case and that the joint violation of independence across two decisions taken months apart by a committee whose membership may change is a bookkeeping curiosity, not a reason to bind ourselves.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — which program to fund, and whether to adopt the standing rule, reject it, or adopt some modified version — and it must do more than restate the arithmetic of expectations. Specifically, it should give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, and it should address the diachronic twist directly: whether a committee's choices, taken sequentially and locally defensible, can genuinely be irrational as a set, and what that implies for whether institutions should pre-commit to decision rules at all. The other members are smart but not decision theorists, so keep the reasoning accessible while still doing justice to the independence axiom's actual content.
