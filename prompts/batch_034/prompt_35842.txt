I'm a novelist — twenty years in, mostly long ensemble books with rotating points of view — and I want the great set-theory fight of the early twentieth century, the one over the axiom of choice, restated in the only technical vocabulary I actually command: the vocabulary of narrative craft. That vocabulary includes scenes and their POV characters; the outline versus discovery drafting; the implied author; the cast list; the saga that outgrows any list of its volumes; and the strange god-term fact that an author can have a character in mind before any description would pick that character out. My mathematics ended at calculus, and my set theory comes entirely from popularizations, so treat me as fluent in craft and a stranger to formalism: every piece of mathematics you need must arrive through my terms or not at all.

I believe the material you will have to translate includes at least the following. That the axiom says, roughly, that given any collection of nonempty sets one can select one element from each, all at once — even when the collection is infinite and no rule distinguishes the selected elements. That choosing one element from each of finitely many sets needs no axiom at all; the fight is only about the infinite case, and about the selections being given as a completed whole rather than made one at a time, forever. That the axiom turned out to be equivalent to some stranger doctrines: that every set can be well-ordered; the lemma about maximal elements that mathematicians reach for constantly; and the result about cutting a sphere into finitely many pieces and reassembling them into two complete spheres the size of the original. That there is an old example involving shoes and socks — a rule can pick one shoe from each pair, but for socks no rule does the job — which I gather was meant to show what "choosing without a rule" means. And that the constructivist complaint was that a selection you cannot describe or produce is not something you are entitled to assert exists, while Zermelo's side answered, in effect, that the selections are determined even where they are not describable. Translate all of this into the terms of my trade: the outline that assigns a POV character to every scene of a book with more scenes than can be listed; the outline nobody can read; the author who "has a character in mind" with no identifying description; the editor who dissects a manuscript and rearranges the pieces into two complete manuscripts. Use the vocabulary of authorial agency only where the mathematics actually licenses it, and flag every point where you refuse to use it.

Finish by testing a suspicion of mine: that what the constructivists refused was the outline nobody can read, and that what Zermelo's side asserted was that the book exists even where the outline doesn't — and tell me whether that suspicion survives the translation or is an artifact of it. I also want your view on the residue itself: does it point to something the mathematicians' debate was missing, or to something narrative craft cannot say? Do not resolve that question in my favor just because I asked in my own dialect.
