I'd like your help predicting how a friend of mine will answer a moral question she hasn't seen yet. Please take the role of a moral philosopher with expertise in normative ethics, particularly contractualism and the aggregation problem. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm the candidate principles her track record is consistent with, and then work through what each one predicts.

My friend is a physician, and over the years she has issued verdicts on hypotheticals about how benefits and burdens may be distributed across people. Her rulings so far:

1. A surgeon could kill one healthy visitor and harvest his organs to save five dying patients. Her verdict: impermissible.
2. A city must route night flights one of two ways: over a district of 100,000 people, each of whom suffers occasional sleep disruption, or over a hamlet of 20 people, each of whom suffers severe chronic sleep deprivation and health damage. Her verdict: spread the disruption over the 100,000.
3. A government may slightly raise taxes on millions to fund life-saving treatment for a few hundred. Her verdict: not just permitted, but required.
4. With a single dose of a strong painkiller to allocate, and a choice between one patient in agony and two patients in moderate pain, her verdict: the patient in agony gets it.

She has never ruled on a case where an enormous number of very small burdens is weighed against a very small number of very large ones. That's the new case, and it's a live policy question she'll be asked about next week: a health ministry with a fixed budget can fund either (a) a national program that would spare 200 million people two weeks of flu each winter — moderate misery, no complications, no long-term effects for anyone — or (b) treatment for 400 people that would spare them permanent, severe pain and disability. Both options only involve funding or withholding benefits; no one is killed, attacked, or used as a means.

Please tell me:

- Which principles is she implicitly running? At least three should be on the table: a strict contractualism on which complaints are never summed across persons and only the strongest individual complaint on each side is compared; Scanlon's own amended contractualism, which lets complaints be aggregated when they fall within a common band of seriousness and which admits a disaster-avoidance escape clause; and a constrained utilitarianism that aggregates everything except actively killing or harming someone. If you see a fourth that fits her record as well, add it.
- What does each principle predict about the flu-vaccine case? Note that on the strict no-summing view the 400 have the strongest complaint by a wide margin, while on constrained utilitarianism the summed relief to 200 million dwarfs it — so the case should split at least two of your candidates.
- Your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her record over its rivals. Pay attention to ruling 4: it is the one place her record could already hint at whether she aggregates at all, since the utilitarian needs the agony to outweigh two moderates.
- The further case, beyond the flu-vaccine one, that would most cleanly discriminate between the surviving candidates — and say which precedent is the closest analog to the new case.
