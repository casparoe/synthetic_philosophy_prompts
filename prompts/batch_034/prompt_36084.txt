so I've drifted over the last year or so into what you might call the "collapse is a category error" camp, and I want someone to stress-test it. the view is basically: there's no such thing as a society *failing*, there's just change, and "collapse" is a narrative frame that archaeologists and pop writers impose because it makes a better story. My evidence, roughly:

- Classic Maya: the southern lowlands depopulated around 800-950 CE, sure, but northern centers like Chichen Itza and later Mayapan were doing fine, trade networks reorganized rather than vanished, and there are millions of Maya speakers today. Calling that a "collapse" seems to just mean "the elite monuments stopped," which is an oddly elite-centric standard for failure.
- Late Bronze Age eastern Med: the palace system broke apart, but that looks more like decentralization — people trickled back to villages, iron production spread, and the loss of Linear B is just administrative tech losing its use case once the palaces were gone. Systems got simpler because simpler was affordable.
- Easter Island: the ecocide version is basically dead. Rat-driven deforestation (and later slaving raids and disease) explains the tree loss better than the islanders cutting down their own life support, and the population crash came after European contact. So the canonical "they destroyed themselves" story was projection all along, which makes me suspicious of the frame everywhere else.
- Tainter's diminishing-returns story, even where I buy the mechanics, ends up cutting my way: simplification is an *adaptation*, not a failure. Nobody says a company that spins off divisions has collapsed.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters, ranked from "this would actually make me drop it" to "merely awkward." What would the archaeological record have to look like for me to concede there's a real phenomenon of societal failure and not just reorganization? Like, if you found mass mortality events, sustained loss of practical technology (not just elite stuff like writing), populations cratering for centuries with no migration destination, health markers in skeletons getting much worse across the board — does my view have to bite any bullets there, and has it already quietly bitten some (the ~400 year literacy gap in Greece is the one I can feel in the back of my head)? Also: what does my view *predict* that the "genuine failure exists" view doesn't, so it's actually risking something? And be honest about whether the transformation-not-collapse position can always retreat to "well, every change is a reorganization" — whether that's a real unfalsifiability problem or whether it's just what you should expect of a view at this level of generality.
