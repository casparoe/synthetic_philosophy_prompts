I teach a 200-level epistemology course, about 18 students, one 75-minute session, and we're about to hit the Meno problem: Socrates' observation that a true belief will serve you just as well as knowledge as long as it sticks around, so why does anyone bother converting one into the other? I want an in-class game where they feel the answer instead of being told it, and I have a specific mechanism in mind which may or may not work — that's partly what I'm asking you to judge.

The mechanic I have in mind: value that lives in the pattern, not in the token. A single lucky true belief and a single known-up belief are worth exactly the same in the moment; the difference only shows up when conditions shift. So the game should let students cash out the same belief twice — once under the original conditions, and again after something small changes — so the lucky ones watch their belief turn to ash while the others' still works. I'm imagining something like a navigation/route task but I'm open to anything with that double-cash-out structure.

Constraints: no real money (I keep a points ledger on the board instead), rules simple enough to explain in under five minutes, and no lecture up front — they shouldn't hear the words "reliability" or "safety" until the debrief at the earliest, and the names "Meno" and "swamping problem" not until afterwards. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto parts of the issue: which round was the true-belief round, which was the knowledge round, where the extra value actually came from, and whether it came from the belief itself or from the way it was formed.

Two things I want you to be careful about. First, I've been told the standard "reliable method is more valuable" move has a known objection — roughly: if what you care about is the truth of this belief, the reliable method adds nothing to the value of this instance, only to other instances you'll never get to live. If that objection applies to my game, say so plainly — don't build me a game whose debrief quietly assumes the contested answer. Second, in the debrief notes, please clearly seperate what's basically settled among epistemologists from what's genuinely contested — e.g. whether knowledge even is reliably-produced true belief, and whether that framing answers Socrates or just changes the subject — and don't present a contested line as the consensus. If some part of the game only makes sense if you take sides on a contested question, flag that part.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical issue a classroom game just cannot reproduce. My own guess: a game can show knowledge being more useful, but Socrates' question is partly about value beyond usefulness, and I don't see how a points ledger gets at that. But you tell me.
