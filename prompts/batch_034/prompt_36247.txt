I teach a seminar on philosophy of mind, and I'm preparing a unit on the meta-problem of consciousness — roughly, the problem of explaining why we make the claims and judgments about consciousness that we do: why we report that there is a hard problem at all, why we say things like "the redness of red" or "what it's like." A popular book on consciousness by a science journalist, which a colleague recommended to me, glosses the meta-problem like this: "Chalmers has now produced a second hard problem — the hard problem of the hard problem. The meta-problem is the mystery of why our brains generate the feeling that consciousness is mysterious, and like the original hard problem, it admits no functional explanation. The key point is that our zombie duplicates would say exactly what we say about consciousness. Since a being with no inner life would produce the same reports, the reports themselves carry no information about consciousness. So once we explain mechanistically why brains produce these reports — presumably some quirk of our introspective machinery, like the feeling that a visual illusion is inexplicable — the original hard problem is revealed to be a pseudo-problem, and Chalmers now concedes that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion."

Something about this strikes me as off — my recollection is that Chalmers officially classifies the meta-problem as an easy problem, not a second hard one, and that he intends the zombie-duplicate point to cut both ways rather than to favor the illusionist — but the book is by a normally careful writer, and I don't trust my memory of the details well enough to lecture from it. Please go claim by claim through the passage: which parts would Chalmers himself accept, which are subtle distortions of his framing, and which are flat wrong? In particular, tell me whether the book's central inference — from "the problem-reports are mechanistically explicable and zombie-like systems would issue them too" to "the hard problem is a pseudo-problem and consciousness is an illusion" — is one Chalmers endorses, one he explicitly resists, or one he regards as an open possibility that the meta-problem by itself neither establishes nor forecloses. And note whether an illusionist like Frankish would be happier or less happy with the book's rendering than Chalmers would be.
