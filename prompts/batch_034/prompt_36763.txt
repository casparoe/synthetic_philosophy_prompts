I run a small bakery, and last year a flood destroyed most of my equipment. A supplier I'd known for years agreed to let me run up a large tab on flour and sugar with nothing in writing — only my spoken promise to settle within a year. When the year was up, money was still tight, and there was nothing legally compelling me to pay; my accountant told me that, from a strictly financial standpoint, I should have walked away. I paid anyway, and my accountant called that sentimental rather than smart.

At a recent trade association meeting, another bakery owner told me she's in a similar bind, and she has already settled on her plan. She'll accept the same kind of informal credit from her supplier, but she has concluded in advance that when the bill falls due she simply won't pay. Her reasoning is that with no contract there's no enforcement, so on the day payment is due, paying will be strictly worse for her than not paying — and, she says, a promise whose fulfillment depends on her later choosing the worse option isn't really a promise at all, just talk. She sees nothing irrational about planning for this now.

Something about her position bothers me, but I can't locate the flaw, and I'd like to before our next conversation. Is her planning actually coherent? Is there a principled difference between her situation and mine — between being, in advance, the sort of person who won't pay, and being the sort of person who did pay? Was my own payment last year rational, or merely lucky for my reputation? And if her plan is defective in some way, would it be enough for her to recognize the defect, or would she need to change something about herself rather than her reasoning?
