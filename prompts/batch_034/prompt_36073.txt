I am a novelist, currently revising a book about two estranged siblings — one who left home and rebuilt herself alone, one who stayed and dissolved into family obligation. At a residency last month, a fellow writer who spent part of her childhood in South Africa pressed a proverb on me as the key to the whole manuscript, commonly rendered in English as "a person is a person through other persons." She used it against my first draft: my self-reliant sister, she argued, isn't just making a psychological mistake but a metaphysical one — there is no finished self prior to one's relations, so a person who withdraws from others is not exercising autonomy but impoverishing her very personhood, and my ending wrongly honors her independence. Another resident, a lapsed graduate student in philosophy, scoffed that the proverb is a truism in ontological costume: obviously selves are causally shaped by others, and dressing that platitude up grants nobody a refutation of anything. They argued past each other for an hour. Before I revise, I need to know what, if anything, I have actually been argued out of.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in metaphysics and in comparative philosophy, think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to a structure. Then lay out, as an ordered menu I can work through while redrafting, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing — from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical — distinguishing at least causal from constitutive readings of "through," and psychological, metaphysical, and moral-status readings of "person." For each, say whether it is plausible, whether it is substantive or a platitude, and, crucially, whether it supports my friend's use of it (that self-sufficiency is a metaphysical error my ending should not reward) or rather the skeptic's deflation of it. Conclude by telling me which reading a novelist ought to take seriously if she wants her book to make a claim rather than strike a mood.
