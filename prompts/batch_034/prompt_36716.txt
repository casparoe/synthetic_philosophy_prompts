I teach an upper-level seminar on personal identity, and this term a colleague in our medical school has asked me to guest-lecture on Ulysses contracts — the psychiatric advance directives under which a patient, while lucid and competent, instructs clinicians to administer treatment during a future episode of psychosis or mania even if the patient then refuses it. I know the standard bioethics framing well enough: precedent autonomy, substituted judgment, and so on. What I want is a different kind of analysis. Legal systems and hospital policies that enforce these directives seem to me to be doing metaphysics without admitting it, and I'd like you to help me reconstruct the implicit theory of the self that the practice presupposes. Specifically: when a tribunal honors the lucid patient's instruction against the refusing patient's contemporaneous dissent, what account of personal identity makes that anything other than one person issuing commands about what may be done to a different person? Is the practice best read as presupposing psychological continuity (the earlier and later patient are one subject because their mental states are causally and qualitatively connected), narrative continuity on something like Dennett's model (the directive identifies the self as a center of narrative gravity, and the psychotic episode is a chapter that the narrator is best placed to interpret), or some further view on which the current patient's refusal carries weight that the earlier author was entitled to discount?

I'd also like you to confront an asymmetry that has always puzzled me, and that my medical students will surely raise. We are fairly comfortable enforcing advance refusals — a patient who stipulates while competent that she not be force-fed once demented, even if the later patient appears content and wants to eat — but considerably less comfortable enforcing advance requests, where the later patient competently-seeming resists treatment the earlier self demanded. Some jurisdictions and hospital policies formalize this by making advance refusals more binding than advance requests. My suspicion is that this asymmetry, if it is principled, has to rest on some substantive view about which self counts, or about what happens to the "self" when the narrative breaks down — perhaps something like the thought that in late-stage dementia the biographical subject has already gone, whereas during a psychotic episode there is a partially intact subject present whose dissent matters. I don't know whether that thought can be made coherent, or whether it just restates the asymmetry in fancier language.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of the self that the practice of enforcing Ulysses contracts presupposes, being careful to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with — I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of personal identity would license it equally. Second, say what Dennett's narrative-gravity picture would actually predict here: does the "center of narrative gravity" metaphor give us a principled way to privilege the lucid author's instruction over the psychotic patient's refusal, or does it in fact make the privileging look arbitrary, since both selves are equally characters in the same unfolding story? Third, tell me whether the asymmetry between advance refusals and advance requests can be justified within any single consistent account of the self, or whether it reveals that the practice is relying on shifting, incompatible commitments — for instance, treating the later demented patient as nobody at all in one case while treating the later psychotic patient as a full subject in another. And finally, if you think no theory of personal identity can ground the practice, say what a coherent policy would have to change, and what it would have to appeal to instead. I should mention that the audience for the eventual lecture is physicians and law students, so while the analysis should be philosophically rigorous, it should stay tethered to the concrete features of the practice — capacity assessments, the doctrine that competent refusals are normally inviolable, the fact that these directives typically concern treatment, not confinement. I'd rather not have a survey of the literature; I want an argument.
