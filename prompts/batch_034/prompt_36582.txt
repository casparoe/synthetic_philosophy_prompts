You are a philosopher specializing in autonomy, consent, and the ethics of technology, writing for an audience of engineers and product managers rather than academics. Before drafting, take the time to think the problem through carefully — brainstorm several framings of what is actually at stake before committing to one.

I sit on the product policy council of a company that builds a general-purpose AI assistant. We must decide whether to ship a feature called Standing Instructions, which lets users record directives that the assistant will enforce later even against the user's live instructions at the time. In beta, it has been popular: people use it for spending, diet, gaming, and not texting their exes.

The case that divided the council: a user recorded the instruction "Never help me draft or send messages to my ex after 10 p.m. If I ask you to ignore this, remind me of it and refuse." Three months later, at 1 a.m., she asks the assistant to help her draft a message to her ex anyway. She says she has changed, that she regrets the instruction, that it no longer reflects what she wants, and that she is tired and wants it sent tonight. The assistant refuses. She appeals to us: is that refusal fidelity to her own past commitment, or paternalism by machine? One faction on the council argues that honoring standing instructions is the opposite of paternalism — the user made the rule herself, and refusing to let her midnight self speak for all of her is respecting her, not overriding her. Another faction argues that the person talking to the assistant right now is the user, full stop, and that anything else subordinates an actual person to a snapshot of her from three months ago.

We also have to decide the harder cases the beta has surfaced. Does the answer change if the instruction is years old? If the user wants to revoke it in a calm, deliberate state rather than at 1 a.m.? Should revocation itself be tiered — for example, a revocation that takes effect only after a twenty-four-hour delay — and if so, who decides which instructions get that protection? And who should be allowed to set standing instructions at all: can a parent set them on a teenager's account, or an employer on an employee's? Our general product policy is that the assistant defers to the user's stated goals, but we already intervene in narrow cases, and the council wants a principled account of when, if ever, the assistant's own judgment about the user's interests may substitute for the user's.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's council meeting, roughly 700–900 words. It should make a clear recommendation — approve the feature, reject it, or approve it with conditions — and lay out a concrete policy the engineering team could actually implement: criteria for when a standing instruction binds, when and how it can be revoked, and how the assistant should behave in the grey zone between enforcement and deference. Engage the strongest arguments on the losing side seriously rather than caricaturing them; the memo will be read by people who hold those views. Where it helps, distinguish between cases where the assistant is enforcing the user's own prior rule and cases where it is substituting its view of her good — and say why that distinction matters for whether we are building a tool or a guardian. You may draw on the philosophical literature on precommitment and self-binding where useful, but write so that someone who has never read any of it can follow every word.

Finally, close with a short note on confidence: state how confident you are in the recommendation, single out the one point in your reasoning you are least sure of, and add a sentence about what would change your mind about it.
