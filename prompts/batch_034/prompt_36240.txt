Here's my problem set for the grad seminar on rational choice and I want a sanity check on it before I hand it out, because I suspect my own views are baked into it in ways I haven't thought through. The package I've been defending: (1) the synchronic Dutch book argument is basically fine -- if your credences at a single time license accepting bets that guarantee a loss, that's a real defect in them, de Finetti was right. (2) Money pump arguments for transitivity and dynamic consistency over time are much weaker than people pretend -- the pumpee can refuse the last trade, and once the whole sequence is foreseeable, the case that rationality requires transitivity looks like it's smuggling in assumptions about sequential choice. (3) Still, I hold that intransitive preferences are irrational, but on domination grounds rather than exploitability grounds: they let you be led, by your own choices, to take a worse option over a better one. (4) Hyperbolic discounting is a genuine rational defect -- someone whose present and future choices systematically fail to hang together is doing something wrong, and I'd resist the move of just saying each time-slice is locally coherent so no problem. (5) But ordinary preference change -- becoming a parent, aging out of tastes, whatever -- is not a rational defect at all, and there's no requirement of diachronic coherence as such; your t1 and t2 selves are allowed to disagree. And (6) I tell students that Ulysses-style precommitment doesn't cure the irrationality, it just manages it -- if you need to bind yourself, something was already wrong. Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity, and say what would have to give. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, so don't be gentle.
