I teach a Thursday-evening seminar at a graphic design school, and in two weeks I have a single ninety-minute session on Otto Neurath and his picture languages — the Vienna Method, later called ISOTYPE. The students are strong visual thinkers with no philosophy background; their default frame will be "pictorial statistics," i.e., ISOTYPE as a charming ancestor of the modern infographic and the icon row on a PowerPoint slide. That frame is not exactly wrong, but it misses what made the project philosophical rather than merely a design innovation, and I want the session to end with them seeing that. I am not asking you to explain Neurath to me; I know the material. I am asking for a teaching plan.

Please give me: (1) the sequence in which the ideas must be introduced, with a note on what each step presupposes from the ones before it — in particular, where in the sequence the Vienna Circle's anti-metaphysical program and Neurath's socialism belong, and whether they must come before the picture language itself or can be folded in afterward; (2) the single carrying example the whole session should be built around, what it must show and what it must avoid showing, and why the standard move of just displaying famous ISOTYPE charts probably will not do the work; (3) the misconceptions the students predictably bring or will form — I anticipate at least "pictures are a universal language, like emoji," and "Neurath wanted pictures to replace words," and suspect there are two or three others specific to design students — and, for each, the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than corrected at the end; (4) the check I can run in the last ten minutes that would show they've grasped Neurath's actual claim rather than simply admired the graphics; and (5) what I should openly decline to cover at this level — for instance how far to go into Neurath's disagreements with other logical empiricists about the unity of language — and how to say so honestly without making it sound like unexplained depth is a failing of theirs.

Two further requests. First, since a teaching plan is a bet about what a particular audience will and won't understand, please close with a short section on where the plan is most likely to fail: which step, if the students don't take it as predicted, unravels the sequence, and what evidence during the session should lead me to reorder or abandon the plan rather than push through it. Second, keep the whole thing under 450 words — terse, in outline form where the dependency structure is the point, prose only where a justification genuinely needs a sentence or two.
