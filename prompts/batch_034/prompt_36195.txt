Two of my oldest friends have been fighting about the French Revolution for years and it never gets anywhere. One is basically a Marxist — 1789 as a bourgeois revolution, class struggle all the way down, the Terror as a response to real threats. The other is a Furet-style revisionist — the Revolution was driven by political culture and ideology, the class story doesn't survive the evidence, and half of what happened was contingency and elite panic. I've been bugging them to do a proper adversarial collaboration and actually write something down together, and they finally agreed, sort of, except they want me to draft the skeleton and I want help.

Draft the joint statement they should be able to sign. I want: the claims they can both accept about the events themselves (the fiscal crisis, the abolition of feudal privileges, the war from 1792, the Terror, the Directory); the disagreements stated precisely enough, and in wording each of them would actually endorse for his own side; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the bourgeoisie's economic position explains the specific political forms the Revolution took (constitutional monarchy, then republic, then Terror) rather than just its timing, and another is whether the Terror was baked into the revolutionary ideology from the start or was a contingent product of war and factional accident, and they talk past each other on that constantly. For each crux, say what kind of evidence or argument both sides would agree bears on it — e.g. who actually sat in the assemblies and voted for what, or comparisons with other revolutions like 1848 — and write down each side's prediction in advance about what that evidence would show, so they can't back out later.

Also, our group has this running half-joke that revolutions devour their children — Danton, Robespierre, the Russian analogues, you name it. Work out whether that saying comes out true, false, or "depends on the reading" on their two accounts, and whether they could agree on which.

Format as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Keep the disagreements real — don't collapse into "it was probably a bit of both," that's exactly the fence-sitting they'd both refuse to sign.
