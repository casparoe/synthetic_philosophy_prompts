I'm writing up my decision-theoretic views for the reading group I sit in with — we've spent the last few months working through Newcomb-style problems, and it's my turn to present my profile so the others can attack it. Before I do, I want to know whether the package below even hangs together. Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and I take that not as an eccentric verdict but as a datum: any theory that tells me to two-box has mislocated what my choice is doing, and I want to know what else such a theory gets wrong. Second, I would pay the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker, and more than that, I think being the kind of agent who pays is the right thing to be — that's not an unfortunate residue of a commitment made in ignorance but the whole point of the commitment. Third, put me in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with no communication, and tell me the other player is a near-copy of me — same training, same reasoning processes, no causal contact between us beyond the game itself — and I will cooperate; defecting there feels to me like exactly the two-boxer's error in a different coat. Fourth, and this is where my friends think I've lost the plot: I think acausal trade — cooperating with agents outside our light cone, or hypothetical superintelligences, or simulated bargaining partners, on the theory that each side will predict the other's cooperation and reciprocate — is not something anyone owes anyone. There is no contact, no agreement, no enforcement; two agents whose behaviors happen to be correlated are not thereby trading, and I'm not even sure what would fix the terms of such a deal, or who could be said to have accepted them. Fifth, on method: I think the unit of assessment in decision theory is the agent or policy, evaluated by how it fares across the environments it is liable to face, not the isolated act viewed from inside a single moment.

So audit this. Which of the five are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and what is the least costly repair in each case? I'm especially unsure whether three and four can coexist — whether the near-copy line is a principled place to stop or just the place where my nerve failed — and whether the way I motivate one-boxing in the first commitment commits me to something the fourth refuses. I can see, too, how someone might say the second is just irrationality about sunk costs, though I don't think that's right, and I'd like to know whether I'm entitled to think so.

Don't give me a menu of positions with the arguments laid out on each side; tell me what you think holds and what has to give. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and if you think one revision would clean up most of the mess, name it and say what it would cost me elsewhere.
