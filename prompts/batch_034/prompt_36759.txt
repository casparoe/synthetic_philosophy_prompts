Last night a friend and I had a long argument about Max Black's two-sphere universe, and his statement of the underlying principle has been bothering me ever since. As best I can reconstruct it, he put it to me as follows: "Leibniz's law says that if two objects share every property, they are one and the same object. Max Black constructed a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, miles apart in an otherwise empty and symmetric space, and proved by this counterexample that the principle is false. Since Leibniz stated the principle as a truth of pure logic, the counterexample refutes him decisively — though in any case the trick fails, because each sphere has the property of being that very sphere, a haecceity the other one lacks, so nothing in the example is genuinely indiscernible." He concluded that the principle is not merely false but trivially so, and that only sentimental attachment to Leibniz keeps the debate alive.

I suspect he has compressed a great deal into a few sentences and that at least one claim runs together two things careful writers keep apart — I have a vague memory that what he calls Leibniz's law is usually the converse of the principle actually under discussion — but I don't know enough philosophy to say where he goes wrong or how much of it matters. Is his summary fair? Please assess it in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: say which parts a defender of the principle or a scholar of Leibniz would accept, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether his sweeping conclusion follows from whatever survives correction.
