for a philosophy of language seminar i have to submit a short "where i stand" statement on later Wittgenstein and i'd like a consistency check before i hand it in. here's roughly what i wrote:

(1) meaning is use — if you want to know what a word means, look at how it actually gets deployed, not for some inner object or mental image it's supposed to correspond to.

(2) a lot of our central concepts ("game", "language", "number") are family resemblance concepts: there's no one property shared by all instances, just overlapping, criss-crossing similarities.

(3) still, there is a genuine fact of the matter about whether someone applies a word correctly in a new case. i'm not a relativist about this. if the whole community tomorrow starts calling the sky "red", they havent thereby changed what "red" meant — they've gone wrong, or started speaking a different language, or something. and the mathematician who is dismissed by all her contemporaries can simply be right, even though nobody around her says so.

(4) a solitary individual, from scratch, could not have language. meaning requires the possibility of checking an application against something public — that's the lesson i take from the private language stuff.

(5) i also buy the method: philosophical problems are to be dissolved by looking at how words actually work, not solved by building a theory of meaning on top of them.

my question: does this package hang together? go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs. i honestly dont know which of these i'd give up if push came to shove, so please don't go easy on any of them — including on whether the last one, taken seriously, undercuts my right to assert the first four as theses at all.
