A colleague of mine (call her R) is basically a group-parity person: she thinks a risk tool that flags members of different groups at different error rates is discriminatory full stop, and equal false positive rates across groups is the non-negotiable bit. Another colleague (call him C) is a calibration guy: he thinks the only thing a risk score owes you is that a "0.3" means 30% risk for everyone it's applied to, and that's what makes the tool honest; where you set the threshold is a policy choice, not part of the tool's fairness. They've been sniping at each other in seminars for two years, mostly past each other, and I've finally gotten them to agreed to sit down for an adversarial collaboration. I have to draft the joint statement they'd both actually sign, and I want your help writing it.

Context you can use: assume a concrete setting so it doesn't stay abstract. Say a pretrial risk-assessment tool applied in two groups where re-arrest base rates differ — you pick the numbers, e.g. 0.4 vs 0.1 — and use them to show exactly where the tradeoff bites. Both of them know the standard impossibility results from around 2016-2017 showing you can't have within-group calibration and equal error rates across groups at the same time unless base rates match or prediction is perfect. They accept those proofs; no point relitigating math.

So the document needs:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign — including about the math itself, about what a risk score is for, and about the fact that the choice is real and can't be dissolved by cleverness;
2. A disagreements table where each residual disagreement is phrased so its own side would accept the wording of its view. This is the hard part — R will veto anything that describes parity as a mere "preference," and C will veto anything that describes calibration as "merely technical";
3. The cruxes. I suspect one is whether unequal base rates in the data reflect real differences in risk or downstream injustice (and whether the tool's job includes accounting for that), and another is what "holding everyone to the same standard" even means when the groups have different distributions. But there may be others you can find.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, dataset, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — e.g. what each would expect to happen if a jurisdiction set different thresholds per group, or if base rates converged over a generation.

Also, if you think part of their dispute is verbal (say, two senses of "fair" doing work behind the scenes), say which part and why — but don't overdo it, my honest guess is most of the dispute is genuine.

Keep it usable: something I can hand to them and have them edit in an afternoon, not a treatise.
