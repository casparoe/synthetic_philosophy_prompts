Here's a small test I do when explaining interpretability to people: is "the model knows French" a fact about the network, or just a convenient label we put on a pile of weights that we're too lazy to itemize? My sociology friends tell me this is basically their whole discipline's founding argument, so now I want it done properly. I work on mechanistic interpretability of transformers, and the debate I want translated is methodological individualism vs. holism in the social sciences -- the fight over whether social phenomena must ultimately be explained at the level of individual people (their beliefs, desires, choices) or whether society-level facts -- institutions, norms, class positions, whatever -- have their own explanatory standing that isn't just a placeholder for individual-level facts. Sociologists apparently have a canonical diagram for this (macro condition filters down to individuals, individuals act, actions aggregate back up to a macro outcome -- I think it's called Coleman's boat or the bathtub or something). Translate both sides of this debate into the vocabulary I actually use every day: weights and activations, features, circuits, decomposition, lossy compression, scaling laws, emergent capabilities, agent-based simulation, distribution shift. I'm fluent in all of that and have read zero sociology, so don't assume I know any of their technical terms without unpacking them in my terms.

To be concrete about the translations I'd expect you to at least consider: the individualist saying "no macro explanation is complete without the micro mechanism" sounds to me like someone saying a behavioral claim isn't interpretable until you've found the circuit; the holist saying "social facts are real and do causal work" sounds like someone insisting that macro-level descriptions (the model knows French, the model has a world model, attention heads form induction circuits) are not just compressed weights but in some sense the right level of description, maybe even the only tractable one. The philosophers' word "supervenience" sounds like "the weights fix everything about the behavior" -- but I suspect something gets lost there, because fixing isn't the same as explaining. And the holists' favorite word, "emergence," is a loaded gun in my field ever since people showed some famous emergent-capability claims were artifacts of discontinuous benchmark metrics, so I'd want to know if the sociologists' emergence claims are vulnerable to the same deflationary move or whether theirs is a genuinely different claim.

So the deliverable is: restate both positions in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in my framework's assumptions and calling that translation -- e.g., don't just declare that individuals are "parameters" and that settles it. Then give me the residue report: which parts of their debate map cleanly onto things I actually argue about, which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. My working suspicion is that holists are just people who won't admit that macro social descriptions are lossy compressions over individuals, the way "speaks French" is lossy over weights -- but I hold that suspicion loosely. So at the end, tell me which it is: does the residue show my vocabulary is missing something the sociologists can see, or does it show their debate is less substantive than it looks? If some of their disagreement turns out to be two words for the same thing, name it; if some of it is genuine and my field simply can't express it, I want to know what exactly I can't say.
