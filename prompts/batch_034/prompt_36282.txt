So I'm a novelist, roughly two-thirds through a draft, and I've got the big setpiece coming up: three siblings and an aunt at a kitchen table, the night the youngest has to accept or decline her university offer, and the argument about who pays her rent turns into an argument about who should pay for higher education, period. One sister did her degree in Berlin and now pays German taxes for the privilege of having gone free; one brother is British and will be paying off income-contingent loans into his fifties; the aunt is American and mostly talks about her nephew's loans and her own long-since-paid-off ones from the eighties. The youngest is choosing between a program in Sweden and one in the States, so the money question is very literally on the table.

Problem: I don't want these people to be strawmen, and I've realized I can't actually tell the positions apart well enough to write the fight. I know "free tuition, Germany, Nordics good" and "US loans bad" as vibes, not as distinct positions with distinct commitments. So the argument I've drafted so far is just everyone being angry near each other. My editor has noticed.

Here's what I want: a "which view on university funding do you actually hold?" decision tree, the kind that starts from something the whole family shares (say, everyone agrees an educated population is better than an ignorant one, nobody's a full libertarian) and then walks them out to named positions at the leaves. The branch points should be concrete cases, not labels — if a question amounts to "do you believe education is a right?" my characters will just say yes and the tree dies. Cases I'd want in there somewhere, though you can swap: whether it bothers the plumber that his taxes subsidized the doctor's degree given the doctor out-earns him for life; what happens to the value of a degree when everyone has one (the arms-race / credential-inflation thing) and whether that's an argument for or against free tuition; the graduate who moves abroad right after graduating, or the one who never earns much at all — who eats that risk, the graduate or the taxpayer; whether living costs are part of the promise the way tuition is (the German distinction between no fees and no support is apparently a real fight there); and whether the aunt is right that her generation's cheap college proves it can be done, or whether the sheer number of people going now changes the math.

Leaves that must show up: flat free tuition for everyone; free tuition with strings attached (means testing, or repayment obligations if you emigrate, that kind of thing); the income-contingent-loan / graduate-tax cluster — and please be honest if your tree simply can't separate a graduate tax from income-contingent loans that get forgiven after 25 years, I'd rather you flag it than paper over it; the "a degree is a private investment so loans are fine but should be cheaper and less cruel" position; and the no-subsidy-at-all one, even if it makes my aunt sound like a villain, because someone at the table should be able to hold it without being an idiot.

Two more asks. First, build it from the logical space, not from a list of names — figure out the branch questions first, see which combinations of answers they actually allow, then locate the real positions in that space, and tell me if there are combinations nobody in the real debate occupies and why you think that slot is empty (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored? — I might put a character there). Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate, since presumably some of my family members should end up circling the same leaf from different directions and it'd be nice to know when that's inevitable rather than me failing to write it. Around 5 or 6 branch nodes and 6 or 7 leaves feels right — enough for a good fight, not so much that I lose track of who's who. Audience is smart laypeople, so gloss any econ jargon in a clause.

Sorry this got long, I've clearly been living with this argument too long. It would genuinely help — right now the draft has the Berlin sister saying "it's free there" and the aunt saying "it wasn't always" and then scene break, which my agent tells me is not a climax.
