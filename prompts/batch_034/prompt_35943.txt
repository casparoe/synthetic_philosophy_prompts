I'm writing a short paper on the metaphysics and normativity of promising, and I'd appreciate critical comments on the draft below — both on the soundness of the argument and on whether the central proposal is doing any real work.

The paper defends a view I call the "transferred authority" account of promissory obligation, against the two views I take to be the main competitors.

Draft:

It is a commonplace that promises bind, but remarkably hard to say what a promise does such that it binds. I consider two familiar accounts and find both wanting. The first, which I call the practice view, holds that promising is a conventional practice, like chess, and that the wrong of breaking a promise is the wrong of breaking the rules of the practice — just as it would be wrong to move a rook diagonally even if nobody is harmed. This correctly explains why a promise binds even when the promisee neither relies on it nor even remembers it. But it faces a difficulty: it seems to get the explanation backwards. The rules of a game bind only those who have chosen to play, and it is implausible that each individual promise is a fresh decision to enter the practice. If the practice view says we are all already players, it owes an account of how we got in the game — and no plausible account of that avoids circularity, since the natural story is that joining a practice is itself something like a promise.

The second, the reliance view, holds that a promise generates an obligation by inducing an expectation in the promisee, thereby exposing the promisee to a risk the promisor has a duty not to exploit. This nicely explains why promises bind proportionally to the reliance they induce, but it cannot explain the bindingness of promises made to people who cannot rely on them: promises to small children, to the severely forgetful, or to the dead. Yet these seem to be paradigm promises, not degenerate cases.

I propose instead that a promise is a transfer of authority. In promising to φ, the promisor grants the promisee a standing to determine that the promisor φ — a private, localized version of the authority a principal grants an agent. The obligation is not owed to a practice, nor generated by the promisee's psychological states, but is simply the mirror image of a normative power the promisee now holds. The promise is like handing someone keys: in ordinary speech you hand over the keys to your house, in promising you hand over the keys to a piece of your own will.

This account predicts, correctly I think, that promises made under duress do not bind: coerced transfers of authority, like coerced transfers of property, are defective transfers. It also explains why third parties can feel the existence of an obligation even when the promisee has forgotten: an unexercised right is still a right.

Comments requested on the following, though please raise anything else you notice:

1. The dead-promisee case is meant to be an objection to reliance views, but does my own account handle it? A dead person seems unable to hold any standing, yet I claim the promise still binds. Is this an embarrassment for my view, or can the appeal to "unexercised rights" be made to work?
2. Does the keys analogy carry any argumentative weight, or is it merely decorative?
3. Is my characterization of the two competitor views fair, or am I attacking weakened versions of them?
