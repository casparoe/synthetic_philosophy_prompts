I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice theory for philosophy majors — 14 students, one 90-minute session — and we've just finished the standard treatment of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma before moving on to Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: the thought that if you know your co-player reasons as you do, the right move may be to cooperate, since your choice is evidence about theirs even though it doesn't cause it. The students can recite the argument but don't feel its pull at all; to them, defecting in a one-shot dilemma is just obviously what a rational person does, and superrationality sounds like a parlor trick. I want an in-class game that reverses this by making them experience, rather than merely read about, the difference between playing against an arbitrary opponent and playing against someone who is effectively reasoning with the same mind.

Here is the structure I half-imagine, which you should refine, extend, or correct: students play the same one-shot prisoner's dilemma under three conditions in sequence. In the first, they're matched with a random anonymous classmate with no communication. In the second, they're told they've been paired with someone who received an identically worded briefing sheet, sat through the same lectures, and was given the same instructions — a "duplicate" pairing that the facilitator stages as convincingly as possible. In the third, some device makes the symmetry of reasoning more vivid — perhaps each player writes a short paragraph of their reasoning before choosing, and matched players exchange and read them aloud, so that each literally watches the other arrive at their conclusion. Payoffs should be real but modest (bonus points, vouchers — nothing involving cash), and I want the payoff matrix chosen so that the tension is sharp. Design the full activity: rules, materials, an exact timeline that fits 90 minutes including setup, the payoff scheme, how pairings and anonymity are actually managed in a small classroom where everyone can see everyone, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinction between causal and evidential views of the choice, and onto why iterated-play explanations of cooperation don't apply here even though students will keep reaching for them.

Two further requests. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect students will talk during the "silent" phases, or someone will defect in the duplicate condition precisely to prove the theory wrong, or the duplicate framing will collapse because the paired students realize they're different people with different grades at stake — and how I should handle each. Second, be honest about which part of superrationality no classroom game can reproduce: real students are not copies of one another, they know it, and any symmetry is a fiction maintained by the facilitator's framing rather than a metaphysical fact. Say where that fiction is load-bearing, and what the debrief has to supply that the game itself cannot. If you think one of my three conditions is doing no work, say so and propose a better one.
