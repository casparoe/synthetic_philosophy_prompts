I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions to inferences about what we can know from our own faculties and instruments, and I'd like to know whether a single principle underwrites the list, or whether some of my verdicts have to go. Looking out the window and concluding that there's a maple tree: entirely fine. Concluding, further, that I'm not a brain in a vat being fed maple-tree experiences: I'm inclined to say I know that, but not by way of that argument — and yet I can't say what's wrong with the argument, since I claim to know the premise and the conditional is trivial. At the zoo, judging from the stripes that the animal is a zebra rather than a painted mule: knowing it straight off, just by looking, seems fine; deploying it as a step toward ruling out painted mules everywhere feels off. The scarf procedure: if I check my color vision by judging that the scarf is red, noting that it looked red, scoring that a hit, and repeating until I've built a track record out of nothing but my own outputs, I think no knowledge of my reliability is gained — that one seems clearly bad. Contrast the lab that calibrates a new thermometer against a certified reference instrument: fine, presumably because the checking source is independent of the thing checked. But here is the case I can't fit: those reference thermometers were themselves calibrated, at some point, against somebody's eyes reading a mercury column, so if bootstrapping is what's wrong with the scarf procedure, it seems to infect the entire calibration tree — a conclusion I refuse to draw. And two more I go back and forth on: instruments A and B calibrated only against each other on the same afternoon, each confirmed by its agreement with the other (bad, I think); and a witness who carefully rehearses what she saw, finds her memory internally consistent, and thereby gains some confidence in her memory (somehow less bad to me, though the structure looks similar). So my question is this: is there one principle — about the independence of the checking source, or about which propositions knowledge is closed under, or something else entirely — that captures all of these? Please state it precisely, say which verdict each clause of it exists to save, tell me what it rules on some cases I haven't mentioned (say, a self-driving car whose lidar is used to check the accuracy of the lidar-built map it navigates by, or a translation app whose output I verify with a dictionary the app itself produced), and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather overturn one of my own verdicts than accept a principle with an ad hoc clause for every case — but I also won't accept a principle so weak that the scarf procedure slips through it.
