I spent most of my career as an environmental toxicologist before retiring, and I have lately been working my way through philosophy of science, so I was particularly engaged by a dispute I witnessed at a reunion dinner between two former colleagues: Marta, an ecotoxicologist who spent decades in regulatory consulting, and Jonas, who moved from bench chemistry into regulatory economics. They argued about the precautionary principle for the better part of an hour, and I found I could not referee it despite — or perhaps because of — my own background. I would be grateful if you could adjudicate the exchange move by move.

(1) Jonas opened by calling the principle self-undermining. It instructs us to take preventive action against plausible severe harms even when the scientific evidence is incomplete; but regulatory restraint itself causes foreseeable severe harms, through delayed or foregone technologies. Since every available course of action, including inaction, carries possible grave and irreversible consequences, he argued, the principle issues guidance only if we apply it selectively — that is, only if we smuggle in the very risk balancing it was meant to bypass.

(2) Marta replied that Jonas was attacking a strawman, treating the principle as a demand for zero risk when its actual content is twofold: a shift in the burden of proof under uncertainty, and a priority weighting on irreversibility. Where potential harms are irreversible and potential benefits are not, she argued, erring toward restraint is defensible even at a cost in expected value; his complaint simply assumes the situation is symmetric when it often is not.

(3) Jonas answered that the asymmetry proves too much. The harms of foregone technology are just as irreversible as the harms of adopting it — deaths from a vaccine never developed, ecosystems lost to continued reliance on an older, dirtier process. One could, he noted, construct an "antiprecautionary principle" of identical logical form urging speed under uncertainty, which suggests that invoking irreversibility does no argumentative work by itself.

(4) Marta then appealed to an epistemic asymmetry: the risks of the status quo are at least roughly known, whereas the risks of a novel intervention include unknown unknowns. Uncertainty, she said, is not symmetric ignorance spread evenly over options; it attaches specifically to the new thing.

(5) Jonas countered that this gets the epistemology backwards: we systematically scrutinize the harms of novel entities while the harms of the familiar fade into the background. Familiarity is evidence of neglect, not of safety. On Marta's reading, he said, nothing novel should ever have been adopted — no pharmaceuticals, no pasteurization, no nuclear medicine.

(6) Marta closed with what she took to be an empirical rebuttal: the historical record — thalidomide, asbestos, chlorofluorocarbons, the fluoroscopes once used to fit shoes — documents a chronic bias toward novelty, not against it, so the principle, sensibly read as severity-weighted rather than absolutist, functions as a corrective rather than a recipe for stasis.

Please assess each of the six moves, indicating where each party is right, where mistaken, and where they are talking past one another. I would also value your view on whether the dispute dissolves once one distinguishes the precautionary principle as a decision rule from the precautionary principle as an evidentiary standard governing burden of proof, and whether any formulation of it survives the objections Jonas raised. My own instincts, honed on dose-response curves, proved unhelpfully indecisive here.
