I train reinforcement-learning agents as a hobby — small game-playing projects and simulated robots, evenings and weekends; I write logistics software for a living and have no academic background in anything. Over the past two years I've gotten stuck on a question I can't shake off: when an agent in training receives negative reward, is something happening to it, or is that just a number moving a loss function? I've never studied philosophy; what I know comes from arguing at meetups and reading whatever turns up without a paywall, so I use words like "functionalism" and "valence" with very little confidence, and I'd rather my uncertainty not be visible from the podium.

Next month I'm giving a forty-minute talk at our local machine-learning meetup, whiteboard only, titled "Could the things we train feel anything?". The audience is engineers who train models for a living. They will arrive split between two snap answers — "obviously not, it's just matrix multiplication" and "obviously yes, negative reward is just pain by another name" — and both answers are arrived at too fast, in opposite directions. I don't want the talk's content from you. I want the plan for it.

Specifically, I want your thoughts on five things.

First, the order of the pieces, because I suspect the sequencing is where I'll fail. My candidate pieces are: the distinction between a danger signal and a felt badness (the thing that makes suffering more than information); the fact that in RL the reward is a teaching signal for the optimizer, not necessarily something the agent "receives" — which quietly raises the question of who the candidate sufferer even is; and a wrinkle I've never seen anyone address head-on, which is that the only phase with a reward channel is training: the deployed policy just acts, with no reward arriving at all. So if anything is a candidate for suffering it's the training run, not the finished artifact — the thing on the shelf can't be the one that felt anything, since the feeling, if any, would have been in the loop between policy, environment, and gradient updates. My instinct is that this wrinkle has to come late, after the audience already holds the signal/badness distinction, or it will be heard as a technicality rather than as the strange thing it is. Is that right, or could it open the talk as a hook?

Second, the carrying example. I've considered two: a sparse-reward game agent that gets a single minus one at failure, which seems to obviously lack whatever would be needed; and a simulated creature with persistent internal variables — a hunger-like state that drifts upward and must be corrected, so that "bad" is a continuous background condition rather than an occasional event. My instinct is that the second carries the talk and the first should be shown only afterwards, as a contrast. What properties must the carrying example have, and which must it lack, so that the audience cannot settle the question just by pointing at features of the example?

Third, the misconceptions, in the order the audience will form them rather than the order I find them interesting. I can predict some: "reward is dopamine," importing half-remembered neuroscience; "it's just math, so no," which skips a step rather than answering it; and the chatbot one, where they'll transfer the whole question onto language models — which I suspect is a genuinely different question because of the training/deployment wrinkle, but I'm not sure how to say that without sounding like I'm dodging. Where in the sequence should each be cut off? And are there misconceptions I'm missing — in particular, any that I, as the person closest to the topic, am most likely to have myself and therefore least likely to see?

Fourth, the closing check. I need one question I can ask that would show whether they understood the question rather than merely picked a side. My current attempt — "could a perfect optimizer with no internal state suffer?" — feels like it tests one sub-point instead of the whole arc.

Fifth, what to refuse to cover. I assume the hard problem proper, panpsychism, and any verdict on the moral status of current systems are out; tell me which of these I should explicitly rule out from the podium, and where I'm wrong to exclude one. I'd also like to avoid the failure mode where everyone leaves having learned only that the question is difficult — so tell me if any part of my plan, as I've described it, is already aimed at that failure.
