Next session I'm doing Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself; I know the derivation fine (knowability premise, knowledge entails truth, conjunction distributes over known conjunctions, reductio), it's the sequencing I keep second-guessing.

So: what has to be in place before I even start the derivation? My instinct is that the load-bearing move is knowledge distributing over conjunction — that if you know p and q jointly you know each — and that I should rehearse it on some mundane example first so it doesn't look like a trick introduced for the occasion. For the carrying example, I keep gravitating toward a sealed resignation letter sitting in a drawer that we stipulate will never be opened: both "there's a resignation letter in the drawer" and "nobody knows there's a resignation letter in the drawer" are true, and the punchline is that they can't both be knowable. Is that strong enough to carry the whole session, or would the undiscovered-fossil style example work better, or something else entirely — and what should the example deliberately *lack* so it doesn't muddy things (like, I worry mine tempts people toward the "well just open the drawer" response)?

Misconceptions: I'm bracing for (a) "so some truths are unknowable, big deal, that's obviously true of the future anyway," (b) "so verificationism is refuted, antirealism is dead, pack up," and (c) reading "possibly known" as "someone could go find out if they bothered," which misses the modal scope entirely. Where exactly in the sequence should I head each one off, and which of the three is most likely to survive my best efforts?
