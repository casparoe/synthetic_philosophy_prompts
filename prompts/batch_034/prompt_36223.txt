I'm on the science and ideas desk at a mid-size outlet, and my editor handed me a freelancer's long draft on "what do we actually owe each other" to fact-check before it goes to copy. The paragraph that's supposed to carry the philosophy section goes roughly like this: ever since Sen posed the question of what exactly we're supposed to equalize, philosophers have known that equality isn't one idea but a family of rival ones. His answer, the capabilities approach, says what matters is not what people have but what they can do and be — staying healthy, being literate, taking part in the life of your community. A bicycle is worthless to someone who can't ride, so the same resources leave people with very different lives. This came out of his attack on welfarism, the view that justice means giving people equal amounts of happiness — a view that sinks on the expensive-tastes problem: if my happiness needs vintage wine and yours runs on tap water, welfare equality hands me the wine cellar. Dworkin tried to patch resource egalitarianism by counting natural talents as part of your resource bundle, so the talented owe the rest of us compensation. Sen's own approach won the policy war — it's behind the UN's human development indices — though critics say capabilities are too vague to measure, and in practice they collapse into roughly what the positive psychologists track with life-satisfaction surveys anyway. The draft then concludes that if capabilities can't be measured, economists comparing income distributions are doing the only rigorous version of the work.

So here's my situation: I know enough political philosophy to smell two or three rats in there but not to locate them, and the freelancer has more degrees than I do, so I can't just go with vibes. Which of these claims would a political philosopher sign off on as basically right, which are fudges you could maybe run past a general readership with a tweak, and which would get us an angry letter from whatever department still reads us? I'm mostly suspicious of the talents-as-resources line and the bit where capabilities turn into life satisfaction, but honestly I'd be grateful if you went claim by claim rather than just validating my suspicions. And separately — does the draft's closing inference, that income comparisons are the only rigorous game in town given the measurement worry, actually follow from anything in the criticism it cites, or is that the writer sliding their own economics in the back door? Please don't give me a reading list, we don't run bibliographies and I wouldn't check it anyway. Just the audit, in terms I can turn into line edits.
