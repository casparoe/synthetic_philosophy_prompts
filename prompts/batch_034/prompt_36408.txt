I work at a proxy advisory firm, and my professional vocabulary is corporate governance. I think in terms of principals and agents, boards and management, dual-class share structures, staggered boards, controlled companies, fiduciary duties, shareholder primacy versus stakeholder governance, and co-determination of the German kind, where employees sit on the supervisory board. I have followed the debate over the European Union's supposed democratic deficit only through newspaper summaries, and I would like it translated into my vocabulary, as faithfully as possible, followed by a residue report.

Here is the debate as I understand it; correct me where my summary is off. The EU legislates through two chambers: a directly elected European Parliament, for which turnout is persistently low, and a Council of Ministers drawn from national governments that are themselves elected. The executive, the European Commission, is appointed rather than elected, and it alone may propose legislation. The Court of Justice has established that EU law has direct effect in national courts and supremacy over conflicting national law, and the founding treaties commit the member states to an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe. Critics call this arrangement a democratic deficit, and the critics divide into two camps. One camp, the parliamentarizers, wants the remedy to run through direct election: a Commission president chosen by the voters, tried out in the Spitzenkandidat arrangement, in which each European party family put forward a lead candidate and Parliament claimed the authority to install the winner, together with a stronger Parliament relative to the Council. The other camp replies that the EU is a treaty organization among states rather than a would-be state, that there is no European demos, and that demanding EU-level majority rule mistakes the nature of the enterprise; legitimacy should instead flow upward through national parliaments, which even have a procedure, the yellow card, for jointly flagging Commission proposals that trespass on matters better left to member states, or should rest on the quality of the outcomes themselves.

Now do the translation. Some mappings suggest themselves immediately, and I want you to take them seriously rather than decorative: the Commission as a management that shareholders cannot dismiss but which drafts all the resolutions; the Council as a controlling shareholder locked in by treaty; the Parliament as a supervisory board that almost nobody turns up to elect; ever closer union as a charter provision the affected shareholders cannot amend; supremacy and direct effect as the group constitution overriding the constitutions of the subsidiaries; the two camps as, respectively, a demand to collapse dual-class shares into one person one vote and a defense of the present control rights of the states. But do not stop where the analogy flatters it. Tell me where the translation distorts, and where it fails outright. Specifically:

Is democratic deficit, in my terms, an agency problem between the member states and Brussels, an agency problem between citizens and their own national governments that the EU merely exposes, or a contest over who the residual claimants on the enterprise are? Does the no-demos argument have any counterpart in corporate governance, where the register of shareholders is simply given, or does it mark a point at which my vocabulary has nothing to say, since a register of voters is not a register of a people until the voters somehow constitute themselves as one? Is there anything in governance theory that corresponds to the claim that legitimacy can be delivered by outputs, or does that claim sound in my vocabulary only as an unprincipled apology for management by results? What should I make of the observation that the two chambers resemble co-determination more than they resemble a shareholders' meeting and a management board, and does that resemblance support either camp? Finally, test the whole translation against the one episode that looks most like the exercise of a shareholder right: withdrawal from the union, as Britain attempted. Is exit a sale of one's shares, an appraisal action against the company, or something no framework of mine predicts — and if the last, say what that failure reveals.

I am not asking for a reading list or a who's-who of the scholars. I am asking for the translation, the honest accounting of what survives it, and, where something does not survive, your judgment on which side is at fault: whether corporate governance lacks the concepts the EU debate needs, or whether the democratic deficit debate was never as precise as it sounded.
