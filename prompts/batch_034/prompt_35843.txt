I am presenting a paper at a small workshop on theories of consciousness, defending a version of the higher-order thought theory on which a mental state becomes phenomenally conscious when it is represented by a distinct, non-inferential higher-order thought, where the higher-order thought is constitutive of the state's phenomenality rather than a merely causal condition. My audience will be hostile in a specific way: mostly visual neuroscientists and empirically minded philosophers who are sympathetic to first-order recurrent processing accounts and who think that consciousness arises from local cortical recurrence rather than from anything like higher-order monitoring; two or three people working within global workspace theory who will treat my view as a confused restatement of theirs; and one hard-line illusionist who denies there is any explanandum at all, plus a researcher on animal and infant cognition who has publicly argued that mice have rich conscious lives. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually face from this specific audience — not the ones I would get in a seminar of Rosenthal specialists — ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary that group would actually use (lesion data, no-report paradigms, backward masking, recurrent processing, prefrontal involvement in report), with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. Make sure to cover the objection from the prefrontal lesion literature, the worry about attributing consciousness to infants and animals or denying it, the mismatch problem where a higher-order thought misrepresents the lower-order state, and the objection that the theory makes the wrong predictions about cases where report is impossible. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why my position is weakest there.
