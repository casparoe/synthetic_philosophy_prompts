The Chinese Room argument has now been debated for over four decades, and what strikes me is not that it remains unresolved but that essentially nothing has changed in the last thirty. Searle's replies to the systems, robot, and other-minds responses were already in place by the late 1980s, and so were the counters: the observation that Searle's intuitions trade on identifying the man in the room with the room's software rather than with its hardware, the many-mansions point that even if syntax is not sufficient for semantics this does not show that no non-syntactic computational architecture could understand, and the charge that under a weak reading the argument is trivial and under a stronger one it begs the question against the functionalist. Meanwhile the live research energy drained off elsewhere — into the symbol grounding problem on one branch, into attributions of machine consciousness on another — while the core argument itself froze in place. Papers still appear, but each side rehearses its classics; I can find no case of anyone having been moved. I want a diagnosis of that stability, not another round of the debate.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two camps are not disagreeing about a single property called 'understanding'. One side treats understanding as something we attribute on the basis of capacities — if a system does everything understanding does, then withholding the word has become a verbal nicety. The other treats it as an intrinsic property a system either has or lacks, independent of our attributive practices, and insists that it cannot be read off from capacities, since the thought experiment grants all the capacities and the intuition that something is missing persists. If that is right, the impasse is a standoff between a deflationary and a realist conception of the phenomenon, and no thought experiment could move it, because each side reads any thought experiment through its own conception. But I am unsure. Perhaps the disagreement is substantive and merely very hard. Perhaps the sides are answering different questions under one name — one about what a system can do, the other about how it must be structured to do it — and the dispute is an artifact of running both through the single word 'understands'. Perhaps, alternatively, the argument is a proxy for a further disagreement about whether intentionality can be derived at all, and the room is just the stage on which that older fight is being fought.

Please give me two things. First, a diagnosis of the stalled disagreement — verbal, substantive-but-hard, two-questions-under-one-heading, or proxy for something else — supported by what the participants have actually said and done, not a survey of every reply and counter-reply. Second, an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a reformulated question, some development in cognitive science or in the practice of building AI systems, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Do not adjudicate the argument itself unless your diagnosis requires it. Limit: 700 words.
