I'm taking an upper-level seminar this semester on moral progress, and a single image has come up in nearly every session: the "expanding circle," from Peter Singer's 1981 book of that title. As it gets used in our discussions, moral concern is pictured as a set of concentric circles centered on the self — innermost the self and kin, then the tribe, the nation, humanity as a whole, and, at the expanding edge, animals, future generations, and perhaps ecosystems or even artificial minds. Moral progress is then simply the circle's growth: history is the story of the boundary being pushed outward, and the ideal is a state in which everything that could matter lies inside. My professor leans on the image to license two claims in particular, and a friend of mine in effective-altruism circles goes further with talk of "moral circle expansion" as a metric of how good a society is. The two claims are: first, that moral progress has a predictable direction, so that whatever currently sits outside the boundary is the frontier of the next expansion; and second, that a society's moral standing can be measured by its radius — how far out concern extends — with something like "moral circle completeness" as the endpoint. I've chosen to write my term paper as an audit of the metaphor rather than a defense of it, and I want to be sure I neither strawman it nor knock it down for reasons irrelevant to the work it's being made to do.

Could you start by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the center, the radius, the boundary, the concentric rings, and the act of expansion? Then I'd like the places where the mapping breaks, sorted by relevance: which disanalogies between actual moral change and literal circles actually matter for the two licensed conclusions, and which are incidental (a metaphor needn't map everywhere, only where it's being used). I have two worries I'd especially like your help with. One is that radius looks like the wrong measure of moral progress even within the image's own terms: the treatment of beings already inside the circle has changed enormously — think of the decline of cruelty within families, of dueling among insiders, or shifts toward equality among compatriots — without any expansion of the boundary, and it's not obvious the metaphor has the resources to count these as progress. The second is that the ripple image, with concern attenuating as it travels outward from a single center, seems to fit the book's original sociobiological framing — altruism evolving for kin and small groups — better than it fits reflective, principled morality, where the ordering of concern by "distance" (biological? temporal? psychological?) is exactly what's in dispute.

Finally, having put the disanalogies on the table, tell me whether either of the two licensed conclusions survives: does the metaphor actually support the claim that progress has a predictable direction, or the claim that radius measures moral standing? And if the circle fails as a picture of what happens in episodes we call moral progress, is there a better image on offer — a leveling of the terrain inside rather than a widening of its extent, a reweighting of concerns rather than an extension of them — or is moral change simply too heterogeneous to be captured by any single geometric picture? I'd rather have an honest verdict than a rescue, but if the metaphor survives for one of the two conclusions and not the other, say which and why.
