I run discussion sections for a second-year moral philosophy course, and I want to build an online diagnostic quiz titled "Which view of moral luck do you actually hold?" The pedagogical point is to sort students by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which labels they find attractive, so no question may simply ask "do you believe in moral luck?" Instead, the quiz should elicit verdicts about cases, and the scoring key should do the labeling.

Please design the quiz as follows.

First, construct the logical space before naming any positions. Cross-cut two dimensions: the kind of luck involved (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and the causal or determinism-related luck that Nagel folds in), and the object of moral assessment at stake (whether the act was wrong, how blameworthy the agent is, what it is appropriate for the agent to feel guilty about, and what sanctions or punishment would be proportionate). Positions should then be located as regions in this space, not recited from a list. Note explicitly that a student's answers may come apart across the four objects — someone can think resultant luck affects how much guilt is appropriate while denying it affects wrongness — and build the key so it can represent such mixed positions rather than forcing everyone into a monolithic view.

Second, include eight to ten diagnostic questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so a student could answer without knowing which position the answer supports. The set must contain: the two drivers who are equally careless at the wheel, only one of whom kills a child, but deployed with a twist that does its real diagnostic work — ask about the surviving driver's later insistence that his spotless record shows he is the better man; Nagel's circumstantial case (two people of the same vicious disposition in 1929 Germany, one of whom happens to be relocated abroad before history can put his vices to use), or a structurally similar case of your own devising; a constitutive case involving a temperament traceable to genes and upbringing; and a question about whether attempted murder should be sentenced like completed murder, framed so the student's answer about the law can be compared with their answer about blameworthiness. Beyond these, include at least two cases of your own invention: one exploiting the near-miss asymmetry (whether the driver whose negligence came to nothing has more reason to feel lucky than relieved of guilt), and one about temporal distance (the same negligent act, the same eventual harm, but in one case the harm arrives decades later, after an otherwise blameless life — does the intervening life change what is deserved?).

Third, provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves: the strict control principle, which rejects moral luck across the board; the common intermediate view that accepts circumstantial and constitutive luck while denying resultant luck; its mirror image, which accepts resultant luck but somehow insulates character and circumstances; full-blooded acceptance of moral luck; the two-level views on which luck affects some objects of assessment but not others; and the revisionist or deflationary reading on which the phenomenon shows that ordinary moral judgment is incoherent and our blame practices should be curtailed rather than extended. Where a leaf has no established name, say so rather than inventing one.

Fourth, flag at least one pair of leaves the instrument genuinely cannot separate — for instance, the hard-line view that one's character is not a matter of luck because it is not something that happens to you, versus the view that character was self-chosen at some prior stage — and either add a question about prior-choice cases that would separate them, or state plainly that they remain indistinguishable given the case set.

Finally, close with a short section on the unoccupied cells of the logical space: which combinations of answers no leaf represents, and for each, a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Format the output as the numbered quiz with its options, followed by the scoring key and the accompanying notes. Do not append reading suggestions; everything the quiz needs should be internal to it.
