Something I keep chewing on: pest control seems to run like three different moral systems stapled together, and I can't figure out if there's an underlying theory or just historical accident.

So first there's the thing they actually teach in agricultural extension programs, the "economic threshold": you don't spray until pest density is high enough that expected crop damage exceeds the cost of spraying. The kill/no-kill decision is literally an accounting calculation — the animal's life gets a price, and it's negative, set by how much it eats. Second, animal cruelty laws in most places specifically exempt pest control: glue-trap a mouse and let it die over a couple days and that's fine, whereas doing the same to a pet would get you prosecuted. Third, there's this whole boutique layer on top — trap-and-release mice devices, "rapid knockdown" insecticides marketed as humane, beekeepers who relocate wasp nests instead of destroying them. Same animals, three normative regimes at once: pure instrumental accounting, zero status, and quasi-welfare consideration.

What I want is the implicit theory of moral status underneath all this. Is the economic threshold just flat-out instrumentalism — a pest's interests count exactly to the extent, and only to the extent, that they conflict with ours? And if so, how do the "humane" products even fit in that frame, or are they presupposing something else entirely that coexists uneasily with the accounting? For the cruelty-law exemption: does it presuppose that pests have no moral status whatsoever, or only that whatever status they have is always and everywhere outweighed — those aren't the same view, and I'd like to know which one the law is actually committed to, if either. And then what is the word "pest" even doing in all this: is it a biological category, or a role relative to human interests, and can a single animal slide in and out of considerability as its role changes (the rat that's wildlife when it lives in the hedgerow and vermin when it enters the barn)?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory, or theories, as best you can, and be upfront about the parts where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — where several rival views of moral status would all license what exterminators and regulators actually do, so no single one is really "presupposed." I'd also like you to say what would change your mind about your reconstruction: if you read the practices as consistent with each other, what would an opponent have to point to to make that fall apart, and where is your own reading most likely to be going wrong? You can assume I know roughly what utilitarianism, rights views, and contractualism say in general, no need to rehash the intro-course stuff.
