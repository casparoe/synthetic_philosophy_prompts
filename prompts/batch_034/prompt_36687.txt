I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a long-form magazine, and for the past year much of my beat has been language: how linguists and philosophers of language think about slurs. The thing that drew me into the topic is a puzzle my readers keep finding delightful and my editors keep finding alarming — a slur seems to keep its sting in contexts where you'd expect meaning to go inert. It offends under negation, in conditionals, inside indirect reports of what someone else said; it offends when quoted, glossed, or used as an example by a speaker who plainly isn't endorsing it. A word like "damn," by contrast, seems to be mostly a matter of tone and speaker attitude, and it drains away in those same environments. My understanding is that this observation is one of the fault lines between theories that put the derogatory content of slurs in the semantics and theories that put it in pragmatics — and that the debate has consequences for when a word's harm travels with the sentence and when it comes from the person uttering it. That was fine as long as it stayed in my notebooks. It no longer does: our standards editor has read my drafts and now wants me to translate all of this into actual newsroom guidance, because our outlet keeps facing these decisions one at a time, ad hoc, and inconsistently.

So this is what I need: a decision procedure a desk editor can run in fifteen minutes on deadline, before the copy goes to the standards desk for sign-off. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence stops with a firm rule (mask it, paraphrase it, or run it verbatim), and — this is the part I most want done honestly — the points at which the theory runs out and the procedure has to hand the file to a person, because the remaining question is one of audience and harm rather than of language. Build it on whatever in the semantics-versus-pragmatics debate is actually usable: the distinction between using a word and mentioning it, the fact that slurs behave oddly under embedding in ways ordinary pejoratives don't, the status of in-group or reclaimed uses, and whatever the best account implies about whose licensing matters when the speaker is quoting someone else. Then run the procedure on four concrete cases. First, a state legislator caught on a hot microphone using the most common anti-Black racial slur, where the story is his use of the word and the question is whether masking it changes the news value. Second, a profile subject, a gay activist in his sixties, who refers to himself with the slur that was shouted at him decades ago and has asked to be quoted exactly. Third, an archival case: we are serializing passages from a nineteenth-century travel writer whose racial terminology for Indigenous peoples was standard in his era and is squarely a slur now. Fourth, a cognitive scientist whose study I'm reporting uses the slur itself as a stimulus, so her sentence is pure mention, in a scientific register, addressed to our general readership.

Two constraints on the deliverable, and one confession. The constraints: the procedure must be stated so that an editor who has never read a word of philosophy of language can apply it, and it must say where it will misfire — I want its failure modes flagged by its own author, not discovered by our readers. The confession: my instinct is that archival material should be printed as written, that bowdlerizing a historical text is a kind of lie about the past, and I've said so in internal meetings. If the procedure, honestly applied, tells me I'm wrong about the travel-writer case, I want to see that worked through rather than papered over. And finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — including whether any procedure built on the semantics and pragmatics of the word can accommodate the reader who is harmed by seeing it in print no matter what the theory says the sentence meant, or whether that reaction is a residual question the theory was never going to answer.
