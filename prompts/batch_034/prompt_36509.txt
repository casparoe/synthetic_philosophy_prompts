A while back at our local rationalist meetup (I've been in this scene since the old Overcoming Bias days, so you can assume everyone involved knows what confabulation means), two friends of mine — one a serious meditator, the other a cog-sci-flavored skeptic — got into it about whether you can trust introspection, and they eventually asked me to referee. I couldn't, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

The meditator opened by claiming that years of practice give him a kind of privileged instrument: when he reports what's happening in his mind, he says, it's like a trained observer reading a gauge, not like a civilian guessing. The skeptic fired back with the standard artillery: split-brain patients confidently inventing reasons for actions their left hemisphere didn't initiate, the Nisbett and Wilson-style experiments where people explain choices that never actually happened (choice blindness, where you swap the face someone picked and they happily defend the one they rejected), and the general moral that introspection is more like press-secretary spin than observation. The meditator said those experiments only show we're bad at explaining *why* we did things — post-hoc theorizing about causes — and that you can be totally wrong about why you picked job candidate A while being perfectly right that you're tasting coffee, feeling a pain in your knee, or (in his case) watching a thought arise and pass. Those are different faculties, he said, and the skeptic keeps scoring points against one by pretending it discredits the other. The skeptic's reply: even the supposedly rock-bottom reports are shakier than you think — there's a philosopher (Schchwitzgebel I think) who argues that when you actually press on questions like whether your visual experience has a determinate grain or whether you noticed the texture of your breath a second ago, it becomes unclear there's even a fact of the matter you're reporting on, just confidence and a story. The meditator came back with what I thought was his sharpest move: the skeptic's own evidence is built out of introspective reports — the confabulation findings come from subjects *telling experimenters what they chose and why* — so if introspection were worthless, the case against it would be worthless too; at best you get that it's a fallible instrument you calibrate against public measures, which is exactly what his meditation practice claims to do, better. The skeptic's last word was that "fallible but calibratable" quietly assumes there's a stable thing being measured, and that the calibration story smuggles in the conclusion.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall — including where each of them overreaches or concedes too much, and whether they're jointly missing some option (e.g. whether "trust your introspection" even parses as one question). Don't end on a wishy-washy "both have a point": take a definite position on how much introspective self-report deserves credence, for which class of claims, and why. And please write in American English.
