I'm a retired biochemist -- 35 years doing protein crystallography, mostly on enzymes -- and since retiring I've gotten properly into philosophy of science for the first time. Right now I'm slowly working through the first edition of Darwin's Origin, and I've hit a passage in the instinct chapter that I keep rereading without quite nailing down what he's doing. Help me unpack it.

The bit I mean, rendered as faithfully as I can without the book in front of me: Darwin sets aside several cases and says he'll confine himself to one special difficulty, one that at first appeared to him insuperable and actually fatal to his whole theory. The difficulty is the neuter or sterile females in insect communities -- the worker ants and bees. These neuters often differ widely both in instinct and in structure from both the males and the fertile females, and yet, being sterile, they cannot propagate their kind. So natural selection looks like it can't get a grip on them at all: the individuals bearing the traits can't breed, so the traits can't be inherited through them.

Here's what I want help with:

1. Why did Darwin consider this fatal to the theory, rather than just one anomaly among many? Every theory has loose ends; what was it about sterile castes specifically that he thought could sink the whole thing?

2. His attempted resolution, if I'm following it, is that selection can act on the family rather than only the individual -- that fertile parents which happened to produce useful sterile offspring would be favoured, so the community gradually ends up with well-adapted neuters. Is that really his argument in that chapter, or am I smuggling in something later? Because something feels off: if the workers can't pass anything on, the selecting has to happen one level up, on the parents or the colony as a unit. But Darwin elsewhere insists he's dealing in individual-level selection. When he leans on the family or community here, is he quietly inventing group selection, or is there a reading that keeps it individual-level (e.g., selection acting on the queen's tendency to produce a certain proportion of worker types)? Walk me through how his answer is supposed to work mechanically.

3. My reason for caring goes beyond ants. I've been reading about this idea that Darwin and Turing each pulled off the same inversion of reasoning -- competence without comprehension, design accumulating with nobody understanding anything. The neuter castes strike me as maybe the sharpest example of the Darwinian half of that: exquistically competent little machines that don't even reproduce, shaped by a process that never "sees" them directly, only their relatives. Does that reading hold up, or am I streching the passage to fit a philosophy I've already decided I like?

I'd prefer answers that stick close to what Darwin actually argues in that chapter, but a bit of the later biology is welcome where it helps clarify where his answer was solid and where it needed patching -- I dimly remember that ant genetics are odd, males being haploid and so forth. Don't give me a reading list; give me the reasoning.
