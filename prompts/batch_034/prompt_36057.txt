"Both attitudes cannot both be right, so one of them must be the more defensible, and in this essay I will argue that it is the American one.

The American Dream goes back to the Declaration of Independence, which already promised that every citizen could rise through hard work, and this promise is why Americans celebrate the successful: if anyone can make it, success is evidence of effort, and admiring winners reinforces the habits that produced them. It is a culture of aspiration, and historians have simply given it its modern name since.

Jantelagen is the opposite. The Law of Jante was set out by Aksel Sandemose in 1933 as ten rules, the gist of which is that you are not to think you are anything special. Scandinavians apply it to anyone who stands out, and Australia has the same phenomenon under the name of tall poppy syndrome, where the tallest poppies get cut down. So the developed world contains one culture that builds people up and a set of cultures that cut them down, and the question is which arrangement is healthier.

The answer is the American one, for two reasons. First, admiration motivates: people strive harder when success brings recognition than when it brings quiet resentment. Second, a culture that punishes distinction drifts towards mediocrity. This is confirmed by the fact that the Scandinavian welfare state is Jantelagen in institutional form: because nobody may be better than anyone else, the state taxes away differences in income, which shows that the attitude, once generalised, becomes egalitarian policy. The Scandinavian countries do well on happiness surveys, but that is because they are rich, not because of their attitude towards the successful, and it would be a mistake to treat their rankings as proof that Jante works — you can cut down tall poppies and still be prosperous, but prosperity does not make the cutting defensible.

The best view is therefore the American one: it is honest about ambition and it rewards contribution. The Scandinavians are right, though, that the successful owe something back, so the ideal is the American attitude towards the successful combined with a safety net for everyone else, which is roughly what the Nordic countries have in fact built."
