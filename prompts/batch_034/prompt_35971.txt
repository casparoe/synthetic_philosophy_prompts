I'm a master's student in philosophy of biology writing a thesis chapter on the species problem, and I've decided that before I referee between the rival species concepts — the biological, the phylogenetic, the morphological, the ecological, the cohesion sort — I should first work out what an adequate concept of species would even have to do. The literature keeps talking past itself because each camp is optimising for a different job description, and I'd like the job description itself made explicit before anyone gets scored on it. So write me the desiderata for an adequate species concept.

What I want is a list of genuine requirements with the motivating case behind each: the phenomena any species concept must accommodate rather than wave away. By way of seeding, the sorts of things I have in mind include that it must say something about organisms that never reproduce sexually (bdelloid rotifers are the poster child but there are far more prosaic cases), that it must cope with populations that exchange genes where they meet and diverge where they don't — ring species and the whole reticulate mess of hybridising plants — that it can't be flatly inconsistent with the population-genetic picture of what holds a lineage together or fails to, and that it has to be usable by an actual taxonomist with a drawer of museum specimens and, increasingly, a DNA barcode rather than a field study of who mates with whom. There is also a cluster of more philosophical requirements I want you to take seriously rather than dismiss: whether species must be countable if classifying is to be non-arbitrary, what the concept owes to the claim that species are individuals rather than classes with members, and whether it must yield a single principled answer across the entire tree of life or is allowed to go pluralist by domain. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it, and then be candid about which of them are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands — I suspect universality is the latter, but argue it out.

The part I most want to see is whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, in the way people allege that monophyly plus something else can't survive reticulate evolution, because if so the list itself is a result: it would show the species problem isn't waiting on a cleverer concept but on dropping a requirement. Close, briefly and without much ceremony, with how the main contenders do against the list — the biological, the phylogenetic, the morphological, and one or two others — but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard; I'll do the scoring in the thesis. A table is fine if it helps.
