I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've settled into a view about the badness of pain that I'd like stress-tested. To me, pain looks like a system with separable modules: a detection layer (nociception), a discrimination layer (locating and characterizing the stimulus), and an affective layer that tags the detection as bad and mobilizes avoidance. My view is that the badness of pain lives entirely in the affective tag — pain is bad because and only insofar as it feels bad, and a pain state correctly detected but not tagged bad is not bad at all. My main evidence is pain asymbolia: people who report that something hurts, can locate and grade it, yet remain utterly unbothered, sometimes curious or amused. To me they seem to have pain without its badness, the way you can have a monitoring dashboard with all alarms firing and no one treating it as an outage.

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: e.g., if asymbolics still show conditioned avoidance or long-term harm-tracking that their affect doesn't explain; if their calm reports turn out to be a flattened affect about everything rather than an intact pain-without-badness; or if the badness of pain partly lives in what the detection layer is *about* (tissue damage) rather than in any tag attached to it. Also assess the circularity worry: if I define pain's badness via negative affect, and then insist that truly untagged pain "wasn't really bad," have I made the view unfalsifiable by stipulation? Distinguish what a defender of the view should concede from what they can legitimately dismiss, and tell me honestly whether the view risks anything.
