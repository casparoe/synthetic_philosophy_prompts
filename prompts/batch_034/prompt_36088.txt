I am a novelist with three published novels and several years of experience teaching in an MFA program, and I think in the vocabulary of narrative craft: a character's want (the visible, scene-level goal) versus their need (the deeper thing the arc is actually about); interiority and how close or distant the point of view sits to the character's thoughts; motivation shown versus told; character consistency as a contract with the reader; backstory as the etiology of motive; stakes; dramatic irony. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've gathered that there is a long debate about weakness of will — akrasia — and that at some point economists walked into it carrying a discount function. Please translate the main positions on weakness of will into my vocabulary, one at a time, and for each one tell me what carries over cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what cannot be said at all in craft terms. Cover at least these four: the Socratic view that no one does wrong knowingly, so that every apparently self-defeating act is really ignorance — which, I suspect, maps onto my world as the claim that a character always acts on what they most vividly want, and the novelist's job is to make that want legible; Aristotle's intermediate position, in which the akratic possesses the knowledge but it is not active in him, his condition likened to that of a sleeper or a drunk — perhaps in my terms, knowledge that exists in the backstory but is not alive on the page in the scene; Donald Davidson's attempt to dissolve the paradox by separating an all-things-considered judgment from an unconditional one, so that the character knew better in one sense and not in another; and the behavioral-economics treatment of akrasia as hyperbolic discounting, in which the value of a reward collapses as it draws near and the person's preferences genuinely reverse over time rather than being overridden by anything. After the translations, give me a residue report: is there anything in the philosophical dispute that has no counterpart in how novelists talk about character, and if so, does the residue mean my craft vocabulary is missing something, or that the philosophers' disagreement is less clear than it looks? Two specific questions along the way. First: the workshop dictum that a character must want something and take action toward it — taken strictly, is that dictum a vote for the Socratic side, such that a novel literally cannot depict weakness of will, only mislabeled desire? Second: if hyperbolic discounting is right that preferences flip rather than fail, does my habit of describing a lapse as out of character turn out to conflate the reader's vantage point with the character's own? Please respond in American English, and keep the translation honest — do not import my craft assumptions and present them as findings.
