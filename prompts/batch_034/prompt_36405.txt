I teach an undergraduate seminar on philosophy of science fiction, and I'm preparing a unit on the Fermi paradox. I'd like you to read Denis Villeneuve's 2016 film Arrival, which is based on Ted Chiang's 1998 novella Story of Your Life, through the lens of Liu Cixin's dark forest hypothesis, and to tell me honestly whether the film answers the hypothesis, merely ignores it, or quietly begs the question against it.

First, the lens, so we're on the same page. In the second novel of Liu's trilogy, the sociologist Luo Ji offers a solution to the Fermi paradox built on two axioms — that survival is the primary need of civilization, and that civilizations grow and expand without limit while the total matter in the universe stays fixed — together with two auxiliary ideas: the chain of suspicion, under which no civilization can verify another's intentions across interstellar distances, and cannot verify that it is trusted, and cannot verify that it is believed to trust, and so on without end; and the technological explosion, under which a civilization that seems harmless today may become a mortal threat within a few centuries. The upshot is that the universe behaves like a dark forest: every civilization that reveals its location invites destruction, and the silence of the sky is the sound of everyone hiding. Luo Ji stakes the survival of humanity on exactly this logic.

Now the film. Twelve ships descend on twelve sites; states hoard their findings; a logogram that one team reads as an offer of a weapon, another reads as a threat to use one; General Shang issues a twenty-four-hour ultimatum, other nations follow, and the world slides toward a first strike. The crisis turns when Louise Banks, having absorbed the heptapod language deeply enough that her perception of time becomes nonlinear, experiences as a memory a future reception at which Shang tells her that her private phone call, in which she recited his wife's dying words, is what persuaded him to stand down. I want a reading that takes the film and the hypothesis equally seriously, in both directions. Specifically:

1. Does the film's resolution constitute a rebuttal of the dark forest, or does it depend on stipulations the hypothesis rules out? The chain of suspicion is supposed to be unbreakable by communication; Louise breaks it with a personal, unverifiable message that trades on grief rather than evidence. Is that an answer, or does it work only because Shang happens to be a sentimental man and the heptapods happen to be benevolent — that is, because the film casts friendly aliens and then congratulates communication for succeeding?

2. The heptapods perceive time all at once; Chiang ties this in the novella to Fermat's principle of least time in optics, and to a teleological rather than causal mode of explanation. Does an actor who can read the future dissolve the game-theoretic structure the dark forest presupposes — hidden intentions, one-shot threats, reasoning about the other's reasoning? Or must the film smuggle in a fixed and knowable future, an assumption the hypothesis never needed and arguably cannot allow, since foreknowledge of a first strike would transform the entire game?

3. Louise's own choice — knowing her daughter will die young, she has her anyway, and the film presents this as affirmation rather than tragedy. What does that imply about the heptapods' end of the bargain: they say they will need humanity's help in three thousand years. What does choosing into a foreknown future do to trust as a concept, and can the film's answer be generalized beyond the two parties, or is it irreducibly about a mother and a daughter?

4. Finally, tell me where the film resists the reading. The geopolitical brinkmanship — the hoarded data, the ultimatum, the panic — is largely the film's invention; Chiang's novella is a quieter, more contemplative story. Yet it is precisely this escalation, not the linguistics, that resembles the dark forest's picture of how states behave under uncertainty. Is the film's real claim, then, that the dark forest is a projection of our own diplomacy onto the stars — that the chains of suspicion are in us, not in the sky — or that understanding can in fact sever them?

Along the way, flag anything a game theorist would object to in the mapping: whether the heptapods' arrival is better modeled as cheap talk or as costly signaling, whether the dark forest is even coherent as a one-shot game given that first contact is asynchronous, and where the film simply never engages the hypothesis at all. Please write the reading in American English, at a level my undergraduates can follow without being watered down, and close by saying what the film adds to the debate that the hypothesis's own argumentation cannot.
