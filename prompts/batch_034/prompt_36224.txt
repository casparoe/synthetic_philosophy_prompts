I'm a graduate student in political theory writing a term paper on official state apologies for historical wrongs — the parliamentary apology to residential school survivors in Canada, the apology to the Stolen Generations in Australia, and the dozens of similar statements issued by national governments and legislatures over the past few decades. The philosophical literature I've read mostly argues about whether such apologies are owed; what I want to understand is what the practice itself takes for granted, because the details of the ritual look philosophically loaded to me, and I can't tell which of them are load-bearing.

Consider the shape the practice actually takes. The apology is issued by the state, often read by a head of government, on behalf of a nation, for acts committed long before most of the current population was born, frequently by officials who were acting within the law as it then stood. The text usually expresses sorrow and regret but the framing documents routinely include an explicit disclaimer that the statement does not constitute an admission of legal liability — so the government apologizes while simultaneously insisting it is not the sort of thing that could be blamed in court. Compensation, where it exists, is negotiated separately and often years later, so the apology is apparently meant to do something other than transfer resources. Legislatures vote on these motions, which means individual members dissent from them — some vote against, and the apology still counts as the state's. And several of the best-known apologies are addressed, at least in part, to people who were already dead when the words were spoken.

My question is what theory of collective agency and responsibility this practice presupposes, if any. Specifically: does the apology only make sense if the state is a moral agent persisting across generations, or can something weaker carry it — say, a claim about the present state's identity-constituting commitments rather than its culpability? Does the liability disclaimer show that the state is trading on a distinction between moral and legal responsibility, or does it show that the apology's function isn't apologetic at all but something else (declaratory, commemorative, reconciliatory)? Who is the addressee, given that the dead cannot receive and that much of the intended audience seems to be bystanders and descendants? And is the practice consistent with itself — could a legislature that treats majority vote as sufficient for a collective apology coherently also maintain that no living citizen bears any responsibility for what was done?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of collective responsibility as best you can, and do three further things: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, so I can see where it is philosophically underdetermined and several rival theories would license it; identify the point where the practice looks internally inconsistent, if there is one, and say whether the inconsistency is real or dissolves under a better description; and tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with one that denies corporate moral agency altogether — for instance a view on which the state can inherit an obligation to remedy injustice without inheriting anything like guilt. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. At the end, state how confident you are in the overall reconstruction and single out the point you are least sure of — my suspicion is that it will be the one about the addressee, but tell me whether you agree.
