I spent thirty-five years as a geochemist before retiring, and old habits die hard: in the lab, a construct earned its keep only if it sorted borderline cases the same way every time. Since retiring I've been reading the philosophy of emotion — the dispute over whether emotions are judgments, perceptions of value, or just feelings — and testing the theories against my own reactions. Here are my verdicts. The jolt of fear when a snake moves under my boot is the clearest emotion I know. So is my fear of a spider I know to be harmless — knowing it's harmless subtracts nothing, and if anything that case feels like the paradigm. My regret at leaving academic research for industry in 1989 seems to be nothing more than the judgment that it was a mistake, plus a taste of the counterfactual life — still an emotion, I'd say. The week of low-grade irritability I had last spring had all the feeling of an emotion, yet I'm inclined to call it a mood, not an emotion. Awe at the Milky Way on a clear night, and something very like it at a beautifully designed experiment: emotions. Revulsion at rotting seaweed is an emotion; the "disgust" I feel at a retracted paper is, I think, really anger borrowing disgust's vocabulary. And the racing heart and jitters I get from too much coffee, which I once briefly mistook for anxiety before a talk: not an emotion at all, just physiology. Is there a single principle about what emotions are that captures all of these? I can see the judgment theory failing on the harmless spider, the pure feeling theory failing on the regret, and I can't work out what the perception-of-value view says about surprise, which I would count as an emotion even though it doesn't seem to represent anything as good or bad. Please tell me what principle, if any, fits my verdicts; what that principle implies for two cases I'm genuinely unsure about — embarrassment at being caught blushing, and being moved to tears by a piece of music I know is sentimental; and which of my verdicts you would overturn first if no principle can save them all. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after, with no preamble; and please be explicit about which of my verdicts you would bet against, and what argument or consideration would move you to the other side.
