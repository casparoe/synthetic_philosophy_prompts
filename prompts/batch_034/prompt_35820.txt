Last quarter a conservation analysis established that the landscape painting I inherited from my grandfather — always spoken of in our family as a minor Dutch Golden Age work — is in fact a twentieth-century forgery, though a skillful one. What struck me after the news settled is that nothing about my experience of looking at it has changed: it is the same object it was before, and it moves me the way it always did. I've come to hold that its aesthetic value is untouched, and that what we lost is historical value — value attaching to the object's provenance and to the achievement it falsely testifies to — which we wrongly fold into our judgments of the work's beauty. Next month I defend this position in a talk to my university's philosophy department, and I need to be ready for the question period. The audience is mostly analytic aestheticians: one devoted Goodmanian who knows the autographic/allographic distinction and its treatment of the perfect fake inside out, a Wittgenstein-leaning colleague deeply suspicious of the very idea of ranking works by aesthetic value, a couple of art historians from the interdisciplinary program, and a department chair who thinks of philosophy of art as a refuge from serious work. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this particular audience will actually raise, phrased in their own vocabulary and in rough order of how much damage each could do, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where the reply is soft. Pay special attention to the Goodmanian's point that once a perfect forgery is possible, even our perception of the picture may incorporate its history. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
