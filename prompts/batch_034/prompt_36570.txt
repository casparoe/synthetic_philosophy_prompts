I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm working on a piece about how "expected value" gets weaponized in marketing — lottery ads, crypto pitch decks, sweepstakes fine print. The granddaddy of them all is the St. Petersburg game: you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if heads first shows up on flip n, you get 2^n dollars. The expected value literally comes out infinite, and yet nobody sensible would pay more than maybe twenty bucks to play, because the median player walks away with $2 and the whole infinity lives in outcomes like "first heads on flip 60," which wont happen while you are alive.

What I need for the piece is the artifact itself: a press release from "Petersburg Palace Casino & Resort" announcing their new headline game, written completely straight, around 500 words. The marketing copy should lean hard on the infinite expectation — taglines along the lines of "mathematically, no admission price is too high" — while the fine print quietly does all the actual philosophy: a payout cap at the house's total assets (which turns the game's fair price into roughly log base 2 of the bankroll, i.e. still single-digit dollars even for a big casino), a disclaimer that past performance in nearby possible worlds is no guarantee of future results, maybe a loyalty program that pays out in utils. I want at least one quote from the casino's chief actuary, who should sound increasingly haunted. The jokes have to come from getting the math right — mean vs. median, truncation at the house's bankroll, Bernoulli's old point that a dollar matters less to a rich man than a poor one — so that a decision theorist reads it and laughs instead of filing a complaint with the regulators. Deadpan throughout; no winking at the reader, the irony should be legible but never announced.

If you've got room at the end, tack on a short note to editors (2 or 3 sentences, my voice) saying which assumption the casino's math only works under — a bankroll that never runs out, or utility that's linear in money — and which of those the marketing department is hoping nobody checks.
