For the past six years I've chaired the volunteer board of a mid-sized community arts center, and every quarter we face the same kind of meeting: one or more projects we're already deep into, and the question of whether to keep funding them. Recent examples include a membership database migration that is about sixty percent complete with vendor costs ballooning, a co-production with a neighboring theater that is behind schedule and under-subscribed, and the annual winter festival that reliably loses a few thousand dollars but that our community is visibly attached to. In these meetings, board members argue by pointing at how much we've already spent and how much we'd be "throwing away" by stopping, and for years my reflex has been to slap that down with a rule I picked up somewhere: the money is gone either way, sunk costs are irrelevant, and the only thing that matters is a clean comparison of future costs against future benefits.

Lately, though, I've stopped trusting my own slap-down. Some of the reasons people give for continuing do seem to track the past in ways that might be legitimate rather than confused. Donors who funded the first phase of a project will reasonably wonder about us if we abandon it midway. Stopping and later restarting carries real costs that a simple forward-looking comparison leaves out. There's also something to the thought that our earlier selves spent that money while holding information we may have forgotten, and that discarding their decision for free is its own kind of waste. And I've noticed the pure rule cuts the other way too: anyone who wants to kill a project can reframe every dollar already spent as "sunk" and thereby win the argument without ever discussing what the project is worth going forward. So I no longer believe the question is simply whether honoring sunk costs is rational or irrational; I suspect the real question is when and in what form the past should enter the decision, and I don't have a systematic way of answering it. I have no background in economics or philosophy, so I'm missing whatever structure this question actually has.

What I'd like is a procedure the board can actually run at one of our quarterly meetings — ninety minutes, seven volunteers with no training, decisions in the range of five to fifty thousand dollars, and a need to leave the meeting with a written one-page record for the minutes. It should be an ordered set of questions, with some indication of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it yields a recommendation to continue or stop, and the points at which it has to hand the decision back to us because the question is one of values rather than facts. Crucially, it should separate the genuine fallacy from the reasons for continuing that survive scrutiny — reputational commitments, restart costs, the information embodied in past spending, effects on partners and staff — and say how each of those should be weighed rather than just named. Then run it on three cases: the database migration, the under-subscribed co-production, and the beloved money-losing festival. I'd like you to be honest about where the procedure will misfire, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and at least one case where the answer it gives goes against what the board — or frankly, what I — would prefer.
