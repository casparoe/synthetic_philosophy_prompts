I'm in a reading group that's spent most of a year on the foundations of computability, and we've hit a wall that I suspect is a wall in the field itself and not just in my living room: what is the actual epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis? Half the group thinks it's basically a definition in disguise — once you take Turing's analysis of a human working through a calculation seriously, the thesis falls out as an explication rather than a discovery, and asking whether it's true is like asking whether chess is true. The other half insists it's an empirical claim, maybe the deepest one we have, because every independent attempt to formalize effective calculability — lambda calculus, general recursiveness, Turing machines, Post systems, and all the others — landed on the exact same class of functions, and that convergence looks like a massive induction over the history of mathematics rather than a convention. And one guy in the group keeps saying both camps are answering the wrong question entirely, because all the interesting content has migrated to the physical Church-Turing thesis, the claim that no physical device can compute a non-recursive function, which he says is a claim about physics and has nothing to do with the original conceptual question at all. Someone else brought up Gandy's attempt to extend the analysis to parallel machines and whether that supports the definitional camp or the empirical one, and that argument alone lasted two sessions.

Anyway, nobody has moved an inch in months and I'm starting to suspect we don't even have one disagreement but three stacked on top of each other: a partly verbal dispute about what the word calculable is being used to mean, a formal question about whether the thesis is provable (and whether that question is even coherent given that it quantifies over an informal notion), and a live empirical question that properly belongs to physics. Is that the right carving up? Or is the definitional camp right that once you grant the analysis of the intuitive notion everything else is downstream, so the people who call it empirical are just confusing the thesis with its physical cousin? If part of the dispute is verbal, tell me which distinction would dissolve which part of it; if there's a substantive core, tell me what would in principle count as evidence against it — a hypercomputer would obviously do it for the physical version, but is there anything that could count against the original thesis itself, or is it insulated from refutation in a way that should make us suspicious of people who call it a great insight rather than a convention? For what it's worth, we've all read Turing's original paper and the standard textbook equivalence proofs, so you can assume that much — what I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a re-litigation of who proved what equivalent to what. Also happy to hear that our group is just having a bad version of a better dispute, if that's the case.
