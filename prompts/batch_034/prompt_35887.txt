I do ML for a living, mostly model selection and evaluation, and there's a rule in my world that's basically sacred: never score a model on data it was tuned on. Held-out test sets, cross-validation, the whole ritual exists because a model that fits data you already looked at tells you almost nothing -- the information got burned when you designed the thing.

So I picked up some Bayesian confirmation theory recently and ran into the "problem of old evidence," and my first reaction was "oh, this is just train/test contamination, philosophers rediscovered data leakage." The setup as I understand it: Bayesian confirmation says evidence E confirms hypothesis H when H raises the probability of E. But a lot of great science works the other way around -- Einstein didn't predict the perihelion precession of Mercury from scratch, the anomaly was known for decades and general relativity was celebrated partly because it entailed this already-known fact. And if you're a good Bayesian who already believed the Mercury data, your probability for that evidence is just 1, so it can't be raised by anything, so old evidence can never confirm anything. Which seems flatly wrong.

Translate this into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: training distribution vs deployment distribution, post-hoc model selection, the difference between a model that was fit to the data and one that merely happens to fit it, penalizing for researcher degrees of freedom, whatever maps. Then give me the residue report, because I suspect my smugness doesn't survive it:

1. My instinct says "just evaluate on held-out data." But in the historical cases there is no held-out data -- you can't rerun the solar system. Physicists did eventually get fresh tests (light bending in 1919 etc.), so was the old evidence doing zero confirmatory work and everyone just waited for the test set? That seems psychologically and historically false. What is my vocabulary even proposing here?

2. There's apparently a fix where you're supposed to counterfactually imagine away your knowledge of E and compute what you would have assigned H back then. I can't help hearing this as "pretend the training set is a test set," which sounds either trivially fine or completely unprincipled, and I genuinely can't tell which. Does this counterfactual move have any analogue in how we actually evaluate models, or is it something my vocabulary has no way to say?

3. And the part that bugs me most: when people say a hypothesis "was built to fit" old data, that sounds like it's about the causal history of how the hypothesis got made, not about the hypothesis itself. Two identical theories, one invented before the data and one invented after seeing it, get different confirmation? In ML we'd call the second one overfitting, but overfitting is about capacity and dimensionality and number of tries, not about the calendar. So is the calendar thing real or not?

Tell me which parts of the philosophical problem map cleanly onto my leakage/holdout/selection-effect concepts, which parts had to be distorted to squeeze in, and which parts my vocabulary literally cannot express. And then the verdict: is the residue there because my vocabulary is missing something, or because the philosophers' problem was less well-posed than it looked? I want to know whether "this is just data leakage" is right, almost right, or a category error that only sounds clever because the words rhyme.
