You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics and the philosophy of social science, with a working social scientist's familiarity with how survey research, demography, and market segmentation are actually conducted. Please think carefully and reason step by step. Before committing to a thesis, brainstorm several candidate analyses of the practice described below, and say why you settle on the one you do rather than its rivals. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say so plainly rather than forcing a resolution.

Here is the practice I want analyzed. Polling organizations, demographers, journalists, and marketing firms routinely divide the population into named "generations" — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z — each defined by sharp, publicly fixed birth-year cutoffs, typically fifteen to twenty years wide. Someone born in the final year of one cohort and someone born in the first year of the next receive different generational labels despite nearly identical life circumstances. The labels are then put to at least three distinct uses. First, trait attribution: pollsters and journalists report that a generation is more anxious, more politically progressive, or more likely to job-hop than its predecessors. Second, managerial prescription: consultants advise employers to manage Gen Z employees differently from Millennials, presupposing that the cited differences belong to the cohorts themselves rather than to a life stage or to the current moment. Third, blame and credit: public argument assigns responsibility to "Boomers" as a group for housing costs, carbon emissions, or political dysfunction. Meanwhile, the organizations that maintain these cutoffs acknowledge in their own methodological notes that the boundaries are analytical heuristics rather than discoveries, and that someone born just outside a boundary shares nearly everything with those born inside it. The practice continues nonetheless, and in ordinary discourse the labels are treated as picking out genuine groups with genuine properties.

What ontology of kinds, if any, does this practice presuppose? Please address the following, though you may reorganize as your argument requires. (a) Does the trait-attribution use require that generations are real kinds of some specific sort — kinds with essences, homeostatic property clusters sustained by shared formative experiences, historical kinds individuated by origin, or mere statistical summaries of a continuous birth-year variable? Distinguish carefully between what each of the three uses strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with. (b) Any plausible causal story about cohorts — a recession, a war, or a new technology hitting people at a formative age — produces gradual and overlapping effects. What, then, does drawing crisp boundaries presuppose, and is that the same thing that legitimate crisp classifications elsewhere (legal age thresholds, species boundaries) presuppose, or something more suspect? (c) What does the managerial use presuppose about cohort effects, age effects, and period effects — and can the practice, as actually practiced, even distinguish among them? (d) What view of collective responsibility or group agency does the blame discourse presuppose, given how heterogeneous every cohort is by class, race, and country? (e) Do the three uses presuppose consistent things, or do they quietly conflict? (f) Consider the self-identification loop: people come to identify with their cohort label, employers treat them according to it, and the label arguably helps produce the traits it claims to find. Does anything in the practice commit it to a view on which the kind is created rather than merely tracked — and if so, is that a defect or a defense of the practice? (g) Finally, if we replaced whatever view you take the practice to presuppose with the rival you find most defensible — for instance, treating birth year as a continuous variable and "generation" as a convenient shorthand — what concretely would have to change? Would the cutoffs go, or only the trait attributions? Could polling and marketing survive essentially unchanged while the blame discourse could not? Please justify each answer rather than merely asserting it, and keep in mind throughout the difference between a classification being useful and its corresponding kind being real.
