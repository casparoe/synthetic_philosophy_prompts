I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and my dissertation chapter is turning into a study of my advisor's methodological position before it's a study of anything else. He is a committed heterophenomenologist, and I want to predict his verdicts on some current disputes before our next meeting, partly to test whether I've actually understood the position and partly to check whether it is internally stable.

His verdicts so far: he champions binocular-rivalry and masking studies that correlate button presses with neural recordings; he dismissed a colleague's attempt to have trained meditators' fine-grained introspective reports taken as literally true descriptions of consciousness, calling it naive; yet he happily used the same meditator reports to fix event boundaries in an EEG analysis; he refuses to grant any argumentative weight to claims about what it is like to be a bat that cannot be operationalized; he regards the hard problem as an artifact of bad theorizing while insisting he is not denying that consciousness exists; and he loves saying that if a subject sincerely reports a yellow afterimage on every trial, the yellow afterimage belongs in the subject's heterophenomenological world whether or not anything yellow happened at the retina.

The new disputes I want predictions on: (1) a neurophenomenology project, in the spirit of Varela, in which phenomenologists' first-person explorations are treated as constraints on models of neural dynamics rather than as a text to be neutrally catalogued; (2) Integrated Information Theory, which derives its central measure from axioms about phenomenology itself rather than from any report-based experiment; and (3) transcripts of a language model describing its supposed visual experience.

Predict his verdict on each, tell me which rules fit his track record — I can see at least two: a fictionalist rule on which any talking system, however constituted, gets a heterophenomenological world of its own, and a stricter rule on which reports matter only insofar as they feed testable third-person models — and say which of the three cases best discriminates between those rules and what further case would settle which one he is really running.
