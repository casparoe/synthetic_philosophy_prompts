For most of the past year I've been slowly working my way through Daniel Dennett's book on consciousness, with no background in philosophy or neuroscience beyond what I picked up along the way. One thing in it keeps resisting me, and my attempts to talk it out with a friend have ended in a stalemate, so I want to lay it out properly and get an explanation that actually resolves it rather than restating it.

Here is the part I think I understand. Dennett argues that a lot of people, without realizing it, picture consciousness as happening in a special place in the brain — a spot where all the various strands of processing finally arrive and get experienced together, the way images from several projectors would land on a single screen. He calls this the Cartesian theater and says it's a place neuroscience will never find, because the brain doesn't work like that. Instead there are many parallel streams of content being edited and revised, and there is no moment at which a given piece of content officially becomes conscious. Fine. What I can't get straight is how the alternative, the multiple drafts model, is supposed to handle the cases he uses to motivate it — the ones where it really seems as though experience happens in an order that can't match the order of the brain events, like the color phi effect, where you see a dot change color and your experience of the change seems to arrive before the second dot was ever shown.

Dennett says the theories he labels Orwellian and Stalinesque — one where the brain edits the record after the fact, one where it fills in the gaps before the content reaches consciousness — turn out to be indistinguishable, and that this indistinguishability is exactly what you'd expect once you drop the theater picture. My friend says the whole argument is sleight of hand: he thinks the two theories only look indistinguishable because Dennett has already decided there's no fact of the matter about when content becomes conscious, so the argument can't be used to establish that there's no such fact, on pain of circularity. I half-suspect my friend is onto something but is also smuggling in the very assumption Dennett is attacking — the idea that there has to be a determinate moment when the second dot's color enters experience. So here is what I want explained, in plain language but without dumbing down the actual structure of the reasoning: first, whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque indistinguishability argument is meant to establish that there is no moment of becoming conscious, or only to illustrate it, and which other premises it needs to do the former; second, whether my friend's circularity objection lands, or whether it begs the question in the other direction; and third — this is the part I most want to get — what it even means to say content becomes conscious at all if there's no place and no moment where it all comes together. When I raise this, people say something like: consciousness is just content being available to control behavior, memory, and report. But a two-frame illusion where the report seems to have a definite temporal structure feels like more than mere availability, and I want to understand whether Dennett has a real answer to that feeling or whether he is, as my friend claims, just changing the subject from experience to talking about experience.
