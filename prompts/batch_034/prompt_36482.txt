I've spent the past year arguing with my housemate — a meticulous consequentialist who stopped eating chicken after working out that a single serving of chicken represents many more individual lives, each plausibly worse off on factory farms, than the equivalent serving of beef — about whether her dietary shift actually tracks the numbers. I keep pressing a contrarian line I picked up from an old exchange in the animal ethics literature: if harvesting row crops kills substantial numbers of field animals (mice, frogs, insects, ground-nesting birds, through ploughing, pesticides, and combines), then a vegetarian diet built on grains and legumes may implicate *more* deaths and possibly more suffering per calorie than a diet centered on beef from cattle that spent their lives on unbroken pasture, eating grass that nobody had to harvest. She counters that almost no beef is truly grass-finished (winter feed, haylage, and grain finishing blunt the point), that pasture is an ecological wasteland for wild animals compared even to cropland, that insect and rodent suffering can't just be summed alongside cattle welfare without some accounting for cognitive differences, and that if the animals killed in crop cultivation have lives not worth living, their deaths might be doing them a favor — a point she hates relying on. We've each conceded things along the way and we've finally agreed to stop talking past each other: we're going to write an adversarial-collaboration statement together, and I want a strong draft to bring to the table.

Draft the joint document the two of us should be able to sign. One of us — call her the "suffering-per-serving" position — holds that chicken is substantially worse than beef per meal by any reasonable welfare accounting, and that plant-based diets are better still. The other — call it the "least-harm-skeptic" position — holds that the arithmetic is far less settled than her side assumes, because wild animal suffering in crop cultivation, the welfare of pasture ecosystems, and the aggregation of wildly different minds could reverse the intuitive ordering. I want a numbered section of agreed claims: everything the two positions can both endorse about the empirical and moral terrain (e.g., the relative number of farm animals per calorie across foods, the fact that agriculture of every kind kills animals, the relevance of cognitive complexity to moral weight, the uncertainty pervading wild animal welfare estimates). Then a disagreements table, with each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would actually accept — including whether pasture-dwelling wild animals have lives net better or worse than those on cropland, whether death is a harm to an animal whose life is net-negative, how to aggregate across taxa, whether genuinely grass-finished beef exists at scale, and whether individual consumption choices shift production at all. For each disagreement, identify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us — and, where you can, name a concrete piece of evidence or a style of argument both parties would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Finally, tell us candidly which parts of our year-long fight you suspect were purely verbal (say, over what "harm" should count: suffering, deaths, or frustrated preferences) versus genuinely substantive, and why. Keep the tone of a document two people who respect each other could actually co-author: precise, a little weary, no dunking.
