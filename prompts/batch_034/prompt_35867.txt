In my philosophy of law seminar we spent two weeks on theories of punishment, and in the closing debate on whether the state is ever justified in inflicting suffering on wrongdoers, a classmate dismissed the retributivist side with the proverb "two wrongs don't make a right," as though it ended the discussion. The professor let it stand, but I've been uneasy about it since: the same proverb gets repeated to scold a child who hits back after being hit, to condemn vigilante justice, and to argue that punishment is just state-sanctioned revenge — and I suspect these uses are trading on different claims of very different quality. Before I bring it up in office hours, help me unpack it.

What are the distinct precise theses that "two wrongs don't make a right" could be expressing when aimed at a theory of punishment? Lay them out as a menu, roughly ordered from trivial to radical. For each reading, tell me: (a) whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or an outright falsehood; (b) whether it actually has any force against retributivism as such, against consequentialist justifications of punishment, or only against some hybrid; and (c) what consequence the reading has in other domains — for instance, whether it would equally condemn a defensive war, defensive harm, or forcing a thief to compensate a victim, and whether that consequence is acceptable or fatal to the reading.

Pay particular attention to the reading my classmate seemed to need: that punishing an offender is itself a wrong, so that punishment can never be justified by the prior wrong. I can see that this presupposes some account of what makes an act a wrong in the first place — whether wrongness attaches to the act-type, to the act-token, or to the agent's standing to perform it — and different presuppositions there give different versions of the argument, some of which seem to prove too much and some too little. It also seems to matter whether the two "wrongs" are claimed to be symmetric: the retributivist's whole point is that punishment of the guilty differs in kind from the original crime because of the state's authority and the offender's desert, so I'd like to know which readings of the proverb have the resources to deny that asymmetry and which just assume it away.

Finally, sketch one or two cases in the philosophy of punishment on which the plausible readings of the proverb come apart — cases where, say, the trivial reading is satisfied but the radical one is violated, or where the proverb's verdict depends entirely on which reading you pick — so I can use them to pin my classmate down. Keep the whole thing under 800 words and lead with the single reading you think my classmate actually needed and whether it survives scrutiny.
