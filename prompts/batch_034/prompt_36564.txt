I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar and am writing this term's final exam. I'd like one 90-minute essay question, worth 20 marks total, on the lottery and preface paradoxes, written so that it defeats the standard recitation in which students observe that Kyburg rejected agglomeration, wave at the preface, and stop. The question should instead force a decision on a genuine crux: whether the two paradoxes admit a single, unified solution or must be treated by dissociating them, and it should test that decision against a hybrid case rather than the textbook versions.

The hybrid case, which the students will not have seen: a demographer publishes a monograph consisting of 400 statistical claims, each the output of an independent, methodologically sound study, each of which she holds with the same 0.99 credence, and all concerning unrelated subject matters. In the preface she writes that, given the base rate of error in her field, she is confident that at least one of the 400 claims is false, though she has no idea which. The case therefore has the lottery's symmetry and purely statistical, uniform warrant, while lacking the lottery's known-exactly-one-loses structure; and it has the preface's confidence-without-knowledge character while lacking the preface's heterogeneous, per-claim warrant. The question should require students to determine which of these features — the source of warrant, the knowledge that exactly one proposition in the set is false, the homogeneity of the set — actually carries the philosophical weight, and to say what the hybrid case does to any proposed solution that leans on a supposed difference between the two paradoxes.

Please produce three things. First, the question itself, phrased for students who know Kyburg's and Makinson's paradoxes but have not encountered the hybrid; the question should state the hybrid case compactly in its own wording. Second, a model answer of about 600 words that reaches a definite verdict — unified solution or dissociation — and defends that verdict against the strongest opposing treatment of the hybrid case, not just the textbook versions. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks built on five must-hit points, with the marks allocated to each and a one-line note on how students typically fail to earn it. Two of the must-hit points must concern the hybrid case specifically. One must-hit point must concern the distinction between agglomeration (closure of rational belief under conjunction) and closure under known entailment, and it should be possible to earn those marks only by showing why some solutions preserve the latter while rejecting the former. The marking scheme should also make explicit that treating belief and high credence as interchangeable without argument, or diagnosing the preface as a merely pragmatic or assertion-norm phenomenon, costs marks.
