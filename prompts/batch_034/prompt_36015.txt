Please compare Quine's indeterminacy of translation with Wittgenstein's considerations on rule-following, as each bears on a single question: whether there can be a fact about what a word means that goes beyond all possible evidence about how the word is used.

To keep the comparison from remaining at the level of slogans, please work through the following invented case. A field linguist is studying a people whose word "gavagai" is uttered only when a rabbit is sighted far off at dusk, so that what is ever observed is a small dark silhouette on the horizon. The people consider touching dead game taboo, so there is never an occasion to see how they would use the word with a rabbit's severed parts in hand; and they keep no domestic animals that could be led past one at a time. Every probe the linguist devises — pointing, holding up drawings, timing patterns of assent and dissent — must itself be explained to the informants in sentences containing "gavagai," and so, the linguist worries, no probe can ever adjudicate between the manual that translates the word as "rabbit" and the manual that translates it as "undetached rabbit part."

I would like you to address two questions about this case.

First: the linguist's worry has the shape of a familiar rule-following concern — that no finite set of applications of a rule determines how it applies in new cases. Is the linguist's worry a legitimate transplant of that concern into the setting of translation, or does it conflate two distinct issues: the question of how a single speaker goes on to apply her own word to cases she has not yet encountered, and the question of which of several rival manuals best systematizes the totality of a community's dispositions to assent? Please be explicit about what, if anything, the two arguments share and where they part ways.

Second: does the taboo matter philosophically? That is, is the indeterminacy affecting a word whose observable use is restricted by social custom any deeper than the indeterminacy affecting a word whose use is unrestricted — or is the restriction merely a vivid reminder that all evidence for any translation is finite? In answering, please distinguish clearly between indeterminacy that arises from rival manuals fitting the same evidence equally well and any alleged indeterminacy about what the speaker herself would say next, and justify the distinction you draw.
