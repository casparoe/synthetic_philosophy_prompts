I'm in a graduate seminar on infinite ethics, and our final session is on how infinite populations wreck aggregative reasoning. The bit that has stuck with me is this: if you rank worlds by the cardinality of their sets of utility levels, then adding one blissful person to an infinitely valuable paradise makes no difference at all — a countably infinite set with one more element is still countably infinite, and the bijection that shuffles every inhabitant along one place proves it. I'd like a poem for my presentation hand-out that makes this felt rather than merely stated.

Write a sestina in which the form does the philosophical work: the six end-words are the fixed population, and the sestina's permutation is the bijection — each stanza reuses exactly the same six words in a new order, none added and none lost, so that the structure itself demonstrates that a single addition changes nothing about the whole. Then let the envoi, where all six end-words must return crowded into three lines, carry the reassertion of the moral intuition: the one person the arithmetic couldn't register was, after all, a whole world. The poem should avoid set-theoretical jargon so that a classmate who has never seen a cardinality argument still finds it a good poem, while someone who knows the result recognises exactly what the form is enacting. Please write it in British English.
