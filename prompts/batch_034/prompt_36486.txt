A podcast I listen to on the philosophy of mind recently ran an episode defending the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism, and the host's summary went roughly like this: Gödel proved that any formal system whatsoever contains arithmetic truths it cannot prove, including a statement of its own consistency. A human mathematician, looking at any such system, can always see that these unprovable statements are nonetheless true. Therefore the human mind surpasses any formal system, and since computers are formal systems, the mind cannot be a computer. The host then added that Roger Penrose extended this conclusion from mathematical reasoning to consciousness itself: genuine understanding is non-algorithmic, so no machine will ever understand mathematics the way we do, in the way that matters. Finally, he said that this argument has never really been answered — philosophers merely repeat that the human might be an inconsistent machine, which he dismissed as a desperate move — and that even Gödel himself was more sympathetic than the textbooks admit, having allowed that either minds surpass machines or there are absolutely unsolvable mathematical problems.

I found this persuasive, but a friend doing a PhD in computer science says the argument is regarded as a non-starter by nearly everyone who works on it, and I can't tell whether that's because it actually fails or because people find the conclusion unpalatable. Please audit the host's summary sentence by sentence. Which parts would a careful defender of the Lucas–Penrose line accept as an accurate statement of the argument, which parts would they have to reformulate, and which parts would a mechanist regard as misrepresentations — in particular, I want to know whether anything is being conflated between the first and second incompleteness theorems, whether the "always" in the claim that human mathematicians can always see the truth of the unprovable statements smuggles in an assumption the argument needs but doesn't state, and whether the leap from "no Turing machine replicates human mathematical understanding" to "no machine will ever understand mathematics the way we do" is the same step the argument proves or an additional one. Also assess the fairness of the host's claim that the argument has never really been answered: what are the standard mechanist replies, how strong are they, and is the host's dismissal of the inconsistent-machine reply as "desperate" a characterization of its actual weight or a rhetorical move? Finally, tell me whether the closing point about Gödel's own caution — the disjunction the host attributes to him — supports the host's argument or actually cuts against the confident conclusion he draws from it. I'd like a verdict I can act on: which parts of the episode should I keep believing, and which should I quietly drop?
