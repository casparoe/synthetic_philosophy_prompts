I am drafting a novel whose premise requires me to think through a philosophical question rather than merely gesture at one. The book centers on a retired classical scholar who, after being cryopreserved against his own stated wishes, is revived in the late twenty-first century into a world where aging has become a manageable chronic condition. He is a lifelong admirer of Cicero's Cato Maior, the dialogue on old age from 44 BCE, and much of the novel's interiority turns on whether the consolations Cicero offers him still mean anything in his new circumstances. Before I commit to my character's reaction, I want to test my own reconstruction of Cicero's position, since my narrator should be sharper on this than I am.

Here is the tension I want you to help me resolve. In the dialogue, Cicero puts the defense of old age into the mouth of Cato the Elder, then in his eighty-fourth year, speaking to Scipio Aemilianus and Laelius. Cato works through four standard complaints — that old age withdraws us from public affairs, that it weakens the body, that it deprives us of sensual pleasures, and that it brings us near death — and argues that each is either exaggerated or offset by something better: the authority of the elder statesman, the superior pleasures of conversation and study, and, borrowing heavily from Plato, the thought that the soul is not destroyed with the body. Yet the same Cato insists he is no passive accepter of decline: he describes actively waging war on old age through moderate habits, exercise, and attention to his health, and he takes up Greek literature late in life as evidence that the old mind keeps its capacity. Meanwhile, Cicero himself, writing as an Academic skeptic in the months before his own death, may not endorse everything his character says — the dialogue form gives him deniability.

So: suppose Cato, as Cicero drew him, were presented with genuine biological life extension — not vague futurism, but a therapy that could add decades of vigorous life, with no clear upper bound. Would he embrace it as the logical continuation of his stated refusal to submit to bodily decline? Or is his whole edifice of consolation secretly built on the assumption that death at the natural term is both inevitable and appropriate, such that removing the deadline collapses the argument — making his dismissal of lost sensual pleasures and his equanimity about death look like resignation dressed up as virtue? I would especially like you to address whether Cicero's famous argument for why we need not fear death survives translation into a world where death has become, in effect, optional. And I would like your view on which reading makes for the more philosophically interesting novel, since I can plausibly write my revived scholar either as betrayed by his old comfort text or as vindicated by it. Please reason from the structure of Cicero's actual arguments rather than from general clichés about the Romans and mortality.
