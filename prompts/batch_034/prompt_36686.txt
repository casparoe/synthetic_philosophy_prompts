Here is a puzzle about the state of the debate over Benacerraf's dilemma that I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated.

In his 1973 paper, Benacerraf argued that we face a tension between two desiderata for a philosophy of mathematics. On the one hand, our best semantic theories treat mathematical language on the same pattern as ordinary language: a sentence like "there are infinitely many primes" purports to be about certain objects and is true only if those objects exist and are as the sentence says, which motivates platonism about mathematical objects. On the other hand, our best epistemological theories, in the broadly causal-reliabilist tradition, suggest that knowledge requires some appropriate connection between the knower and the known; and since abstract objects, if they exist, stand outside space and time and exert no causal influence, it becomes utterly mysterious how we could know anything about them. The dilemma: satisfy the semantics and inherit an epistemology that can't license mathematical knowledge; or satisfy the epistemology by reinterpreting mathematical language along nominalist lines, at the cost of saying that a vast swath of plainly successful and apparently true mathematics is in fact false.

What strikes me is that fifty years of responses have produced no convergence whatsoever. The platonists have developed escape routes: some drop the causal requirement and opt for a more Quinean epistemology on which mathematical entities earn their keep through the indispensable role mathematics plays in our best scientific theories; others keep something like a causal constraint but argue for reliable, non-causal belief-forming mechanisms attuned to the mathematical realm. The anti-platonists, meanwhile, have built programs like Field's, which undertakes to show that mathematics is in principle dispensable to science, precisely so that the indispensability escape route can be closed off. Every argument on both sides is well understood by the other side, and no one has moved.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is this: the disagreement is not really a disagreement within the philosophy of mathematics at all. It is a disagreement about general epistemology — about what knowledge requires, what a theory of reference must do, and how much weight the surface grammar of a discourse should carry in fixing its ontological commitments — which simply gets played out on the stage of mathematics because mathematics makes the tension unusually vivid. And if that is right, there may be no fact of the matter, accessible from within the philosophy of mathematics, as to whether numbers exist. The two positions could differ on every question internal to mathematics — every theorem, every proof, every case study in the history of the discipline — while remaining equally correct, or equally beyond adjudication, at the metaphysical level; and in that case the impasse is not a hard open problem awaiting resolution but a sign that the question being contested is not one that philosophy of mathematics, considered on its own, is equipped to settle. Mathematics would then have philosophical subject matter only in a way that is ultimately derivative from general epistemology and philosophy of language, rather than generating substantive questions of its own.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a genuine question about mathematical objects that survives once the epistemological and semantic disputes are bracketed — some further fact, over and above the truth of our mathematical theories and the reliability of our methods, about which the platonist and the anti-platonist disagree? In answering, distinguish carefully between these alternatives: a substantive metaphysical disagreement in disguise; a verbal dispute about the words "exists" and "about"; two research programs answering different questions under one heading; or a case where the shared mathematics fixes everything and the residual disagreement is idle. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says and does, not with a survey of positions. And tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new distinction, a result in the semantics or epistemology of other domains, a change of question — or whether no progress should be expected, and why.

Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of my question.
