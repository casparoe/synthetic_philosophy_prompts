I'm a novelist, and I've gotten myself stuck on what I suspect is actually a philosophy problem wearing a plot-problem costume. My current book is about a type designer — let's call her Marta — who spends eight years on a typeface, and then a rival releases one where not a single glyph is a copy: every curve is nudged, the terminals are different, the contrast axis is tilted a few degrees. And yet anyone who knows typefaces looks at it and says "that's Marta's font." There's a lawsuit brewing, and the whole third act turns on a deposition where experts argue about whether anything was actually stolen. The rival's expert says you can't own an alphabet, no curve was copied, case closed. Marta's expert says the typeface is hers the way her handwriting is hers. And here's my problem: I don't know which expert is philosophically right, and I want the novel to take a side that I actually believe.

Because the question I keep circling is: what did he even take? If a letterform is just its shape, he's innocent, since no shape is identical. But then what is it that everyone recognizes instantly when they see his font? I keep noticing how weird letters are — a kid's circle-and-stick 'a', a Trajan capital 'A', my terrible cursive 'a', and the 'a' in the font you're reading right now have basically nothing geometrically in common, and yet not one person on earth would read them as different letters. So the letter can't be IN the shapes somehow. But if the letter is some essence or role or style-thing that lives above the shapes, then maybe the rival copied THAT, and maybe you can steal something that isn't a shape. (I dimly remember Hofstadter had some research program about exactly this question of how a letter gets recognized across a thousand forms — if you know about it, great, but don't stress about getting its details right.) One more wrinkle I'd love your take on: in my drafts of the backstory, Marta's own early sketches for the font look nothing like the finished glyphs — every letter mutated through dozens of versions — so is her typeface even "the same typeface" as itself, or is that also a fiction?

So, your job: act as a professional philosopher who works on categories and universals and also knows design theory, and think carefully before answering — maybe start with some quick brainstorming of the main candidate accounts of what makes every 'a' an 'a' (Platonic essence, family resemblance, functional role in a system, statistical prototype, whatever else you can come up with). Then for each account, tell me what verdict it hands down on Marta's lawsuit, and tell me honestly which one you'd defend yourself, because that's the side the deposition scene has to land on. Also please flag if you think my framing smuggles together two seperate questions — sameness of letter ('a' as 'a') versus sameness of style (Marta's font as Marta's font) — since I have a feeling I've been treating them as one thing and the novel would be smarter if it split them apart. Don't give me a bibliography, just the reasoning; I want to be able to write the scene like Marta would actually think it.
