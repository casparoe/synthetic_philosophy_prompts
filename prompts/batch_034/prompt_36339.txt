Last term in my history class we covered the Industrial Revolution, and at parents' evening I got caught in an argument between my history teacher and my uncle, who works in economic forecasting, about whether Malthus was simply wrong. The exchange went in steps, roughly as follows. (1) The teacher said the falsification is plain: Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population predicts that population, growing geometrically, will always press against a food supply that grows only arithmetically; since the nineteenth century, per-capita food production has risen while fertility has fallen, so the theory was refuted and survives today only as a name attached to other people's ideas. (2) My uncle answered that this is like calling a weather model falsified because it forecast rain for Tuesday and the rain arrived on Friday: Malthus was right about the mechanism but wrong about the clock speed — for every society before roughly 1800, he said, the Malthusian regime did bind, wages hovered near subsistence, and population did expand to absorb any productivity gains; the Industrial Revolution changed the timescale without abolishing the mechanism, so the honest verdict is that the theory was right but early. (3) The teacher replied that this is precisely the move that makes long-run prediction unfalsifiable: any prediction with an open-ended due date can be declared right but early after the fact, and a theory that cannot fail on a date cannot fail at all — if the horizon gets extended every time a deadline passes, Malthusianism has traded a testable claim for a safety net. (4) My uncle's comeback was that this rescue is not ad hoc, because it carries independent content: the theory says when the check binds and when it loosens, predicting that a society with stagnant technology and windfall resources will convert the surplus into more people rather than into higher living standards, which held for millennia and, he said, still governs any stagnant-technology setting; deferral was not a patch but a specification of background conditions, the way the boiling point of water is not falsified by a mountaintop. (5) The teacher's last word was that this concedes his point rather than answering it: once you have added the background conditions, the revised dates, and the auxiliary assumptions about technology, you no longer have Malthus's theory — you have a different theory wearing his name, and crediting him for it is ancestor worship. (6) The uncle closed by noting that Malthus hedged from early on — the later editions built in a preventive check, which Malthus called moral restraint, that delays the crunch — so the possibility of deferral was internal to the theory all along, and calling it a rescue is anachronistic. By then neither of them had noticed I was still standing there, and I honestly could not referee it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six numbered steps, say whether that specific move is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why — not just who wins overall. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing; in particular, I would like your view on whether right but early is a coherent verdict on a theory at all, or whether it quietly trades in a claim about the world for a claim about a model. Throughout your adjudication, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and at the end single out the one point you are least sure of, explaining what would have to be established to settle it.
