I'm taking a metaphysics course on personal identity, and I had actually made peace with Parfit's teletransporter: if a machine scans me, destroys this body, and produces on Mars a person with all my memories, intentions, and character, formed in the normal way, that seems to be about as good as ordinary survival, whatever we say about strict identity. Then a classmate brought up fission — the version where the surgeons copy my psychology into *two* later bodies, each of them exactly as continuous with me as my ordinary tomorrow-self would be — and he used it to run an argument I can't get out of and can't swallow. Here it is. (1) In fission, neither later person is me: numerical identity is one-one, so I can't be both, and since there is no ground whatsoever for my being one rather than the other, I can't be just one either; so I don't survive fission, not even in a single branch. (2) What each of the two bears to me is the very same relation my ordinary future self bears to me tonight — psychological connectedness of the same kind and the same degree, caused in the same normal way. (3) Whether the relation between me and a later person amounts to survival cannot depend on facts elsewhere, on whether in another room a second person also happens to stand in that relation to me; survival is between me and that person, not a comparative or extrinsic matter. (4) So the relation the fission products bear to me is not survival. (5) Since it is the same relation, and by (3) its status can't shift with context, the relation my tomorrow-self bears to me is not survival either. (6) Therefore nobody ever survives the night; going to sleep and dying are on a par, and "survival" is just a flattering name for a pattern of psychological connectedness.

The worst part is that my classmate is thrilled by the conclusion. He says this is just Parfit's view, or close to it — that identity is not what matters, that what I care about when I fall asleep is fully preserved by connectedness, so nothing of importance is lost. That move makes me suspicious in a different way: it feels like conceding (6) about "strict survival" while insisting nothing rides on it, which is suspiciously cheap. Also, I keep being drawn to (3). It seems to capture something real: imagine the scanning machine has a coin-flip switch that, if it lands heads, makes a second copy in a lab I've never heard of; how could whether *I* survive depend on a coin flip in another room that no one even looks at?

So, three questions. First, where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does — which step, and is it a hidden equivocation (e.g., between numerical identity and whatever I care about), a scope slip, or a genuinely valid derivation of a conclusion we must learn to live with? Second, why did the guilty step look so fine in the first place? Third, what does rejecting it cost: if I give up (3), I owe an account of how survival can be extrinsic without collapsing into arbitrary prejudice; if I give up part of (1), I owe a non-arbitrary way of being one of two identical candidates; and if I accept everything and take the Parfitian line, I want to know whether "identity isn't what matters" is a substantive resolution or a relabeling of the problem — in particular, whether the person on Mars can sensibly anticipate *being me*, or only something slightly weaker, and whether that slight difference could ever show up in how he should feel about tomorrow. I'd most like to keep (3) and the ordinary verdict that I survive the night, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying for whatever I reject.
