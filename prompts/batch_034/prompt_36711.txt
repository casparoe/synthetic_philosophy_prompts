I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the past month reporting a piece on machine consciousness. The centerpiece is an interview with a logician who studies self-reference, and he closed our session with an argument he described as the real moral of Gödel, Escher, Bach -- one he thinks Hofstadter circled without landing on. It bothers me because every premise sounds plausible to me, and yet the conclusion seems to prove far too much. My piece goes to the editor soon, so I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis rather than reassurance.

His argument, as I have reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Genuine self-awareness requires an accurate self-model: a system is self-aware only if it can, at least in principle, predict its own behavior -- what it will output, judge, or decide next.
(2) Any computational system is equivalent to a program, so a self-aware computer would have to be a program that predicts its own behavior.
(3) By diagonalization, no program can correctly predict the behavior of arbitrary programs: for any candidate predictor, one can build a program that does the opposite of whatever is predicted of it. He adds that a program's self-prediction is just the halting problem applied to its own source code, and that any machine that could handle its own case could handle every case, since arbitrary code can be loaded as data.
(4) So no computational system can be genuinely self-aware.
(5) We are genuinely self-aware.
(6) Therefore the mind is not a computer, and no AI system, however articulate its self-reports, is or can ever be self-aware.

Three things trouble me, and I would like your help with each. First, when I run the same premises on a human being, I get the result that unless each of us can predict our own next thought in full generality, we forfeit self-awareness too. The logician bites this bullet, saying human self-awareness is partial and approximate and therefore not what premise (1) demands -- but that sounds to me like conceding the game while insisting the game continues. Second, I suspect the real slippage is between predicting arbitrary programs and predicting oneself, somewhere in premises (2) and (3), but I cannot state the distinction cleanly, and his rejoinder about loading code as data seems at first glance to close the gap. Third, my own memory of Hofstadter's book runs the other way: there, self-reference is what makes an "I" possible at all -- the self as a tangled hierarchy, a pattern that partially and imperfectly represents itself and thereby becomes a strange loop -- which would make the Gödelian analogy a resource for the possibility of machine minds rather than an argument against it.

So, the questions. Which premise or inference in the argument should be rejected, and why did it look fine in the first place -- where exactly does the diagonalization stop doing the work it is asked to do, and does his claim that self-prediction is the halting problem applied to one's own source code survive scrutiny? What does rejecting the faulty step cost: is there a repaired version of the argument that still establishes limits on machine self-knowledge without proving the same limits about us, and if so, how much weaker is its conclusion? And finally, does the Gödelian analogy, drawn as he draws it, fit what the incompleteness and halting results actually say, or does the strange-loop picture in GEB fit those results better? Please be blunt about where the analogy is loose -- I would rather correct the logician in print than flatter him.
