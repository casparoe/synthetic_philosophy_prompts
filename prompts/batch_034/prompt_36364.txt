I spent the better part of four decades as a working physicist — lattice field theory and some phenomenology, mostly at a national laboratory — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy of science in earnest: Popper, then Kuhn, then whatever the journals are quarreling about this decade. My old department has asked me to lead a seminar session, for graduate students and a handful of emeritus colleagues, on the epistemology of theories that make no testable predictions, with string theory and the cosmological multiverse as the motivating cases. I do not want to lecture; I want to find out what my audience actually holds, and I suspect several of us hold views we cannot yet name.

So please build me a diagnostic decision tree rather than an essay. It should start from commitments everyone in that room will share — we all trust the Standard Model in its domain, we all accept what the Bell experiments settled — and terminate in leaves labeled by positions: strict falsificationism; non-empirical confirmation of the kind Richard Dawid has defended, on which the absence of alternatives and a theory's unexpected internal coherence count as genuine evidence; a Bayesian skepticism that grants the form of those arguments but finds them too weak or circular to carry any real weight; a pragmatism on which the fruitfulness of a research program, not confirmation, is what the whole question should be about; and the anthropic optimist who counts selection-effect reasoning of the kind behind the famous predicted upper bound on the cosmological constant as ordinary science rather than a retreat from it.

Deliver the tree as exactly five numbered items, with nothing before or after the list. Each item is to be one branch-point: a concrete case on which those leaves genuinely come apart, posed as a question my colleagues could answer without knowing which school it routes them toward. After each question, in a sentence or two, state what each way of answering indicates about the leaf it leads to. To convey the material I want without dictating the cases: consider a historical theory that went unchallenged for want of alternatives and later fell; an episode in which the same predicament preceded a triumph; a moment where a theory's surprising internal unification was eventually shown to be an artifact; a setting where selection effects did real inferential work; and something from my own working life, where a computation was intractable for decades and the community's confidence rested on the structure of the theory rather than on any data.

One constraint, from a lifetime in the laboratory: argue from the cases, not from the literature — several of my colleagues will not have read a page of philosophy, and I want them sorted by what they say about cases, not by which names they recognize. And within item five, please flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot cleanly separate rather than presenting the tree as sharper than it is.
