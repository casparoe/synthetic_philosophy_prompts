Before we argue anymore about whether string theory or evolutionary psychology or forensic bite-mark analysis "is science," I want the specification that argument presupposes. Write the desiderata: what an adequate criterion of demarcation has to deliver, given that the criterion will actually be used — not to settle a seminar question but to decide, say, what a public school may teach as science and what weight a regulatory agency should give a body of research. With each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable for the purpose at hand and which are merely the prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands of reason.

A few things I want you to address along the way, because I suspect the interesting work is there. First, any adequate criterion must handle the asymmetry between judging a theory and judging a practice: a community can persistently do unscientific work within a formally scientific theory, and good science gets done under theories that, taken as literally stated, nobody would call falsifiable. Decide whether the criterion is about theories, about research programs, or about the behavior of scientists, and make the desiderata match that choice. Second, there is an obvious tension between demanding falsifiability and demanding that a criterion not condemn historical science: Newtonian mechanics accumulated anomalies for decades without its practitioners becoming unscientific, so a criterion that retroactively expels successful science from the category it is defining has something wrong with it. Say whether you think that tension is a genuine joint-unsatisfiability result or can be dissolved, and if so how. Third, I want you to confront the statistical corner: modern empirical science rarely faces a single observation that refutes anything, since auxiliary hypotheses and error models absorb anomalies as a matter of routine. If a criterion requiring crisp falsifiability excludes most of modern science, either the criterion or the purpose needs revision — say which. Fourth, consider whether the criterion needs to be usable by non-experts (judges, school boards) or whether it can be a philosopher's criterion that only functions when applied by an expert community; this should show up as a desideratum either way.

You should also tell me which single requirement on your list does the most work excluding candidate criteria — I suspect it is something about ad hoc immunization rather than falsifiability itself, but argue your own view — and then, briefly, score falsificationism, Lakatos's picture of research programs with a hard core and protective belt, and a Kuhnian appeal to puzzle-solving normal science against the list. Keep the scoring short; the criteria and the motivating cases are what I want.

Please write in continuous prose only, as in a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic text, and no tables. Use full paragraphs, and make the structure of your argument carry the organization that formatting would otherwise do.
