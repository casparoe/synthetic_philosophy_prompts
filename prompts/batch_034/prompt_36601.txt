So I've been reading up on the South African TRC and something keeps bugging me. The amnesty deal worked like this: a perpetrator who came forward and gave full disclosure of all relevant facts about a politically motivated act could get individual amnesty for that act — no criminal prosecution, and no civil liability either, which meant victims couldn't even sue afterwards. But the weird part is the Act never required anyone to be sorry. You could give a flat, self-serving, totally unapologetic account of what you did, and as long as it counted as full disclosure and the act passed the political-objective and proportionality tests, you walked. Meanwhile the victim hearings ran on a completly different logic — victims testified in non-adversarial hearings where their statements were basically taken at face value (they were never cross-examined the way amnesty applicants were), and the whole point was restoring their dignity, not gathering evidence for anything. So the machine seems to run on the idea that what victims are owed is mainly knowledge — who did it, where the bodies are, why their son never came home — and that a public confession under oath is itself a form of accountability that can stand in for a trial. What theory of justice is that presupposing? Does it only make sense on a restorative view where the wrong is partly epistemic, or is it a kind of second-best story where truth is just the best currency available when prosecuting thousands of perpetrators is impossible? And I can't tell if the two halves of the commission are even consistent with each other — the amnesty committee treated confession as a cost the perpetrator pays, while the human rights violations committee treated testimony as a benefit the victim recieves. Same speech act, opposite direction of fit. Also: what would have to change if you rebuilt the whole thing on a straight retributive theory — does it just collapse, or is the disclosure requirement basically a plea bargain with a moralized label slapped on it?
