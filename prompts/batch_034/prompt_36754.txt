I'm writing a term paper on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and I've hit a boundary case I can't construct cleanly myself, so I'd like you to build it to specifications.

Popper bans prophecy — unconditional prediction of large-scale historical developments — while licensing what he calls technological forecast: conditional prediction of the form, if such-and-such conditions are brought about or persist, then so-and-so will follow. That distinction does a lot of work for him, since it's what allows him to forbid the historicist's grand predictions about history's overall direction without also forbidding economics, demography, or actuarial science. What I want is a thought experiment in which a prediction is conditional in strict logical form, and therefore passes Popper's gate as a technological forecast, while functioning in practice as exactly the historicist prophecy he meant to ban — a claim about where history is heading that its audience treats as revealing the future's fixed shape rather than as policy advice.

Specifications: the setting must be realistic (energy markets, demographic transition, or the long-run finances of a pension system — no revolutions, no singularities, no exotic technology), the horizon should be on the order of fifty years, and the conditional's antecedent must be a condition that is not under anyone's deliberate control, so that no government or coalition could simply choose to make it false; something like if no presently unforeseeable breakthrough occurs, then such-and-such is the right kind of antecedent, whereas if we fail to raise the retirement age is not, and I'd like you to stick to the first kind. Moreover, the prophecy-like character should come from the audience rather than the predictor: the forecast must be one that people who cite it in public debate treat as a statement about where things are inevitably going, not as a menu of options. After presenting the scenario, please do four things: walk through why it slips past the prophecy/forecast distinction as Popper draws it; identify which single feature of the case is doing the work; show what happens to the case if that feature is removed (I expect it then collapses back into ordinary, unobjectionable technological forecasting, but I want that argued rather than assumed); and then give the Popperian's best defense — I suspect the repair involves demanding that licensed conditionals have antecedents a policymaker could actually act on, but I'd like you to assess whether that repair costs Popper anything elsewhere in his argument. The point of the case, if it works, is to show that the prophecy/forecast distinction cannot do the anti-historicist work Popper assigns it without importing an independent and non-obvious restriction on what the antecedents may look like.
