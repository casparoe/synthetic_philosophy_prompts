The essay question was:

"The historian cannot escape the present: the concepts available for description, the standards available for judgement, and the questions worth asking are all in some sense ours rather than theirs. Distinguish the different ways this claim could be true, and assess which of them, if any, the working historian could avoid. Illustrate with a case of your own choosing."

And here is the student's answer:

As Collingwood put it, "The past is a foreign country: they do things differently there." If historians could really live in that foreign country, presentism would not be a problem at all. The question is whether they can, and I will argue that they can, at least far enough to make fair judgement possible. My test case is the British response to the Irish famine.

Presentism means judging the past by the standards of the present. It is essentially the same thing as what Butterfield called Whig history, except turned on its head: where the Whig reads the past as a story of progress towards us, the presentist reads it backwards through our disapproval of them. In both cases something that is not past — a verdict — is smuggled in.

Between 1845 and 1852 roughly a million people died in Ireland and a similar number emigrated while officials like Trevelyan treated the famine as, at most, an occasion for public works, because they held that the market had to be left to correct itself. A presentist historian condemns them as guilty of something like a crime against humanity, a concept that did not exist in their world, and that is unfair in the same way it would be unfair to blame a medieval peasant for not believing in germs. What is fair is measuring them against their own standards, and there they do badly enough: they professed Christian charity and the civilising mission of empire while a million people died under their administration. Judging them by their own lights is more damning, not less.

The deeper worry is that even description is contaminated: to call the relief policy "inadequate" is already to measure it against our own idea of adequacy. But Collingwood's re-enactment answers this. The historian re-thinks the thought of the historical agent in its context, and in doing so he becomes, for that episode, the agent himself, thinking with the agent's own concepts rather than ours. So provided the historian suspends judgement, understanding without condemning, she escapes the present altogether. The concepts problem and the standards problem have the same solution: withhold the verdict and re-enact the thought.

Finally, selection. No historian studies the famine because it mattered to people in the 1840s; we study it because it matters now, to Ireland and to arguments about empire. This is unavoidable, but it does not distort anything — it only sets the agenda, and an agenda is not a bias.

In conclusion, since we can never fully get outside our own standpoint, there is no neutral ground from which one set of standards could be shown better than another, and the choice between them is arbitrary. Every history is equally a product of its own time, and the honest thing is to admit it.
