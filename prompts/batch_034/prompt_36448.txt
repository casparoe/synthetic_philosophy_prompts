I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm toying with writing a piece about weird corners of decision theory. Somewhere along the way I've talked myself into a position and want it stress-tested: surrogate goals as a defense against acausal extortion.

The setup, briefly: imagine a very capable AI (or just a person, really) that can be threatened not only by actors in the here and now, but by hypothetical agents who can predict its behavior -- say, a simulator or distant future actor who says "hand over resources or things go badly for you in a world I control." The worry is that a rational agent pays whenever paying is cheaper than the threatened harm, and if alot of would-be extortionists can run that calculation, the agent gets bled dry by demands it can't distinguish from noise. The surrogate goal idea is that the agent commits, in advance and publicly, to treating threats against something it doesn't intrinsically care about -- call it a random patch of desert -- exactly as it treats threats against what it actually values. Now every extortion attempt, however small, triggers the same never-negotiate response, so extortion becomes unprofitable across the board, and in equilibrium nobody extorts anyone and the commitment never actually has to be executed. You get protection for free, sort of.

I find this pretty convincing and would like to know what would count against it. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: does the whole thing lean on a contested decision theory, the kind that one-boxes and refuses blackmail even once the threat is already in motion? What would have to be true of the extortionists -- how rational, how good at observing the commitment -- for the scheme to actually work, and what happens when those assumptions fail? Does the commitment itself open up new attack surfaces, like actors who deliberately provoke the costly never-negotiate response to hurt you? And since I'd be writing this up for a general audience: please keep clear about what's genuinely settled here versus what's still hotly contested among people who actually work on this stuff -- I dont want to present some LessWrong-adjacent position as expert consensus in print.
