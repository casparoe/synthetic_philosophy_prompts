I'm in a philosophy of language reading group and we've spent most of a year on the semantics of slurs, and the debate has gone exactly nowhere. Everyone knows everyone else's arguments, nobody budges an inch. I don't want you to pick a winner — I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is.

The positions, roughly. Camp one holds that the derogation is part of literal, truth-conditional meaning: a slur doesn't just denote the group, it asserts something contemptuous about them, so "X is a [slur]" and "X is a [neutral term]" differ in what they say. Camp two agrees the contempt is real but says it's not at-issue content — it's more like a conventional implicature or presupposition, the kind of thing that projects out of negation ("he's not a [slur], he's a decent guy" still drips contempt) and can't be coherently cancelled ("he's a [slur], but no offense intended" just doesn't work). Camp three says nothing semantic is going on at all: slurs are neutral group terms deployed with contempt, and all the sting is pragmatics plus brutal history. And there's a sub-fight over whether slurs express an attitude (making them closer to interjections than predicates) or describe one.

The data everyone circles around is embedding: slurs under negation, in conditionals, in belief reports — when I say "he claimed they're [slur]" I seem to report the word without endorsing the view — plus in-group reclamation. Each camp reads the exact same embeddings its own way, which is what makes me suspect the fight isn't really about the data. My working hypothesis is that it's about where the semantics/pragmatics line gets drawn, and since nobody agrees where that line sits for literally any expression, the dispute might be verbal. Or maybe camp three is answering a different question entirely — what these words do to people and communities — while camps one and two are answering what sentences containing them encode, and the three camps just haven't noticed they're not in the same argument.

So: substantive, verbal, or several questions under one heading? And what would progress even look like here — a decisive test of embedding behavior, an agreed criterion for the semantics/pragmatics boundary, or just giving up on the boundary question and asking about the words' social function instead? Be honest if you think no progress should be expected and why. And one more thing: if my setup is confused, or if one of these camps as I've described it doesn't really exist as a serious position, say that plainly instead of politely surveying the views back at me. I'd rather be corrected than flattered.
