The debate between selected-effect (etiological) and causal-role accounts of biological function has now run for roughly half a century, and from where I sit it looks like a fixed circle. The etiological side says the function of the heart is what hearts were selected for doing -- circulating blood, not making thumping noises -- and trades on two strengths: it answers the why-is-this-trait-here question, and it makes sense of malfunction, since a diseased heart's function is still to pump blood even though it can't do it. The causal-role side says the function of a component is its contribution to a system-level capacity we've specified, which is what working biologists seem to mean when they assign functions in physiology and molecular biology, and which also applies where there is no selection history to appeal to -- developmentally novel traits, alien biochemistry, perhaps the very first self-replicating systems. Then the familiar exchange: causal-role theorists point out that on the etiological account a trait that has never yet performed its selection-relevant activity has that function anyway, and that first-time uses look badly handled; etiological theorists point out that causal-role accounts let you gerrymander the system so that almost any causal contribution counts as a function, and that they cannot distinguish function from mere effect. Then come the repair jobs: weak etiological accounts, modern-history accounts, accounts of derived and relational proper functions, generalized selected-effect theories that count non-selective trait-type-cum-process histories as etiologies. Each repair preserves some intuition by giving up something the original account was supposed to deliver, and the cycle begins again. Meanwhile a large pluralist middle has formed, saying roughly that there are two legitimate concepts and everyone should calm down -- and then the two camps keep arguing anyway, which is itself a datum worth explaining.

I need a diagnosis, not a survey of the positions, which I know. My own working hypothesis is that this is a verbal dispute in the strict sense: two groups using one word for two distinct concepts, one backward-looking and explanatory (what a trait is for, given how it came to be), one forward-looking and organizational (what a part contributes to a capacity of a containing system), and that once the ambiguity is removed the remaining disagreements are just sociological. But I have two worries about that hypothesis, and I'd like you to assess them. First, the malfunction issue: in the philosophy of medicine, the standard account of disorder analyzes it as harmful dysfunction, and it seems that only an etiological reading of dysfunction delivers the normativity that job requires -- an appendix can fail to contribute to any capacity without being diseased. If one concept has a theoretical load the other cannot carry, that looks like a substantive dispute about which concept deserves the word's normative work, not a mere ambiguity. Second, the definitional arms race: if the dispute were merely verbal, the repair jobs should have converged or died out, yet each new etiological amendment is defended as capturing what the causal-role view captures without its costs -- which suggests the two camps do think they are competing to analyze a single concept.

So: is the impasse verbal, substantive, or something else -- for instance, a disagreement about what the biological sciences actually need from the concept, or a clash of starting intuitions about whether normativity in nature can be history-free? Where, exactly, do the pluralists' two concepts make contact, if they do? Does the medical case genuinely force a choice, or could a causal-role theorist give an alternative story of disorder that doesn't trade on malfunction? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical result from, say, systems biology or synthetic biology, a decision to divide the labor and stop using one word, or an argument that the dispute is ill-posed? Please engage with what the two sides actually claim rather than re-describing them, and be candid about whether you think anything could end this debate or whether the pluralists' truce is the rational terminus.
