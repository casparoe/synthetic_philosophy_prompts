I've settled into a pretty specific egalitarian position and want to know how exposed it actually is. The view: equality should be measured in resources, not welfare or happiness, but with a hypothetical insurance machinery on top. Compensation for brute bad luck (disabilities, bad native talents) should equal what people would have insured for behind ignorance of their own circumstances; risks you knowingly took on get nothing. And lately I've added a twist my friend hates: payouts should be in kind where possible — the state hands the blind person a guide dog and training rather than a check — because cash compensation prices disability in the currency of the able-bodied and lets people trade away what they actually need. What would count against this whole package? I want the defeaters ranked, from decicive down to merely awkward — expensive tastes, adaptive preferences, the brute/option luck line going fuzzy (is being born with a tendency to gamble, or to be careless, itself brute luck?), the sober subsidizing the lush, and above all whether my in-kind amendment quietly turns the whole thing into a capabilities view in denial, which would make my "resource egalitarian" label false advertising. Which of these would defenders of this kind of view concede as real costs, and which would they wave off? And has the brute/option luck distinction been qualified so many times that it can absorb any counterexample you throw at it — and if so, is that fatal for a view of this kind, or is it just how reflective equilibrium works?
