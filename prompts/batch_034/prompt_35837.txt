So, context: I'm a safety engineer at a fulfillment center and I just got voluntold into chairing the incident review panel. We run about 40 autonomous forklifts and tug units on the floor mixed in with people, and roughly once a month something happens — a near miss, an injury, a dropped pallet — and the panel has to write up who, if anything, is answerable. Not legal liability, that's for lawyers and insurers; our writeups drive retraining, sanctions, and process changes, and they go in a file management actually reads. The last chair had basically one question, "who was closest to the button?", which everyone agrees was terrible, and after a messy incident last month half the floor thinks the panel exists to protect management while the other half thinks it exists to punish operators for the robots' behavior. So I'm rebuilding the whole thing.

Here's my suspicion, from the philosophy of responsibility stuff I've been reading my way through: when an autonomous system does something, it can look like nobody is responsible — the programmer didn't foresee it, the operator couldn't intervene in time, the manager just followed the vendor's spec — and people call this a responsibility gap. But I bet most cases that LOOK like gaps aren't. Either the relevant knowledge was somewhere in the organization (the many hands thing), or the specific event wasn't foreseeable but the kind of risk was, or someone quietly accepted the risk when they signed off. And apparently there's machinery for this that I only half-understand: tracing responsibility back through contributions, the difference between being at fault for the past and being answerable for fixing things going forward. I need that machinery turned into a procedure that five people (two engineers, an ops lead, an HR rep, me) can run in a 90-minute session per incident, none of us with a philosophy degree, and the HR rep has to sign off on anything involving a sanction.

So please write me that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure concludes "no individual is answerable, this is a genuine gap, and here's what that means for the writeup" versus "the gap is only apparent, keep tracing," and the points where the panel just has to make a values call and the procedure should admit it's out of its depth rather than dress the judgment up as a step. Then run it on three incidents from our past year: (1) a tug unit rerouted around a spilled pallet and pinned a picker's cart against a rack — the trajectory wasn't in any test plan, no one could have named it in advance, and the shift operator's hand controller was in a locker across the floor; (2) a forklift refused to yield in a narrow aisle (it's programmed to claim the aisle), a new hire jumped back onto a wet floor and tore a ligament — except the floor-safety scanner had been erroring for six weeks, and the maintenance log shows three seperate people knew and nobody escalated; (3) the one I dread: management set a cycle-time target last spring, everyone below them executed reasonably, and that target is plausibly what pushed the vendor into disabling a conservative slowdown zone — nobody on my panel wants to write up the VP who wrote the target, but if the procedure conveniently stops one level below him, the floor will say we protect our own. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire and what it flatly can't do — including whether it can say anything at all about case 3 before the lawyers take it away from us.
