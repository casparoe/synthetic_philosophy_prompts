I am a novelist working on my fourth book, and before I go further I would value a rigorous outside reading of a craft essay I have drafted for a literary magazine. I should say at the outset that I want you to treat me as a thinker to be pressed, not a writer to be flattered; my training is in sentences, not arguments, and I suspect the essay's weakest spots are exactly where I would not notice.

Some background. The novel draws on the surviving papers of a household I will call Ashwell, an estate in northern England in the 1860s. What survives is this: the steward's wage book, the mistress's letters to her own mother (her mother's replies are lost), a room-by-room inventory, and a recipe book kept by the cook. The servants exist in these papers as entries and almost nothing else. The nursery maid appears exactly once, in the wage book, as "E. Wilmot," with the single word "Left" beside her name in April 1863. One letter of the mistress's refers obliquely to "sad business at Easter" and says no more.

Here is the draft.

---

Every verb in the wage book belongs to the house. Wages were allowed; a place was given; a servant left. I have come to think of this as the ledger's passive voice: the grammar itself assigns agency upward, so that even the one moment in which a servant acts — leaving — is recorded in a form that tells us nothing about whether she walked out, was dismissed, was sent away pregnant, or died. The archive does not merely omit the servants' lives; it is built in such a way that certain things cannot be said in it at all.

This has forced me to distinguish three silences, and the distinction is the spine of my method. First, the silences of the hand: what no one thought worth writing down — what E. Wilmot thought of anyone, whether the cook's recipes were her own. Second, the silences of the rule: what the forms were incapable of expressing — the wage book has a column for leaving and no column for why, so the word "Left" absorbs dismissal, flight, and death into one blank. Third, the silences of loss: what was perhaps written and did not survive — the mother's replies, which I conjecture were kept and later destroyed. An anthropologist I admire, Michel-Rolph Trouillot, argued in a book on the Haitian revolution that silences enter at distinct stages in the making of history — at the making of sources, the assembling of archives, the writing of narratives, and again in retrospect — and my three silences are, I think, a domestic version of that, though I am not certain the analogy holds at the scale of one household.

Out of the second silence I have drawn a rule for the novel: I invent nothing the record would have caught. I may give E. Wilmot thoughts, since no record could ever have held them. I may not give her an event — no discovered letter, no scene with the steward, no pregnancy — because events leave traces, and Ashwell's records, kept as they were, would have caught any event large enough to carry a chapter. The constraint is what separates witness from ventriloquism: the archive silenced her hand, never her mind, and I will write only on the side of the mind.

A writer in my group objects that I have this exactly backwards. The mind, she says, is the one thing no record of any kind, however meticulous, could ever contain; inventing E. Wilmot's thoughts is therefore the purest appropriation in the whole enterprise, while an event, whatever its liberties, at least belongs to the same shared world the archive surveyed. She thinks my rule licenses the greater theft and forbids the smaller one.

I will confess the ending to you as well. In the final paragraph I concede that my constraint may be a ritual performed for my own benefit: the dead cannot be harmed, and the reader cannot verify a single one of my inventions, so why the discipline? My attempted answer is that the constraint is not for E. Wilmot but for the reader — it certifies that the novel knows the difference between the world and invention, and that a reader can therefore trust the book's interiority in a way she could not trust an unconstrained one. I then admit I cannot tell whether this is a rescue or a sophistry.

---

What I would like from you, then, are comments in three parts.

First, the taxonomy. Press on whether the silences of the hand and the silences of the rule are really distinct, or whether they collapse at exactly the point that matters: one might argue that no one wrote down the servants' inner lives because a rule, tacit or explicit, made them unwritable, in which case my first two silences are one silence seen from two angles, and my method inherits a distinction it cannot defend.

Second, the constraint and the objection to it. Take my colleague's argument seriously and tell me who has the better of it — or whether the dispute rests on an assumption about what an archive is *for* that neither of us has stated.

Third, the concession. Does relocating the beneficiary of the constraint from the dead servant to the living reader save the essay's claim, or does it quietly abandon everything the essay was asserting up to that point? If you think the paragraph is sophistry, say so and say why.

Comment on the argumentation; do not comment on my prose style, and do not suggest further reading — I am interested in what you can establish on the merits, here, from the essay in front of you.
