In my graduate seminar on reference we've just finished the Kripke–Putnam material, and I'm struck by how quickly students affirm that water is necessarily H2O and that this is knowable only a posteriori, without noticing how many independent commitments that single verdict bundles together. Give me your credence, as a number, that a sentence like "water is H2O" expresses a truth that is both metaphysically necessary and genuinely a posteriori — but more importantly, give me the decomposition. At minimum I want terms for: (a) the probability that natural-kind terms are directly referential rather than descriptivist; (b) the probability that, given direct reference, the underlying chemical structure is an essence of the kind rather than a contingent role-property — here Putnam's jade case, where a term fixed by centuries of use turned out to cover two chemically unrelated minerals, seems to me the strongest pressure point; (c) the probability that the a-posteriori component survives the two-dimensionalist maneuver, i.e., the thought that conditionals like "if the actual watery stuff is H2O, then water is H2O" are available a priori, so the residual necessity is not a discovery about the world; and (d) a generalization term: suppose Twin-Earth's clear, drinkable, ocean-filling stuff is three chemically distinct compounds, each indistinguishable in ordinary use and mixed across regions — how much of your credence rests on water happening to be a clean, single kind? One line of justification per term, then the combination. Finally, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or finding would move it most. Throughout, please distinguish what is broadly settled (as I take the rigidity of ordinary proper names to be, though say if you disagree) from what remains genuinely contested among specialists — essentialism about natural kinds and the two-dimensionalist reply are both live — and do not present either side of those controversies as the consensus view.
