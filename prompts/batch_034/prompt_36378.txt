I've been arguing with my roommate about death (morbid, I know, but it started because we watched a documentary about cryonics). She's basically an Epicurean: she says death can't be bad for you, because when you're dead there's no you around for anything to be bad for. No subject of harm, no harm. I keep reaching for the standard reply, the deprivation account — death is bad because it robs you of the goods your future would have contained, the same way a stolen lottery ticket is bad even if you never learn it was stolen. But she has two comebacks I find annoyingly hard to answer. First, the Lucretius thing: if the deprivation of your future is what makes death bad, why isn't the deprivation of your PAST bad too? Nobody thinks the eons before their birth were a tragedy, and she says the symmetry shows the deprivation story can't be the whole story. Second, the timing puzzle: if death is bad for you, WHEN is it bad for you? Before you're dead (you're not deprived yet), during (you don't exist), after (same problem)? She says a harm with no time at which it's had is no harm at all.

To make it worse, my cousin threw in a variant idea he came up with himself, which I haven't seen anywhere: he thinks even if you grant the deprivation account, the badness of death should depend on the SLOPE of your life at the moment you die, not on the total goods remaining. On a steep upward trajectory (young, happy, things finally clicking) death is a catastrophe; on a flat or declining one, less bad, maybe not bad at all, even if you'd still have had a decent chunk of okay years ahead. He calls it the momentum view, and honestly I suspect it smuggles in something about the shape or narrative of a life mattering separately from its contents, which is its own controversial commitment. Does the deprivation account even have room for shape-of-life value, or is that a different theory of death's badness pretending to be a refinement?

So: write an essay on whether death can be bad for the person who dies. Weigh the Epicurean arguments (the no-subject point, the symmetry with prenatal nonexistence, the timing puzzle) against the deprivation account and whatever fixes philosophers have proposed for the timing problem, and take my cousin's momentum view seriously enough to say whether it's a coherent refinement or just confused. Don't end on a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" — I want you to actually reach and defend a verdict, even if it's a qualified one (like "death is bad for you only when X and Y hold"). Also I'd appreciate it if you addressed whether your verdict implies anything about the badness of death for someone whose remaining future would have been mostly suffering, since that's where my roommate thinks she has me cornered for sure.
