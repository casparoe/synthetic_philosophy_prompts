I'm a TA for a large intro-level applied ethics course, preparing a discussion-section handout that will sort students by what they actually believe about abortion rather than by the labels they walked in with. Please write the decision tree for that handout. Build it from the logical space first — settle on the branch questions, see which answer combinations they allow, and only then locate the actual positions in that space.

The audience has no prior philosophy, so every node must be a concrete case or claim a student can respond to without knowing which position it leads to. The branch questions I want you to work in: whether it would be wrong to refuse nine months of bodily life-support for a stranger (Thomson's violinist); whether having voluntarily taken a known risk changes that (her people-seed analogy); whether unplugging is killing or letting die; whether the wrongness of killing is located in what the victim loses rather than in the victim's current properties; and whether a view locating it in what's lost ends up condemning contraception, and what to say about that. Nothing should ask whether a fetus is a "person" without cashing that out in cases.

Required leaves: strict sanctity-of-life opposition, Marquis-style deprivationism, Thomsonian bodily-autonomy liberalism, psychological-capacities liberalism, and a gradualist view; add others only if the space demands them. Note that the tree must not route all anti-abortion answers through a single node, since deprivationism and sanctity views get there by different roads. Keep it to somewhere between eight and twelve branch nodes.

Please structure your response as follows: (1) "The tree" — the full tree, numbered nodes and named leaves, roughly a page; (2) "Unoccupied branches" — one sentence each on whether the missing combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; (3) "What the tree cannot separate" — any pair of positions that answer every node the same way, briefly explained.
