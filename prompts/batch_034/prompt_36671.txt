Two friends of mine, Maya and Daniel, got into it at dinner about the stock market and I couldnt really referee. Maya works at a quant fund, Daniel is doing a PhD in economics. It started because Daniel claimed that most trading volume is basically noise -- that if you take the no-trade theorem seriously, rational investors should almost never trade with each other, since any trade reveals disagreement, and if both sides are rational and share a prior, the disagreement should dissolve once they think it through. So the zillions of shares traded every day are evidence of mass irrationality.

(1) Maya's first move: the theorem assumes common priors, and nobody actually has common priors. People's beliefs before seeing any evidence differ because of their whole lives, and those differences are legitimate, not irrational. So trading on disagreement can be perfectly rational, and the volume proves nothing about rationality.

(2) Daniel: if you let priors differ without limit, then "rational" stops doing any work at all. Whatever crazy belief I have, I can retreat to the prior and say it wasn't arrived at irrationally. And people's priors differ most in exactly the places that matter for investing -- how optimistic they are about the economy, how much they trust management. So Maya's move proves too much: it would make it impossible to ever call any investor irrational, and he doesn't think she wants that either.

(3) Maya: fine for big-picture stuff, but think about what the disagreement is actually about in liquid markets. It's not "how much do you value a dollar" -- it's stuff like "will this company's earnings beat expectations next quarter." For narrow factual questions like that, the common prior assumption is actually pretty reasonable, so Daniel's argument does apply there and the volume is still suspicious. But that's a much smaller claim than the one he started with, and she thinks it's the only one the theorem licenses.

(4) Daniel said that concession costs Maya more than she thinks. If common priors hold for narrow factual questions, then two informed traders disagreeing about earnings should converge once they pool their evidence -- and in the real world they dont converge, they double down. So either they're irrational, or something is wrong with treating disagreement about factual stuff the way the theorem does. He said he's actually happy with either horn, which seemed suspiciously convenient to me.

Then they both looked at me like I was going to settle it. Can you go through the four moves and say who's right at each one, and whether either of them quietly changed the subject from the theorem to something else along the way? I'm also curious whether the whole framing -- that high trading volume is a puzzle the theorem makes acute -- is even the right way to read the theorem, but if you think they're both missing something simpler, just say that.
