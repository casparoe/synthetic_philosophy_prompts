A philosophy blogger I follow defends average utilitarianism with a grade-point analogy. Maximizing total welfare across lives, she says, is like maximizing total grade points by enrolling in ever more courses even when each new course is an easy filler that drags down what you actually get out of your education; obviously what matters is your GPA, so what matters in population ethics is the average quality of lives, not the total. She uses this to argue that Parfit's repugnant conclusion refutes total utilitarianism and that we should be average utilitarians about population. Audit the analogy: give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the student, the transcript, the new course, the grade), then work through where the mapping breaks. I can see that a newly added life doesn't get recorded on anyone else's transcript the way a new semester enters the same GPA, but I want you to separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the ones that don't. I've also heard the average view has embarrassing implications of its own -- something about whether adding lives just above the historical average makes the world better, and about how well-off people were in ancient Egypt -- but I can't reconstruct the details, so spell those out. Finally, tell me whether her anti-totalist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the GPA comparison was carrying the argument the whole time.
