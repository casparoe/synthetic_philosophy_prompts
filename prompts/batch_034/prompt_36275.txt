I'm a novelist (three books out, quiet realist stuff mostly, nothing flashy) and my fourth novel is narrated by a mid-career asylum caseworker for a country that's basically the UK but never named. The structure is the book: each chapter is one claim file, and the reader learns how the deciding actually works the way you learn any job in real life — by watching it done over and over, no exposition. So I need the decision procedure itself to be right, or at least right enough that people who do this job for a living wouldn't throw the book across the room.

Take on the role of someone who spent twenty years as an asylum adjudicator and now trains new caseworkers. Think carefully before you write — actually work through what the questions are and what order they come in, since the order matters legally — but only show me the finished product, no brainstorming on the page and no restating my question back at me.

Here's what the procedure has to cover, following how refugee law gets applied in practice rather than the textbook version: what separates persecution from merely living somewhere awful (this distinction is doing enormous work and I want it honest); whether the feared harm connects to one of the protected grounds, including the messy "particular social group" category; whether the home state could actually protect the claimant, and whether she could reasonably be expected to live somewhere else in her own country instead of fleeing; how you assess credibility when most claimants arrive with no documents at all; and how past persecution, delays in claiming, and small inconsistencies in testimony are supposed to be weighed. Make it a real working procedure: numbered questions in the order a caseworker would run them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the chain gives you a clean refusal, and — this is the part I care most about — where the procedure runs out and the caseworker is just making a judgment call dressed up as a finding of fact. Flag those points explicitly, because that's where my novel lives.

Constraints, so it's realistic: my narrator gets maybe a day per case, is legally trained but not a country expert, and her decisions get reviewed but she can't pass the hard ones upward without deciding something first. The procedure has to be runnable by someone in that position, not by a court.

Then run it on three claims I'm considering as chapters: (1) a woman from a country that prosecutes adultery, who was acquitted but says her husband's family will kill her for the shame and the police where she lives won't touch family matters, though they function fine in the capital; (2) a man from a country where homosexuality is criminalized on paper but almost never enforced, who was never personally attacked but says he can't live a life; (3) a political protester whose story is vivid and mostly consistent but who got the date of his own arrest wrong twice. On at least one of these the procedure should land on an answer my narrator — and the reader — will hate, and you should say so plainly rather than softening it.

Format: give me the bottom line in your first sentence (the core logic of the procedure in one line, the thing my narrator would say is the whole job), then the full procedure and the worked cases, no preamble and no commentary about the novel at the end. Sorry for the long ask, I've just been burned before by getting legal texture wrong in reviews.
