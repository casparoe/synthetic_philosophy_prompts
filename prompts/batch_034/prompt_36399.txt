I teach an upper-level philosophy of mathematics seminar and am writing the take-home final. Write one essay question, answerable in about 1000 words, on the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the history of Church and Turing; in particular, it should force students to take a stand on what kind of claim the thesis is — a definition, a quasi-empirical conjecture about human calculators, or a framework commitment that is partly constitutive of what we mean by "effective method" — and to say how that stand bears on whether the thesis could ever be refuted. To make them confront the issue concretely, the question should include a short hypothetical scenario I want them to analyze: a physicist announces a device that solves the halting problem by exploiting an exotic physical process, and two commentators react differently — one says the announcement, if genuine, refutes the Church-Turing thesis, while the other says it refutes only a stronger physical version of it and leaves the original thesis untouched. Students must adjudicate this dispute, and their adjudication should reveal whether they understand that the original thesis connects an informal notion (effective calculability) with a formal one (Turing-machine computability), and what follows for how evidence could bear on it.

Then provide: a model answer of about 600 words that argues for a clear position on the scenario rather than surveying options; and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain — at minimum, distinguishing the original thesis from its physical strengthening, explaining the asymmetry in how the two claims are supported, and giving a reasoned verdict on which commentator is right — with the marks each move is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically mishandle each move.
