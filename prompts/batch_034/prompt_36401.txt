I keep coming back to the preregistration stuff in psychology and something about it bugs me. The deal now is that if you write down your hypothesis and analysis plan before collecting data, your result counts as confirmatory evidence, but if you didnt, the exact same finding from the exact same data is "exploratory" — a hypothesis for future research, and a lot of journals won't treat it as real support for anything. There are badges for it, and registered reports where your methods section gets peer reviewed and accepted before you've even run the study.

On its face this was sold as a frequentist clean-up job: kill p-hacking and HARKing by making the error rates honest. But heres what I can't shake. The classic Bayesian complaint about frequentist testing was always that the same data should give the same evidence regardless of the researcher's intentions or sampling plan — the likelihood principle, the stopping-rule stuff, all of that. And preregistration seems to double down on the opposite view: the epistemic value of a finding partly depends on what was in the researcher's head (and in a timestamped OSF file) before the data arrived. The reform that everyone frames as fixing frequentist practice seems to presuppose the exact thing Bayesians said was wrong with frequentism.

So what does the preregistration regime actually presuppose about evidence? A few sub-questions. Is it just straight frequentism, where procedures and intentions are legitimately part of the evidential story? Or is it really an epistemology of the researcher rather than of the data — the preregistration being evidence of the person's trustworthiness, which is a different thing from evidential support proper? Could a good Bayesian sign on to the whole package coherently, or only to parts of it (pre-committing priors, sure, but discounting the data itself because of when the hypothesis was formed, no)? And where does the confirmatory/exploratory line actually break — pilot data, someone preregistering twenty hypotheses and reporting the one that hits, a "prediction" that was basically the whole field's prior expectation anyway? Basically: reconstruct the implicit view of evidence this practice needs, tell me which rival views it quietly rules out, and what would genuinely have to change if the field went fully Bayesian instead.
