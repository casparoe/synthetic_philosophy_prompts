So I need this written up properly, and I'm still a bit wound up, because I spent most of last night arguing about it with my friend Elena and we basically ended nowhere.

Background: I'm on the board of trustees at a small municipal gallery. For eighty-odd years we've hung a big oak-panel landscape catalogued as being by a Dutch Golden Age painter — I'll just call him "the Master", the name doesn't matter for this. It's the centrepiece of the permanent collection, it's on all the merchandise, the postcards, the coffee mugs, everything. Last year we sent it off for cleaning and analysis ahead of a planned loan, and the report we recieved back says the execution is almost certainly workshop: assistants working under his supervision, possibly with a few passages from his own hand, but the current catalogue entry is wrong. So not a forgery, exactly — nobody faked anything, it's a genuine seventeenth-century painting made in his studio, in his lifetime, probably with him hovering over it. The question for us is what the label says, and by extension what we do about the loan agreement (which names it as an autograph work), the insurance schedule, and the family of the donor who bequeathed it to us in 1951.

Here's where Elena comes in. Her position: nothing about the object changed. It's the same panel it was before the swabs and the X-rays, every aesthetic fact about it is identical, and the only thing that changed is a belief about who moved the brush. So the collapse in its monetary value — and, she says, in visitors' awe — is a collective illusion we'd be choosing to participate in by relabelling it. Her line at about midnight was that you wouldn't reprice a sunset if you found out it was a reflection off a mirror (she put it better than that, but you get the gist). My position, badly stated at 1am, was something about originality working like a performance: the object is a record of an achievement, and a workshop piece records a different achievement even if the surface is indistinguishable. We also fought about whether there's a genuine difference between a fake, a misattribution, and an honest period copy, and whether that difference should show up in how the object is treated or only in what's said about it. And whether the wall label and the valuation are seperate questions or not.

What I need from you: a decision memo I can circulate to the other trustees before the meeting on the 12th. It should recommend one course of action concretely — relabel now with an explanatory wall text, relabel quietly at the next rehang, hold pending further analysis, or something else you think is better — with a realistic timeline that covers the loan, the insurers, and the donor family. Crucially, it has to engage Elena's argument on its own terms rather than just repeating "the evidence says workshop", because she already concedes the evidence. If you think the performance idea helps, use it; if you think it collapses under pressure, say so honestly. Keep it to something the trustees will actually read — call it 900 words maximum.
