I'm a philosophy grad student (3rd year, diss chapter on population axiology-adjacent stuff), and last week my flatmate Dev (econ PhD, very confident) and a guy from my reading group, Marco (post-Parfit utilitarian of the bruised variety) had a genuinely multi-hour argument in my kitchen that I could not referee. Context: I'd brought up threshold lexical negative utilitarianism as a view nobody in the room actually holds but everyone kept circling -- the view that there's a severity threshold in pain such that no amount of pleasure can outweigh a single instance of pain above it, while below the threshold everything trades off the ordinary utilitarian way.

The exchange, best I can reconstruct:

(1) Marco opened by saying the threshold is unmotivated: either pleasure and pain are commensurable or they aren't, and drawing a line somewhere in the middle of the pain scale is, in his words, arbitrary with a philosophical paint job. Below the threshold you do exactly the tradeoffs the classical utilitarian does, so you can't appeal to incommensurability, and above it you've given no reason for the discontinuity.

(2) Dev defended it: severe pains aren't just bigger than mild ones, they're different in kind -- no number of delicious meals compensates a week of torture, and everyone's intuitions confirm this. The threshold just formalizes what we already think.

(3) Marco: if the difference is in kind, fine, but then the burden is to say what property changes at the threshold, because you still treat headaches as fungible with pleasures. And notice your view implies that a world with a billion slightly-positive lives containing one instance of torture is worse than a world of a billion neutral lives with none. Sure about that?

(4) Dev bit the bullet and then counterattacked: he said Marco's commensurability assumption licenses the reverse atrocity -- building a paradise on top of a torture rack so long as the paradise is big enough -- and that's a reductio of commensurability, not of the threshold. The contrast between being tortured and being mildly annoyed is, he claimed, the clearest datum in ethics.

(5) Marco's reply, where I got lost: the threshold view has a recombination problem. Take two pains each just below the threshold, spread across two people; the view says each is outweighable by enough pleasure, so presumably the pair is too. Nudge each up one unit and suddenly no pleasure in the universe compensates? Any threshold is either unstable under recombination or it's incomparability masquerading as an ordering -- and incomparability gives no verdict in mixed cases at all: "no amount of pleasure outweighs this" is not the same claim as "pain wins," and if neither wins you have literally nothing to say about whether to accept a torture-with-compensating-pleasure deal.

(6) Dev's last word: that argument only treats the threshold as a point on a continuous scale, but he'd draw it between catagories of experience, not between magnitudes -- recombination stings only if you think torture is just "more discomfort," the same kind of thing, more of it. And anyway Marco never explained why an infinite tradeoff is more absurd than permitting the finite one.

They both left feeling like they'd won, which tells you how it went. Can you adjudicate move by move -- who's right about what in each of the six moves, whether Marco's recombination point actually lands against a category-based threshold, whether Dev's reductio of commensurability is dialectically any good, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the threshold view is even for? No waffling "both make valuable contributions" -- I want specific verdicts. End with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: NAME -- one clause saying who is more right overall.
