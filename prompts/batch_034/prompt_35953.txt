At a meeting of my democratic-theory reading group the conversation turned to judicial review, and a classmate shut down my worries about a plebiscite by appealing to "the will of the people": the people, he said, speak with one voice at the ballot box, and any institution that overrides that voice — courts, constitutions, legislatures mid-term — is by that very fact anti-democratic. I'm sure that one phrase is packing several different claims together, some harmless and some quietly doing a lot of work, but I can't disentangle them on my own. Would you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to the group rather than a defense of any one reading, the distinct precise theses "the will of the people" could be expressing in an argument like his — running from the near-tautological (something was, after all, voted for) up to the strong claim that there exists a single corporate will of the people, distinct from the aggregate of individual wills, which some institutions track better than a poll does? For each reading I want a short verdict: whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but dubious, or simply false; and, most importantly, whether that reading actually supports the specific use my classmate put it to — namely, that unelected institutions ought never to override an electoral outcome. Somewhere in the menu, address the old distinction, which I understand goes back to Rousseau, between a unitary "general will" aimed at the common good and the mere sum of private opinions, and say whether it can be made to do any work in a modern democracy or just lends the phrase an aura; and flag which reading, if any, makes it literally coherent to say the people "speak with one voice." If two of the readings turn out to collapse into each other, say so rather than padding the list. Please hold the whole answer to a strict limit of 650 words.
