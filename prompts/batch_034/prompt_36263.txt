I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on Wittgenstein's later epistemology, and we've reached the point at which hinge epistemology splits in two. Everyone agrees that hinge commitments — that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born, that my memory is not systematically deceiving me — stand outside the reach of evidence. The dispute is over their status. On one view, hinges are genuine beliefs: propositionally structured, truth-apt commitments that happen to be evidentially unsupported and are therefore beyond knowledge. On the other, hinges are not beliefs at all but something more like rules or framework presuppositions — the rails along which justification runs rather than claims travelling along it — and so not the sort of thing that could be true, false, or rationally revised. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, one on each side, staged as they leave a talk in which a speaker claimed, Moore-style, to know he has hands. The dialogue should be genuine back-and-forth rather than trading positions: the rules-proponent has to say what she tells the ordinary person who insists that of course she knows she has two hands, and the belief-proponent has to answer the charge that calling unevidenced framework commitments beliefs stretches the notion of belief until it does no explanatory work. Let both sides land their strongest points, and leave the exchange unresolved.
