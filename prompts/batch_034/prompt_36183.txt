I've pretty much committed to what you might call feature realism about neural networks: the picture the interpretability crowd works with, where a trained network's activations contain discrete, causally real features — things like "code written in French" or "flattery directed at the user" — which usually live as linear directions in activation space, which our tools (probes, sparse autoencoders, ablations) discover rather than invent, and which the network itself uses to compute its outputs. Not the weak claim that a probe can find regularities, but the strong one: that carving the network at these joints carves it the way the network carves itself.

Tell me what would count against this. Specifically:

- What observations or results would favor the deflationary rival — that the "features" are observer-side artifacts, projections onto a distributed representation that has no privileged basis and no joints of its own?
- Which known difficulties do realists themselves concede as awkward — probe directions the network never actually uses, SAE features that fragment or "split" depending on hyperparameters and training run, the lack of any principled answer to how fine-grained the carving should be? How much does each of these actually cost the view?
- Which would committed realists bite the bullet on versus explain away? And has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability ("there are features at every grain, whatever tool you point at the model finds real ones") — and if so, is that a fatal problem or just the normal condition of a hypothesis at this stage?

Please keep what's actually settled among people who work on this separate from what's genuinely contested — don't present the linear representation hypothesis, or the claim that SAEs recover the model's "true" features, as consensus if they aren't. Rank the defeaters from fatal-if-true to merely inconvenient, and if you think the view is actually pretty well insulated, just say so — don't manufacture doubt for balance.
