I've spent the past year in a reading group working through the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and the thing that has struck me most is how completely the debate has calcified. On one side: the objection, roughly, that utilitarianism treats individual lives as mere containers for wellbeing that can be summed across persons the way it can be summed within a single life, and that this smoothing over of the boundaries between people is the deep moral error -- it licenses sacrificing one for the many in a way that no amount of compensating benefit to others can make right, because there is no social entity whose good is being promoted, only distinct persons each with a claim to their own life. On the other side: the standard battery of rejoinders, which everyone in the group can recite and which never seems to move anyone -- the aggregation theorem showing that rational impartial choosers behind a veil of ignorance would end up with something structurally utilitarian; the argument that refusing to trade off one person's loss against another's is simply a failure of rationality once you've accepted interpersonal comparability; the Parfitian point that the sharp boundaries between lives the objection leans on are themselves somewhat arbitrary, since identity is not what matters and the "separateness" of persons starts to look like a metaphysical hangover. Everyone knows all the moves, and after a year of weekly meetings the utilitarians in the room are still utilitarians and the contractualists are still contractualists.

Here is my hypothesis about why, and I'd like it tested rather than confirmed. I suspect "the separateness of persons" has quietly become a family of quite different complaints that get argued over as if they were one: sometimes it's a claim about the sources of reasons (that losses to a person give that person a claim no one else's gain can cancel), sometimes a claim about aggregation specifically (against summing across lives but allowing it within one), sometimes a claim about the epistemic or metaphysical status of interpersonal welfare comparisons, and sometimes just a label attached to whatever anti-utilitarian intuition is currently doing the work -- the crowd at the trolley switch, the transplant surgeon, the framed innocent. If the utilitarian's formal rejoinder answers the decision-theoretic version while the contractualist's intuition tracks the reasons-based version, then the parties are answering different questions under one name, and the forty-plus years of apparent deadlock is partly an accounting error. But it's also possible the disagreement is substantive all the way down -- a genuine clash over whether practical reasons are fundamentally agent-neutral or agent-relative that no formal result or thought experiment could settle -- and the stall simply reflects that this is bedrock.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the positions (we've read them all). Tell me which version of separateness, if any, is doing the real work in the strongest statements of the objection, whether the utilitarian rejoinders actually engage it or only its weaker siblings, and whether any of the proposed reconciliations -- treating separateness as a constraint on aggregation rather than a prohibition on it, or grounding it in something other than the metaphysics of identity -- succeed in making the dispute tractable. And given your diagnosis, say what progress would even look like: a sharper distinction, a formal result, cases that split the factions differently, or an argument that the question should be dissolved or reframed. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, or that it rests on a misreading of what either side actually claims, or that the debate is in fact not stalled but has genuinely moved and I'm mistaking movement for noise, tell me that plainly rather than trying to make my framing work.
