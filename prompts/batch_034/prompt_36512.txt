A bit of background so you know who this is for: I spent most of my career as an atmospheric physicist, retired a few years back, and since then I've gotten more and more into philosophy — mostly through a small reading group I put together with two other retired scientists and my neighbor, a former hospice chaplain. Next month it's our turn on Pascal, and the Wager keeps generating the same argument over dinner without anybody actually landing anywhere. The chaplain says she can't just decide to believe, but she can "walk toward" belief — go to the services, pray the words, let practice do its work — and that this is the rational move given what's at stake. One of my old colleagues says the expected value math is broken the moment you put infinity in it, and that any argument with an infinite payoff in it proves everything, so it proves nothing. The other says the many-gods objection sinks the whole thing (what about the god who only rewards honest skeptics?), yet he happily uses dominance reasoning and expected utility in every other part of his life, which seems like special pleading. I can't tell anymore which of them is actually disagreeing with which, or whether the chaplain's position even differs from Pascal's own.

So what I want is a "which response to the Wager do you actually hold?" quiz for the group — eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto positions. The point is to sort us by what we say about cases, not by which objection we happen to have read, so no question should just ask "do you think prudence can trump evidence?" — give us a scenario instead. Since we're all comfortable with probability and decision theory (decades of risk models will do that), please don't soften the cases: I specifically want the quiz to use Pascal's mugging (some guy threatens an astronomically bad outcome unless you hand over your wallet — tiny probability, gigantic stakes), the skeptic-rewarding god, the option of flipping a coin or otherwise going half-in when you find you can't believe, the practice route (attending, kneeling, saying the words for years) versus deciding once and up front, and the question of what's actually lost if you wager and are wrong — nothing, or your intellectual honesty and the one life you get to live on your own terms.

As for the leaves of the scoring key: at minimum the orthodox Pascalian (prudential reasoning can license belief-forming, no special restrictions), the Jamesian pragmatist (it's fine for live, forced, momentous options only — which needs to be tested by a question where the option is clearly dead or avoidable), the mixed-strategy view that partial commitment is the rational response when full belief isn't available, the evidentialist who won't have it at any odds, the infinity-skeptic whose problem is with unbounded utilities as such rather than with this particular bet, and the person who grants all the decision theory but thinks the wager is underdetermined once you count rival gods. Also, be honest about where the quiz can't seperate positions — I suspect the chaplain's "practice first" answer is going to score identically for at least two of those leaves, and I'd rather know that than have the key pretend otherwise. And if there's some combination of answers no one in our group will plausibly give, or that no published position actually occupies, say so briefly rather than inventing a name for it.
