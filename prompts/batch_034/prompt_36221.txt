My department's philosophy of science working group contains two senior members who have been arguing about the interpretation of probability for the better part of a decade, always at a volume that suggests neither thinks the other is merely confused about words. One is a fairly doctrinaire frequentist of the older statistics tradition: for her, a probability claim is a claim about relative frequency in a real or hypothetical series of trials, and statements about single events either reduce to such claims or are strictly meaningless. The other is an unabashed subjectivist in the de Finetti tradition: for him, probabilities are coherent degrees of belief, the whole apparatus is grounded in Dutch book arguments and scoring rules, and the frequentist's "hypothetical infinite sequence" is a metaphysical fiction dressed up as empiricism. At the last session, in exasperation, they agreed to try a structured adversarial collaboration, and I have been tasked with drafting the joint statement they will work from — but I want it to be a genuinely strong draft, not a diplomatic fudge.

Please produce the document such a collaboration should issue. It should contain, first, a numbered section of claims both could sign, and I want these to be substantive rather than vacuous — e.g. points about the mathematical formalism being shared, about finite-frequency and limiting-frequency methods having genuine empirical successes, about the operational usefulness of betting quotients — not "probability is interesting." Second, a disagreements table: for each point of divergence, a formulation phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view, and crucially a column saying whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. I suspect a good deal of it is verbal — that the two parties are explicating different concepts that both happen to be called "probability," the way "law" means different things in law and in physics — but I want you to actually work through which parts are verbal and which are not, rather than asserting a verdict. Pay particular attention to the hard cases: single-event probabilities (a radiologist saying a patient has a 30 per cent chance of a bad outcome — the frequentist wants a reference class, the subjectist wants no such thing, and both seem to speak intelligibly); quantum mechanical probabilities, where repeated trials exist but each individual event's propensity seems to be doing real work; and the observation that in many practical settings frequentist confidence procedures and Bayesian credible intervals deliver numerically identical answers, which each side nonetheless interprets differently.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, with the evidence or arguments both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance — for instance, what each would expect from calibration studies of expert forecasters, or from the behaviour of statistical methods under misspecification. If you think one or both parties would have to concede something surprising in the course of drafting — the frequentist conceding that subjective priors are smuggled into any choice of reference class, the subjectivist conceding that coherence alone is too weak to constrain belief in practice — say so, but in wording the conceding party could still sign. The deliverable is a constructive text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as their positions allow.
