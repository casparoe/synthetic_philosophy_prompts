For a graduate workshop on political ethics I am compiling model adversarial collaborations, and I would like one on the problem of dirty hands in politics. The two parties are faculty members who have argued past each other for a year. The first — call her the dirty-hands theorist — holds, roughly in the spirit of Michael Walzer's 1973 essay in Philosophy & Public Affairs, that a good political leader will sometimes be obligated to do genuine wrong (torture the informant, deceive the electorate, bomb the civilians) in order to avert catastrophe; that the wrong remains a wrong even when it is obligatory; and that the guilt a leader ought to feel afterwards is neither neurosis nor theatre but an appropriate response to having violated a real prohibition. The second — a thoroughgoing consequentialist — denies that the category does any work: if an action really does avert the catastrophe, then it was the right thing to do, all things considered, and if it was the right thing to do, a decent leader should feel no guilt for doing it, whatever residual displays of anguish may be socially or psychologically useful. He suspects that talk of dirtying one's hands is a way of having it both ways — keeping the deontological language of prohibition while behaving exactly like a consequentialist — and that, as public doctrine, the category chiefly functions to license escalation. She suspects that his view leaves nothing to say to the official who authorised the atrocity and then slept soundly, and that it has no conceptual room for the thought that politics sometimes presents a choice between wrongs rather than a simple optimisation over outcomes.

Draft the joint statement their collaboration should have produced. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — at minimum covering what both accept about the phenomenology of political emergencies; about the epistemics, including officials' well-documented tendency to overestimate necessity and urgency, and the self-serving drift of supreme-emergency reasoning from genuine existential threat down to mere policy convenience; and about where the Kantian absolutist who refuses ever to dirty her hands goes wrong; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — I would expect one to concern whether guilt in these cases is a moral remainder tracking a genuine wrong or an instrumentally useful commitment device, and another whether the claim that an act would be wrong though the leader ought nonetheless to do it is a coherent piece of moral thought or a confusion; (3) the cruxes, with any purely verbal disputes identified as such and pruned away; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — the natural candidates are Walzer's ticking-bomb torture case, the decision not to warn a city of an impending raid in order to protect an intelligence advantage (the Coventry episode, though note its historiography is disputed), and a subtler case such as a prime minister choosing between breaking an explicit election promise and triggering a constitutional crisis.

Sartre's Hoederer asks, in effect, whether anyone imagines they can govern innocently. Part of your task is to state precisely what each of these two thinks the answer is, and exactly where and why their answers come apart. Please write the whole statement in British English.
