Read the bishop's candlesticks scene in Les Misérables — Bienvenu telling the gendarmes that the silver was his gift to Valjean, and then adding the candlesticks — through Augustine's prohibition on lying. In De mendacio and Contra mendacium, Augustine maintains that lying is never permissible, even to save someone from death or to protect fugitives from persecutors, since a lie corrupts the liar even when it rescues the endangered; he counsels silence, evasion, or endurance instead. Hugo's bishop, by contrast, lies outright and the lie is presented as the act of charity that makes Valjean's transformation possible. I'd like a reading that takes both the scene and Augustine's casuistry seriously: does the scene refute Augustine, dramatize a case his hierarchy of goods cannot absorb, or can his framework explain the bishop in some other way (perhaps by treating what the bishop says as not a lie at all, or by relocating the moral weight onto the candlesticks he truthfully gives afterward)? Where does Hugo anticipate the objection that a world of permissible merciful lies is more dangerous than Valjean's despair, and where does he simply not care? I'm teaching a course on the ethics of deception, so please say plainly whether you think the scene shows something the Augustinian position misses, or something it already contains.
