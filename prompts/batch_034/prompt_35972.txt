I am preparing a political philosophy seminar on conceptions of liberty — negative liberty in Berlin's sense of non-interference, positive liberty as self-mastery, and the neo-republican conception defended by Quentin Skinner and Philip Pettit, on which freedom is non-domination rather than mere absence of interference. For our session on republican liberty I want to screen Florian Henckel von Donnersmarck's The Lives of Others (2006), and I would like a philosophical reading of the film that takes both the film and the debate seriously.

The premise seems almost engineered to illustrate the negative/republican contrast. Georg Dreyman is a loyal, celebrated East German playwright who is barely interfered with: he writes what he likes, his flat is untouched, his work is staged. On the negative conception he looks comparatively free — indeed freer than most of his friends, such as the blacklisted director Jerska. Yet everything about his conduct is calibrated: he self-censors, he watches what he says at his own dinner table, and the film shows precisely how the mere possibility of surveillance reshapes a life before any actual obstruction occurs. By the time Captain Wiesler is bugging the flat, has anything of substance really changed in Dreyman's situation? That question — whether the bug is the beginning of his unfreedom or merely its revelation — seems to be exactly the wedge between Berlin and Pettit.

I would like the reading to address the following, though please go beyond them where useful. First, does the film dramatise the republican claim that unexercised arbitrary power is itself domination — the idea that the dominated must scan the powerful for the intimation of threat, in a way that leaves no room for the frankness of the free person? Consider Christa-Maria Sieland's coerced relationship with Minister Hempf, which shows how domination without overt interference corrodes agency. Second, Wiesler himself: the neo-republican insists that arbitrary power is bad for its agents too, not only its victims, and Wiesler is plainly answerable to Grubitz and to the Party in ways he does not control. Does his arc bear that out, or does his redemption by art and conscience introduce something the theory has no place for? Third, the ending: after the Wall falls, negative liberty is trivially and universally restored, yet the film lingers on Dreyman in the archive, reading his own files, discovering who betrayed him and who shielded him, before the final bookshop scene. What theory of freedom is implicit in the claim that this knowledge matters — that liberation is not complete until one understands one's own domination? And finally, be honest about where the film resists the reading: one could argue it is a film about art and conscience rather than political liberty at all, and that the lens of non-domination distorts it if pressed too hard.

Please indicate which scenes a seminar leader should foreground, and close with your view of what the film adds to the Berlin/Pettit debate that argument alone cannot supply. Please write your response in British English.
