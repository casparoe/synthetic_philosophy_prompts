I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and my committee has turned out to be a case study in the biology of a stalemate: my advisor holds that a trait's function is whatever past selection shaped it to do, roughly the view associated with Millikan and Neander, while my second reader is a Cummins-style causal-role theorist who thinks function is just the contribution a part makes to some capacity of the containing system, analyzed in the here and now. They have been disagreeing, as far as anyone can tell, since before I was born, and they disagree in exactly the same way at every committee meeting.

What I want is a diagnosis of why this disagreement has never resolved, and what kind of disagreement it actually is. The standard battlegrounds are familiar: the dysfunction cases, where an etiologist can say the job of a diseased heart is still pumping even though it pumps badly, and the causal-role theorist apparently cannot; and the drift cases, like spandrels or traits fixed by drift, where the causal-role theorist happily assigns functions and the etiologist has to either deny them or stretch the notion of history. But each side has a well-rehearsed answer to its hard cases, and the answers never change anyone's mind. Reconciliation attempts — saying there are simply two legitimate concepts of function, a selected-functions concept and a causal-role concept, and the word is just ambiguous — have been around for decades too, and they have not produced convergence either; everyone concedes the pluralist point and then keeps arguing.

I have two hypotheses, and I would like to know whether either survives scrutiny. First, that the dispute is not really about the word 'function' at all, but about which analysis gets to own the notion of dysfunction: both sides tacitly assume that malfunction is just the failure to perform a function, that this single predicate underwrites talk of disorder, pathology, and psychiatric classification, and that therefore whichever analysis of function fails to ground a notion of dysfunction loses. If that is right, the debate would move once it argued about dysfunction directly rather than about function. Second, that the two sides are implicitly disagreeing about the time-index of biological explanation — whether what a trait was selected for can appear in an explanatory premise at all, or whether all legitimate explanation is synchronic and systemic — and that this is a substantive disagreement about explanation that has been conducted, misleadingly, under the heading of function. Please evaluate the hypotheses against what each camp actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is when the hypotheses fail (verbal, methodological, bedrock intuition about what biology is for?), and be honest about whether anything — a distinction, a class of cases, a downstream research program like teleosemantics or evolutionary medicine becoming vindicated or collapsing — could actually end it.
