I'm taking a philosophy of physics course this term on the second law of thermodynamics, and I'm writing my seminar paper on Maxwell's demon. The course got interesting in week six, when our seminar leader and a guest lecturer (a condensed-matter person) spent the entire back half of the session arguing past each other about why the demon fails, and I've since realized they represent two recognisable camps. Camp one, which I'll call the information-theoretic exorcists, hold that the demon's failure is a discovery about the physics of information: measurement of a molecule's speed can in principle be done reversibly and cheaply (this was the point of Bennett's reply to Brillouin's measurement-cost argument), so the real bottleneck is that the demon's memory fills up, and erasing the record of each measurement to reset for the next one costs at least kT ln 2 per bit; on this view the second law survives because information is physical, and the demon episode is what first showed it. Camp two, the statistical-mechanical skeptics, think this is all backwards: the second law is a statistical regularity about macroscopic systems, the demon fails for the same ordinary Boltzmann reasons any improbable fluctuation fails to be exploited, and Landauer's principle is at best a thermodynamic corollary dressed up as a foundation — at worst it quietly presupposes the very second law it is supposed to be defending, and the slogan that information is physical, whatever its rhetorical force, doesn't add content to the statistical mechanics.

For my paper I'm supposed to open with a neutral framing of the debate before taking a side, and I've become convinced that the best way to do that — and honestly the best service to the literature — would be to imagine the two camps conducting an adversarial collaboration: a sustained, good-faith attempt by each to state the other's view so the other would accept the wording, before either argues for anything. So that's what I'd like you to draft: the joint statement these two camps could produce. Please give me (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign — covering at least the Szilard engine's one-bit extraction analysis, whether measurement itself must be dissipative, whether any complete demon cycle can yield net work, and what the fluctuation theorems do and don't establish about small systems; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the information-theoretic side would accept for its own view and once in wording the statistical side would accept for its own — I especially want the erasure question handled carefully, since the two camps apparently even disagree about whether erasure is a natural kind of physical process or a bookkeeping convention; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether Landauer's principle can be independently warranted or is unavoidably circular, and another is whether the second law needs a demon-proof at all — and for each crux at least one argument, thought experiment, or experimental result that both camps agree bears on it (single-particle memory-erasure experiments and fluctuation-theorem tests are fair game), with each side's prediction about the outcome written down in advance. Finally, if you conclude that some part of this dispute is merely verbal — I have a suspicion the phrase "information is physical" is doing a lot of equivocal work — please identify which part and explain why, in wording both camps would accept. The whole thing should be the kind of document a Benthamite lecturer and a Lipmann-style skeptic could actually put their names to, not a disguised victory for either side.
