I spent a long career in condensed-matter physics and have come late to the philosophy of perception and concepts, so I ask for specifications the way I used to ask for them at the start of a design review. The question I have become stuck on is the one Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project put at its center: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? I am less interested in that particular architecture, however, than in what any theory of the phenomenon would have to deliver before I would call it adequate. Before I evaluate anyone's answer, I want the requirements list.

Write the desiderata a theory of letterform categorization must meet, where the standard of adequacy is explaining the human reader, not powering a piece of engineering — a system that recognizes glyphs reliably in a database is not thereby a theory of the concept. The cases I want covered include, at least: first-exposure recognition of an 'a' in a font never seen, with no feedback and no deliberation; the structural distance between the two-story and one-story forms of the lowercase letter, and further to blackletter, where the commonality becomes hard to name at all; tolerance of the distortion, fragmentation, and sloppiness of handwriting, up to the point where it fails; the fact that category and style are orthogonal groupings that must be extracted at once — within a font, the 'a' is more similar to its sibling letters than to another font's 'a', and a type designer can invent an alphabet in which no glyph copies any existing one, yet readers parse it instantly — so the theory must say what is transferred in that case, both what preserves the identity of each letter and what preserves the character of the family. It should also say whether uppercase and lowercase are one concept, two, or a structured pair, and whether a purely structural description of an 'a' as a bowl plus a stem plus other roles can survive the letters that lack the parts the description names. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one research tradition, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, if demanding invariant, font-independent structure conflicts with the context effects in which a letter is identified faster inside a word than alone. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the familiar candidate accounts — template matching, feature lists, prototype averaging, and structural description. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your judgment of the requirement that carries the most weight, or of the jointly unsatisfiable pair if you think there is one — and give the reasoning afterward, with no preamble and no restating of my question. American English, please.
