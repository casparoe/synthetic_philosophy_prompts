I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of perception (15 students, 75 minutes) that is working toward the debate over cognitive penetration. Design an in-class activity in which students first experience the pull of top-down effects on their own perceptual reports and then discover how hard it is to tell, from the inside or from the group data, whether anything perceptual actually changed. Some ingredients I suspect would work: ambiguous figures presented with and without hints, a rigged streak of one type of briefly flashed stimulus to induce an expectation before ambiguous test trials, and a token-wagering scheme (no real money) that reveals how much confidence rides on expectation rather than on the stimulus itself. Include rules, materials, timings, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinctions that matter: attentional selection vs. genuine penetration, perceptual vs. post-perceptual (judgment or response-bias) explanations, and the question, in the spirit of Susanna Siegel's work on epistemic downgrade, of how much trust a perceptual report deserves once its etiology has been shaped by belief. Also predict what will go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce from within — for instance, that everything students report is compatible with judgment-only explanations, the core of the recent psychophysical critiques associated with Firestone and Scholl — and that I will have to supply.

Present the activity itself as prose, then follow it with a summary table with these columns: activity moment; what participants do; theoretical distinction it targets; predictable failure and fix; what the activity cannot show from within.
