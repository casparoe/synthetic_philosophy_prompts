You are a professional philosopher specializing in value theory, moral axiology, and the ethics of wellbeing, with a side interest in the metaphysics of vagueness. Please think carefully and systematically: begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses before committing to one, and make sure your final answer prices the alternatives.

My thesis advisor defends negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold. On her view, ordinary discomforts and mild pains can be outweighed, and even redeemed, by pleasures; but sufferings beyond a certain intensity cannot be compensated by any amount of pleasure, however large. I think I've built an argument that destroys her position, but it seems to prove far too much, so I suspect it is flawed somewhere and cannot locate the flaw.

The argument:

(1) On her view there is a threshold t such that pains below t are commensurable with pleasures, while pains above t are not commensurable with any pleasures at all.
(2) There is no natural gap in the continuum of possible pain intensities: for any possible pain of intensity x and any of intensity y greater than x, a pain of some intermediate intensity is also possible.
(3) So take a pain p just below t and a pain q just above t, where p and q differ by less than the smallest difference anyone could possibly notice. By her lights, p can be outweighed by, say, a few hours of moderate pleasure, while q cannot be outweighed by any pleasure whatsoever — not by eons of bliss, not by infinitely many ecstatic lives.
(4) That means a difference in pain intensity too small to be detected is the difference between "redeemable by modest pleasure" and "no amount of bliss could redeem it," and it settles whether an entire world of eternal bliss containing one instance of that pain is better or worse than an empty world.
(5) A difference that small cannot ground a difference that enormous. That is absurd.
(6) Therefore no such threshold exists: either every pain is compensable (ordinary hedonic utilitarianism) or none is (strict negative utilitarianism, on which even a pinprick outweighs all the pleasure in history).
(7) Worse, the same reasoning generalizes to every morally loaded threshold placed on a continuum: the point at which a system becomes conscious, the cognitive threshold for personhood or full moral status, the point at which a life ceases to be worth living. If (5) is right, all of these thresholds are absurd too.
(8) But surely not every such threshold is absurd — or is it? I would rather not accept (6), and I cannot bring myself to accept (8) wholesale, so I assume something in the chain is rotten.

Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please address both the local question (whether my advisor's threshold view can survive, and on what reformulation) and the general one (whether the argument really does extend to consciousness, personhood, and worth-living thresholds, and what a friend of such thresholds can say in reply). Answer in American English.
