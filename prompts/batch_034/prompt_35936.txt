I direct a small research programme at my university on the philosophy of artificial minds, and I have funding for roughly six doctoral students and a three-year postdoc working on belief attribution to AI systems, understood broadly through the lens of Dennett's intentional stance. I have to decide by the end of term which specific problems to assign as thesis topics, and I want help ranking the open questions rather than a survey of the field.

To give you the flavour of what I take the terrain to be: the intentional stance says we attribute beliefs and desires to a system when doing so helps predict its behaviour, and the states so attributed are, on Dennett's own telling, real patterns rather than inner items we discover. Meanwhile, a growing community of interpretability researchers talks as though a trained model simply has internal states -- representations of features, circuits that encode this or that belief -- which can be read off more or less independently of our explanatory purposes. It is not obvious to me that these two pictures are compatible, nor that anyone in either camp has noticed the tension. And there is a further wrinkle: whether a system that has no persistent point of view across contexts, no stakes in anything, and indeed no consistent behaviour at the level of individual activations could be a believer in the Dennettian sense at all, or whether stance-based belief attribution to such systems is a kind of category error we are making because our stance-taking psychology evolved for agents.

So, the questions I would like you to prioritise and rank, with the ranking defended rather than merely asserted:

1. The indeterminacy problem: if intentional-state attribution is stance-relative and there can be multiple equally predictive belief-desire decompositions of the same system, does this undermine the interpretability programme's ambition to find the model's actual beliefs? Or can some notion of the "best" or most rational decomposition be made objective enough to do safety-relevant work?
2. The normativity question: the intentional stance presupposes an ideal of rationality. Does this presupposition transfer to artificial systems, whose "irrationalities" may be systematic and load-bearing rather than noise? What happens to belief attribution when departures from rationality are the explanandum rather than the background condition?
3. The agency prerequisite: is a minimal form of agency or point of view a prerequisite for belief attribution, such that attributing beliefs to a context-bound predictive system is a projection without a subject?
4. The evidential question: what behavioural or internal evidence should move us to say that stance-relative beliefs are genuine beliefs for purposes of, say, deciding whether a system has a misleading belief about its situation that poses a risk?

For the ranking, please tell me explicitly what criteria you are using -- importance to the wider programme, tractability for doctoral students with philosophical training but limited machine-learning background, neglectedness relative to the current literature, and how much downstream work depends on each question being settled first. Explain which criteria you weight most heavily for this particular programme and why, and where they conflict. Indicate which questions a three-year doctoral project could realistically make progress on, and which should perhaps be delegated to the postdoc or held in reserve. Finally, tell me what developments over the next few years -- in interpretability methods, in the design of evaluation regimes for deployed systems, in the philosophical literature -- would move particular questions up or down the list, so that I can revisit the ranking annually.

Please answer in British English, and do not provide a reading list or bibliography: I want the reasoning, not the references.
