I'm an undergraduate taking an upper-level metaphysics course, and we've spent the last month on the special composition question — under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? Our TA, a fourth-year grad student, is openly hostile to unrestricted composition but equally dismissive of van Inwagen's view that only living things compose; she thinks stones and chairs exist, but she refuses to state her own principle, telling us that if we want it that badly we can reverse-engineer it from her verdicts. Between discussion section and office hours, we've now assembled quite a track record. Her rulings so far: the atoms of a stone compose a stone; the parts of a living human compose one thing; a chair is a genuine composite; the students seated across the front row do not compose a further object; the keys, the phone, and the earbuds lying a few inches apart on my desk do not compose one thing, even though they're all in the same place at the same time; the Eiffel Tower and my coffee mug do not compose anything over and above the tower and the mug; and the beads strung on a necklace do compose one thing.

Next section we've been told to bring new cases, and I want to be the one with the interesting ones. I'm planning to spring three on her. First, dry sand poured through a funnel so that it sits as a neat cone on a lab bench: the grains touch their neighbors and the pile has a perfectly determinate shape, but nothing binds them — tip the bench and it all runs. Second, a line of demonstrators linked arm in arm stretching down several blocks: contact all along the chain, but each demonstrator can unlink and walk away at any moment, and the line has no fixed shape. Third, a spiral galaxy: its stars are gravitationally bound into a single persistent system, but no two stars touch, and the spiral is a statistical pattern rather than a fixed arrangement. I designed the first and third as a pair — the first has the parts touching but not bound together, the third has them bound together but not touching — because my hunch is that her rulings so far never separate those two features, and that if her principle exists at all, it has to pick one of them as the thing that matters.

Before section, I'd like your help thinking it through. What rules could she be running that fit all seven of her earlier verdicts — and are there several, or does the track record really narrow it to one? For each rule you think is live, tell me what it predicts for each of my three cases, and where the rules disagree. Then give me your best guess as to how she'll actually rule on each, with your reasons — I care less about the answers than about which rule is most plausibly hers, given that she's already rejected both universalism and organicism. Finally, tell me which further case, not necessarily one of mine, would best discriminate between the surviving rules: if she answers my three the way you predict, I want to know what to bring to section after that.
