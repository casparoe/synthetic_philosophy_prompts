I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this term and we just did a couple weeks on introspection and self-knowledge, and I've realised I have a bunch of gut verdicts about when to trust people's introspective reports that I can't make line up, so I want to figure out what principle, if any, is behind them. Here they are. When someone says they're in pain, or that the coffee tastes bitter, I basically trust that completely. When someone says they chose the left-hand queue at the shop because it looked shorter, but actually the right one was shorter and everyone else had gone right, I don't trust the reported reason at all -- I think they're just making up a plausible story for a choice whose real cause they never saw. There's that experiment where people pick which of two faces they find more attractive, a sleight of hand swaps the photo, and they still happily explain why they picked the one they'd actually rejected -- and my gut is that ordinary explanations of our own choices are barely better than that. But then, when someone says they're not sure whether they're genuinely still in love with their partner or just attached out of habit, I think the introspective report is at least part of the evidence -- not infallible, but real. When someone says they understand how a bicycle or a zip works, I trust the feeling of understanding almost zero, since everyone's felt it and been wrong. When someone meditating says they just experienced a few seconds with no thoughts at all, I'm honestly not sure what to think -- part of me says who could possibly know that better than them, part of me says the noticing itself comes way after the fact and gets filled in. Same with someone claiming they were listening to the whole lecture when they were clearly zoning in and out -- I trust their report less than an observer sitting next to them. And the case I keep going back and forth on: someone says they hold some political view for moral reasons rather than self-interested ones, and I find I trust this more when the person loses money because of the view than when they don't, but I can't tell if that's a principle about introspection or just about incentives. So: is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts -- something like introspection being reliable for current phenomenal states but not for causes of behaviour -- and if so, which of my verdicts does it end up overturning? And what does the principle say about a case I haven't listed, say someone who insists that their introspection tells them they have no inner monologue at all, ever? Please answer in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you'd write an email or a letter, and please write in British English. I'd rather get an honest account that forces me to drop a verdict than a clever principle that gerrymanders to save all of them.
