I teach an upper-level seminar on population ethics—fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, mid-semester. We've just covered ordinary deprivational harm and welfare tradeoffs, and I want the next class to make them collide with the nonidentity problem before they've read or heard the label. Design a staged role-play along these lines: a small coastal polity's development council must choose between two policies (say, an intensive-extraction settlement plan versus a slower conservationist one), where the plans differ in timing and settlement patterns enough that different people end up being born a generation later—I'll want you to make that identity-shifting mechanism concrete and internally plausible without it reading as science fiction, and to supply whatever props or cards would let participants actually see that "the people of generation two" are different people under the two policies. The centerpiece should be a tribunal in which generation-two citizens lodge complaints about the world they inherited, with an apparently innocuous standing rule they'll formulate themselves: a complaint goes forward only if the complainant is worse off under the chosen policy than someone would have been under the alternative. I want them to discover, in the tribunal's failure to find a complainant with standing, the gap between "a wrong was done" and "someone was wronged"—and to have to articulate whether they think there is a residue of wrong, and if so whose it is.

Please give me: the setup and cast, the mechanics of the identity shift (including how to keep students from just denying it), the tribunal procedure, a debrief that maps each moment onto the live philosophical options—person-affecting restraint, impersonal claims about comparative-quality-of-life thresholds, and simply denying the intuition—without lecturing up front; the term "nonidentity problem" should not appear until the debrief. Include the predictable failure modes on a first run (e.g., students relitigating the policy's merits instead of standing, or letting the tribunal declare a victim by fiat) and how I should handle them. Finally, be candid about which part of the problem no in-class activity can reproduce—the comparison between an existence and a nonexistence that contains no subject to make it, for instance—and that I'll have to supply at the board.
