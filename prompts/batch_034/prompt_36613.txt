Compose a villanelle in the voice of someone standing at the decision point of a badly over-budget, half-finished project — a renovation, a dissertation, a years-long research program, take your pick — in which the two refrains enact the two sides of the sunk cost debate. One refrain should voice the orthodox economist's position: what's spent is spent, the money and years are gone, and only marginal costs and benefits counted from here should bear on the choice. The other should voice the strongest case for honoring sunk costs: not sentimentality, but the thought that being a person who finishes what they start functions as a standing policy — a self-binding rule, like those used against weakness of will — whose value exceeds any single decision, even if each individual application looks irrational at the margin. The villanelle's form should do the philosophical work: the refrains, like the costs themselves, cannot be discharged and keep returning no matter how often dismissed, and their final convergence in the closing couplet should be the moment of decision itself. Someone versed in the escalation-of-commitment literature should be able to identify which refrain is which and recognize that the pro-sunk-cost refrain is the philosophically serious one rather than a straw man, while a reader who knows nothing of the debate should still find it a good poem about not being able to let go.
