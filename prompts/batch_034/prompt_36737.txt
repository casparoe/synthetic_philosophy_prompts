For the last several months I've been stuck in a low-grade recurring argument with two friends from a LessWrong-adjacent meetup about commons problems. One is a hard property-rights type: every tragedy of the commons is ultimately a missing-titles problem, and privatization or at least tradeable quotas is the general fix. The other thinks markets are the disease and the state is the cure: taxes, caps, and provision by fiat. After finally reading some of the empirical common-pool-resource literature (the Ostrom line, the fisheries and irrigation cases), I've come to suspect that both of them are arguing about answers to a question nobody has actually specified, and that the specification itself is where the interesting work is. So before we go another round, I want the spec written down.

What I'm asking for is this: write out the desiderata that any adequate solution to a commons problem has to meet. By "commons problem" I mean the family that includes overgrazing, overfishing, groundwater depletion, and climate change, but the list should make explicit the distinctions a theory can't collapse along the way -- common-pool resources versus public goods (rival versus non-rival consumption, and why the fix for one can misfire on the other), open access versus a resource genuinely held in common by an identifiable community, one-shot versus iterated interactions, symmetric versus wildly asymmetric appropriators, and, if you think it belongs, the mirror-image anticommons case of a resource fragmented among too many exclusion rights to be used at all. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: incentive compatibility with rational appropriators, robustness to entry by outsiders and to capture by the most powerful users, monitoring costs, whether the solution survives when discount rates are high or population is large, and consistency with the empirical record that long-lived self-governing commons institutions actually exist and have for centuries, which rules out any story on which they couldn't. Then mark which of your requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are, on inspection, just the prejudices of the privatization camp or the state camp smuggled in as adequacy conditions, and tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, whether mechanism-design style results about efficiency, budget balance, and voluntary participation mean some pair of the requirements can't coexist, which would itself be the main finding. Close with a brief, honest scoring of privatization-style solutions, centralized regulation, and polycentric self-governance against your own list, in no more than a short paragraph each, since the criteria are the part I care about.

One formatting note: please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I realize a requirements list practically begs to be bulleted, but I'd rather see whether the criteria survive being argued for in sentences, and a numbered list always makes everything look equally load-bearing.
