I write novels, and my current book turns on a broken engagement promise — a woman who, three years into a promise she made casually at twenty-four, breaks it and cannot afterwards say when she first knew she would. I found I could not write her convincingly until I had settled, at least privately, on a view about what promises actually are, and the view my characters have pushed me towards is this: a promise binds not by social convention, nor solely by the wrong done to the promisee, but because the act of promising rewires the promisor's own future. Once I promise you, the promised course of action becomes the expected continuation of my life; my future is, until further notice, the future of someone who keeps this. Breaking the promise is therefore never just a disappointment delivered to you — it forces me to redescribe myself retroactively, to become someone for whom the promise was never really mine, a person who was improvising all along rather than a person who changed. On this view the bindingness of promises is a special case of the cost of rewriting a self mid-narrative, and what we call the seriousness of a promise measures how much of the self has been staked on its keeping.

I am aware this is a novelist's theory of promising, assembled from the inside rather than from the literature, and that is precisely why I want it stress-tested. Tell me what would count against it. Which intuitions about promising — about when we think a promise is released, when breaking one is merely sad rather than culpable, when the bindingness seems to survive the promisee's death or the promisee's own forgiveness — would, if they held firm, sink the view? Which cases can it not describe without contortion: coerced promises, promises extracted by fraud, promises made by children, promises to those who cannot remember them? I am particularly interested in whether the view collapses into a rival it pretends not to be: if the real work is done by the wrong to the promisee, the narrative machinery is decoration; if the real work is done by the cost of self-redescription, then every act that reshapes a self — a career abandoned, a faith renounced, a marriage quietly allowed to die — seems to become something like a broken promise, and I cannot tell whether that is an insight or a category error.

Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those a committed defender of this kind of view would concede as counting against it from those they would dismiss, and be candid about whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if it can, whether that matters for a view of this kind, or whether it is the sort of thesis whose exposure to counterexample is not the main test of it. Where it helps, use the materials I actually have: the writer's cases of promises that fiction keeps returning to, and the fact that fictional characters are, in an odd way, nothing but narrative, which may make them either the best or the worst possible test cases for a theory on which the self is a story one has to keep telling. Please write your answer in British English.
