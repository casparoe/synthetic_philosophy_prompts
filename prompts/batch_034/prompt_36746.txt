Draft:

"The puzzle of moral testimony is usually stated as an asymmetry: testimony seems perfectly fine as a source of belief about geography or chemistry, yet something seems off about forming moral beliefs that way — even when the source is clearly trustworthy. The standard moves are familiar. Either we deny the asymmetry, or we explain it via the knowledge/understanding distinction (you can get moral knowledge by testimony but not moral understanding, and understanding is what you need for acting well), or we argue that moral deference is somehow self-undermining. I want to suggest that all of this rests on a fiction: that our moral beliefs are normally the product of deliberation in the first place. They aren't. Nearly every moral view a normal adult holds — about promise-keeping, punishment, what we owe strangers — was absorbed from parents, peers, and ambient culture long before any deliberation occurred, and testimony played no role because nothing so structured as testimony was going on. So the puzzle only ever arises in the rare case where deference would conflict with someone's self-image as a deliberator, and what we are seeing is not an epistemological anomaly but wounded vanity. Once we see this, the asymmetry with geography and chemistry dissolves: in those domains we never had the self-image to wound. And the real problem the literature keeps dancing around is not deference but correlated deference — echo chambers, communities testifying to one another in a closed loop. That is a social epistemology problem about trust networks, and no amount of precision about moral understanding will touch it."
