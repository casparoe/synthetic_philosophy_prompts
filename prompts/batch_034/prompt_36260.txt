I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and for years the unit on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness felt like an exercise in label-swapping: students could recite that illusionists deny phenomenal properties, name an objection, and collect their marks. That changed this spring. I developed a scotoma following a retinal vascular problem, and for weeks I was unaware of a patch of blindness near the centre of my left visual field: my introspection reported a seamless expanse of colour even as I was failing to find objects on my own desk. I have mostly recovered, but the episode convinced me that my old exam questions sorted memorisers from memorisers. I want to replace them, and I would like your help drafting the replacement.

Please write one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The question should be built around the following crux rather than around exposition. Empirical cases of introspective error — my filled-in scotoma, change blindness, the ordinary blind spot — show that introspection can be wrong about the contents of experience. But the illusionist claims something stronger: that introspection is wrong about the category of phenomenal properties altogether. The question must force students to say whether the weaker, well-documented fallibilism supports the stronger, category-level thesis, and to address what the illusionist can offer as the content of the illusion. An experience that seems to have irreducibly phenomenal properties while having none is either a further datum that the illusionist owes us an account of, or the thesis quietly collapses into reductive realism, and a strong answer will say which and why. Somewhere in the answer, students should have to locate the exact point at which the illusionist's explanatory burden becomes a restatement of the problem of explaining consciousness, and to judge whether that burden is fatal or merely the ordinary cost of any naturalistic theory of mind.

The marking scheme should be prose, not a table of criteria: state in sentences which specific moves an A answer must contain, which omissions should cap an answer at a B, and how candidates typically go wrong. The characteristic errors I want the scheme to name include equating illusionism with the outright denial that consciousness exists, treating the apparent immediacy of experience as a knockdown argument without argument, and answering as though invoking the difficulty of explaining consciousness were itself a refutation of the illusionist rather than something the illusionist is trying to explain away.

Please deliver the question, model answer, and marking scheme in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a letter. Do not include a reading list or bibliography, since I will supply references myself.
