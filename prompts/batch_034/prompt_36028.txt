I'm taking a seminar on moral luck, and to test whether the positions in the literature track anything real I have been collecting verdicts from my brother, who has never read any philosophy. His record so far, all delivered with great confidence: (1) two equally drunk drivers leaving the same bar, one of whom kills a child at a crossing while the other gets home because the crossing happened to be empty — he blames the first far more, would give him prison, and would give the lucky one a fine; (2) two surgeons who each perform the same properly consented operation carrying a twenty per cent risk of death, one patient dying and the other living — he assigns them equal blame and calls the death bad luck for the surgeon who lost; (3) a woman with a naturally vicious temper who does something cruel — he refuses to accept that she cannot help how she was made as an excuse; (4) two equally ruthless men, one born in a country where his ruthlessness could express itself only as sharp but legal business practice, the other born under a dictatorship where the same trait made him a denouncer of his neighbours — he blames the denouncer considerably more; (5) a man who would certainly have become a camp guard had he been born in Germany in 1920, but who was born elsewhere and lived blamelessly — no blame at all. Next week I intend to put three new cases to him: (a) a surgeon who takes a risk well below the standard of care and whose patient dies, set against a colleague who takes the same substandard risk and whose patient survives; (b) a man who drove home drunk every Saturday for thirty years without ever harming anyone and has only just been caught, set against the one-time drunk driver who killed; (c) a drunk driver who mounts the pavement and stops inches short of a pram without making contact. Please reply in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: give his likely verdict on each of the three cases; identify the rule or rules that best fit his record, noting that at least two plausible rules fit it and that they diverge on at least one of the three; say which rule he is most plausibly running and why; and name the further case, beyond the three, that would best separate the candidate rules. Allocate the five points as you see fit, so long as every one of those tasks appears.
