I write crime novels, so I spend an unhealthy amount of time thinking about violence, and my friend Dana — also a novelist, more historical stuff — has this thing she does whenever the "is the world actually getting less violent" debate comes up (you know, the Pinker thing). She insists the whole debate is pointless until you pin down what even counts as violence, and she has very firm rulings about it. We were making lists over wine the other night. Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them:

- a knifing in an alley: violence, obviously
- battle deaths in WWI: violence
- a husband beating his wife, no death: violence — "the death was never the point"
- a seige where the army starves a city into surrender: violence
- executions, say the public hangings in 1800s England: violence — "the state doing it doesn't launder it"
- a famine the government could have relieved but just didn't bother with (no plot, pure indifference): not violence. "a sin, but not violence"
- schoolmasters caning kids in 1900: not violence, "that was just school back then"
- car crash deaths: not violence
- deaths from factory smoke in a mill town: not violence

She also got very worked up about how you count. She says only per-capita rates make any sense, but then she went quiet and admitted the absolute numbers have to matter somehow, because to the person who dies it hardly matters how big the population was, and she couldn't say how both things could be true at once. I'd like to be able to hand that back to her in better shape than she left it.

So. We have three test cases left over from the argument, and I want to know what she'll say before I bring them up again, because she's insufferable when she thinks she's caught me unprepared. (1) Solitary confinement in a modern prison — deliberate, fully authorized, nobody bleeds. (2) A drone strike aimed at one man that kills him plus four people standing near him — I'm confident she'd call the target's death violence, but what about the four? (3) A fraternity hazing that puts a pledge in the hospital, where the pledge agreed to everthing in advance.

Can you work out what rule or rules she's actually running? I suspect more than one rule fits her list, and I'd like to see them laid out side by side. Then predict her verdicts on the three cases, tell me which prediction you'd actually bet money on and why, and suggest the one case I should hold in reserve that would settle which rule she's really running — something she hasn't ruled on yet that splits the candidates cleanly. And if you can untangle the per-capita-versus-absolute-numbers thing at the end, that's a bonus, because she will absolutely bring it up again.
