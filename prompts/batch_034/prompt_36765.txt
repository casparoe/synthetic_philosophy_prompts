I'm a nursing educator putting together some teaching materials on why we do pain screening the way we actually do it, and I keep hitting something I can't quite articulate, so I'm hoping you can reconstruct the philosophy underneath.

The practice: at basically every hospital, intake includes a 0-to-10 pain rating, and the chart treats that number kind of like a vital sign -- some places literally called pain the fifth vital sign. A higher number justifies stronger intervention, and a patient who keeps reporting an 8 while shrugging and watching TV still gets flagged for escalation. Nobody asks "does it bother you," they ask "how bad is the pain," and the number is what counts. People have even blamed this -- taking the number to directly measure the harm -- for some of the opioid overprescribing mess.

Here's what's nagging me. There's this strange condition, pain asymbolia, where patients (usually after certain brain lesions) say they feel pain just fine -- they can locate it, rate its intensity, describe its quality -- but it doesn't bother them at all, and they don't even withdraw from a pinprick. And on the other side, the same hospitals treat the distress component of pain with anxiolytics as a seperate, sometimes optional matter from the pain itself. So the 0-10 practice seems to fold everything into one number and treat that number as the badness.

My question, in the form my students will eventually ask it: what theory of the badness of pain does this practice actually presuppose? Is it committed to pain being bad in proportion to its felt intensity -- in which case the asymbolia patient's 8 is exactly as urgent as anyone else's 8, which seems insane -- or to pain being bad in virtue of its aversiveness, in which case the scale question is the wrong question? Or is it committed to nothing that specific, such that several rival views of pain's badness would all license the same bedside practice? Distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with. And then tell me what would actually change at the bedside if we swapped in one of the rival views -- e.g., if badness tracked aversiveness, should intake ask "how much is this bothering you" instead, and what does that do to the patient whose pain is mild but who is terrified of needles?

One more thread: does the practice of treating the number as more authoritative than the patient's own attitude (calm patient, self-reported 8, still escalate) even fit with our other institutional line, that pain is subjective and the patient is always right about it? Or do those two commitments point in different directions?

Since this is for teaching, please be explicit about which parts are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this -- the empirical claims about asymbolia and the sensory/affective components of pain versus the philosophical theories of what makes pain bad. Don't hand my students one theory dressed up as the consensus.
