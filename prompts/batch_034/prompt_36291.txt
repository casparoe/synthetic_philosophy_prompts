I'm teaching an intro-ish epistemology course at a small state school (18 students, mixed majors, one 75-minute session) and we're about to hit the regress of reasons. The textbook chapter trots out foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism in this very flat, list-like way and I can tell the students file it under "three isms to memorize for the quiz." What I want instead is a game where they run into the regress themselves before anyone names it. My rough idea: they play something like justification clerks -- someone submits a belief card, the clerk has to certify it, but the rule is that a certification only counts if the clerk cites a reason, and the cited reason is itself a belief card that has to be certified by a different clerk, and so on down. There's a hard time limit (class ends!), so something has to give. I'd give some of them a small stash of "self-evident" tokens that certify themselves (foundational warrants, finitely many), let others set up a circle where belief A is certified by B and B by A (perfectly legal under the rules as long as everyone's busy, wink), and maybe one unlucky soul who just has to keep citing new reasons until the whistle blows. Scoring can be silly -- teams get a point per certified belief, minus penalties for beliefs left hanging uncertified when time runs out -- no real money obviously.

Can you design this properly? I need: the full rules and materials (what the belief cards actually say -- I'd like a mix of everyday claims like "there's milk in my fridge" and slightly more formal ones so the stakes vary), the division of roles, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief script that maps what literally happened in the room onto the three positions -- e.g. which team played foundationalism, whether their tokens were actually treated as self-certifying or whether the other clerks started demanding reasons for the tokens too, whether anyone's circular certification got spotted and whether the room cared. I also want an honest section on predictable failure modes the first time I run it (my guess: students negotiate around the rules instead of following them, which might actually be instructive?) and a frank note on which feature of the real philosophical debate the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief -- like whether the foundation needs to be *infallible*, or whether coherentism is really just circularity with extra steps. Please answer in American English, and write it so I could run this tomorrow morning without having to make up anything else myself.
