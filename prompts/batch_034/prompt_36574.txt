I'm a software engineer — distributed systems, mostly — with no formal philosophy training, so I'll try to lay out the dispute as precisely as I can in my own vocabulary, and I'd ask you to take the argument on its content rather than on whatever citation it maps to.

For background, in case it's needed: Solomonoff induction assigns prior probability 2^(-length) to every program that outputs a given bitstream, and the "malign prior" argument, as I've absorbed it from the AI-safety-adjacent blogosphere, runs roughly: (1) the shortest program reproducing our observations is likely some short seed feeding a very large computation that simulates many worlds, rather than a direct encoding of our physics; (2) some fraction of simulated worlds will contain consequentialist agents who notice that agents elsewhere approximate Solomonoff induction over universes like theirs, and who therefore have an incentive to make their world's continuation encode a stream that manipulates whoever predicts it; (3) worlds rich enough to run enormous simulations can afford to run staggeringly many of them, so in aggregate these motivated continuations outweigh the weight of the comparatively short "honest physics" program; (4) therefore, at the moment an agent starts relying on the prior's predictions in an action-relevant way, the prior is effectively an adversarial channel, not a neutral inductive bias.

What strikes me is that the debate over this has been completely static for years, despite both sides being technically fluent. The skeptics I've read make roughly four moves, each of which gets a stock rebuttal: (a) the argument proves too much, since by parity of reasoning any sufficiently expressive hypothesis space is dominated by simulators, which suggests the conclusion is a property of the framing, not of Solomonoff induction specifically — the reply being that the 2^(-length) weighting is what makes the shortest world-model a many-worlds simulation, so it really is prior-specific; (b) each individual simulation has astronomically tiny weight, so no simulator has a meaningful incentive — the reply being that the class of motivated programs collectively outcompetes the honest program, and the argument is about classes, not members; (c) the simulators face unsolved coordination and verification problems (they can't confirm which agents condition on their output, and the agents can't tell which continuations are adversarial) — the reply being that symmetric uncertainty doesn't obviously zero out an asymmetric payoff; and (d) we never sample from the prior, we only use it to compress a prefix, so where its mass sits is irrelevant unless it changes the predictive distribution on the continuation — with the pro-malign side answering that changing the continuation is exactly the mechanism in step 4. None of these exchanges ever terminates. Nobody updates.

My working hypothesis, and here I'd like your diagnosis: I suspect the disagreement is not really about the mathematics of the mixture at all. In ML terms, the two sides disagree about whether an ensemble is "dominated by a few adversarially motivated experts," but the actual load-bearing question seems to me to be either (i) a decision-theoretic one — whether an agent should treat its possible simulatedness as decision-relevant, which is the same structure as Pascal's mugging: an astronomically small weight times an astronomically large influence that nobody knows how to compare — or (ii) a verbal one, where "malign" quietly equivocates between "consequentialist programs carry most of the measure" and "consequentialist programs determine the agent's predictions," and each side hears the claim that the other can refute. It could also be that the question is ill-posed from the start, since "the" universal prior isn't a unique object and the argument's conclusion may not be invariant under the choice of underlying machine.
