Two friends of mine, a physics graduate student and a philosophy graduate student, spent an evening arguing about the nature of time at my kitchen table, and they finally asked me to referee, which I'm not confident I can do. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move?

The exchange went as follows. (1) Maya, the philosopher, opened by defending presentism: only the present exists; the past has ceased to exist and the future does not yet exist, and this is what makes time genuinely different from space. Daniel, the physicist, replied that special relativity undermines exactly that picture: simultaneity is relative to an observer's frame of reference, so there is no fact about which distant events are happening *now* — your present and my present can include different events, and no observer's slice is privileged. A universe-wide objective present, he said, is exactly what relativity denies.

(2) Maya answered that relativity only tells us that our *measurements and coordinates* are frame-relative, not what exists; a theory with a privileged but undetectable frame of reference, in which there really is an objective present, would make all the same predictions. Daniel said that postulating invisible structure that has no empirical consequences whatsoever is the kind of thing scientists rightly shave away, and that Maya is just re-inventing an ether for time.

(3) Maya then changed tack. She asked Daniel to explain, on his preferred view — the block universe where past, present, and future events all equally exist — why we feel relief when a painful experience is over. When the dentist's drill finally stops, we are not comforted by the fact that the pain is earlier than this moment; we are comforted because the pain no longer exists. Relief seems to be precisely relief that something has ceased to be, and that only makes sense if the past is genuinely gone.

(4) Daniel replied with a spatial analogy: a pain in your foot is not made better by being far from your head, but a war being far away in time or space is a genuine comfort. We care about our temporal location the way we care about our spatial location, and the word "now" works like the word "here" — it picks out a place in the block from a perspective located in the block. Nothing about that requires the distant pain to not exist.

(5) Maya's last move was that the analogy fails because "here" moves: I can travel, and what was far becomes near. On the block view, by contrast, nothing moves along the time axis at all — the whole of history is simply there, static, and there is no fact about which moment is the one where you experience time as flowing. The felt passage of time, she said, is not captured by any fact in the block. Daniel closed by saying that passage is a feature of how creatures embedded in the block process information — memories accumulating, anticipations updating — and that a static object can contain, at each moment, a state that *represents* neighboring moments, which is all "flow" ever was.

Please go through their five exchanges and say, at each step, who is right or wrong and why — rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right — and then say who is more right overall. Also indicate what, if anything, both of them are missing (for instance, positions that sit between theirs, or objections that would trouble both equally).
