I teach political philosophy — thirty years on Hobbes through Kant, with a long-running seminar on the republican tradition — and I have lately been pulled into conversations about AI safety by a graduate student who works on it. She insists on a distinction I want to understand on my own terms: the difference between "control" and "alignment." As she explains it, control comprises the safety measures that work even if the model turns out to be misaligned: having a less capable but more trusted model screen the outputs of the more capable one before they reach users; seeding the environment with decoy tasks to see whether the system behaves deceptively when it believes no one is looking; granting privileges in stages as evidence accumulates; keeping a shutdown button that no single component can disable; and in extreme cases withholding capabilities altogether, on the model that could lie fluently about things we cannot verify. Alignment, by contrast, is the attempt to make the model itself want the right things during training, so that safety does not depend on catching it. My student thinks the distinction is crisp; I suspect it will look familiar and strange at once when I force it into my vocabulary.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary of constitutional design, as faithfully as you can. I take the control side to be the Federalist's "auxiliary precautions" against the backdrop of its famous observation: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary." The relevant maxim — that ambition must be made to counteract ambition, and that institutions should be designed as if for knaves, in Hume's spirit — is the grammar I know best, alongside the republican counterclaim that no system of checks can sustain a free state without the formation of virtuous citizens, which I take to be the natural home of "alignment." Map the concrete control measures onto their constitutional analogues: the trusted weaker monitor, the decoy tasks, the staged privileges, the shutdown button, the withheld capabilities. Then produce the residue report I actually care about: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I already see two candidates for the third category. First, in my tradition, the deliberate shaping of the governed's values is the nightmare — the whole case for checks is that the ruler's soul-craft cannot be trusted — whereas in her usage "alignment" is the friendly term and the institutional machinery is the fallback; I want to know whether that flip in valence reflects something real or just whose side you imagine yourself on. Second, constitutional control is a self-enforcing equilibrium among parties who can retaliate, whereas she seems to contemplate an adversary that may simply outgrow the controls; I do not know what, if anything, in the tradition of mixed government corresponds to governing something that becomes more capable than every institution combined. Give me your view on whether the residue shows that my political vocabulary is missing something, or that their distinction is less clear than it looks.
