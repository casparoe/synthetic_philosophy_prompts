I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I need a referee for an argument that broke out at my company's monthly tech-radar session. The trigger was innocuous: one of my colleagues proposed running an internal prediction market to forecast which projects would actually ship on time. That got us onto futarchy — the system proposed by the economist Robin Hanson, with the slogan vote on values, bet on beliefs, where an elected legislature defines a measure of national welfare and conditional prediction markets decide which policies are adopted based on which ones the markets expect to raise that measure. The argument ran six moves, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

The two disputants: Anil, who works on our trading systems and is broadly a markets-solve-this person, and Bianca, who works on data infrastructure and is the resident skeptic of anything that smells of Silicon Valley political engineering. Here is the exchange as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it.

1. Anil opened: the problem with democratic governance is that nobody pays for being wrong. Politicians and voters make predictions about what policies will do, face no penalty when those predictions fail, and so predictions get made on the basis of identity and rhetoric. Traders on a prediction market pay for being wrong every time. We already have evidence that aggregation with skin in the game beats expert panels and pundits. So route the predictions through markets: let the legislature set the target, let markets pick the means.

2. Bianca replied that the entire machinery hangs on the welfare metric, and that this is where it dies. Whatever number the legislature writes down — GDP, some composite index, whatever — traders will discover and exploit the divergence between the metric and the thing it was supposed to proxy. This is Goodhart's law, and markets are the most efficient Goodharting machines ever built. So futarchy doesn't remove politics; it relocates it upstream into a one-time fight over the definition of a number, and once that number is installed, fixing it will require the same gridlocked political process that futarchy was supposed to bypass, except now with the added claim that the market has spoken.

3. Anil conceded that metric design is hard but said relocation is still a win: it quarantines value disagreements from factual disagreements, which currently contaminate each other in every budget fight. And the status quo already Goodharts GDP, just without any market correction at all; a futarchy with an imperfect, revisable metric beats a status quo with an equally imperfect metric and no discipline whatsoever.

4. Bianca then argued that the market correction Anil keeps invoking only works when the feedback loop closes within the horizon of the people doing the correcting. Policy consequences play out over ten to thirty years. A trader who correctly believes a policy is bad must lock up capital for decades against a payoff that arrives after her fund has wound down, so rational traders won't take those positions at scale. Long-horizon policy markets will therefore be thin, and thin market prices reflect liquidity needs and hype rather than probability — at which point you've replaced democratic rhetoric with market noise and given the noise constitutional authority. She added that even Tyler Cowen, hardly an enemy of markets, said of futarchy: "I would bet against the future of futarchy, or its likelihood of succeeding were it in place."

5. Anil answered the manipulation worry (implied by thin markets) with the standard line: manipulation is self-defeating, because a deep-pocketed actor who pushes the price away from the truth is offering free money to informed traders, who take the other side, correct the price, and profit at the manipulator's expense. Manipulators don't distort futarchy; they subsidize its accuracy.

6. Bianca closed with what struck me as the sharpest point of the evening: the self-defeating-manipulation argument only works if manipulation is repeated, indiscriminate, and treated as an investment. But consider a one-shot, timed purchase. An actor — a state, an industry cartel — that stands to gain trillions from a particular policy being adopted can rationally burn billions holding the price up for the final forty-eight hours before the adoption decision. Yes, informed traders eventually profit at the manipulator's expense, but the policy has already been adopted, and the manipulator never wanted the money back. The money-pump defense treats manipulation as an investment when, for some actors, it's a purchase — and futarchy has no mechanism that distinguishes the two.

They ended by agreeing to disagree, which in practice means Bianca thinks she won.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification that engages the argument as actually made rather than a better version of it. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — since I suspect the real weakness is somewhere neither of them looked — what consideration both of them are missing. I'd particularly like your view on whether Bianca's sixth move is fatal to futarchy as such, or merely a design constraint that a more careful version of the system could engineer around, and what such a workaround would have to look like.
