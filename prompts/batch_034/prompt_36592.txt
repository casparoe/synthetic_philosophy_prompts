Two friends from my behavioral econ reading group, Priya and Jonas, had a long argument at dinner last week about weakness of will and I want someone to actually referee it. Background: we'd been reading about hyperbolic discounting — the fact that people's discount curves are steeper in the near term, so a reward that looks clearly better when it's a week away can flip and lose to a smaller, sooner reward as it gets close. (1) Jonas opened by saying akrasia is kind of a fake category: the person who at 9pm sincerely resolves to run at 6am and the person at 5:59am who hits snooze aren't one agent failing itself — they're two vantage points with different preference rankings, and once the curves cross, the rankings genuinely flip. Calling the 9pm preference the "real" one and the 5:59am one a "weakness" is arbitrary; there's no view from nowhere saying the long-term preference deserves to win. (2) Priya said that dissolves the phenomenon in name only: if there were no real conflict, why does everyone buy alarm clocks and put them across the room, why do people delete apps and buy small packages of cookies? You only build fences against something inside you that you expect to win. So the conflict is real and so is the loser. (3) Jonas conceded the conflict but said it still doesn't add up to irrationality — each choice maximizes discounted utility from its own vantage point, and "irrational" needs a normative standard; hyperbolic discounting is a mechanism, not a verdict. All Priya had shown is that the evening self loses the fight, not that it should win — and by what right does the earlier self get to speak for the person, anyway? (4) Priya answered with personal rules: if you treat "I run every morning" as one standing decision rather than a fresh negotiation every day at 5:59, then a single morning off isn't just one slightly bad bargain — it undermines your confidence that you'll comply tomorrow and the day after, so the whole bundle of future mornings collapses together. That's a standard of rationality generated from inside the agent's own practice, no view from nowhere needed, and on that standard the snoozer is making a detectable mistake, not just losing a fair fight. Jonas's last word was that this just means the morning self is bad at bargaining, and "bad at bargaining" was never what anyone meant by weakness of will.

They finally turned to me and I honestly couldn't say who had won what. Can you go through the four moves and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall — and also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing? And please be careful to flag which parts of this are actually settled among people who study it (like whether the preference reversals are real) versus which parts are genuinely contested (like whether akrasia is irrational or just, as Jonas would put it, the evening self losing a fair fight) — I don't want you presenting one side of some contested question as if it were consensus.
