I chair the Graduate Studies Committee of a mid-sized university mathematics department, and I need a decision memo for our meeting next month on a policy question none of our existing rules cover: what to do about AI-assisted proof submissions in graduate coursework.

Here is the situation. Over the past two years, AI assistants have gone from a curiosity to the default tool our PhD students open when stuck on a problem. Our course policies are mostly silent on this; the few that speak say students may not submit "work that is not their own," and no one on the committee agrees on how that clause applies to a tool that iterates with you, proposes constructions you'd never have found, and argues back when you object.

The case that forced the issue: a second-year student in our model theory course submitted a problem set containing a correct, elegant back-and-forth construction. At office hours the following week, the grader asked him to walk through a variant; he could not reproduce any part of the argument, including the move his own write-up presented as the natural next step. He said, without embarrassment, that he had arrived at it after a long conversation with an AI and had checked each step carefully. Under our current rules he is not accused of anything, and no one thinks he was cheating. The grader's question is more unsettling: what is a proof worth as evidence of learning when its author could not regenerate it — and we have no answer.

For want of a better term, the committee has started calling this the *oracular case*: a submission whose author treats it as an oracle's pronouncement — verified step by step, accepted as correct, but with no residual ability to participate in the reasoning that produced it. The memo should take a position on whether the oracular case is a failure of honesty, a failure of learning, or an acceptable division of cognitive labor — because our policy choice follows from that position, not the other way around.

Three proposals are on the table, and I want the memo to recommend one (or a defensible hybrid), with reasoning the grader and the students could actually engage with:

1. A prohibition on AI use for all submitted graduate work, enforced how we enforce other integrity rules.
2. A disclosure regime: AI assistance permitted but cited, with the student required to state which parts they could not have produced unaided.
3. A reconstructability standard (my own suggestion, and the one I'm least sure about): no restrictions on how students reach a proof, but every student must be prepared, on demand, to rederive a submitted argument from a clean sheet within a fixed time; scheduled or sampled "reconstruction defenses" carry consequences for failure.

The memo must also confront our department's own inconvenient history honestly. We already accept, as a discipline, computer-assisted proofs no human has fully checked, and we teach results — the classification of the finite simple groups is the obvious example — that no individual mathematician, including the specialists who teach it, can verify in full from scratch. If collective, tool-mediated, and trust-based verification is legitimate at the level of the research community, the memo needs to say whether the student's relation to his AI is different in kind or merely in degree — and if in degree, whether the degree matters: whether there is a line at which "understanding the proof" stops being an achievement of the student and becomes a report about someone else's achievement. Please give the losing proposals their strongest form rather than a strawman, since the memo will be circulated to the whole department, including colleagues who think all three options are overreactions to a tool mathematicians have always had in some form.

Deliverables, in this order: first the memo itself (addressed to the committee, with a clear recommendation and an implementation sketch — who is affected, when it takes effect, how the first year is reviewed); then, appended after the memo, a structured summary as a single JSON object with the following fields:

- "recommendation": a one-line statement of the chosen policy;
- "position_on_oracular_case": one of "failure of honesty", "failure of learning", or "acceptable division of labor", with a clause of justification;
- "conditions": an array of implementation requirements, each a short string;
- "strongest_dissent": the best argument for the most serious losing option, stated in one or two sentences;
- "affected_parties": an array of strings naming the groups the policy touches;
- "review_trigger": the concrete event or date that should force the committee to revisit the policy.
