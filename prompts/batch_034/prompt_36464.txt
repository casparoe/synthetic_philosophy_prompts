I'm a software engineer at a mid-size ML company — backend systems background, no formal training in philosophy or ML research, though I've read enough interpretability blog posts to be dangerous. Our team builds evaluation and monitoring infrastructure for the models we deploy. Next month I'm running a forty-minute brown-bag session for fellow engineers on mechanistic anomaly detection. These are smart people, comfortable with transformers at the level of "attention, layers, weights," but with zero interpretability experience. They already understand behavioral anomaly detection intuitively; I don't need help with that part. The thing I want the whole session to orbit — and the thing I keep failing to explain crisply to myself, let alone to them — is the question of what counts as "the same mechanism."

Here's the situation that motivates the session. Our monitoring currently flags anomalous outputs. The pitch we keep hearing from the safety team is that we should additionally flag inputs where the model produces a perfectly unremarkable output via a different internal route than it usually takes for inputs of that type — because a benign-looking answer computed by a mechanism we never saw during evaluation is exactly the case our dashboards are blind to. My engineers will immediately ask: same route relative to what? How do you decide that Tuesday's sarcasm-detection and Friday's sarcasm-detection are "the same mechanism" when the weights differ numerically between the two runs, the activations are never identical twice, and any two mechanisms anyone has ever described get described at different levels of granularity — features over neurons over heads over layers? I have a growing suspicion that this is a conceptual problem dressed up as a metrics problem, which is why I'm asking for a teaching plan rather than a tooling recommendation.

So: plan the session; don't deliver it. Specifically, I need:

1. **The order of ideas.** What has to be established before the "same mechanism" question is even well-posed for this audience, and what depends on what. I suspect I can't open with a definition of "mechanism," because they'll all quietly import "code" as their mental model and get stuck on the fact that a network has no call sites — but you should tell me if that suspicion is wrong.

2. **The one carrying example.** It should be concrete enough to picture without any interpretability tooling, and it should exhibit two genuinely different internal mechanisms producing overlapping behavior. My tentative candidate: a model that learned to detect "this text contains a digit" via some tokenizer-level regularity during training, but after a fine-tune on newly rendered digit images, starts catching digits via an entirely different, visual-features route — with output accuracy unchanged. Choose or design the example, state what it must have and what it must lack (in particular, it cannot rest on any claim about what interpretability researchers have actually found inside real models, because I can't defend that in Q&A), and say why the obvious alternatives fail.

3. **The misconceptions they'll bring or form.** At minimum these four, in the order you predict they'll actually surface, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: (a) "mechanism = the weights," so same weights means same mechanism and different weights means different; (b) "same mechanism = same behavior on probe inputs," which quietly collapses mechanistic back into behavioral anomaly detection — this is the one I most expect to lose the room to; (c) the code analogy run amok: that mechanisms have exact boundaries the way functions do, so sameness is all-or-nothing; (d) "it's just clustering activations," i.e., that sameness is a solved technical matter of picking a similarity metric, with no irreducible judgment call about the level of description. If you'd predict a fifth misconception for this audience, add it.

4. **The status of my pet analogy.** I keep wanting to use "did the refactor reuse the existing helper function or reimplement it inline?" as the intuition pump, since everyone on my team has argued about exactly that in code review. Tell me whether that analogy is load-bearing enough to build on, and if so, at what point in the sequence it has to be explicitly broken — and how — before it hardens into misconception (c).

5. **A check for understanding** I can pose in the last five minutes: a question or mini-exercise whose correct answer requires them to distinguish "different behavior, same mechanism" from "same behavior, different mechanism," rather than just nodding at the vocabulary.

6. **What to openly refuse to cover, and say so.** My assumption is that any actual interpretability technique — probing classifiers, attribution methods, whatever — is out of scope in forty minutes; the session is about the concept of mechanistic sameness, not how to measure it. Tell me if you disagree, and what else belongs on the "we are not doing this today" slide so the session doesn't dissolve into tooling questions.

One hard constraint: no readings, no citations, no appeals to what some paper showed. Anything I put on a slide I have to be able to derive or defend myself at the whiteboard.
