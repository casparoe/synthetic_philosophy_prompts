I'm an early-career philosopher, about three years out from a PhD in physics-flavored structuralist metaphysics, and I'm deciding whether to sink my next few years into the panpsychist combination problem. Please think of yourself as someone who knows this corner of philosophy of mind well and reason carefully before prioritizing.

The background: panpsychists say consciousness is already there at the micro level, but then they owe us a story about how micro-experiences add up to one macro-experience like mine. Within that, people gesture at several distinct sub-problems -- roughly, a subject-summing one (why is there a single subject here at all, rather than a swarm of micro-subjects?), a structural one (what causal or organizational fact about the brain makes it the right kind of thing to host a unified consciousness?), and a palette one (where do macro-level qualities like the specific feel of red come from, if micro-experience is too dim and simple to contain them?). Some also say there's a mirror-image problem, a "decombination" one: if there's a cosmic consciousness, why does it divide into subjects like you and me at all -- though you can tell me whether that's really a distinct problem or just the original one run in reverse.

What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey. Which of these sub-problems is most worth my next three years, in order, and why? For each, tell me whether it's genuinely upstream of the others (does progress on it make the rest easier, or are they mostly independent?), whether it's tractable for someone with my background rather than, say, someone in phenomenology or neuroscience, and what would count as real progress, since a lot of writing in this area seems to move labels around rather than settle anything. If you think the honest answer is that the field hasn't earned a ranking yet and some of these "problems" are artifacts of panpsychist bookkeeping, say that too -- but defend it rather than just shrugging.

Also, two requests about the response itself. First, please separate what's actually settled among people who know this literature from what's genuinely contested -- e.g. don't present one proposed solution to the subject-summing problem as if it were the consensus view when it's really one contested position among several. Second, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the ranked list plus your headline reason -- and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
