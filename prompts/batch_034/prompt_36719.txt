I run a philosophy discussion group — a dozen graduate students, mixed subfields, comfortable with expected utility and with causal and evidential decision theory from a seminar unit, and they have already watched causal decision theory lose Newcomb's problem to a show of hands. Next session is ninety minutes, one whiteboard, and I want to teach updateless decision theory: the idea that a rational agent chooses the best policy or decision function in advance and then acts from it, rather than updating on each observation and deciding afresh. Plan how I should explain it, rather than explaining it to me. What has to be established before the updateless move can be presented as a response to a problem rather than an eccentric taste, and in what order does the material have to go? Which example should carry the session — I am torn between the standard counterfactual mugging and something less pre-loaded, since I suspect the mugging smuggles in exactly the intuition about the other branch that the session needs to earn — and what must that carrying example have, and lack? Which misconceptions should I expect — I have already caught one student saying updatelessness means never updating on evidence at all, and another treating it as ordinary precommitment that you are simply unlucky not to have already made — and at exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off? I have also drafted my own variant, which I call the sealed verdict: the coin is flipped and its outcome sealed before anyone chooses, and the group must adopt a single policy that individual members, later assigned to different branches, are then bound to execute. Where should I deploy it, and what should it be doing that the standard examples cannot — is it a check or a teaching load-bearing element? Close with one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they have the idea rather than the slogan, ideally one whose updateless and intuitive answers come apart in a way we have not yet discussed. And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I am thinking logical uncertainty, proof-based agents, and disputes about which mathematical facts a decision can be correlated with — and where to draw that line so that it reads as a statement of scope rather than evasion.
