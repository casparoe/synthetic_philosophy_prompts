I am a philosopher by trade, though my own work lies in ethics and the history of philosophy, and a pattern in contemporary talk about "generations" has been bothering me for some time. I have looked for the standard concept and cannot find one, so let me ask whether the literature contains one — and, if not, what I should call it.

The pattern has three stages, and it is the third that interests me. First, a label — Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z — is introduced as a convenient way of grouping birth years. Everyone concedes, when pressed, that the cutoffs were drawn more or less arbitrarily, variously by demographers, journalists, and marketers, and that the borders have drifted over time. Second, once the label is in circulation, people begin to live inside it: they are addressed as members of the kind, they identify with it, and they report their anxieties, purchases, and politics partly in its vocabulary — I have watched students born within a year or two of a disputed border describe themselves differently depending on which side of it a website has placed them. Third — and this is the step I want named — the dispositions that the label's circulation has partly produced are then cited as evidence that the label was tracking a real kind all along. Surveys reveal that the cohort is anxious, or thrifty, or attached to its phones; the results are treated as a discovery about a group that existed, with those very properties, prior to and independently of the labeling. What began as a filing device is retroactively converted into a finding.

The nearest familiar idea is the self-fulfilling prophecy, but that seems not to capture it. In a self-fulfilling prophecy a prediction acts upon a group that exists independently of the prediction; here the classification, the group's boundaries, and even the visibility of the boundary-drawing are part of what gets produced, and the output is not a prediction that came true but an alleged discovery that was manufactured. Plain definitional drift also fails to fit, since nothing about the extension needs to change for the retroactive step to occur.

Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on kinds — social, natural, or otherwise? If it does, I want to know specifically how the established concept handles the third stage, the conversion of an artifact into a discovery. If it does not, I would like you to coin a term for it: supply a definition, state precisely what the phenomenon includes and excludes, and test the term against boundary cases — astrological signs, which label without any feedback; diagnostic categories, which feed back without the retroactive discovery claim; and national identities, which involve both but with the initial conventionality rarely conceded so candidly. Please do not simply hand me a label; I want to know where the phenomenon ends.
