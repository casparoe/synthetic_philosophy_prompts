In a recent faculty seminar on historiography, a colleague of mine waved off a graduate student's reconstruction of a defeated peasant revolt with the remark that history is written by the victors, as though that single sentence settled whether the reconstruction could be trustworthy. The student, to her credit, refused to concede, and the exchange quickly talked past itself, because it became obvious that the proverb was doing several different jobs at once for different people in the room. I want to prepare a handout for the next session that disentangles the slogan rather than adjudicating the dispute directly.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that the sentence could be expressing. For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim about the sources and institutions that shape which accounts survive, a strong epistemic claim about our inability to know what actually happened, or a fully relativist claim on which there is no fact of the matter to be known. Distinguish carefully between claims about the production and preservation of sources, claims about narrative framing and emphasis, claims about which interpretations gain institutional authority, and claims about the availability of the vanquished's perspective at all — and note where oral history, history from below, and work on defeated or suppressed groups bear on each reading. For each reading, state whether it would actually have supported my colleague's use of the proverb to dismiss a reconstruction based heavily on the victors' own records, or whether it leaves such a reconstruction at most partially undermined. Conclude by saying which reading you take to be the one people most often intend when they deploy the proverb, and whether that reading is true.

Please present the readings as a numbered list, since I intend to distribute it for discussion.
