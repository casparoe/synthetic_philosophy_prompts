A colleague of mine argues as follows: refusing to pay in Pascal's mugging is intellectually dishonest, because the structure of the mugging is exactly the structure of insurance, which we all buy without hesitation. In both cases you hand over a small, certain sum in exchange for a low-probability, high-magnitude outcome: with insurance, a payout you would desperately need if catastrophe struck; with the mugger, enormous promised benefits if his story is true. The only visible differences are cosmetic — the insurer has an office and a license, the mugger has a knife and a story. So, my colleague concludes, either you should cancel your insurance or you should pay the mugger, and refusing to pay while keeping your policies is inconsistency dressed up as common sense.

I am half convinced and would like a proper audit of the analogy before I decide which half to keep. Please set out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the premium, the actuarial table, the insurer's balance sheet and claims-paying ability, the regulator, the reputation the insurer stakes on paying out, the adverse-selection and moral-hazard effects that make insurance markets what they are — and say which of these have no counterpart in the mugging at all. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's consistency argument from the ones that do not. A few worries I want addressed along the way: with insurance, the probability estimates are grounded in loss data and the insurer's demonstrated solvency, whereas the mugger's are generated by the mugger himself; the mugger can raise the claimed payoff without limit, and it is at least arguable that a sane prior over his claims shrinks more slowly than the payoff grows; and the pool structure of insurance — many premiums funding few payouts — seems to have no analogue when a single stranger makes a single offer to a single mark.

Finally, tell me whether the dilemma survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does expected-utility reasoning really commit you to treating the two cases alike? Is there a principled way to buy insurance and still refuse the mugger — through the shape of the prior over adversarially generated hypotheses, through bounded utility, through decision rules that handle unspecified payoffs differently from specified ones — or does every such move amount to ad hoc patching of a framework that was never meant for offers of this kind? If the insurance analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say what a better analogy for the mugger's offer would be, if there is one.
