I'm a retired analytical chemist -- 35 years, mostly instrument calibration and measurement traceability -- who got into philosophy a couple of years back after retiring, and my reading group has spent months on Hume's essay on the standard of taste and the question whether beauty is objective. The debate has completely stalled, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's how it looks from where I sit. Everyone in the group accepts Hume's starting point: beauty isn't in objects the way mass is, it's a response in spectators. Everyone also accepts his move that some verdicts are still better than others, via his ideal critics -- the people with delicate taste, long practice, wide comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense, whose joint verdict is supposed to settle things (his crack about preferring Ogilby to Milton being the stock example). And there it stops. The objectivists in my group say once you have that, beauty IS objective in the only sense worth wanting. The skeptics say the move is circular or empty: either you pick the judges independant of their verdicts, in which case they disagree and there is no standard, or you pick them because of their verdicts, in which case "objective" means nothing more than that tastes happen to align among people we already decided to credit.

My hunch, and it probably comes from my old job: in calibration you never certify an instrument against a standard that was itself certified by that same instrument -- you need a seperate traceability chain, or the whole thing collapses into "the instrument agrees with itself." So the skeptics' regress complaint feels very natural to me. But I can also see that Hume's side never claimed the kind of objectivity mass has, so maybe the two halves of my group aren't disagreeing at all: one is answering "do qualified judges converge?" and the other is answering "are there beauty facts that would hold even if no judges existed?", and both keep calling their answer "beauty is objective" or "beauty isn't."

So: is this a verbal dispute about the word "objective," a real substantive one about whether aesthetic convergence is stable enough -- across cultures, generations, new art forms -- to carry normative weight, or a collision of bedrock intuitions that no argument could move? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, splitting "objective" into a weak convergence sense and a strong sense, actual data on how much ideal critics agree and over what timescales, a change of question -- or should I expect no progress at all, and why? I'd also take a view on whether Hume himself quietly settled for the weak sense while sounding like he was answering the strong one, since half my group thinks he did, and if so that might explain why everyone keeps talking past each other.
