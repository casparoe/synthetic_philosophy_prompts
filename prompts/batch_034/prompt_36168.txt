I edit a small epistemology newsletter, and for our month on higher-order evidence — the cases where the most important thing you learn is something about the quality of your own evidence rather than about the world — I want to run a reader-facing diagnostic: a "which view do you actually hold?" quiz. The questions must sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they've read about, so no question or answer option may mention akrasia, conciliationism, steadfastness, defeat, or any position's name.

Build the quiz from the logical space first, then locate the positions in it. The axes I want you to use are: (1) whether it can ever be rational to believe a proposition while also believing that one's total evidence doesn't adequately support it (the akratic combination); (2) whether the rational force of higher-order evidence depends on its source — a physiological impairer such as sleep deprivation or a drug, a statistical calibration report on one's past track record, disagreement with an acknowledged peer, or the verdict of an expert who has inspected one's reasoning; (3) whether higher-order evidence affects first-order credence continuously (each increment of higher-order doubt nudges the object-level credence down) or only at a threshold (nothing happens until the higher-order case crosses a bar, after which it defeats outright); and (4) whether higher-order evidence functions as ordinary evidence, weighed alongside the object-level considerations, or as a structural constraint on rationality that operates at a different level. If you think a fifth axis is needed, add it and briefly say why.

The cases: eight to ten, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, and at least half of them invented rather than drawn from the literature — I don't want readers recognizing a textbook case and pattern-matching to the conclusion they remember. Two cases you must include, both new: (a) a scenario in which a person receives, after the fact, a reliable-but-not-infallible report that their reasoning during some calculation was subtly impaired, where they cannot locate any specific error and their object-level evidence still strikes them as compelling; and (b) a scenario in which the person's higher-order evidence is itself known to be misleading in this instance — they know the calibration report flagging them is a false positive — which tests whether readers think misleading higher-order evidence still exerts rational pressure. Each option should be phrased as a claim about what the person rationally ought to believe, or how confident they ought to be, and a reader must be able to choose it without knowing which position it feeds.

The following leaves must appear, under names of your choosing but with accurate glosses: the view that akratic combinations are sometimes rational; the coherence or fixed-point view on which one's current credences always constrain what it is rational to conclude from further evidence; the view on which higher-order evidence gets weighed in and typically lowers first-order confidence; the view that higher-order evidence matters only through ordinary object-level facts, if at all; and the conciliationist and steadfast readings of peer disagreement. Note any combinations of axis answers that no one in the literature occupies, and for each, say whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate. For example, a reader who is steadfast about peer disagreement because they think self-trust is default-justified, versus one who is steadfast because they take the peer's evidence to have been screened off by their own — if your questions cannot tell these apart, say so rather than pretending the scoring key does.

Format: deliver the whole instrument as a JSON object, preceded by a prose preamble of at most 150 words. The schema: an "axes" array, each element an object with "name" and "diagnostic_question"; a "cases" array, each element with "id", "scenario", and an "options" array of objects with "label" and "text"; a "scoring_key" mapping option-patterns to leaf names, at whatever granularity you can honestly support; a "leaves" array, each element with "name", "gloss", and "awkward_commitment" (the single consequence of the position that its defenders find hardest to swallow); an "unoccupied" array, each element with "combination" and "diagnosis"; an "unseparable_pairs" array; and a final "fragility" object. Use "fragility" for the epistemic honesty I want from you: list the three places the quiz is most likely to misclassify a reader; name the single case you would revise first and why; state what consideration about the axes themselves would lead you to rebuild the taxonomy rather than adjust a question; and say, briefly, what an expert on higher-order evidence would most likely object to in your construction. If any axis turns out to do less work than the schema implies, say that too rather than quietly keeping it.
