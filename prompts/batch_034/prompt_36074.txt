I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, currently at the reporting stage of a profile of a computational neuroscientist — I'll call her Dr. R. — whose lab models decision circuitry in foraging animals. She is cheerfully impatient with philosophy of mind, and when I raised the causal exclusion problem in our interview, she answered with an analogy she says she uses with her graduate students every year. My editor wants a closing paragraph on whether her confidence is earned, and before I commit to a framing, I'd like the analogy itself stress-tested.

The worry I put to her was Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument, roughly: the physical domain is causally closed, so every physical event that has a sufficient cause has a sufficient physical cause; mental events, if they do any work in behavior, must do it by causing physical events; and if mental events are distinct from physical events, they seem to be left either causing nothing at all or sharing every effect with a physical cause that was already sufficient on its own — a firing squad in which each bullet alone would kill, and in which the two firings are mysteriously synchronized in every single case without any shared source. Kim's point, as I understand it, is that such pervasive, coordinated overdetermination is not something you should accept, and that the least mysterious diagnosis is that the mental cause was never there.

Dr. R. waved all this off. Her analogy: nobody thinks a sorting routine in a spreadsheet is causally idle just because transistors are doing the work at their level. Software is to hardware, she said, roughly as the mental is to the neural — both levels are real, both cause the output in their own idiom, and nothing the hardware does puts the software out of a job. On that picture, the exclusion argument is an artifact of treating causes as competitors fighting over a single slot, when really each level has its own coherent story about why the output appeared. Her conclusion: mental causation is no more mysterious than software causation, and anyone who has ever trusted a laptop to balance the books has already made peace with it. (She also remarked, in passing, that temperature is in the same position — it's "just" mean molecular kinetic energy, and nobody concludes thermometers work by magic.)

I described this over dinner to a philosopher friend — T. — who has written about the exclusion literature, and he thinks the analogy is doing precisely the work it cannot do. His points, as best I can reconstruct them:

(1) The analogy is unstable between two readings. Read one way, the routine's causing the sorted column just is the transistor configuration's causing it, described coarsely — in which case the mental analogue is a version of the identity theory, and Dr. R. has quietly conceded that there is no second cause for the exclusion argument to exclude. But then, he says, the live question becomes whether anything causes anything qua mental: a red patch causes the pigeon to peck, but is that because it is red or because it is scarlet, and a merely abstract description of an event does not, by itself, push anything around. Read the other way — software as a genuinely distinct cause over and above the hardware — Kim's principle applies to it with full force: the transistor-level story is already causally sufficient, so the algorithm either is that configuration or is riding along, and the analogy has merely relocated the problem from minds to Microsoft.

(2) Software is a poor hostage regardless, because it is designed: engineers' intentions stand behind the correspondence between the algorithm level and the hardware level, and there is no designer in the head to keep the two levels from coming apart.

(3) Yet T. conceded something that unsettled me. If the analogy fails, so does his own comfort with saying that the recession caused the layoffs or that temperature made the mercury rise. He says this is fine — the special sciences are all in the same boat, and the boat demonstrably floats, since higher-level explanations predict and control. But that rescue seems to leave Dr. R. with something other than what she claimed: on T.'s fallback, mental causation survives only in whatever way software causation would survive if it turned out to be a bookkeeping convenience.

So here is what I need. Audit the analogy as she was using it. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the mental state, the neural state, the behavior, the physical laws, the multiple realizability that both sides invoke — and mark where the mapping holds. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the ones that don't; I don't care that laptops are dry and brains are wet. Then adjudicate between R and T where they actually collide — is the two-readings dilemma in (1) fatal to her use of the analogy, is the design point in (2) doing real work, and does (3) amount to a rescue or to a different verdict wearing her verdict's coat? Finally, tell me whether some other analogy in the same family — the temperature one she tossed off, or something better if you know of it — would carry her weight more honestly, and whether her relaxed conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

Please close with a machine-readable final line of exactly one of the following forms: \answer{survives}, \answer{survives with caveats}, or \answer{does not survive} — reflecting your considered judgment on whether her relaxed conclusion comes through the audit intact, qualified, or defeated.
