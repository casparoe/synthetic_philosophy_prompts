Been down a huge Sidgwick rabbit hole lately (blame the LessWrong-adjacent posts about him being the actually-good Victorian utilitarian), and the part nobody talks about enough is his side gig: he helped found the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and was its first president, then spent basically the rest of his life on mediums and thought-transference experiments, personally sitting in dark rooms investigating people like Florence Cook, while his wife Nora did a lot of the heavy lifting on the statistics for the telepathy trials. The man was an agnostic who was openly bummed about death and wanted real evidence of survival — he kept insisting the whole point was to bring actual scientific method to the ghost stuff, neither sneering at it nor buying it.

So here's my ask: he died in 1900. Predict what he'd have made of what happened to his little corner of inquiry after he was gone. Concretely:

1. The Rhine era — psi moving out of the séance room and into the lab with card-guessing experiments and stats departments. Would he have seen that as the natural continuation of what he and Nora were doing, or as losing something (the actual mediums, the theory of survival) while gaining rigor?

2. Bem's precognition paper in 2011, published in a flagship psychology journal, then the failed replications and the general fallout — which, as everyone here knows, became one of the poster children for the replication crisis. Put Sidgwick in that timeline: does he defend Bem (the methods were standard at the time, and he knows what it's like to chase weird results), or does he go full preregistration evangelist?

3. The modern open-science / rationalist epistemics stack: preregistered protocols, base rates, the "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" line (which postdates him), explicit priors, adversarial collaboration. There's a real temptation to read Sidgwick as proto-rationalist — "careful guy tries to apply hard method to claims everyone else either mocks or swallows" is basically our genre. Is that reading right, or is it us doing the thing we accuse other people of, retconning a Victorian into our tribe?

4. The motivated-reasoner question, which is the one I actually care about. A bunch of the mediums he investigated got exposed as frauds after his death, and cold reading as an explanation for mediumship got way better articulated later. Given how much he emotionally wanted survival to be true (and he was pretty honest about that in print), where does his rigor break, if anywhere? Would he have been the most rigorous guy in the room in 2011, or a very smart man running sophisticated statistics in service of a conclusion he couldn't let go of? Is there a version where he'd have updated against survival evidence entirely, and how much of a personal crisis would that have been for him given his whole eschatology-of-ethics thing where death threatens to break the rationality of egoism?

Take actual stances please, "he was a complex figure who was both rigorous and motivated" is a cop-out unless you tell me in which situations each Sidgwick shows up. And since this is all counterfactual: tell me how confident you are in each of your predictions, and explicitly flag the single point where you feel least sure — I'd much rather get calibrated guesses with the soft spots marked than a confident-sounding hedge. For what it's worth I've read the Methods of Ethics and a bunch of SPR-adjacent stuff, so you can assume I know the basics, but I havent read the bulk of his psychical writings firsthand, so if you're leaning on a specific episode of his career, say a sentence about what the source of that claim is for you.
