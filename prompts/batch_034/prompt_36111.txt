The argument between strong defenders of the precautionary principle and their cost-benefit critics has, as far as I can tell, been running in place for three decades. The defenders hold that under deep uncertainty and irreversibility, catastrophic downside risks deserve disproportionate weight and the burden of proof falls on those who would act; the critics reply that precautions are themselves risky actions, so a principle applied consistently either forbids everything or forbids nothing — the familiar charge of paralysis — and that whatever coherent residue survives is just ordinary risk analysis with extra steps. Both sides have heard the other's standard points many times, and nobody moves. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "precaution," with one side in fact naming a decision rule for deep uncertainty and the other a policy presumption about burdens of proof? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, say about how often regulatory inaction turns out worse than premature regulation? Or does it bottom out in bedrock intuitions about the asymmetry between doing and allowing harm that neither side can argue for? My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the genuinely live part of the dispute is a question about option value under irreversibility — whether, when an effect is irreversible, waiting is generally the better-buy — and if that question were settled, the remainder of the debate would collapse into ordinary expected-cost analysis. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says, say which parts of the disagreement are substantive and which were about words, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal model, a class of cases both sides would accept as data, or nothing — and if nothing, why.
