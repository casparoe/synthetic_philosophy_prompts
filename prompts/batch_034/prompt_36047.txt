I am a novelist, and the premise of my current book depends on a conceptual question I now realize I cannot handle casually. The novel concerns a fictional engineered organism, a lichen-like synthetic construct released into a dying estuary, which metabolizes, repairs itself, and spreads — but whose designers built in error-correction so effective that it copies itself without meaningful mutation, and therefore does not evolve in the Darwinian sense. My narrator, a compliance officer sent to decide whether the thing is an ecosystem, a product, or a contaminant, keeps reaching for a definition of life that turns out not to be sitting on any shelf. A university outreach program has now invited me to give a fifty-minute public session on "what counts as alive" for adult readers with no science background beyond school, and I want to do justice to the fact that my novel's crisis is a genuine crisis in the philosophy of biology rather than a metaphor I invented.

I do not want you to explain the concept to me; I want a plan for explaining it. What has to be established first before the question of definition can even be raised — whether the audience needs to grasp that "life" may be a cluster concept or a family resemblance term before they see why each proposed criterion (metabolism, self-replication, homeostasis, evolvability, autonomy) gets its own counterexample — and what depends on what. Which single example should carry the whole session: I suspect the virus alone will not do, since audiences treat it as a trivia question with a yes-or-no answer, so advise me on what the carrying case must achieve and what it should be instead. Which misconceptions should I expect — I anticipate the belief that scientists hold a settled definition and are merely withholding it, the sense that life is one hidden ingredient or spark, and the slide from "is it alive" to "does it matter" — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left to fester until questions. Finally, what check at the end would show the audience has grasped the shape of the problem rather than merely enjoyed the borderline cases, and which cases I should openly refuse to deal with at this level.

Two constraints, please. Assume no readings, no handouts, and no chemistry beyond what a metaphor can carry. And tell me frankly whether my fictional organism helps or hurts: whether a designed non-evolving replicator is a sharp test case that will make the definitional strain visible, or a distraction that lets the audience file the whole problem under science fiction and stop doing the work. If you think certain borderline entities — prions, sterile worker insects, fire, the biosphere considered as a whole — earn their place in the sequence, say where and why; if they muddy the water, cut them.
