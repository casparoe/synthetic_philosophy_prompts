I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on democratic theory, and next week I have one 75-minute session with 18 students on the Condorcet jury theorem. Some context: I have just come out of a very long argument with a close friend — a mathematician — who maintains that the theorem is a simple, unconditional result: if each voter is even slightly more likely to be right than wrong, then sufficiently large majorities are essentially infallible, and everything else is noise. I kept replying that the premises do all the work — competence strictly above one half, independence of errors, a binary choice with an objective fact of the matter — and that the theorem says nothing about experts, wisdom, or value disagreements; but I never got him to feel it. I want my students to feel it, in the room, before we read the formal statement.

Design the session as a game they play rather than a lecture they receive. My sense is that it needs at least three phases: one in which each student's individual accuracy is engineered to sit just above chance on binary questions with verifiable answers, so the class can watch majority accuracy climb as the electorate is enlarged (perhaps by having students generate independent "clone" voters whose answers are drawn from their own error rates); one in which individual accuracy is unchanged but the errors are correlated — questions built on a misconception the whole room happens to share — so that the majority is confident and wrong; and one in which accuracy sits just below chance, so that adding voters actively drives the majority away from the truth. But I am open to a better structure if you see one.

Please provide: concrete rules and materials, including how to make individual accuracy tuneable without the students realizing it is being tuned; a payoff scheme that involves no real money; a way of recording and displaying results so the pattern is visible in real time; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theorem's premises and onto my friend's overreach. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which features of the theorem no classroom game can reproduce — a genuinely large electorate, sincere rather than strategic voters, questions where "correct" is not contestable — so that I know where the activity ends and I have to supply the rest by talking.
