In my quant-adjacent circles, the following argument keeps coming up. A gambler who reinvests winnings is like a population of organisms in a fixed environment: each bet is a generation, wealth is population size, and ruin is extinction — an absorbing state no lineage ever returns from. Just as evolution "selects" for the highest growth rate without any organism doing arithmetic, the long run "selects" for the betting strategy with the highest geometric growth rate, i.e., the Kelly strategy; and just as natural selection rewards the fast-but-ruin-prone lineage with extinction, overbetting positive-expectation edges leads to certain long-run ruin. On this picture, maximizing expected wealth is supposed to be a kind of category error — it averages over parallel gamblers in an ensemble, while each real gambler lives in a single time-series — so rational bettors are geometric, not arithmetic, maximizers, which is also supposed to make diversification a near-free lunch and to vindicate risk aversion without any special utility function. My friend takes this to show not just that log-utility happens to be a reasonable preference but that betting above Kelly, or accepting any meaningful chance of ruin for expected gain, is straightforwardly irrational, full stop. Audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the organism, the generation, fitness, extinction, the environment), identify where the mapping breaks down — including features of the evolutionary source that the gambling target quietly lacks, and vice versa — and then say whether the anti-expected-utility conclusion, or my friend's stronger claim about irrationality, survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
