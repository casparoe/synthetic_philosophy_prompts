My reading group spent last month on the Metamagical Themas material where Hofstadter introduces superrationality, and two members — Dana and Marcus — have kept the argument going by email ever since, with me copied on everything. I've lost track of who is winning and want an outside referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move.

Background first, so you don't need the book. Hofstadter poses two games. In the Platonia dilemma, an eccentric trillionaire assembles twenty people and announces that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the next day, that lone sender receives a billion dollars; if two or more telegrams arrive, or none do, nobody gets anything; the twenty cannot communicate. In the Luring Lottery, which Scientific American actually ran for its readers, you enter once by mailing a postcard bearing the number 1, but you may instead write any positive integer on a single postcard and thereby submit that many entries; the million-dollar prize is divided by the total number of entries received and awarded to one entry drawn at random. (As it happened, readers submitted astronomically large numbers and the prize evaporated.) Hofstadter's superrational proposal for Platonia is that since twenty ideal reasoners facing the same problem will settle on the same strategy, each should ask which strategy is best when all twenty use it — and the answer is to send a telegram with probability 1/20. The parallel proposal for the Lottery was to roll a die with one side per expected entrant and submit a single entry only if you roll a 1.

The exchange, in order:

1. Dana opened by endorsing superrationality as the correct account. The textbook analysis of Platonia says each person's sending can only help and never hurts, so everyone sends and the trillionaire pays nothing — a result so bad it refutes the method that produced it. The fix is to notice that the twenty acts share a common source: they will all be outputs of the same reasoning. So the chooser is not picking one act but one act-type for all twenty, and the best act-type is the 1/20 mixture, which gives the group a real shot at the billion. Rationality is about getting the answer right, and identical problems have identical answers.

2. Marcus answered that identical reasoners producing correlated acts is a fact about evidence, not about control, and that Dana's rule lives off confusion between the two. His sharpest point: suppose he grants Dana everything — that the other nineteen each roll a fair twenty-sided die and send only on a 1. Then his expected payoff from sending with certainty is about twenty times what he gets by following the mixture himself, roughly $377 million against $19 million. A decision rule that each adherent has overwhelming reason to violate precisely when she believes it is generally followed is not a rationality; it is a mood with a group glow. And if Dana's reply is that one should aspire to be the kind of agent who refuses to conditionalize like that, she has stopped doing decision theory and started doing moral education.

3. Dana said the conditionalization is the very mistake. You cannot hold the others' acts fixed while varying your own when all twenty acts are outputs of one computation — that is like a calculator asking what it should print on the assumption that the other calculator working the same problem prints a different digit. "What if I alone deviate" describes a world that the premise of shared reasoning excludes; once you see your deliberation and theirs as copies of a single process, the only live options are all-sends-at-1/20 or all-defects, and the mixture is better. And this is not causation at a distance: her sending does not make the others send; rather there was never a pair of independent choices to relate in the first place.

4. Marcus replied that this proves too much. It obliges Dana to call the readers who wrote down googolplexes irrational, when all they did was respond to the incentives the magazine actually printed — ordinary self-interest, with no coordination at all, explains the outcome perfectly. Worse, superrationality goes silent exactly where guidance is needed: it says what to do in a population of perfect copies of your own reasoning and nothing about what to do if even one of the twenty is ordinary, which is the only kind of population there has ever been.

Their closing shots: Marcus said a rule that is well-defined only for agents who already follow it is a definition, not a decision rule. Dana answered that honest textbook rationality has no recommendation at all in the Lottery — each player's best reply is to submit a number of entries matching everyone else's combined total, which three or more would-be maximizers cannot jointly do — so the textbook faces a runaway, while the superrational answer is at least stable. That is where they left it.
