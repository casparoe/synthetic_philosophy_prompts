Six months ago my mother was diagnosed with early-stage frontotemporal dementia, and I have since become her primary caregiver — a role I did not choose and cannot easily hand off. The experience has pushed me from skimming care ethics out of curiosity to reading it as if it were an operations manual, and it has also given the debate a sharp personal edge: last month I declined a promotion that would have let me donate far more to effective charities but would have made me unavailable to her, and my sister — who is roughly a consequentialist — thinks I chose sentiment over lives. I want to locate where our disagreement actually lives, and I want a number, not a survey.

Here is the proposition: care ethics is best understood as a self-standing foundational moral theory — not merely as a corrective highlighting what impartialist theories neglect, and not merely as a virtue theory with caring among the virtues. My credence that this is true is about 0.6; my sister's is about 0.15. Please give your own credence, decomposed into the terms that generate it. At minimum I would expect terms for: whether morality is unified enough that there is such a thing as the foundational theory to be had; whether facts of relationship and dependency ground genuine special obligations that cannot be reconstructed from impartialist premises; whether, granted that, care ethics specifically — rather than, say, a contractualist story about duties we can justify to those affected by our partiality — is the right account of those obligations; and whether care ethics can extend to distant strangers without simply absorbing impartialism wholesale (I gather Nel Noddings tried to distinguish caring-for from caring-about, and I am unsure whether that distinction holds or concedes the game). Add any further terms you think belong, and drop any of mine you think are doing no work. Assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how you combine them, and then answer two further questions: which single term does your final number most depend on, and — given that my sister and I differ by roughly 0.45 — which term is our disagreement most likely located in? It would also help if you told me what argument or consideration would move your own number the most, since I suspect I have been anchoring on whichever philosopher I read most recently.
