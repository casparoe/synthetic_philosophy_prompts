Two of my friends, Maya (math phd student) and Dani (philosophy), got into it over coffee about the continuum hypothesis being independent of ZFC, and they somehow decided I should referee, which I'm not qualified for, so you're it.

Maya started: Gödel and Cohen's result isn't just a fact about a formal game — we learned there's a genuine question about whether there's a size strictly between the integers and the reals that our axioms can't settle, and the fact that there's a real question there at all, one humans can formulate and grind on for a century, is exactly what you'd expect if the number structures exist independently of us.

Dani said that's backwards: all independence shows is that axioms we invented underdetermine a sentence in a game we invented, like finding a chess position that's a draw under the current rules. Nothing was learned about the world, because there's nothing for "truth about the continuum" to be true OF.

Maya replied that the axioms weren't picked out of a hat — they were adopted because they're true, and if set theorists eventually add new axioms (large cardinals or whatever) it'll be becuase those seem true too. Same fallible, truth-aiming process as physics.

Dani: physics answers to measurements. What does set theory answer to?

Maya: intuition. You can just see that any coherent condition carves out a subset of the reals — that seeing is data, the same way observing Mercury's orbit is data.

Dani: your "seeing" already blew up once. Naive intuition about what counts as a set gave us Russell's paradox. So intuition about sets is demonstrably unreliable — it's not a truth-meter.

Maya: but we didn't junk set theory after Russell, we sharpened it, the way physics absorbs an anomaly. Sharpened intuitions are still our best instruments.
