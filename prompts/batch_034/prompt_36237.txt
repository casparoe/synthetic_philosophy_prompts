My nationalism reading group has spent the better part of a year on what I thought was a straightforwardly empirical question — how old are nations? — and we've completely ground to a halt, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict. The modernist side keeps wheeling out the same points: that before roughly the late eighteenth century the vast majority of people simply didn't think of themselves as members of nations, that mass national identity required print, standardised schooling, censuses, conscription and railways, and that the whole apparatus of flags, anthems and ceremonies which *feels* ancient was largely manufactured in the nineteenth century (the invention of tradition stuff). The other side, smaller but stubborn, says this proves too much: there were named peoples with shared myths of descent, shared sacred homelands and a genuine sense of collective solidarity centuries before modernity, and if you insist only modern nations count as nations you've rigged the question by definition. One of them keeps bringing up pre-modern ethnic cores as the raw material modern nationalism worked with, and the modernists keep saying fine, that's exactly what we claim — raw material, not nations. Nobody has budged in months.

My working theory is that it's not really a factual disagreement at all but a standoff about what a explanation of nationalism is supposed to do: the modernists think you've explained nationalism when you've explained the political form (the mass citizen-nation with claims to sovereignty), and the perennialists think that's like explaining religion by explaining churches — you've explained the institution and missed the thing that made people susceptible to it in the first place. But I'm not sure, and there's a competing thought that it's just verbal, a fight over whether the word 'nation' is allowed to stretch back before the thing it names existed in its modern sense, in which case we're all wasting our Thursday evenings.

So: is this substantive or verbal? Are the two camps answering different questions under one heading (dating the political form versus dating the sentiment or the group), do they differ about which cases count as data, or is there a real empirical question buried in there about continuity — whether you can trace an unbroken chain from a pre-modern people to its modern nation, and what would even count as the same nation across that rupture? And whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper definitional distinction, some kind of evidential standard for continuity claims, or an argument that we should just split the word and move on. I'd also take recommendations for how to reframe our discussions so we stop having this same fight every fortnight.
