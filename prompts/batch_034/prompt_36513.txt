Next week I am running a fifty-minute section of our intermediate decision theory course on the independence axiom and the Allais paradox. The students came through the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem in last week's lecture, so they have seen independence stated as one condition in a list, but they have no feel yet for what it demands or why anyone would accept it. My previous experience with this material has been discouraging: students walk away believing either that Allais simply refutes expected utility theory outright, or that the whole thing is an artifact of stakes being too large relative to a person's lifetime wealth — an objection my economics colleague assures me, whenever we argue about it, dissolves the paradox entirely. Last year one student even reconstructed the Allais choices as rational by appeal to regret, and I improvised badly.

I do not want you to explain the material to me; I want a plan for the explanation. Decide the order in which the pieces must arrive — the axiom itself, the original gambles, the two-stage lottery reformulation, whatever else you judge essential — and defend the dependency structure: what cannot be understood until what else is in place. Choose the single carrying example and say what it must have and what it must lack; in particular, tell me whether the original certainty-versus-gamble pair should carry the section or whether a common-ratio variant, or a reconstruction in terms of regret or disappointment, does the job better and why. Then map the misconceptions I should expect — independence conflated with transitivity, the paradox taken as refuting expected utility wholesale, the scale objection, the student who insists the preference is perfectly defensible and therefore the axiom is not a rationality requirement at all — and tell me exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than merely corrected after it has formed. Finally, name what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (the proof machinery, prospect theory, the dispute over whether the axiom is best motivated via consequentialism or via the sure-thing principle), and give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would reveal whether they understood the dialectic rather than merely its conclusion.

I want a committed plan, not a menu of options: take a definite position on whether the section should present the Allais preference as a normative threat to the independence axiom or as a descriptive embarrassment that leaves the axioms standing, and structure the sequencing around that verdict, giving your reasons. Begin your response with that single most consequential choice stated plainly in the first sentence, then unfold the reasoning and the plan, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for here.
