I'm writing my term paper on the boundary problem in democratic theory, and my professor keeps saying it's one of those issues everyone acknowledges in the abstract and then conveniently ignores whenever a real vote comes along. So instead of doing yet another thought experiment about whether the forest gets to vote on logging policy, I want to actually work through a real case, and the 2016 Brexit referendum seems almost suspiciously well suited. The franchise was basically borrowed from UK parliamentary elections: a Latvian nurse who'd lived in Cardiff for a decade and was directly affected couldn't vote, an Australian student on a two-year visa could, a British retiree who'd moved to Spain ten years earlier could vote from abroad, a 16-year-old who'd live her whole life under the outcome couldn't, and Gibraltar was in but different EU citizens resident in the UK mostly weren't. And then there's the extra wrinkle that the thing being decided was itself partly about the demos's boundary in a different sense — free movement, who gets to join the political community, the whole take-back-control thing.

So here's what I want: analyze the referendum through the lens of the boundary problem. First the mapping direction — take the standard candidate principles for who belongs in the demos (all affected interests, being subjected to the laws, stakeholder views, maybe competence or duration-of-membership ideas) and say what electorate each one would have produced for this specific vote, and how far that is from the actual one. I'm genuinely unsure whether any principle comes out looking sane here, since all-affected seems to enfranchise half of Europe and all-subjected has to say weird stuff about that Latvian nurse versus the Australian student. Second, the reverse direction — what does the episode actually show about the theory? Is it just that nobody in Westminster paused to think about the boundary question at all (the franchise looks like a lazy administrative default rather than any principle), or is there some principled defense of the Westminster franchise that I'm missing? And does Brexit count as evidence that the boundary problem is unresolvable in practice and not just in principle, or is that reading too much into one messy vote? Also, if you think the episode exposes a spot where all these theories are just silent — like future generations or the residents of Northern Ireland who had the Good Friday Agreement reshaped around them without any of them choosing it — please say so rather than forcing the theories to speak. I don't want a survey of democratic theory, I want a case analysis that goes both ways, and I'm not interested in the merits of Leave vs Remain at all.
