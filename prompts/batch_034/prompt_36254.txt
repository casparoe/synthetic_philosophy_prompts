For the final paper in my civics course, I am supposed to defend a principle for who belongs in a democracy's electorate, but before defending any particular principle I want to know what such a principle actually has to accomplish. Please write out the requirements any adequate answer to the boundary problem — the question of who belongs to the demos — must meet: the cases it has to classify correctly (children, tourists, permanent residents, people convicted of serious crimes, citizens living abroad, future generations affected by today's environmental laws), the distinctions it cannot collapse (being affected by a decision, being coerced by it, and having a long-term stake in the community), and the constraints it cannot violate — including that it not be circular, since a demos cannot democratically vote itself into existence without already presupposing a demos. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp. Then address the part I most want to see: whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect the demand that everyone affected by a decision be included and the demand that the demos remain a bounded, deliberating unit cannot both be satisfied, given how easily "affected" spreads across borders. Finally, briefly score the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, and stakeholder views against your list; keep that part short, since the requirements are the substance.
