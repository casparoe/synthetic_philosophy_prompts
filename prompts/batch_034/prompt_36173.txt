I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I serve as the elected employee representative on the steering committee of a mid-sized civilian software company (about 600 employees) headquartered in a small country that maintains armed neutrality and universal male conscription organized on a militia model — think Switzerland or Finland, though the details of our own situation are what matter here, and I set them out below.

Our Ministry of Defence has opened enrollment in a pilot Cyber Reserve Corps. The terms are these. The participating company, not the individual employee, is the unit of obligation: each company pledges a quota of service person-days per year — ours would be 900, roughly thirty working days each for thirty employees. The company may fill the quota however it wishes: volunteers first, but the contract expressly permits the company to "nominate" employees to cover any shortfall, subject only to a twelve-month notice period. Employees on reserve duty keep their salaries, but the company absorbs continuity costs (backfill and overtime for colleagues), estimated at forty percent of the absent employee's fully loaded cost per day. In exchange the company receives a flat tax credit, early access to security advisories, and preferred standing in ministry contracts. The contract contains a conscientious-objector clause, but only the company may invoke it, on an employee's behalf, and invoking it forfeits a quarter of the tax credit.

There is an alternative track: the ministry will contract professional security firms for the same work, funded by a defense levy on companies that decline. A third option is declining outright and paying the levy — which some colleagues argue is what a firm like ours, with no military customers and a workforce that skews pacifist and international, should do on principle.

The committee must decide at its meeting on the 14th whether we join the pilot, and I need to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. The surface debate has been practical (cost, continuity, recruiting), but the disagreements underneath it strike me as older than that. Several colleagues hold that defense is a civic obligation that should be spread across everyone rather than bought as a service from specialists, and that a company enjoying the protection of a neutral state owes person-hours, not just money. Others hold the reverse: that obligating employees who never enlisted, through their employer, is conscription by proxy, and that the nomination clause converts our workforce into a personnel pool the state can draw on without ever asking them directly. A third group says the whole framing is wrong — the state should either draft fairly or hire professionals, and a pilot in which levy-payers ride free on other firms' volunteers is neither.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should:

- recommend a concrete course of action — join, decline and pay the levy, or a conditional variant you specify precisely — with reasons the committee can act on;
- engage the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than disposing of it;
- address the nomination clause head-on: under what conditions, if any, is it legitimate for an employer to obligate an employee to state service the employee never agreed to, and what does the answer imply about how we should fill the quota;
- say something about the fairness of the resulting distribution, both across firms and across employees — including the international staff who owe this state no military duty of any kind;
- remain usable by a committee with no philosophical training: no jargon, or where a term is doing real work, a one-sentence definition.

Write the memo in American English, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, addressed to the committee under my name as employee representative.
