As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have reviewed, or commissioned reviews of, most of the books at the border of history, anthropology, and popular science over the past decade — Harari's Sapiens, Diamond's Guns, Germs and Steel, and more recently Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything — and I have noticed that the same quarrel attaches itself to each of them in turn. I have come to suspect it is not really a quarrel about the past at all, and I would like a diagnosis of its structure rather than a verdict on any of the books.

The pattern is familiar. Academic historians and anthropologists line up to charge the book of the moment with cherry-picking, teleology, and factual error; the synthesis, they say, is a just-so story that no conceivable evidence could dislodge. The defenders reply that the professionals have retreated into the archive and abandoned the public square, that any synthesis requires selection, and that no critic ever spells out what an admissible synthesis would look like. Then The Dawn of Everything added a twist that I find diagnostic: two academics attacked the standard grand narrative — the inevitable slide from agriculture into hierarchy — by way of a counter-narrative of their own, and were promptly met with exactly the same charges. That suggests the dispute may not be about which narrative is correct but about narrative as such.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading: one group asking whether these books are responsible scholarship, the other asking whether they are good explanations of the large-scale human past, each with its own implicit standard of evidence, and neither noticing the divergence because both parties use the same word, history. Is that right? Or is there a hidden substantive question doing the work — for instance, whether large-scale causal explanation of the human past is possible at all, or whether the critics' real target is determinism rather than synthesis? Please give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction that cleanly separates permissible synthesis from just-so storytelling, a standard of evidence that both camps could in principle accept, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the two enterprises have different success criteria and can never intersect. I would also like to know, as a practical matter for my reporting, whether there is a formulation of the disagreement that both sides would recognize as fairly stating their own position — and if not, what that tells us about the debate.
