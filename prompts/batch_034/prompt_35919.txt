Each spring, the service club I help lead at my high school donates the roughly four thousand dollars we raise over the year to causes the members vote on, and as this year's treasurer I have to write the recommendation that breaks a deadlock. I have come to think the deadlock is not really about the charities at all but about something underneath, and I would like help deciding which side of it is right.

One faction wants the entire sum to go to an organization that funds corrective surgery for children in low-income countries who were born with cleft lips and palates and never received repair. Each surgery costs only a few hundred dollars and removes what would otherwise be a lifetime of pain, infection, and being treated as an outcast. The other faction wants half to go to that organization and half to a local program that pays for students from families like ours, who could never afford it, to attend a summer arts camp — a program whose graduates describe those weeks as among the happiest of their lives.

The president of the club, who backs the first faction, says the suffering-first position follows from a simple claim: pleasure and pain are not just positive and negative readings on one shared scale, such that a large enough pile of the one can pay off the other. On her view, sparing one child that suffering cannot be outweighed by any amount of added happiness anywhere, however much of it there is; so once you have a fixed budget, relieving the severe pain takes everything, and the camp is a luxury by comparison.

The sponsor of the split, a faculty member, replies that this proves far too much. If no amount of happiness could ever compensate any amount of pain, then, he says, we ought to be indifferent between a world where everyone has headaches and a world where everyone is miserable but nobody is headachy, or something close to it, and between all sorts of outcomes nobody is actually indifferent between. He thinks benefits are benefits and we should just count them all up.

My friend on the executive board, whose arguments I find myself responsible for writing up, has taken a middle position: mild suffering and mild pleasure do trade against each other roughly normally — stubbed toes really can be outweighed by a good concert — but suffering that crosses some level of intensity stops being tradeable at all, so that no quantity of enjoyment, added up over any number of lives, could compensate one instance of it. He admits he cannot say where the line is, or defend any particular place for it.

My worry is that this middle view gets its appeal entirely from dodging the two unwelcome conclusions, and I have been taught to distrust positions that exist only to avoid embarrassment. So my questions are: is the middle position a genuine principle or just a truce, and does either of the two more extreme views actually follow from its own premises? And given that whichever I recommend will probably pass, is there a way to frame the case that a room of seventeen-year-olds and one economics teacher would find persuasive without my having to invent numbers where none exist?
