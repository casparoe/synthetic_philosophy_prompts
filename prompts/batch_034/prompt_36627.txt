I teach a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and our final session of the semester covers Nāgārjuna's use of the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) and his account of emptiness (śūnyatā) as dependent origination. Rather than ending with a summary lecture, I would like to close with a poem that the students can unpack together.

Write a pantoum on the tetralemma. To fix the form: in a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and in the final stanza the first and third lines of the opening stanza return as the second and fourth, so that every line in the poem appears exactly twice, in two different surroundings. Put that structure to philosophical use. The four horns of the tetralemma — that something exists, that it does not exist, that it both exists and does not, and that it neither exists nor does not — should circulate through the poem as recurring lines, and each time a line returns it should mean something different, or visibly fail to mean the same thing, because of the new lines beside it. The form should enact the central Madhyamaka thought that phenomena are empty of intrinsic nature precisely because they arise dependently: no line should carry a meaning that survives transplantation, and yet the poem as a whole should cohere. The poem should endorse none of the four horns, and if you can manage it, the final stanza's return of the opening lines should do the work of the emptiness of emptiness — the negation of all four positions refusing to settle into a fifth position of its own. You might anchor the poem in a concrete case to which Nāgārjuna applies the tetralemma, such as the question of the Tathāgata's existence after death, though this is optional. A specialist should be able to identify which line is which horn and to say why the form suits the doctrine; a reader who knows nothing of Madhyamaka should still find it a good poem.

Please append a note of roughly 150–200 words distinguishing what in the poem is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars. For instance, the identification of emptiness with dependent origination is relatively stable in the tradition, but it is contested whether Nāgārjuna's rejection of the four horns is best read classically — as denying four exhaustive positions about intrinsic nature — or whether the tetralemma admits paraconsistent readings on which some horns are jointly true from the conventional standpoint, and it is contested whether emptiness is a metaphysical thesis about what there is or a therapeutic device for loosening conceptual fixation. Flag any place where the poem leans on one of these readings rather than staying neutral, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view.
