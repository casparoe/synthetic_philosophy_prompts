Reinforcement learning inherited its entire working vocabulary — reward, punishment, exploration, policy — from operant conditioning, a research program that deliberately bracketed inner states as unobservable. That vocabulary now runs the fine-tuning of production systems: in reinforcement learning from human feedback, when raters score an output badly, or a reward model trained on their preferences assigns it a low number, the next gradient step makes that output less likely. Nothing in the pipeline explicitly represents what, if anything, the system experiences; the reward is treated purely as a training signal, a bookkeeping device for credit assignment. At the same time, the institutions running these pipelines are not agnostics about experience everywhere else. They employ researchers to think about model welfare and digital minds, and they operate under animal-welfare regimes that have grown steadily less demanding about the metaphysics of suffering: recent UK law extended sentience recognition to cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans largely on behavioral and physiological grounds, and animal researchers are expected to minimize distress in their subjects even when doing so slows the science. Yet the training run itself gets no welfare review of any kind — the ethics scrutiny lands entirely on the human raters, the data, and the downstream users, never on the system being shaped by the punishment signal.

What theory of valence, if any, does this pattern of practice presuppose? Please do the following. First, reconstruct the implicit view — presumably some claim to the effect that reward is not valence, that a training signal only becomes suffering under conditions the pipeline doesn't touch (a particular functional organization, persistence across time, integration, embodiment). Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: would several different views of valence license the same no-review status quo? Second, say whether that implicit view is internally coherent, given the Skinnerian pedigree of the vocabulary — behaviorism bracketed inner states as a methodological move, not a metaphysical one, so it is worth asking whether the pipeline has quietly promoted a methodological agnosticism about experience into a substantive negative claim. Third, test the presupposition against the evidential standards the same institutions accept elsewhere: animal-welfare science increasingly takes learned avoidance of noxious stimuli, motivational trade-offs, and self-administration of analgesics as evidence of valence rather than demanding a particular neural substrate. A model fine-tuned away from negatively rewarded outputs is, after all, exhibiting something that looks at least structurally like learned avoidance. Does the no-review practice survive those functional criteria, or does it depend on a substrate-based view of suffering that the animal law has already abandoned? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in training practice — during the run, not just at deployment — under each of two assumptions: that reward is a necessary but not sufficient condition for valence, and that reward-as-experienced-valence is a coherent hypothesis but one we cannot yet test.
