I've been in a small online reading group on the surprise exam paradox for about two years now, and the discussion is completely frozen. We use the classroom version: the teacher announces on Friday afternoon that there will be an exam next week, that it will be on exactly one of the five weekdays, and that on the morning of the exam day the students won't know it's that day. Every new member runs through the familiar backwards induction -- it can't be Friday, because Thursday night they'd know; it can't be Thursday, because by then they'd know it can't be Friday, so they'd know it's Thursday; and so on down to "there can be no exam" -- and then the exam happens Wednesday and everyone is, in fact, surprised. Every person lands in one of the same three camps and nobody has ever switched camps. I don't want another attempt on the puzzle itself, I want a diagnosis of the disagreement.

The camps, roughly:

Camp one says the students' reasoning is sound and the announcement is self-refuting. Strip away the talk about knowledge and what the teacher has asserted amounts to: next week there's an exam day which, given this very announcement, cannot be identified in advance. But for every day, the announcement plus the exam-day facts lets the students identify it, or the announcement goes false for some other reason. So the announcement is inconsistent or false, full stop, and the "paradox" is just the feeling of a teacher successfully asserting something incoherent.

Camp two says the announcement is plainly fine (teachers do this all the time and it works) so the induction must break somewhere -- but they fall out over where. The sub-branch I hear most says the bogus step is the last one: on Thursday night the students can't *know* the exam is Friday, because that conclusion requires treating the teacher's announcement as a settled premise, and if the announcement were false the exam could still be Friday without them being able to rule it out. Others in this camp point instead at the very first step, or at the assumption that "the students will be surprised on day X" has a fixed truth value on every day, independent of what the students then go on to infer from the announcement.

Camp three, where I sit (part of why I want an outside view), says the real lesson is that the announcement is true but unknowable to its audience: it couldn't be true if the students believed it. The students are right that they can't be surprised, the teacher is right that they will be, and both can be right because the announcement is *about* the students' future epistemic state while the students are trying to use it as a premise *in* that epistemic state. It behaves like the Moorean sentence of the shape "p, but I don't believe p" -- perfectly fine when asserted about someone else's beliefs, impossible to assert coherently about your own.

Nobody converts. Camp one tells camp three that "true but unknowable" is a fancy way of conceding that the announcement can't do what announcements are for. Camp two tells us that if the audience can't coherently believe the thing, it's not an announcement in any ordinary sense, so we've quietly changed the setup. Camp three tells camp two that pointing at "the bogus step" is hopeless, because which step is bogus depends entirely on how you idealize the students' knowledge -- perfectly logical students, students who know the teacher is truthful, students who know the teacher is truthful *and* know that they know it -- and nothing in the puzzle forces one idealization over another.

So here's what I want from you. Is this a substantive disagreement about whether a certain idealized sentence is consistent or satisfiable -- something a careful formalization could in principle settle, and if so why hasn't it? Is it a verbal dispute about "know" or about "surprise" (a property of the exam vs. a property of the students on a particular morning)? Are the camps actually answering three different questions -- is the teacher's utterance coherent, where does the student's argument go wrong, what does the utterance *do* to the students' epistemic state -- under a single heading? Or is the impasse a sign that the puzzle itself is ill-posed, e.g. that "surprise" just doesn't admit the crisp day-by-day truth values all three camps quietly assume?

My own hunch, offered only as a hunch: the camps don't disagree about the puzzle at all, they disagree about what counts as *dissolving* a paradox -- whether showing the announcement is inconsistent counts as a solution, or whether that's changing the subject to a different announcement. If that's right it explains why no one moves. But give me your own diagnosis with reasons, and then tell me honestly whether anything could break the deadlock: a sharper formalization, a distinction no one's drawing, or maybe just getting the group to agree on a verdict about the one-day version of the puzzle (exam tomorrow and you won't know it -- on which even we can't agree). We're amateurs, so no citations or literature talk needed, just the reasoning.
