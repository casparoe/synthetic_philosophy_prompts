My friend Maya and I took a philosophy of causation seminar together in grad school and she never really recovered from it — she now works in insurance liability and treats basically every news story as a chance to hand out causal verdicts, which is honestly half the fun of knowing her. When she argues about this stuff she has two verbal tics I've noticed: she asks whether "wiggling" the thing would have changed the outcome, and she keeps invoking "what was supposed to happen" in the situation. Over the years I've collected her verdicts on a bunch of cases: (1) drunk driver runs a red light and kills a pedestrian — caused; (2) Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle, Suzy's gets there first and shatters it while Billy's was right behind and would have shattered it anyway — Suzy caused it, Billy didn't; (3) a server farm fries during a storm where a lightning strike and, independently, a power surge would each have been enough to kill the servers, and both hit at the same instant — both caused it; (4) a gardener whose contract says he must water the plants doesn't, and they die — he caused their death, but when I asked whether everyone else in town who also didn't water them caused the death she looked at me like I was an idiot (no); (5) a match is struck in an oxygen-rich room and a fire starts — the striking caused it, the oxygen didn't, even though the fire needed both.

We've got dinner coming up and I know three new cases will come up, so I want to go in with predictions instead of my usual improvising. Case A: two factories discharge into the same river, and the fish kill only happens when both discharges are present — but Factory 1's discharge is fully within its permit (just the normal baseline everyone on the river does) while Factory 2's is an illegal dump. Does she say both factories caused the kill, or only the dumper? Case B: a night guard follows the security manual to the letter, but the manual (stupidly) says no patrols after 2am, and a theft happens at 3am that a patrol would definately have caught. Did the guard's rule-following failure cause the theft? Case C is basically the pen case from our seminar with a twist: the admin assistant is allowed to take pens from reception and the professor is not, both take one, and the receptionist can't sign a form — but in my version the form would have failed anyway because of a system crash that morning. Does she still say the professor, the norm-violator, caused the problem?

Predict her verdicts on A, B, and C. Then tell me what rule she's actually running under all those tics — is the "wiggle it" thing (the interventionist test, roughly) doing the real work, or but-for dependence with the "supposed to happen" norm stuff doing the pruning, or something else? Say which of her earlier five verdicts should carry the most weight in figuring this out, and what further case, short of hooking her up to a polygraph, would cleanly split the candidate rules. I'm less attached to any particular prediction than to understanding what her track record commits her to.
