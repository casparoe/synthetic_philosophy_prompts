I recently had a long argument with a friend about the extended mind thesis, and we ended up defending two different criteria for when an external resource counts as part of someone's mind. I would like you to compare these two criteria carefully and help me figure out who, if either, was closer to the truth.

The background: Clark and Chalmers famously argue for the extended mind by appeal to a parity principle. The formulation from their 1998 paper reads: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." The idea is illustrated by the case of Otto, who suffers from memory loss and carries a notebook in which he records facts he wants to retain. When Otto wants to know where a museum is, he consults his notebook. Clark and Chalmers argue that if Otto's notebook reliably plays the role that biological memory plays for someone without memory loss, then the notebook's entries deserve to be treated as Otto's beliefs. They supplement this with further conditions of trust and accessibility: the notebook must be reliably available and automatically invoked, its contents easily accessible, and, in normal cases, previously consciously endorsed by Otto.

My friend was not satisfied. He argued that the parity principle is too permissive, since almost any stable environmental arrangement could be gerrymandered into "cognitive" parity with some internal process, and he proposed a rival criterion that I will call the seamlessness criterion: an external resource counts as part of your mind only if, at the moment of use, you cannot tell from the inside whether the contribution is coming from within your skull or from outside. On his view, Otto's notebook is not part of Otto's mind precisely because Otto must deliberately open it and read it; the retrieval is experienced as an act of consulting an artifact, not as remembering. By contrast, a well-integrated neural implant or a cochlear implant could qualify, because its contributions are subjectively indistinguishable from native processing.

Please compare the parity principle and the seamlessness criterion. In doing so, address the following questions. First, does the seamlessness criterion avoid the over-permissiveness my friend worried about, or does it introduce problems of its own — for instance, making the boundary of the mind depend on introspective access, so that a resource counts as mental for one person but not another purely because of differences in how the retrieval feels? Second, how do the two criteria handle the fact that Otto can presumably always tell, at some level, that he is consulting a notebook? If the seamlessness criterion is never satisfied in such cases, does that make it trivially narrow? Third, consider whether the two criteria are genuinely rivals, or whether they might be answers to different questions — say, one about the metaphysics of what a cognitive process is, the other about the phenomenology of what mental ownership feels like. Finally, take a stance: which criterion better captures the intuition behind the extended mind thesis, and are there mixed cases, such as a practiced smartphone user or someone navigating by GPS, where you think the two criteria clearly come apart? Please justify your assessment with concrete reasoning rather than mere appeals to intuition.
