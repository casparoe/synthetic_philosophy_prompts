My colleague and I work on rational choice theory, and we have spent the past several months arguing about how decision theory will ultimately digest the Pasadena paradox. Rather than continue trading intuitions, I would like a considered credence from you, decomposed properly.

The proposition I want you to evaluate is this: at some point in the next fifty years, the settled view in decision theory will include a principled generalization of expected-utility theory that (a) assigns games with conditionally convergent expectation-series, like the Pasadena game, a determinate place in the rational preference ordering relative to sure outcomes; (b) respects weak dominance in cases where the ordering of payoffs seems to force trouble, as in the Altadena variant; and (c) coincides exactly with classical expected-utility maximization for every gamble with a finite expectation. I do not want a lecture on why the question is hard. I want a number and the reasoning that produces it.

To fix terms so we are talking about the same cases. In the Pasadena game, a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and if the first heads arrives on flip n, the payoff is (−2)^n / n. Each outcome has probability 2^−n, so the series of terms in the expectation is the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally to −ln 2; by the rearrangement theorem, permuting the order of summation yields any real number, so the game has no well-defined expectation in the classical sense. In the Altadena variant, one unit is added to every Pasadena payoff, so Altadena weakly dominates Pasadena outcome by outcome, yet the natural ordering of terms in each series yields the same sum, which suggests that evaluating by that ordering fails to register an unambiguous dominance relation.

Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, assigning each a rough probability with one or two lines of justification. First, whether gambles of this kind are comparable at all: perhaps the correct settled response is that the preference relation is simply incomplete over such games, and that the appearance of a forced choice between playing Pasadena for a price and declining is an artifact. Second, whether weak dominance is a constraint any acceptable resolution must honor, or instead a principle that the infinite cases teach us to qualify — notice that honoring it in the Altadena case seems to require the evaluation to be sensitive to which series a game belongs to, not just to its distribution of payoffs. Third, whether the resolution will restrict the domain rather than extend the evaluation rule, for instance by arguing that agents with bounded utility functions never actually face these games, or that any physically offerable game truncates the series and thereby restores an ordinary expectation. Fourth, whether approaches in the neighborhood of what has been called strong expectation — where a game is evaluated by limits of partial sums in the natural order, or by conditions on the tails of the series — can be made stable under the relabelings and payoff shifts that generate Altadena from Pasadena. Fifth, whether aggregating by something other than the arithmetic mean, such as order statistics or median-like functionals, can retain enough of the von Neumann–Morgenstern axiomatic structure to count as a generalization of expected utility rather than a rival theory. If you think there is a sub-question missing from this list that your number depends on, add it.

Then combine your sub-credences into an overall number, however you think the combination should work, and tell me which single term your overall credence is most sensitive to. After that, help me localize my disagreement with my colleague: I am at roughly 0.7 on the proposition, chiefly because I believe the weak dominance constraint will be vindicated and that some evaluation rule in the strong-expectation neighborhood will eventually be shown to accommodate it; she is at roughly 0.25, chiefly because she suspects the dominance intuition here is a conservative reflex of the same kind that gets overturned elsewhere in the infinite cases, and that the mature view will declare these comparisons undefined. Between the two of us, which sub-question actually carries the disagreement, and what would each of us have to be shown to move? Finally, identify what would move your own number the most: for example, a proof that any evaluation rule satisfying dominance together with a stated continuity axiom must agree with classical expected utility wherever classical expected utility is defined, or, in the opposite direction, a demonstration that some non-arithmetic aggregation rule already handles a canonical infinite case without any pathological consequence anywhere else.
