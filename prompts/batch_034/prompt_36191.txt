I am preparing a seminar on virtue ethics for third-year philosophy students, and the final session is devoted to the situationist challenge — the line of argument, associated with John Doris's book *Lack of Character* and Gilbert Harman's essay on virtue ethics and the fundamental attribution error, that the social psychology of the 1950s to 1970s shows ordinary people's behaviour to be driven by morally trivial situational factors rather than by stable character traits. I want to structure the session around a ranking exercise rather than a survey, and I would like your help preparing it. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in both virtue ethics and empirical moral psychology, and before you commit to a ranking, spend some time brainstorming what each study actually shows, what it does not show, and what a virtue ethicist's most promising reply to it would be (consider, e.g., appeals to local or highly specified traits, to the distinction between virtuous performance and reliable prediction, and to the empirical criticisms of the studies themselves).

Here is the task. Rank the following five pieces of situationist evidence into tiers according to how much genuine damage they do to a broadly Aristotelian virtue ethics: (1) Milgram's obedience experiments on electric shocks; (2) the Hartshorne and May studies of schoolchildren's honesty, with their famously low cross-situational behavioural consistency; (3) Darley and Batson's seminarian study, in which whether theology students stopped to help a slumped confederate depended mainly on whether they were in a hurry; (4) Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment; and (5) the mood-manipulation studies in which finding a small coin in a phone booth dramatically increased subsequent helping behaviour. Each tier should come with a one-sentence justification keyed to the actual content of the studies, and after the tier list I would like you to single out the one experiment a virtue ethicist should be most worried about, the one most easily defused, and the single most common mistake you expect students to make in weighing them against each other.

Finally, having given the virtue ethicist's side a fair hearing, please switch roles and argue, as a convinced situationist, that your ranking is systematically too generous to virtue ethics, and identify the tier boundary you would most want to move. A closing paragraph of no more than four sentences, in your own voice, adjudicating the disagreement, would then round off the exercise nicely — and if you are willing, give the seminar leader's own answer as a single number from 0 to 10 for how confident a modern virtue ethicist should be that the tradition survives these results essentially intact.
