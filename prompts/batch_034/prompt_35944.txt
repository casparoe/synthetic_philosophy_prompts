I'm writing a chapter on reproductive torts and want to get the implicit philosophy straight before I get to the doctrine. In most common-law jurisdictions, parents can recover for wrongful conception and wrongful birth — the costs of an unplanned pregnancy after a failed sterilisation or negligent genetic counselling — while the child's own claim, wrongful life, is almost uniformly rejected, even where the negligence (a failure to diagnose rubella in utero, say) determined not that the child exists but only the condition the child exists in. Courts justify the refusal by appeal to the supposed impossibility of comparing impaired existence with nonexistence, and sometimes to the principle that life itself cannot be a harm; meanwhile some courts, in wrongful conception suits, offset the rearing-cost award by the benefits of parenthood. What view of harm — and of the nonidentity problem — does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose a person-affecting constraint, and is it coherent, given that in the misdiagnosis cases identity is fixed and no nonidentity problem arises? Distinguish what the doctrine strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, and say what would have to change if the courts instead adopted the Parfitian line that the only wrongs here are done to the parents. Please answer in British English.
