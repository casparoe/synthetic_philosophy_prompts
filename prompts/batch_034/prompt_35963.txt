Last month I was in residence at a small writers' retreat in a mountain valley, where part of my next novel is set. The retreat's director took the three of us on a hike that climbed out of mature forest into a recent clearcut — stumps, slash, and a carpet of fireweed. That evening my two fellow residents fell into an argument about it, and I was asked to referee. As a novelist I am trained to see both sides of everything, which proved useless here, so I am turning to you.

Dana, an essayist with a background in ecology, said the clearcut had made her heartsick. Elias, a philosopher by training, said — half seriously at first, but with increasing insistence — that he wasn't sure it should. His reasoning: (1) a songbird's clutch of five typically loses most of its members within the year; if that pattern is at all representative, average welfare in the wild is negative, so leveling a forest doesn't merely inconvenience the living — it prevents whole cohorts of prospective lives that would mostly have been miserable.

(2) Dana granted the premise for the sake of argument and attacked the inference. The chainsaw, she said, doesn't subtract animals cleanly: the displaced populations are pushed into the remaining forest, the roadside margins, and the farmland beyond, where their deaths may be slower or more crowded. More fundamentally, "preventing future sufferers" improves the lot of no one — there is no individual made better off, because absent individuals have no welfare to raise.

(3) Elias replied that the displacement point cuts in her favor not at all: land cleared for crops or pasture supports far lower densities of animals than standing forest — a soy field holds a fraction of the vertebrates a hectare of woodland does — so over decades, conversion plausibly means fewer animal-lives per acre, whatever their quality. And the person-affecting view Dana was leaning on pays a price he doubted she'd want to pay: it implies it's no loss to bring miserable beings into existence.

(4) Dana answered that the density comparison smuggles in an assumption — that total suffering tracks population size — when fragmented landscapes are notorious for irruptions and crash cycles: deer eruptions, starvation winters, pest outbreaks that a mature forest dampens. If per-capita welfare after clearcutting can be worse, then the direction of the whole effect is an open empirical question, and his confident conclusion was a coin flip presented as a finding.

(5) Elias's last word was a retreat. All he had meant to establish, he said, was that "habitat loss is bad for wild animals" is not axiomatic: it presupposes that the lives being lost are worth living, and that is precisely what is in doubt. Dana said that if non-obviousness was all he'd claimed, she'd accept it — but then he had no positive case for the clearcut either, and the burden of proof should rest on whoever proposes to level something.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why; identify which of the two is right about which specific points; and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I stayed neutral at the table and mean to stay neutral on the page — but my narrator has dinner with these two characters all through the book, and I need to know which of them argues well.
