I've been going down a rabbit hole on the philosophy of pain after my dentist asked me (while my mouth was full of cotton, thanks doc) whether I'd want a drug that made pain not hurt. Anyway, last night I listened to a pop-neuroscience podcast episode that tried to connect this to a real condition, and the presenter's summary went roughly like this: 'There are patients with a condition called pain asymbolia, caused by damage to the insula, who cannot feel pain at all -- their nociceptors simply don't send the signal. These patients will hold their hand on a hot stove without withdrawing it. Philosophers have made a big deal of this, because it supposedly shows that pain is not intrinsically bad: the badness of pain was supposed to be part of the very feeling, but here we have the feeling without the badness. So pain is really just a signal plus a dislike, and the dislike is optional. This refutes hedonism, since on that view pain is bad just in virtue of how it feels, and it also shows that if we could selectively switch off the suffering part of pain with neurotechnology, we'd lose nothing of importance -- anesthesiologists are basically already doing this with certain drugs.'

I'm inclined to believe the philosophical conclusion (that the awfulness of pain could in principle come apart from the sensation), but something about the way it was presented felt off -- like the presenter was blending together different patient populations and moving way too fast from a small clinical literature to refuting an entire ethical theory. But honestly I don't know the literature well enough to say where it goes wrong. Can you go through the summary bit by bit and tell me which parts a pain researcher or a philosopher of mind would sign off on, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether the conclusion about hedonism actually follows from whatever accurate parts remain? I'd especially like to know whether the patient description is right, since that seems like the load-bearing bit for everything downstream.
