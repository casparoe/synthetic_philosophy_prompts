So I just left my PhD program for a job at a small proprietary trading firm, and my old advisor is not letting me hear the end of it. He's got this argument he's clearly been polishing for years and I genuinely can't find the hole in it, even though the conclusion is insane.

(1) If rational agents share the same priors and differ only in what they've learned, none of them can expect to profit by trading on that information — the very attempt to trade reveals the information, and the gain evaporates before anyone collects it. (He calls this the no-trade theorem and says everyone in the field agrees with it.)

(2) So every actual trade must be explained by something other than information: risk-sharing, liquidity needs (retirement saving, rebalancing, hedging), or error.

(3) Risk-sharing and liquidity account for only a sliver of daily volume — buy-and-hold indexers barely trade at all, yet volume is enormous.

(4) So the overwhelming majority of trades are errors, i.e. noise trading.

(5) If most trading is noise, prices are mostly noise, and markets don't do the information-aggregation thing that's supposed to justify them.

(6) Therefore my firm's profits, and basically the entire industry, come from picking the pockets of noise traders, and the efficient-market story everyone at work tells is a fairy tale.

And he says the alternative is no better: if the volume is rational, then the traders didn't share priors, and since your priors are just whatever you walked in the door with, "different priors" means disagreement nobody can justify — irrationality with a fancy name. (He's big on Aumann's agreement theorem here.) Either way I'm a parasite on a casino.

The easy move is attacking (1)'s common-prior assumption, but he's got that comeback pre-loaded, so I want to know whether there's a cheaper exit. I'd rather keep (5) if I can, since I'd like to believe prices carry real information, but tell me what keeping it costs. Where exactly does this go wrong, and is he actually right that heterogeneous priors are just irrationality in a suit?
