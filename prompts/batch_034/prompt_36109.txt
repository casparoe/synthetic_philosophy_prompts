I've been asked to write a short position statement for a workshop on machine minds, and before I send it around I want to know whether my package of views is actually stable. Here is roughly where I stand:

(1) Dennett's intentional stance is basically the right story about belief-attribution: attributing beliefs is adopting a predictive strategy, and where that strategy genuinely compresses a system's behavior, the beliefs it attributes are real patterns rather than useful fictions.

(2) Because belief is like this, it doesn't require consciousness or language — a vervet monkey has beliefs, and I'm even prepared to say a thermostat has a very minimal one, about the room being too hot or too cold.

(3) Current LLMs nonetheless do not have beliefs in any interesting sense: they were optimized to predict text, not to deploy representations in the service of goals, and nothing that detached from the world's being a certain way counts as believing it.

(4) Whether a system has beliefs is ultimately an internal matter — it turns on the functional organization of the mechanisms inside the system, not merely on how successfully we can predict it from the outside.

(5) No current AI system is a proper object of moral concern, and I take that to be a genuinely separate question from whether it has beliefs, so I don't think (3) and (5) need to stand or fall together.

Go through these and tell me which commitments are in real tension, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest repair would cost. I'm not sure myself where the pressure is, so please don't pull your punches on any of them. Structure your reply as follows: "Verdict" (one short paragraph up front), "Genuine tensions" (one short paragraph each, ordered from most to least severe, each ending with what would have to give), "Merely apparent tensions" (one short paragraph each, explaining why the appearance dissolves), and "Cheapest repair" (one short paragraph recommending a single revision and what it costs me elsewhere).
