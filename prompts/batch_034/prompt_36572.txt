Consider the outbreak of the First World War in July and August 1914, and analyse it as a case study in hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy rather than as a diplomatic or military history in its own right. The basic facts you need are common knowledge: the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914, the German 'blank cheque' to Austria-Hungary, the Russian mobilisation, the rigid Schlieffen Plan timetable that turned a Balkan quarrel into a general European war, and the century of historiography that followed — from the Versailles 'war guilt' clause and the Fischer controversy over deliberate German escalation, through to the more recent 'Sleepwalkers' framing of the belligerents as stumble-blind actors.

I want a careful, two-directional mapping. First, say what the theory of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy predicts historians and readers will do with an episode like this: the compression of a sprawling, contingent month of decision-making into a coherent causal chain, the retrospective inflation of the Sarajevo assassination into the trigger it only became later, the sense of inevitability that the mobilisation timetables seem to supply after the fact, and the way 'who caused the war' becomes a question about intent rather than about the interaction of half-understood moves. Which features of the actual historiography of 1914 does this explain well — for instance, why the pendulum has swung between blaming a guilty party and blaming no one, and why the inevitability narrative grew stronger precisely as the war's catastrophes became known?

Second, be honest about where the theory breaks. Some of the coherence in the standard story of 1914 is not narrative smoothing but signal: the German General Staff really did have a single war plan that made escalation nearly automatic, and some actors really did make comprehensible, self-interested decisions. Distinguish the distortions hindsight introduces from the genuine structure of the case, and say something about how one tells the difference in practice — what marks a post hoc narrative as bias-laden rather than as an accurate reconstruction?

Third, assess the remedies. Contingency-focused historians have tried to write the July Crisis as it appeared from within, month by month, resisting the pull of the outcome; some have even proposed the methodological device of writing 'as if' in July 1914, not knowing what August would bring. Does that kind of deliberate counterfactual discipline genuinely debias historical explanation, or does it merely invert the bias, manufacturing arbitrariness where real causal weight existed? Apply your answer concretely: would the 1914 case, so written, be more accurate, or just differently distorted?

Finally, tell me what the case shows about the theory. Is the historiography of the First World War's origins a confirmation of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, an anomaly for them, or a case they never anticipated — and does it suggest that bias-correction can be built into historical method at all, or only argued about after the fact? Reach a conclusion and defend it; do not end on a non-committal note.
