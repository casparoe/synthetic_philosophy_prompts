I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last year I started reporting a feature on why people vote the way they do when they despise every viable option. The reporting kept surfacing two camps who had long since stopped listening to each other. The first camp — call them the harm-minimizers — holds that in a first-past-the-post election with two viable candidates, a ballot is damage-control equipment and nothing else: the probability that yours is decisive is tiny but nonzero, the stakes are enormous, and since the cost of voting is trivial, you vote for the less bad of the two, full stop. The second camp — call them the clean-hands camp — holds that a vote is an act of political self-authorship: casting it for a candidate you find odious, merely because other people's behavior makes that candidate's rival a live threat, makes you complicit in what follows, and no multiplication of a tiny probability by a large benefit figure launders that. Each camp thinks the other has misunderstood what a vote even is. The harm-minimizers say the clean-hands voters are mistaking a soapbox for a lever. The clean-hands camp says the harm-minimizers have a theory that can explain why to vote but cannot explain why anyone should care how.

I eventually persuaded a representative of each camp, with a couple of their respective fellow travelers, to spend two days in a closed room on the adversarial-collaboration model I've watched work in psychology disputes, with me as rapporteur. My assignment for you is to draft the joint statement I was supposed to file afterwards. It must contain the following. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — I expect this to include the old rational-choice point that turnout needs something like a duty term to be explicable at all, an agreed acknowledgment that virtually no individual vote alters an outcome, and, crucially, the concession they eventually reached that a voter in a jurisdiction whose result is utterly foregone may, even by the harm-minimizers' own lights, vote sincerely. Second, a table of the disagreements that remain, each row worded so that the camp it describes would accept the phrasing — the two of them spent ninety minutes fighting over the sentence that begins a vote is a tool, so keep the wording scrupulously neutral. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, each paired with a concrete case both sides agree bears on it and with each side's prediction written down in advance. Three cases they consented to treat as tests: a vote-swapping scheme in which a third-party voter in a swing jurisdiction pairs with a major-party voter in a safe one, each casting the other's preferred ballot, so that each cause nets one vote; a referendum on a civilizational-scale risk whose benefits accrue over centuries, where the decisiveness probability is minuscule and the stakes astronomical; and the secret-ballot problem — if no one ever learns how you voted, what exactly is being expressed, and can clean-hands reasoning survive the removal of an audience? Finally, both camps asked me to establish which parts of their dispute are merely verbal. They have come to suspect that the phrase supporting a candidate means preferring that candidate's victory to the harm-minimizers, but approving of the candidate to the clean-hands camp. The statement should say which disagreements dissolve once those words are pinned down and which are as hard as ever — and it should be a document either of them could put their name to without wincing.
