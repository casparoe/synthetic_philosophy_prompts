I run a student group that's basically a rationality-and-philosophy reading club — mostly second and third years, a mix of maths and philosophy people, no decision theory background beyond having seen the Prisoner's Dilemma and maybe Newcomb's problem once. Next session I've got 90 minutes and a whiteboard to teach updateless decision theory, and honestly what I want is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself, because my previous attempts at this have gone badly and I suspect it's because I ordered the pieces wrong.

Some things worth knowing about the audience: they'll have half-learned "maximise expected utility given your current beliefs", so the whole move — choosing a policy in advance of observations rather than an action in light of what you've observed — will feel pointless to them unless the dependencies are set up right. I'd also like to at least gesture at the distinction people draw between the original formulation (commit to your entire policy before you see anything) and the later refinement where you choose a mapping from observations to actions, without drowning anyone in version numbers or LessWrong archaeology. And every time I've touched this material someone asks whether updatelessness means you should never update on evidence at all, like literally ignoring your own eyes in everyday life, which I'm fairly sure is a misconception but one I've stuggled to defuse cleanly rather than just deny.

So what I'm after: the order the pieces must go in so that "decide as if choosing your whole policy" lands as the natural move rather than a weird technicality; the one scenario that should carry the whole thing and what it must have and lack — I'm torn between transparent-box Newcomb and the variant where the predictor punishes agents whose policies are exploitable (I think this is sometimes called playing chicken with the universe?) but I worry the second is too wild as an opener; the misconceptions they'll predictably form and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off; what I should openly refuse to cover in 90 minutes and say so; and one question at the end that would actually tell me whether they understood the idea rather than just nodded along.

If you think the transparent-box version is the wrong carrier example, say why and pick a better one — don't just bolt it on top of the classic version.
