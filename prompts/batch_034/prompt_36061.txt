so every time the AI race comes up in the blogs/forums I read, someone eventually drops Hardin on the table: the labs are the herders, the commons is everyone's shared future (some people say "the lightcone" which is very us, I know), each capabilities push is another cow put out to graze, and the conclusion drawn is that this is a tragedy of the commons — a genuine coordination problem — so the only exits are coordination-shaped ones: regulation, an international regime, mutual coercion mutually agreed upon, the whole enclosure package. the framing also carries a subsidiary claim I see a lot: that no malice is needed, each lab is just doing the locally rational thing while the pasture dies, and this is supposed to make the situation more tractable, not less.

lately I've been wondering how much work the analogy is actually doing versus how much is vibes. like, a commons is a rivalrous resource that gets depleted — grass that gets eaten is gone. what exactly is being depleted as labs scale? the remaining time to get safety right? that's not really rivalrous in the grazing sense. or is the "commons" here actually a threshold public bad — everyone loses everything past some point, and nothing much accumulates before that — which is a different structure from cumulative overgrazing? also in Hardin's story each herder keeps the full benefit of his extra cow and only eats a symmetric share of the overgrazing cost, so defection is strictly locally rational. in the AI version, if it goes badly the defectors burn too. that seems like it changes the game from prisoner's-dilemma-ish to chicken-ish or stag-hunt-ish, and I don't know which of those the "regulate it like a commons" conclusion actually survives.

audit the whole thing for me: give me the correspondence table (who's the herder, what's the cow, what's the grass, what's carrying capacity, what plays the role of the town meeting or the enclosure), then the places the mapping breaks, then sort the disanalogies into ones that matter for the conclusion people use it for — coordination is needed and regulation-shaped fixes will work — versus ones that just make the rhetoric more vivid. and tell me whether there's a better game to model it with, and whether the "regulation is the only way out" conclusion survives the switch or was quietly carrying the analogy the whole time. separately: my strong prior is that the "it's all locally rational defection, no malice needed" bit is empirically false anyway — some of the actors do know better and push ahead — but tell me whether that's load-bearing for the game-theoretic analysis or just a moral side-note.
