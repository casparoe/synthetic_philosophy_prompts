So I'm a novelist, literary-ish spec fic, and my next book is basically a long argument disguised as a story about the repugnant conclusion (my agent says I'm not allowed to call it that on the jacket copy, which tells you something about how this is going to go). The premise: a colony fleet's ship-mind has to allocate remaining resources between two futures -- one where a few million people live genuinely rich, flourishing lives, and one where it stretches everything out so billions exist at, roughly, lives just barely worth living. In the book, a handful of characters get to look at both futures up close before the choice is made, and the ending commits to the smaller world -- I let the ship-mind choose it, and a character who I think speaks for me calls the big world a "victory of arithmetic over literature," which was fun to write and is probably going to get me killed at this event.

The event: a university "philosophy and fiction" evening, and the department that's co-hosting it turns out to have actual population ethicists. Two of them are total utilitarians who think the repugnant conclusion isn't repugnant, or at least that all the alternatives are worse. There's also a Kantian who's going to hate my ending for completely different reasons, and a grad student, I'm told, who's deep in the impossibility-theorem literature -- the stuff showing that basically no theory of population ethics can satisfy all the intuitions everyone agrees on.

My defense, such as it is: I deliberately wrote the "barely worth living" lives as real people -- small joys, friendships, songs -- so the reader can't picture them as bad lives, and then still has the smaller world feel like the right choice. My bet is that once the big world is made vivid rather than a slogan, the intuition flips. I basically weaponized characterization against a philosophical position, which maybe a novelist shouldn't do.

I need a prep sheet for the Q&A. Give me the objections these people will actually raise, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my ending: the total utilitarians will presumably run some version of the argument that adding lives with any positive welfare can't make things worse and I lose if I deny that; someone will ask whether I've just smuggled in average utilitarianism or a critical-level view without defending it; someone will press on whether my "barely worth living" characters are actually barely worth living or whether I quietly made them miserable to cheat. For each objection: a two-minute reply I can deliver without notes, what I should concede up front so I look sane, and where my reply is soft. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it's the grad student's theorem thing, the argument that whatever I pick, I'm just choosing which intuition to betray, so my ending's confidence is unearned -- so if that's it, tell me how to survive it, and if it's not, tell me what the real one is.
