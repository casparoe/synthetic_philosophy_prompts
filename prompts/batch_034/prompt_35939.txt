I've come to believe there's a genuine third kind of epistemic support in math, sitting between full proof and mere guesswork. The idea is being pushed by people in the AI safety crowd trying to formalize "heuristic arguments": we're entitled to near-certainty that the output of a good pseudorandom generator looks random to any cheap test, long before anyone could prove it, and cryptographers bet actual security on that kind of confidence every day. Same with what gets called the presumption of independence -- assuming structured objects behave like random ones unless you can point at a specific correlation. My claim is that this isn't just "having a prior over unproven statements": the arguement itself does real work that a prior can't, and there's a principled epistemic category there. What would count against it? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: formal results that'd show the category collapses into ordinary Bayesian reasoning, kinds of cases where these heuristics go systematically wrong in a way we couldnt have spotted in advance, and which of these the view's defenders would actually concede versus wave off. And be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability -- "no true heuristic argument" style moves -- and whether that even matters for a claim of this kind.
