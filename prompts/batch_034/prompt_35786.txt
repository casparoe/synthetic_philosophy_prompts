I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form piece about acausal trade: cooperation between agents who never communicate, bargain, or verify one another's existence, yet who nonetheless strike deals because each can model the other and has bound itself in advance to honor its half. The toy case I keep returning to in my notes: two civilizations at opposite ends of the galaxy, so far apart that no signal could make a round trip before both go extinct, each of which can infer a great deal about the other from astronomical observation alone — the composition of their home star, the presence of megastructures, the leakage of their radio traffic. Suppose one is far better at, say, designing self-repairing probes, and the other at some unrelated problem, and each has "commitment technology": the capacity to rewire its own motives or source code so that cheating later becomes impossible, not merely disapproved of. The claim I want to examine is that such civilizations could trade — each unilaterally executing "I do the costly thing for a stranger, if I predict the stranger is doing the costly thing for me" — without a photon ever passing between them.

For the piece I'd like you to write a dialogue between two competent partisans. The proponent is a decision theorist who takes this seriously, treating the arrangement as a natural extension of reasoning about what one's counterpart will do — a cousin of what Douglas Hofstadter called superrationality, though she should be pushed to say whether and where her position differs from his. The skeptic is an economist with a physicalist streak whose line is roughly that trade is a loop — offer, acceptance, performance, enforcement — and that severing the loop leaves you with prayer, not commerce. Please make both of them genuinely good at this: the skeptic should concede, unprompted or under pressure, that ordinary iterated trade with observation is unproblematic, so that the disagreement gets localized precisely to the unobserved case; and she should press the proponent on the two points that strike me as the crux. First, the counterparty problem: the entity each civilization "deals with" is its own simulation of the other, so in what sense is this anything but an elaborate transaction with oneself? Second, the moment-of-performance problem: when the costly act actually comes due — decades after the commitment was made, with the expected benefits long since banked into planning — what makes honoring it rational then, rather than a noble mistake? The proponent, for her part, should press back rather than only defend: for instance, on whether the skeptic's position quietly assumes causal conditioning in decision-making and simply begs the question against the evidential view.

I have one firm requirement about the ending. I don't want a diplomatic draw or an "we'll have to agree to disagree" truce, which is how most treatments of this subject I've read manage to be both interesting and inert. By the close, one of the two should concede the specific point that actually moves her — the skeptic revising some concrete prediction about what rational agents can achieve without contact, or the proponent admitting the scheme rests on auxiliary assumptions she hadn't flagged, such as the reliability of the mutual modeling or the bindingness of the commitment technology — and say what she'd now do differently. Have the exchange run long enough to do the arguments justice, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, in a register I could quote from directly. And please append a short note, three or four sentences, identifying which moves in the dialogue lean on contested assumptions or on one particular reading of decision theory, so that I can flag them for my readers rather than passing them off as settled.
