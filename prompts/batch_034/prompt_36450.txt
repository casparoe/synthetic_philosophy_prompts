My office mate in the metaphysics program thinks he's got me trapped and honestly he might. It's an argument for mereological universalism (or something close) and every step sounded fine when he said it, but the conclusion is that there's an object composed of my coffee mug, the northern half of Lake Michigan, and some squirrel's tail. Help me find the exit.

1. Either some arrangements of parts compose an object and others don't (restricted composition), or every arrangement composes something (universalism), or no arrangement does (nihilism). I want restricted: statues and cats exist, but there's no mug-lake-tail thing.
2. Any restriction has to draw a line between the arrangements that compose and the ones that don't.
3. Any candidate line you'd actually propose -- parts must be stuck together, must form a life, must make up a "unified" object -- admits of sorites series. You can go from clearly composing to clearly not composing via a long sequence of tiny changes with no principled cutoff anywhere.
4. So a restriction on composition would have to be vague: some arrangements would be borderline cases of composing.
5. If it's a borderline case whether some arrangement composes, then it's indeterminate whether a thing with those parts exists.
6. But it can't be indeterminate whether something exists, or how many things there are. Existence isn't like baldness. For any thing, either it exists or it doesn't, and the total count of things isn't the sort of fact that could be fuzzy.
7. So restricted composition is out. Nihilism says the statue I'm literally holding right now doesn't exist, which is insane. So: universalism. Mug-lake-tail thing it is.

Here's why I'm stuck. When he got to (6) I nodded along because it sounded like the most obviously true premise in the whole argument, but I have this nagging feeling the sleight of hand is exactly there -- that "it's indeterminate whether there exists something composed of these parts" got quietly turned into "there's something such that it's indeterminate whether it exists," and those don't look like the same claim. Or maybe the problem is earlier, in the move from (3) to (4), since I'm fine with vague predicates elsewhere ("there's no fact of the matter whether that pile is a heap") without thinking reality itself goes fuzzy.

For what it's worth, here's what I'd like to keep: statues exist, the mug-lake-tail thing doesn't, and I'd rather not say composition facts are just brutely sharp in a way that ignores how the parts are arranged. Which of these am I going to have to give up, and what's the least bad way to give it up? And please actually pick a step -- don't just tell me the literature is divided.
