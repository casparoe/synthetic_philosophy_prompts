I recently started work as a work coach at a public employment office — the person who sets weekly job-search requirements for unemployment-benefit claimants, reviews the evidence they submit, and, when someone misses an appointment or can't demonstrate that they applied for the required number of jobs, decides whether to refer them for a sanction (a suspension or partial cut of their benefit) or to accept that they had "good cause." I took the job assuming the benefit system was a safety net, and I've been unsettled by how much of my week is spent adjudicating conduct rather than assessing need. The deeper puzzle, now that I have to apply the rules myself, is this: sanctions are only sensible if being unemployed is substantially within the claimant's control — you attach consequences to job-search behavior on the theory that the behavior causes the outcome. Yet the same ministry's own labour-market briefings, which I read for another part of my job, describe most vacancies as being filled through informal networks and word of mouth — channels a claimant cannot exactly apply to. And in the very next regulation, the same system cuts hardship payments to claimants whom a sanction would otherwise leave destitute, thereby conceding that for some people compliance is not something they can be moved to by threat. We seem, in one office, to treat unemployment as chosen conduct, as bad luck, and as chosen conduct again.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on distributive justice and the welfare state, and think carefully before answering; I'd like you to begin with some brainstorming — lay out two or three candidate theories of responsibility or desert that could be implicit in conditionality (e.g., a reciprocity view, a deterrent-behaviorist view, a view on which the state is merely signaling deservedness to taxpayers) — before settling on a reconstruction. Then tell me: what exactly does the practice of sanctioning presuppose about the claimant's control over his situation; is that presupposition consistent with the hardship-payment concession and with the ministry's own picture of how jobs are actually filled; and which parts of the practice are philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival theories would license them just as well? Conclude by saying which of your reconstructions you are least confident in, what argument or piece of evidence would move you to a different verdict about what the system presupposes, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong. I'd rather have an honest map of the shaky parts than a tidy theory.
