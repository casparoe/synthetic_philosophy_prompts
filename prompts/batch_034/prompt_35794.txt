I'm writing a paper on causation by omission — the plants died because the gardener never watered them, the patient had a stroke because the anticoagulant was never prescribed. Before I argue for any particular account, I want the specification an adequate theory of negative causation has to meet. Give exactly five desiderata, numbered, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. At least three of the five must concern: (i) the profligacy problem — what marks out the gardener's not watering as the cause, rather than the infinitely many other absences (Napoleon's not watering, the plumber's not watering) that equally accompany the effect; (ii) whether an omission can be a cause even though there is no event of omission for a counterfactual to range over, and what the theory owes us on that score; and (iii) whether the theory must remain neutral on whether absences are part of the furniture of the world, or may instead take a stand. Within each item, mark the desideratum non-negotiable or negotiable, and if you suspect some pair of the five cannot be jointly satisfied, say so inside the item where the tension appears.
