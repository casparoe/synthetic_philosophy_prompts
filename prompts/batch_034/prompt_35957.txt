I've been a working stage actor for about a decade, trained in the Stanislavski tradition as it's usually taught in studios in the English-speaking world — objective work, breaking scenes into beats and tactics, sense memory, some Meisner repetition early on. My closest friend is doing a master's in philosophy, and we recently spent an entire evening arguing about what an emotion actually is. She laid out what I gathered are three main camps: theories on which an emotion is essentially a judgment or appraisal (fear is the judgment that something dangerous is at hand, and the racing heart is downstream); theories on which an emotion is a kind of perception of value or significance, analogous to how vision perceives color; and older body-first theories in the spirit of William James, on which the emotion just is the feeling of the bodily upheaval — the trembling and quickened pulse — rather than any thought that causes it. We got nowhere, mostly because I kept forcing her positions into my own working vocabulary and she kept telling me I was mangling them.

So here is what I'd like you to do, for each of the three theories in turn: restate it in the vocabulary I actually use in rehearsal, as faithfully as you can. That vocabulary includes given circumstances, objectives and obstacles, playable actions and tactics (the near-universal studio rule that you can't play an emotion directly, only an action under a circumstance, and the emotion is what happens to you when you do it truthfully), beats and their shifts, sense memory and personalization, the "as if," and the physical life of a scene. Say for each theory which claims translate cleanly into those terms, which have to be distorted to fit, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I'll admit my instinct during the argument was to treat the judgment theory as basically "play the objective" and the James-style theory as "do the physical action and let the feeling follow," but I suspect I was smuggling in my training's own commitment — that emotion is a byproduct you must never chase directly — and calling it a translation. Please watch for that kind of thing on my behalf.

After the three translations, give me the residue report in earnest. Is there anything these philosophers are disagreeing about that has no counterpart in how actors talk and work — some live question that my vocabulary simply cannot pose? And then adjudicate: is that residue a defect of the actor's toolkit, something it pays no attention to because it's in the business of producing emotions rather than understanding them, or is it a sign that the philosophical disagreement is less substantive than it looks from the inside, a distinction with nothing at the bottom of it? I want an honest verdict, not diplomatic mush. My friend claims the philosophy matters even to someone like me; I half-believe her, but I want to know whether anything in her debate would actually change what I do in the rehearsal room, or only what I call it.
