The exam question (25 marks):

"Consider a sorites series for 'heap': grains are removed one at a time from a pile that is clearly a heap, until what remains is clearly not a heap. For epistemicism and for supervaluationism, explain: (a) what truth-value, if any, a borderline sentence in the middle of the series has; (b) what truth-value, if any, the sentence 'there is a number of grains n such that n is enough for a heap and n−1 is not' has; and (c) one problem that higher-order vagueness raises for each theory."

The student's answer:

"Epistemicism, associated with Timothy Williamson, holds that vague predicates have perfectly sharp boundaries. There is a last number of grains that makes a heap; we simply cannot know which number it is. Vagueness is thus a species of ignorance rather than a semantic defect, and bivalence is preserved throughout the series. The boundary is fixed by the totality of the community's usage, but this does not make it accessible: the connection between use and extension is itself soritical, so the cutoff is unknowable even in principle — an exhaustive survey of usage would confront its own vagueness. Higher-order vagueness is handled the same way: we are likewise ignorant about where 'knows the boundary' applies, so the ignorance iterates, and the epistemicist accepts this regress as no more paradoxical than any other limit on knowledge.

Supervaluationism agrees that there is a sharp boundary. A vague language admits many admissible precisifications of 'heap', and one of these is the correct interpretation, though which one it is remains unknowable. A sentence is true simpliciter when it is true on the correct precisification. And because supervaluationism preserves every classical tautology, it preserves bivalence as well: every sentence in the series, including the borderline ones, is true or false. So on questions (a) and (b) the two theories converge — both assign a truth-value to the borderline sentences and both make the sharp-cutoff sentence true — differing only in their account of why we cannot tell which values they have. Higher-order vagueness is a genuine problem here: the set of admissible precisifications is itself vaguely bounded, there being precisifications it is borderline whether to count as admissible, so 'true on all admissible precisifications' inherits exactly the vagueness it was introduced to remove, and the view threatens a regress of admissibility predicates."
