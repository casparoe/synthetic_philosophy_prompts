I've been recording my honest verdicts on cases that turn on the nonidentity problem, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them — and I'd like you to be ruthless about which of my verdicts has to go.

First, the cases and my verdicts:

1. A provincial government allows a mining operation to deplete a watershed. The resulting economic shifts ripple through marriages, migrations, and school enrollments; two centuries on, the people alive are different people from those who would have existed under a conservation policy. Their lives are worth living but noticeably harder. My verdict: the policy wrongs them, and it isn't enough to say the wrong was done to no one in particular.

2. A couple is told that conceiving this month carries a serious chance of a child with a painful, disabling condition, and that waiting three months would produce a different, unaffected child. They conceive this month anyway, because this month is convenient. My verdict: they wrong the child who results.

3. Same setup, except the condition is mild: the child will be a few inches shorter and will need glasses. My verdict: not wrong, or at most a trivial wrong. But I notice this is only a difference of degree, and I can't say where the line falls or whether there is one.

4. A city builds a much-loved library. The library alters commuting patterns; three generations later, different people exist than would have otherwise, and by bad luck their lives are, on average, slightly worse than the counterfactual population's would have been. My verdict: the library decision was not wrong. This is the verdict I trust least, because structurally it looks like (1) with the signs reversed, and the only difference I can point to is that I like libraries.

5. The child from (2), now an adult, tells me she would not trade her life for a different one, and resents the suggestion that her parents wronged her. My verdict stands: they wronged her. But I cannot say what she lost.

Alongside these, I've been carrying a tentative principle: an act can wrong a person only if that person is worse off than they would have been had the act not been performed. I hold this fairly firmly — it seems to me to be what distinguishes wronging someone from merely doing something bad near them — and yet it appears to flatly deny both (1) and (2), since in each case the people are better off than they would have been under the alternative: they would not have existed at all.

So, the questions: Is there a principle that saves (1), (2), (3), and (5), adjudicates whether (4) is genuinely different from (1) or merely different in my imagination, and either keeps or repairs my person-affecting commitment? If no single principle can do all of that, tell me which verdict to abandon and why that one rather than another. Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts about a case I haven't mentioned — say, a government policy that delays every conception in a cohort by exactly one month, changing who exists thereafter without affecting anyone's quality of life at all. Does that policy wrong anyone, and if so, whom? I genuinely don't know what I think about it, and I'd like the principle to tell me.
