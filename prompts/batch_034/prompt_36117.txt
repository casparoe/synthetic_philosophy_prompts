I spent thirty-five years as a Quaternary stratigrapher before retiring, and in the last few years I have been reading seriously in philosophy of history — which is how I landed a colloquium slot I am now slightly regretting. Next month I defend the following thesis to an audience of about thirty environmental historians, several STS scholars, and a couple of historical geographers, none of them geologists: that the Anthropocene was, and remains, worth formalizing as a geological unit with a mid-twentieth-century boundary marked chiefly by the radionuclide signal from thermonuclear weapons testing — and that keeping it as an informal humanistic shorthand for "the age when everything became history" is a mistake, because the signal is stratigraphically real even though my own discipline voted the proposal down in the spring of 2024. My working argument is that boundary-marking and period-interpretation are separable acts: geologists have always chosen markers by committee and left the meaning of a period to others, so the complaint that a plutonium spike "flattens" unequal human responsibility mistakes a physical datum for an interpretive claim.
