I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background (my last formal contact with any of this was a statistics course years ago), and I own the estimation process for my team of eight. Every two weeks we commit to sprint-sized work, and twice a year leadership asks us for multi-quarter numbers that end up in customer-facing commitments. Our track record is bad in a consistent direction: we hit the two-to-three-sprint items roughly on time but blow well past the multi-quarter ones, and I've been asked to fix this. A friend pointed me at the planning fallacy and at "reference class forecasting" as the standard remedy: stop reasoning from the inside of this particular project ("the schema is 80% mapped, the only tricky part is the cutover") and instead ask how long projects *like this one* have actually taken, then adjust from there.

I like the idea, but when I tried to run it I hit what I assume is a well-known wall: the forecast depends entirely on which "projects like this one" you average over. Our upcoming ingestion rewrite belongs to the class of all rewrites we've done (n=4, overran badly), the class of projects touching the payments vendor (n=9, mixed), the class of projects led by this particular tech lead (n=11, mostly on time), and the class of projects started during quarters when we were also on-call-heavy (n=7, catastrophic). Nothing in the method tells me which average to trust, and narrower classes seem more relevant but have fewer members and noisier averages. My teammate says this makes the whole thing circular: the mechanical procedure that was supposed to remove judgment just relocates it, because I pick the class with the same gut that produced the bad estimates in the first place. He adds a second objection — our data is survivorship-biased, since the only past projects we have completion times for are ones that shipped, and the two we know of that got cancelled mid-flight never generated a number at all, so even the averages we can compute are drawn from a filtered class.

Please turn what philosophy and statistics actually know about this — the reference class problem proper, not just "use the outside view" — into a procedure my team can run in about twenty minutes per estimate at planning, with no statistician and nothing fancier than a spreadsheet over our Jira export. I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure outputs a number, and where it should stop and explicitly hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of judgment or values rather than facts. It has to handle at least these cases: a class with only three or four members; a genuinely first-of-a-kind project where no class has any obvious claim; and a project that differs from its natural class in one known, directional way (we've used this exact vendor before, but never with this much existing load on the system). Then run the procedure on three estimates we actually face: the ingestion rewrite described above; a two-sprint refactor of our notification service, where the class data says these run about 60% over but the assigned engineer has produced a detailed task-level breakdown showing it fits comfortably; and a greenfield internal tool the sponsoring VP wants scoped by Friday. I expect the procedure to give at least one answer I won't like — please don't sand it down to keep me comfortable — and please be explicit about what the procedure fundamentally cannot do, including whether my teammate's circularity charge is ultimately right or whether there's a principled (if imperfect) response to it.
