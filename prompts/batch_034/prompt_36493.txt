I spent thirty-five years in fisheries physiology, the last twenty working on stress responses in farmed salmon, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology and animal ethics in earnest. I now want to make the transition from interested reader to contributor, and I have been offered the chance to submit a proposal for a twelve-month part-time project to a small foundation that funds work at the interface of animal welfare science and policy. I have a half-formed idea and need it turned into a proper proposal.

The idea, such as it is: everyone agrees that welfare standards for aquatic animals should follow the science, but I have come to suspect that the phrase conceals a gap. When my former colleagues say a cod or a shrimp has good welfare, they mean something measurable — lesion scores, cortisol, feed conversion, mortality. When the philosophers I now read say an animal has good welfare, they mean something about how its life feels from the inside. For terrestrial livestock, these two notions were more or less calibrated against each other over decades: the behavioral and physiological indicators were validated against mammalian biology and mammalian intuitions. For fish and decapods, I suspect the calibration never happened. The animals' physiology, sensory ecology, and behavioral repertoires diverged from ours and from the mammals' hundreds of millions of years ago, and the indicators simply migrated over from terrestrial systems largely unexamined. Meanwhile, the philosophical side has its own problem: theories of welfare built with paradigmatically human or mammalian cases in hand — hedonism, preference satisfaction, objective-list accounts — get applied to animals whose preferences and pleasures we can barely characterize. So when a standard-setting body concludes, from the science, that a given stocking density is acceptable, or that electrical stunning is humane while live chilling in ice slurry is not, some part of that conclusion is empirical and some part is philosophical smuggling presented as empirical. My working hypothesis is that the two parts can be separated in principle, that nobody has done it systematically for aquatic animals, and that doing so would change what both the scientists and the philosophers think they are entitled to claim. I have privately taken to calling this the calibration problem, though the name may be bad.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal with the following components. First, a precise statement of the question — something more disciplined than what I have given you, with a determinate answer rather than a topic. Second, an account of why the existing positions leave it open: I am aware that animal-welfare science already distinguishes biological functioning, affective state, and natural behavior as broad conceptions of welfare, and that sentience attribution for decapods has recently become a live policy question in the UK; explain what these frameworks leave undone rather than re-describing them. Third, the method — what I would actually do, quarter by quarter. My own instinct, from a life in the lab, is to work outward from a handful of real welfare standards for farmed finfish and decapod crustaceans and decompose each into its empirical premises and its value-laden premises, testing whether any two of the standards share the same hidden structure; tell me whether that is the right approach or whether it should be built up from the conceptual side instead, and why. Fourth, what a result would look like — what would count as the project succeeding and what would count as it failing, in terms that would embarrass me if I missed them. Fifth, an honest assessment of scope: I suspect this is really two projects, one about the epistemology of welfare indicators and one about the philosophy of welfare itself, and I would rather hear that now than in month nine. A note on references: do not supply a bibliography or attribute specific arguments to named authors — I have the animal-behavior literature in my head from thirty years of reading it, and I will supply the philosophical citations myself once I know which positions I actually need.
