You are a formal epistemologist with a strong side interest in the philosophy of law, and you have been asked to write an analytical essay for a graduate seminar on collective judgment. Please think carefully before writing, and begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you map out the moving parts before committing to a thesis.

The topic is what, if anything, the design of the criminal trial jury presupposes about the epistemology of collective belief.

Here is the practice, concretely. In the United States, a felony conviction generally requires the unanimous vote of twelve jurors selected through voir dire, a screening process in which lawyers on both sides can remove candidates who seem biased or unusually knowledgeable. Jurors then deliberate together for as long as it takes, sharing evidence, arguments, and doubts, until they converge on a verdict or hang. A hung jury produces a mistrial rather than a verdict. Until recently, a couple of states permitted convictions by supermajority rather than unanimity, but the dominant design is all-or-nothing: one skeptical juror can block a conviction, and near-uniform acquittals at trial coexist with heavy plea bargaining upstream.

Your task is to read this institution against the framework of the Condorcet jury theorem and ask what view of group knowledge it quietly commits to. The theorem says that if each of n voters is independently better than a coin flip at tracking the truth, majority rule approaches certain correctness as n grows. But notice how badly the actual institution fits: unanimity is not majority rule and tilts toward acquittal; deliberation deliberately destroys the independence the theorem requires; voir dire both raises average competence and selects for a correlated profile of jurors; the common-values premise sits awkwardly with reasonable doubt being a shared standard while verdict preferences diverge; and the hang is a designed third outcome that no aggregation function in the theorem's family produces. So does the jury presuppose an epistemology of signal aggregation at all, or an epistemology of reasoned convergence, on which jurors should end up agreeing because they ought to be moved by the same evidence? Are these two pictures even compatible, or does the institution presuppose something inconsistent — aggregation for the group size and screening, rational persuasion for the deliberation room and the unanimity rule?

In roughly 900 to 1200 words, reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Distinguish what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; note where the practice is underdetermined such that several theories of collective judgment would license it; and close by saying what a jury built on a purified Condorcet view would have to look like — and what, if anything, we would lose in exchanging the deliberating unanimity jury for it. Treat the asymmetry between conviction and acquittal as central rather than incidental: if the theorem is symmetric between two hypotheses and the institution is not, say where you think the asymmetry comes from and whether the theorem can be made to carry it.
