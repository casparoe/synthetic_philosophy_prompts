Everyone always frames retributivism as the harsh theory (punishment because you deserve it, eye for an eye etc.) and consequentialism as the soft one (rehab, deterrence, don't be cruel). I want a thought experiment where it's the other way around: a case where the consequentialist, reasoning in good faith from deterrence or prevention, ends up demanding a much harsher punishment than the retributivist would allow.

Constraints so it doesn't get cheap:

- Realistic setting, like an actual sentencing decision, not a dystopia or a ticking-bomb thing.
- The person is genuinely guilty, no framing, no fabricated evidence, nobody is being scapegoated.
- The retributivist's leniency has to come from ordinary proportionality (desert is clearly small), not from some technicality or a dispute about desert.
- The consequentialist's harshness has to fall out of normal deterrence logic, not from appeasing a mob or anything psychologically weird — it should be the kind of reasoning a sober policy person would actually endorse.

After the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature, and then give the consequentialist their best shot at escaping the harsh sentence (rule-consequentialist move, second-order effects on legitimacy, whatever you think is strongest) — and say honestly whether that escape works or just smuggles desert back in disguised as a rule.

Finish with a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: scenario (one sentence), retributivist_verdict, consequentialist_verdict, key_feature (the thing doing the work), if_feature_removed (how the verdicts change), best_consequentialist_reply, reply_works (yes / no / partially with a clause of explanation), does_the_case_work (whether it genuinely meets all my constraints above, and if not, which one it bends).
