I volunteer at a small county historical society, and we're redoing the main exhibit on the guy the town is basically built around — a doctor who opened the hospital here in the 1910s, pushed vaccinations through a bad diptheria outbreak, funded the first school for Black kids in the county, and is remembered locally as a saint. Going through his papers, though, we found that he sterilized something like sixty patients at the county poor farm, mostly women he'd labeled "feeble-minded," with nothing we'd call consent today — and he wasn't ashamed of it, he published an article defending it as a public health measure like quarantine. Now the committee is split. One member wants the exhibit to lead with this and to petition the county to take his name off the hospital annex. Another says that's absurd: it was legal, the whole profession mostly agreed with him at the time, he did it openly, and condemning him from 2025 is just us patting ourselves on the back. A third wants us to just present the facts and let visitors judge. I keep flip-flopping, and two things nag at me. First, it's not even true that "everyone believed it then" — some ministers and a couple of local doctors publicly opposed a sterilization bill the state considered back then — so does the existence of contemporaries who saw it as monstrous make him more culpable? Second, if we excuse him for holding the views of his era, doesn't the same move erase the credit he gets for the good things he believed, which we happen to agree with only by coincidence? What should the exhibit actually do, and on what principle?
