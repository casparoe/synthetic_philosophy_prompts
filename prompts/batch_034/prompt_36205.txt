I've been thinking on my own about what happens when two parties to a negotiation can each bind themselves before the other does, and I've arrived at this view: whoever can lock in a tough demand first wins. If I can publicly and irreversibly commit to "I get 70% or I accept disaster for both of us" before you've locked in anything, your options reduce to giving me the 70% or eating the disaster, and if you're rational you pay. So the rational policy is to commit as early and as rigidly as possible, and the small risk that both sides go over the cliff barely matters, because as long as I'm first the cliff is your problem, not mine. On this picture, in any world of agents who understand this, everyone races to bind themselves, and the whole game is decided by who can commit fastest.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want the arguments and scenarios that should actually move me, ranked from things that would sink it to things that are merely awkward — including what happens when commitments can't be verified, when both sides can lock in nearly simultaneously, and whether a policy of always racing can survive once everyone knows you follow it. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether, once the view has accumulated enough "commit first, except when..." clauses, it has been insulated from any possible counterexample, and whether that insulation is a fatal flaw or just what strategic claims of this kind are like.
