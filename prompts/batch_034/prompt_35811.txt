I'm a quantitative trader who came up through professional poker. The vocabulary I actually reason in: conditional expected value, variance and drawdown, Kelly sizing, martingales and stopping rules, well-defined sample spaces, and the discipline of checking whether a backtested edge survives conditioning on the information you actually had when the trade was placed. I have no measure theory beyond what traders use, and no philosophy training.

Restate the two-envelope paradox in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Cover the versions that matter to me and, presumably, to the people who write about it: the closed-envelope version, where a symmetry argument seems to give a self-financing swap positive expected value with no information at all; the open-envelope version, where conditioning on the observed amount still seems to leave you a one-third-of-the-stake edge for every value you might see; the diagnosis I've half-absorbed, that the open-envelope calculation quietly replaces a conditional expectation you can't compute with an unconditional average over amounts that aren't on the table; and the variant where the amounts are drawn from a distribution that makes the expected content infinite, which I'm told resists that diagnosis.

In my world, a round trip that shows positive expected value in both directions is an accounting error until proven otherwise, so my instinct is that the closed-envelope version is exactly that — a conditioning failure dressed up as a paradox — and that the infinite-expectation variant is the only part with real content, since I've seen Kelly-style reasoning do strange things when the variance is unbounded. But I want this checked rather than confirmed.

So end with the residue report: which parts of the philosophers' debate translate cleanly onto phenomena I already know (and name the analogue), which parts survive translation only with distortion, and whether anything has no expression in my vocabulary at all — and if so, whether that is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers are disagreeing about something less substantive than it looks. If my two instincts above are wrong in different ways, say so.
