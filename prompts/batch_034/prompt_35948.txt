I have been invited to contribute a position statement to a symposium volume on art and the emotions, and the editors have asked each contributor to lay out their commitments on the paradox of tragedy up front. Before I draft the paper, I want an audit of the position I intend to defend: I would rather discover now than in the question period that I am relying on premises I have disavowed elsewhere in the same document. My commitments are as follows:

(1) The pity and fear aroused by a tragedy are full-blooded emotions of the same types we have toward real misfortunes. I take the side of the party that refuses to dilute them into Walton-style quasi-emotions: when I am moved by the end of Lear, I am genuinely pitying, and the state differs from pity for a real casualty only in the fictionality of its object.

(2) I reject all three of the familiar accounts of where the pleasure comes from. I do not accept the Humean conversion story on which the uneasiness of pity and fear is transmuted into pleasure by the eloquence of the vehicle; I do not accept the Feagin-style meta-response account on which the pleasure is our satisfaction in finding ourselves aptly moved by what we rightly take to be a worthy work; and I do not accept any reduction of tragedy-pleasure to pleasure in craft, diction, music, or staging.

(3) The phenomenon to be explained is real and robust: attending to tragedy is, on balance, pleasurable, we seek it out deliberately, and the pleasure grows with the intensity of the distressing content — the works we prize are the ones that devastate us.

(4) At the same time, the suffering must be at a certain remove: tragedy is enjoyed only when what is depicted does not implicate the spectator's own life or griefs. Footage of actual catastrophe, however distant its victims, is not enjoyed in anything like the same way, and I regard the pleasure of the one and the refusal of the other as tracking the same constraint.

(5) My pleasure in a tragedy I know by heart is undiminished, and often deepened, by repetition; suspense is inessential to tragedy's emotional power.

(6) The value of tragedy — the thing that justifies the practice and separates it from melodrama, which may equal it in pathos — lies in the cultivation of sympathy: tragedy educates and extends our capacities for pity.

(7) Methodologically, the paradox of tragedy is a conceptual question about the nature of these emotions and their objects, answerable a priori. I am skeptical of importing findings from experimental psychology into aesthetics; whatever those studies show, they do not touch the philosophical problem.

I believe this package is defensible, but I have held some version of it for twenty years and can no longer see it fresh. Tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and how badly, which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve on inspection, and, for each genuine one, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — whether the set is jointly tenable, and where the real problem lies — and then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restatement of my question.
