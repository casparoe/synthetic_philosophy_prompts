I teach a seminar on social ontology, and this term we are reading David Lewis's Convention alongside the later literature on the evolution of norms. For our unit on how conventions change, I want a case study written up on Sweden's 1967 switch from left- to right-hand traffic, analyzed through Lewis's framework. Let me give you the episode's salient facts, so you need not rely on your recall of the details, and then tell you what I want the analysis to do.

The episode: on Sunday, 3 September 1967 — the day the Swedes call Dagen H — Sweden moved from driving on the left to driving on the right. The facts that matter are these. Sweden's neighbours, including Norway and Finland, with which it shares land borders, all drove on the right. More than ninety percent of Swedish cars were left-hand-drive (steering wheel on the left), a configuration poorly suited to left-hand traffic and a factor in head-on collisions when overtaking. In a 1955 referendum, some eighty-three percent of Swedish voters had voted to keep driving on the left; the Riksdag legislated the change anyway, in 1963, and a four-year public-education campaign followed — the changeover itself involved a nighttime traffic ban, roughly 350,000 road signs replaced, and the reconfiguration or retirement of buses and trams. In the weeks after the switch, accidents and insurance claims fell markedly, but within two years the accident rate had returned to its previous trend. And note one wrinkle: rail traffic, including the Stockholm metro, did not switch and continued on the left.

Now the analysis I want, in five parts.

First, the mapping, done carefully. Lay out driving-side as Lewis would: a recurrent coordination problem among essentially arbitrary alternatives, solved by a regularity in behaviour sustained by mutual expectations and common knowledge. Say who the participants are, what generates the preference for general conformity, and how precedent did the work of sustaining the old equilibrium. Then locate exactly where this episode deviates from the textbook coordination problem.

Second, the arbitrariness condition. Lewis holds that a convention is arbitrary in the sense that, given everyone else's conformity, some alternative regularity would have served about as well. But here one equilibrium appears to have been better all along — the right-hand alternative, given the steering-wheel configurations, the borders, and the crossings — so left-driving looks like a Pareto-inferior convention that nonetheless held for generations. Does this threaten Lewis's account of arbitrariness, or is arbitrariness compatible with the entrenched inferiority of one equilibrium? Distinguish carefully between "either would have served" and "both were equally good"; the episode seems to force that distinction on us.

Third, and this is the heart of the paper: re-coordination by decree. Lewis's conventions are self-perpetuating — conformity breeds the expectations that breed conformity — which suggests a story of gradual drift, punctuated perhaps by piecemeal local shifts. Yet this convention was replaced in the course of a single morning, by legislation, against the expressed preference of a large majority. How, on Lewis's machinery, did the decree work? Did the state simply manufacture new common knowledge and make right-hand traffic salient at a stroke, so that the day after Dagen H the regularity was a convention again — or is something doing the enforcing that Lewis's account does not acknowledge, in which case we should ask whether the new regularity became a convention only once the legal threat receded into the background of precedent? Consider also the referendum: when eighty-three percent voted to keep left, were they preferring the left side, or preferring coordination on whatever side was established? What does that vote, read through the framework, actually reveal?

Fourth, the evolutionary angle. Lewis's account needs supplementation from the evolution-of-norms literature — best-response dynamics, risk-dominance versus payoff-dominance, stochastic stability. On those models, could Sweden's convention plausibly have migrated to right-hand traffic without state action, given the payoff asymmetries, the small and sticky interaction neighbourhoods of drivers, and the lumpiness of the switching costs? Say what those models predict, and where they are silent about an episode like this.

Fifth, the residue report — what the theory cannot see. Some candidates, which you should evaluate rather than merely list: the unpopularity of the change suggests a symbolic or identity dimension to convention-adherence that no equilibrium analysis captures; the temporary drop in accidents reflects changes in perceived risk that belong to psychology, not game structure; and the persistence of left-hand rail traffic shows that a single "convention" can be several, with different populations and different equilibria, in a way that cuts against treating a nation's traffic as one coordination problem.

Throughout, be disciplined about the mapping and honest about silence: where Lewis's account does not speak, say so, rather than forcing it to. I would rather read that the theory is indifferent to some feature of the case than a clever-sounding assimilation of it. The paper's conclusion should say whether Dagen H is a confirmation of Lewis, an anomaly for him, or a case his framework never anticipated — and defend that verdict.
