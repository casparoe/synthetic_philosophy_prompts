I'm taking a seminar on nationalism, and the phrase "imagined communities" has become the room's all-purpose trump card. It comes, I gather, from Benedict Anderson's 1983 book on the origin and spread of nationalism, but it has long since detached from its context: last week a classmate deployed it to argue that national identity is a kind of collective hallucination — that "imagined" means fabricated, that the older a nation claims to be, the more certainly its traditions were manufactured for political ends, and that national pride is therefore pride in a fiction, no more rational than loyalty to a character in a novel. The professor nodded along, but the slide from "imagined" to "fake, and therefore dismissible" felt too quick to me, and I couldn't articulate where it goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all.

Take the phrase apart for me. Lay out, as a numbered list I can bring to seminar, the distinct precise claims "a nation is an imagined community" could be expressing, ordered from the weakest to the strongest. For each, say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, substantive but contested, or simply false, and what kind of evidence or argument would bear on it. At minimum I'd want these kept apart: the sociological reading (Anderson's own point, as I understand it, is just that members of any nation will never meet or even hear of most of their fellow-members, so the sense of shared belonging is mediated rather than face-to-face — in which case every community larger than a village where everyone knows everyone is "imagined" in exactly this sense, including my seminar); the historical reading associated with Anderson and with the "invention of tradition" literature around Hobsbawm — roughly, that the nation is a distinctly modern form, and that many traditions claiming remote antiquity, Highland clan tartans being the standard example, are demonstrably recent constructions, often created quite self-consciously; the conceptual point that being constructed does not make something unreal, since money, universities, and promises are all constructed and perfectly real; and the strong debunking reading my classmate needed — that national attachment is thereby irrational or illegitimate, or that nations' claims to antiquity are generally bogus. For each reading, state explicitly whether it would actually support the conclusion he drew from it.

Since some of our seminar disputes may be purely verbal — different people meaning different things by "imagined," "real," or "invented" — close by telling me which of the disagreements on your list are merely verbal and which are substantive, and, if you're willing to take a position, where you would personally draw the line on whether national pride can be rational.
