I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I've somehow ended up as the person in my department who defends the doomsday argument at pub nights, so I want to stress-test it properly. The version I hold is the Carter-Leslie one: I should reason as if my birth rank is a random sample from the class of humans who ever exist, I'm roughly the 100 billionth-ish human, and so the total pool of humans is probably not trillions of times larger than that -- because if it were, my being this early would be a wild coincidence. Not a guarantee of imminent doom, just a big bayesian update toward shorter futures. I've dealt with the standard "but it predicts the same doom for every past generation, and they were wrong about being near the end" objection and I think the response works, so don't just hand me that one.

Here's what I want: a ranked map of what would actually count against my view, from things that would honestly sink it to things that are just uncomfortable. Specifically I want you to cover at least these, plus anything I'm missing:

1. The self-indication assumpion move -- the claim that finding yourself existing at all is evidence for worlds with more observers, which cancels the doom update. My worry is that this "cancelation" feels like it proves too much: it seems to imply I should think there are infinitely many observers on other planets in otherwise identical universes. Is that a real cost or am I confusing myself?

2. The reference class problem. If I restrict the class to "humans," fine, but a friend in stats keeps pushing the German tank problem analogy at me and insisting the estimator is only as good as your sampling story, and he says any story about "randomization" here is a metaphor we're not entitled to. When, if ever, does the sampling actually happen, and what would have to be true for there to be no fact of the matter?

3. A twist I've been chewing on and can't find anywhere: suppose you restrict the reference class to people who have ever *thought about* the doomsday argument -- call it the anxious class. Awareness of the argument only shows up very late in any civilization's run, so on that class the update flips and you should expect *lots* of observers after you. It seems like I can rig the reference class to get whatever conclusion I want, which is either the best objection to me or evidence that reference-class picking was never doing real work. Please tell me which.

4. Whatever the strongest *empirical* defeater would be -- something the world could look like that would make me drop the whole thing. Not just "we survive," because the argument never said we wouldn't, only that long futures are less likely.

And one more thing: I'm sick of the seminar move where someone lays out SSA and SIA and reference classes and then says "where you land depends on your intuitions." I want you to actually take a position. Is the argument sound, does one of these defeaters break it beyond repair, or does it survive in a weakened form -- and if you think it survives weakened, say exactly what the weakened version claims and how I'd know if even that were wrong. If you think I've quietly insulated the view so nothing could ever count against it, call that out too, and tell me whether that's fatal for an argument that never made a testable prediction in the first place.
