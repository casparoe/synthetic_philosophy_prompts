My book club spent last month on a book about how moral revolutions happen, and afterward two members, Dora and Felix, stayed behind and argued for so long that I ended up just listening and taking mental notes, since I could not referee it myself. The book's central idea, as we had taken it, is that practices like dueling among gentlemen, foot-binding in China, and the Atlantic slave trade ended not primarily because someone finally produced new arguments against them — such arguments had been circulating for centuries without much effect — but because something happened to honor: the practices stopped functioning as credentials of status and started functioning as embarrassments, so that renouncing them was what signaled standing. Dora and Felix got into whether this honor mechanism is genuinely explanatory or merely a polished restatement of the thing to be explained.

Their exchange had four main moves. First, Felix opened by saying that honor is a placeholder, not an explanation: to say that dueling ended because it became dishonorable is just to say that the norm changed while using the word for that norm's own currency, and a real explanation must cite something outside honor — economic interest, fresh argument, or a changed social structure. Second, Dora replied that honor has its own machinery: a practice survives only while it reliably signals status, and such a signal is subject to sudden collapse once enough high-status people defect or refuse to participate, because what makes the signal work is precisely that others still read it; so the honor explanation predicts a distinctive signature — long stability followed by a very rapid collapse within a generation once defection reaches a critical mass — whereas interest-based explanations, which change slowly and gradually, cannot produce that signature at all. Third, Felix granted the point about rapid collapse but said it only relocates the mystery: what he wanted explained was why the first defections happened at all, and since honor is defined by whatever the group currently respects, it cannot by itself push anyone out of the practice; the first defector must be moved by argument or by interest, so the honor story is at bottom a story about how a change spreads once triggered, not about what caused it. Fourth, Dora answered that this sets the bar impossibly high: on that demand no social explanation would ever work, since every mechanism requires some initial trigger from elsewhere; and she added that the trigger and the mechanism may be quite different things — an argument that had lain inert for two centuries could suddenly grip because a status community's circumstances made it humiliating not to act on it, which is exactly why moral revolutions seem to cluster around public renunciations by respected figures rather than around the publication of treatises.

As I watched, I could not tell whether Felix's demand for an external trigger was a legitimate condition on explanation or a quiet refusal to accept social-level mechanisms at all, and I could not tell whether Dora's argument from the collapse signature genuinely favors her honor mechanism or merely favors any contagious social process whatever. Please go through their exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step — not merely who wins overall — and, if you can, say what both of them seem to be missing about how the honor explanation is supposed to work. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
