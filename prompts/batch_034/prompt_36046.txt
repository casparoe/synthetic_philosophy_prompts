I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about when a person is morally responsible for what they do, and I'd like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether some of them have to go. Here are the verdicts.

1. A man has four drinks, decides he is fine to drive, and kills a cyclist. Fully blameworthy — for the death, not just for the drinking.
2. A bank teller hands over the vault combination because a robber is holding a gun to her son's head. Not blameworthy at all.
3. A woman raised from childhood in an isolated cult, with every one of her information sources controlled, steals her family's savings for the leader. Not blameworthy, or nearly not.
4. A man raised in terrible conditions by negligent parents mugs a stranger. Blameworthy, though somewhat mitigated.
5. Another man is secretly administered a drug that produces in him an overwhelming desire to harm a neighbor, which he experiences as fully his own; he acts on it. Not blameworthy.
6. The same man, tumor removed, later mugs a stranger out of ordinary greed. Fully blameworthy again. What changed between these two muggings was a medical fact about his brain, and it changed everything.
7. A long-term heroin addict who wants to quit but relapses and steals to get the next dose. I go back and forth on this one.
8. A scientist wins a major prize for work that depended on unusual intelligence, curiosity, and stamina, none of which she chose or earned. I want to say she deserves the prize and the honor. But the same reasoning that clears the manipulated man in 5 seems to say she doesn't deserve the qualities that produced the work.

The pair I can't reconcile is 4 and 5. In both cases, the agent's desire to harm was fixed by factors outside his control — genes and upbringing in the one, a drug in the other — and in both cases the desire felt like his own. Yet my verdicts differ sharply, and I don't seem to believe that the difference is just the rarity of the causal route. A friend suggested that instead of asking whether free will exists, I should ask which of these verdicts is worth keeping, since that tells me what kind of control I actually care about — the kind of freedom worth wanting — and that the principle I'm looking for should be read off those verdicts rather than off metaphysics.

So, please: state a principle, or a small set of candidates, as precisely as you can, that delivers or nearly delivers these verdicts, and identify which clause is motivated by which case. Then tell me what it says about the cases I haven't listed: the psychopath who knows the moral rules perfectly well but cannot feel their force; the person who, after a sudden brain injury, becomes selfish in ways he endorses and reports he prefers; and an artificial agent whose values were deliberately engineered by its makers, which behaves impeccably and insists it cares. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the friend's suggestion — keeping the verdicts and letting them define the freedom worth wanting — is stable, or whether some verdict, once I see what it costs to keep, should be given up instead.
