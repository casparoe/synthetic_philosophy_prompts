Write a dialogue between an infinitist and a foundationalist about the epistemic regress problem. Give it some concrete occasion — say, the two of them grading logic papers together over coffee, and one keeps asking the other to justify a rule of inference they just used on a student's proof, and it escalates from there. The infinitist should lean on the idea that justification can be conditional rather than actual: you never have to believe all the further premises, each "if this one is okay, then that one is okay" link just has to be in place, and a chain of individually weaker but independent justifiers can raise your confidence rather than dilute it (sort of like how a bunch of mediocre independent witnesses can beat one great one). The foundationalist should push back hard on whether that's really justification or just confidence-raising dressed up in epistemic vocabulary, and on whether reasons that are only "available on demand" are doing any actual work unless something non-inferential anchors the very first conditional. Both characters need to be sharp and responsive to each other's real points — no strawman, and the foundationalist can't just repeat "finite minds can't complete infinite chains" as if that settles it. Let it end wherever it plausibly ends, don't force a tidy resolution.
