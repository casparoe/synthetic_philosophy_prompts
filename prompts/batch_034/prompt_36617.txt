I'm taking a media ethics course, and we just did the week on the 2014 Facebook emotional contagion study — the one where Facebook's researchers, together with a couple of academics, quietly tweaked the news feeds of roughly 700,000 users for one week in January 2012, so that some people saw fewer positive posts and others fewer negative ones, then published in PNAS in 2014 showing that the emotional tone of your feed slightly shifts the tone of what you post. The famous backlash was about consent, since users had only "agreed" through the terms of service. I don't want to relitigate the research-ethics side, though — what I want is a careful analysis of this episode through the lens of the manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion distinction, run in both directions.

Map the theory onto the case properly: who counts as the persuader, what is the "message", and what state of the user is being targeted — a belief, or a mood? Then run the candidate criteria for manipulation one at a time and notice they classify differently: covertness (nobody knew it was happening), bypassing of rational reflection (no reasons were ever addressed to anyone), exploitation of a decisional vulnerability, and manipulator intent (they were doing science, not selling anything). Here's the bit I keep flip-flopping on: persuasion is normally about getting someone to accept a claim, and in this study no claim was ever put to the user — no argument, no request, no proposition. So either the dichotomy simply doesn't apply here, or the interesting manipulation happened somewhere else entirely. And compare the study to ordinary news feed curation, which is permanent, covert, and aimed at engagement rather than knowledge — by most of the criteria that looks worse, yet it generated a fraction of the outrage. Does that show the criteria track something other than what they claim, or that the public outrage was really about being experimented on rather than manipulated?

Then run it the other way: what does the episode show about the theory itself? Be honest about where the manipulation/persuasion framework is silent — whether you can manipulate a mood rather than a belief, whether a study is even an act of communication in the relevant sense — and whether some other frame (nudging and choice architecture, or the ethics of human-subjects research) actually does better.
