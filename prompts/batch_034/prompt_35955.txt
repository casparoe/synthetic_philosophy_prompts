My two most stubborn grad students — one a die-hard hedonist, the other a desire-satisfaction theorist — have been fighting about the experience machine since September, and I've finally persuaded them to do an adversarial collaboration write-up instead of talking past each other for another term. I need a draft of the joint statement they'd both sign. Here's what they've actually conceded to each other in seminar, which should shape the agreed-claims section:

- Both accept that felt quality and hedonic tone are real and matter to well-being; the dispute is only over whether anything else does.
- The hedonist has conceded that people's refusal of Nozick's machine is real, robust data, but insists it's contaminated by status-quo bias — De Brigard's reversed version, where you learn you've been in the machine all along and are offered the exit, gets plenty of people to say they'd stay plugged in. The desire theorist has agreed this cuts at least somewhat against taking the original refusal at face value.
- The desire theorist has conceded that the deceived businessman (beloved, successful, lied about constantly, feels great) is at least as hard for her side as the machine is for his, since the frustrated desires there are arguably ones the businessman would want to keep frustrated if he knew.
- Both have agreed that the heterogeneity of pleasure — bodily pleasure vs. the pleasure of an achievement vs. the weird pleasure of watching a tragedy — is a genuine problem that neither side has solved, and that any joint statement has to own up to that.

Now for the structure I want:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — only claims both could actually put their names to, nothing vacuous.
2. A disagreements table: for each disagreement, a wording the hedonist would accept for HIS side and a wording the desire theorist would accept for HERS, phrased so neither feels slanted.
3. The cruxes — I count at least three worth including: (a) whether desires whose frustration is never noticed by the subject still lower well-being (the bereaved-mother type case); (b) whether the De Brigard reversal defeats the machine intuition or just reveals the intuition was tracking loss aversion all along, and what, if anything, would settle that empirically; (c) whether pleasure can be made into one measurable thing at all. For each crux, name the case or argument BOTH would sign off on as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance about what it shows — no hedging, actual predictions they could later be embarrassed by.

One more thing. I want them to address a variant we dreamed up in class, which I've started calling the annuity machine: you enter for a fixed ten years, and during those years the machine quietly arranges the outside world so that the pleasure you're being fed exactly matches how things actually are — your kids really do flourish because the machine made it so. You recieve the true experiences of a person whose life is going wonderfully, and it is going wonderfully, just not because of you. The hedonist thinks this case is trivially fine for him and the desire theorist keeps insisting it does real damage to someone, though she's been cagey about exactly whom. Make them take a position on it in the statement — it's done more to focus our arguments than anything else this term.

Also, if any part of their dispute turns out to be partly verbal — e.g., whether "pleasure" means a felt quality or something more like liking-and-getting — flag which part and why, rather than papering over it.

Hard limit: the whole document, table included, under 900 words. I want something the two of them could literally sign at the bottom, not a treatise — they'll each bolt on a dissenting footnote later, that's fine, but the shared text has to be tight.
