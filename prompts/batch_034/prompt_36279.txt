I'm a decision theorist starting a two-year postdoc in a philosophy group that works on AI safety from the conceptual side — no lab, no compute, just me, my training in decision theory and formal epistemology, and one collaborator who knows the machine learning literature far better than I do. I want to spend the postdoc on corrigibility and the shutdown problem: what it would take for a capable agent to leave its own off-switch alone and defer to its principals, and why so many formal constructions that seem to buy this property turn out to buy something weaker. My advisor thinks the central open question is whether corrigibility is compatible with rationality at all — whether the indifference-style proposals that make an agent unmoved by news that its button was pressed inevitably also make it unmoved by the button being tampered with, shielded, or quietly disconnected, so that "not caring whether you're shut down" collapses into "not caring to keep shutdown possible." I half-believe this, but I also suspect that even if it's true, it may not be what I should spend two years on.

Here's the menu of open questions as I see it, though I'd welcome additions and deletions: (a) the rationality-versus-corrigibility tension above, including whether it can be dissolved by carefully distinguishing the agent's own influence over the button from everyone else's; (b) whether corrigibility is a property of an agent's goals at all, or is instead something like a virtue of the process that produced the agent — a view a colleague of mine pushes, on which the real question is which training or selection environments make shutdown-respecting behavior the kind of thing that survives optimization pressure, not which utility functions encode it; (c) whether "shutdown" is even the right primitive, or whether the notion should be generalized to a gradient of deference — agents that treat a principal's interventions as evidence, as causes, or as goals to be traded off against their own, rather than as a special sacred category; (d) whether corrigibility is stable under self-modification and self-modeling — whether an agent that can reason about its successors has an incentive to make them less corrigible; and (e) what, if anything, the formal work implies when principals are human — where they disagree, change their minds under pressure, and can themselves be shaped by a deferential agent that learns what makes them press or not press. My colleague thinks (e) is the whole game; my advisor thinks (e) is applied hand-waving until (a) is settled.

Rank these for me — or restructure the menu if you think it's carved at the wrong joints — for someone with my background, two years, and a tolerance for impossible problems. I want the dependency structure made explicit: which of these, if cracked, would make the others easier or moot, and which are downstream even though they look urgent. Say something about tractability, since I can prove theorems but can't run experiments, and about what developments over the next few years — conceptual or empirical, in work I'd only hear about secondhand — would move items up or down the list. Defend the ordering; don't just tell me what's unresolved. Hard limit: 800 words, and no reading lists or name-dropping where you can help it — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.
