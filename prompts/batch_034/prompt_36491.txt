You are to act as an expert in the history of economic thought with a strong command of computational economics. Please think carefully and reason step by step, working through your analysis before committing to any numbers, and write the final answer in American English.

Some background on where I am coming from: I spent thirty-five years as a numerical analyst, first in operations research and later running large-scale atmospheric simulation codes, and since retiring I have been working through the socialist calculation debate of the 1920s through 1940s and its modern echoes. My training makes me unable to stop reading this debate as a question about computation, and I would like your help pinning down whether that reading is a distortion.

To make sure we are working from the same history, as I understand it: Mises argued in 1920 that without private ownership of the means of production there are no genuine prices for capital goods, and hence no rational way to compare the costs of alternative production plans. Lange, in the 1930s, answered that a planning board could set initial prices, observe shortages and surpluses, and adjust iteratively — managers instructed to equate marginal cost and price — so that the market becomes a special case of a solution procedure for a system of equations. Hayek then shifted the ground, arguing that the problem is not solving equations but gathering knowledge that is dispersed, tacit, and never exists anywhere in articulated form — the knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place. And late in life Lange reportedly suggested that the whole debate had been rendered quaint by the computer: the market, on that view, was just a computing device of the pre-electronic age, and the equations could now be solved directly. There has even been a more recent line of argument — I believe associated with Paul Cockshott and Allin Cottrell, though I may be garbling the details — that input-output matrices are sparse and that iterative methods make economy-wide planning tractable on hardware we have had for decades. Treat the substance of these positions as fixed, but correct me where my history is wrong.

Here is my question, and I want a number, not a survey. What is your credence that the economic calculation problem, understood as the problem of producing and allocating existing goods to satisfy existing preferences, is in fact computationally tractable for a sufficiently resourced planning agency with modern computing — that is, that a computation-based objection of the Mises type fails at bottom, whatever one concludes on other grounds? Please decompose your credence into at least these terms: whether the optimization problem, given the data, is solvable in practice rather than merely in principle; whether the needed data, especially preferences over nonexistent goods and tacit production knowledge, exists in a form that can be fed to any algorithm at all; whether any objective function to be optimized is well defined given the aggregation of individual preferences; and whether the incentive problem of truthful reporting is separable from the computational problem or collapses it. Assign each term a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine, tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence would move it most. Please also give a separate credence for the stronger claim that a planned economy could match a market economy in the dynamic sense — generating new goods and methods rather than allocating existing ones — and note where the two credences come apart.
