For thirty-five years I ran a lab in renal physiology, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology with the enthusiasm of a convert and the impatience of someone who spent a career watching nephrons do their work. The debate that has really seized me is the one between etiological (selected-effect) accounts of biological function and causal-role accounts in the tradition of Cummins: whether the function of a trait is the effect for which it was retained by natural selection, or the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system. I understand the standard positions well enough. What I want is help deciding where to spend my own effort, because I have been invited to write a trade-press book — roughly two years of part-time work — on what biologists actually mean when they say a part has a function, and I cannot treat every live controversy as though it deserved a chapter.

So: which open questions in this debate matter most for a book like that, and in what order should I take them on? The candidate puzzles I keep circling are these. First, whether a trait fixed largely by drift, or a spandrel-like byproduct that has only recently become useful, can properly be said to have a function at all — my sense is that practicing biologists happily grant functions to such structures while the etiological account has to strain, and I don't know whether that reflects a defect in the theory or sloppiness in the lab. Second, how either account handles a single structure with multiple effects and multiple selection histories — a gut that digests, hosts a microbiome, and houses a large fraction of the body's immune tissue — and whether "the function" of such a structure is even a coherent notion. Third, whether the concept of dysfunction in medicine, and by extension our criteria for calling something a disease, presupposes one account over the other, or whether clinicians can remain neutral. Fourth, whether selected-effect accounts can survive the shift to levels below the organism, as with programmed cell death, where a cell's "function" involves its own demise for the organism's benefit. And fifth, whether engineered organisms with minimal, designed genomes — where functions are assigned by fiat rather than inherited through a selection history — show that the historical component was always doing less work than philosophers assume.

Please rank the two or three most worth my sustained attention and defend the ordering. My criteria are not purely theoretical: I care about what a scientifically literate but non-philosophical reader would take away from the book, about tractability for someone trained at the bench rather than in a seminar room, and about which questions are genuinely unsettled rather than merely unfashionable. I also want to know which of these puzzles are, in your judgment, artifacts of the philosophers' framing — cases where the apparent clash between the two accounts dissolves once you stop asking for a single unified notion of function, and where I should say as much in a paragraph and move on. Finally, flag anything important I have left off the list entirely.
