I'm a recently retired physicist — spent most of my career on nonlinear dynamics and control theory, so things like limit cycles, bifurcations, slow-fast systems, and Lyapunov functions are basically my native tongue. Since retiring I've been reading a lot of history of ideas, and I keep bumping into these theories that say history runs in cycles: Polybius with his rotation of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun with group solidarity rising and decaying, Spengler with cultures as organisms, Toynbee with challenge and response. My instinct is that these are all, at bottom, claims about the trajectory of a dynamical system, and that if that's right I should be able to restate each one properly in my vocabulary — attractors, transients, control parameters, damping, bifurcations, the works — instead of the vague organic metaphors they actually use.

So here's the task. Take each of the four (Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee) and translate their view into the language of dynamical systems as faithfully as you can. For each one I want three things clearly separated: what maps cleanly (e.g., does Polybius's cycle become a stable limit cycle, and his mixed constitution a damping term or some stabilizing feedback? does Ibn Khaldun's ~four-generation rhythm look like a relaxation oscillation with a slow variable?), what you had to distort or stipulate to force the fit, and what simply has no expression in my vocabulary at all. I suspect, for instance, that Spengler is the odd one out — if each culture runs its course exactly once and never repeats, that's not a cycle in my sense at all, it's a transient, and calling it cyclical seems like a category error he wouldn't have cared about. But maybe I'm wrong about that.

Then give me the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about most. Where the translation fails, tell me whether you think it fails because the historian's framework is genuinely vaguer than it looks (a metaphor dressed up as a mechanism), or because dynamical systems talk is missing something real — like meaning, or actors who react to predictions about themselves, or the fact that the "parameters" are people. And is there anything in these debates that philosophers and historians argue about which simply evaporates when restated my way — and if so, does the evaporation show the argument was less substantive than it looked, or that my vocabulary is quietly assuming nothing in history corresponds to? Please be honest about the fudges; don't smuggle in stability assumptions just because my language makes them natural. Take your time and go thinker by thinker.
