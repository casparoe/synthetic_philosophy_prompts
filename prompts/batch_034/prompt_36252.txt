I'm a high school junior whose ethics seminar just finished Mill's Utilitarianism, and I need help with an argument a friend has built against hedonism as a theory of well-being. I believe it proves too much, but I cannot locate the joint where it slips.

His argument, as he presents it:

(1) For hedonism to be a substantive theory — one that could turn out false — rather than a disguised definition, the word "pleasure" must pick out its instances by some feature they all share, and that feature cannot simply be that we call them "pleasures," on pain of circularity.

(2) If the instances share no such feature, then the claim that pleasure is the only thing good in itself either says nothing (it attaches a label to a label), or, to avoid saying nothing, stretches "pleasure" to cover everything anyone pursues — in which case hedonism cannot lose any argument and is thereby empty.

(3) There is no such shared feature. Every candidate fails. A distinctive sensation fails, because the pleasure of finally solving a hard problem, the pleasure of watching an enemy fail, and the pleasure of warm sunlight on a cold day seem to share no felt quality, unless the felt quality is just the calm judgment that they are pleasant. A feeling of liking fails the same way: to describe the feeling, we must reach for the very word we were trying to analyze. We could not construct a different candidate that survived a weekend of testing.

(4) Therefore hedonism is either false as stated or empty, and in both versions it is not a theory that anyone could believe for reasons.

What makes me nervous is the standard in (3), because when I apply my friend's test to other concepts it proves far too much. Take "no shared non-circular feature, therefore empty label" and run it on games: no feature is common to chess, solitaire, tug-of-war, and a child counting passing cars, unless we help ourselves to "being a game." The same happens with chairs, with sciences, and with my grandfather's notion of a fair election. On my friend's principle, every such general term is either a mere label or secretly covers everything — which is absurd, since those concepts do real work: they sort the world and can surprise us.

So here is what I would like from you. First, tell me whether the argument against hedonism commits the same fallacy I suspect, or whether "pleasure" is genuinely worse off than "game" and the argument survives even though the generalization does not. Second, name the step to reject and price the rejection. If your answer is that (3) is false, state the candidate feature precisely enough that I can check it against the hard cases — malicious pleasure at someone's downfall, the masochist's pleasure in pain, the idle pleasures of a dull afternoon. If your answer is that the culprit is (1) or (2), explain what kind of unity a concept can have without a shared feature, and say what hedonism gives up if "pleasure" has only that weaker kind of unity — in particular, whether it can still present itself as a theory of well-being rather than as a mood.

For what it is worth: I would most like to keep (2). I have no patience for theories that cannot lose. But I am prepared to be told that (2) survives only because (1) smuggled in an impossible standard.
