I'm a graduate student working on welfare, and in three weeks I'm presenting to my department's colloquium a paper defending a version of tranquilism: the view that how well a life goes for the person living it depends entirely on the absence of craving — unsatisfied desire, restless attachment, the felt lack that accompanies wanting things to be otherwise — and the suffering that flows from it. Comfortable tranquility is the ceiling of well-being; positive states on top of it add nothing. The argument I'm planning runs roughly like this: (a) hedonic adaptation makes positive states self-erasing in a way that chronic craving is not — the pleasure of a promotion fades in months, but the anxiety of tenure-track life renews itself daily, so over a whole life the craving variable dominates; (b) in the cases where they come apart — the serene hermit versus the high-strung striver who gets everything he wants — our considered judgments about who is better off track the craving, not the pleasure; and (c) tranquilism, unlike negative utilitarianism, is a claim about personal welfare, not about aggregation or what we owe to others, so standard complaints about the sadistic-facet conclusion don't apply.

The audience is the problem. The colloquium regulars are mostly mainstream hedonists and desire-satisfaction theorists, and one of the latter has built her career on adaptive preferences — she will see my serene hermit as a textbook case of sour grapes. There's also a senior scholar of Buddhist philosophy who thinks any Western "tranquilism" that treats the absence of craving as a stopping point rather than as one strand of a path involving other cultivation is a Schopenhauerian pessimism in disguise, and he has said so at other talks. Finally there's a logician who doesn't care about welfare but delights in finding views that can't state their own necessary and sufficient conditions, and a colleague who works on well-being measurement in policy who will want to know what my view predicts about the contentment of people with depression who report no desires at all, only flatness.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this particular room — not the generic ones I'd get from a utilitarian blog — ordered by how much damage they would do to my thesis, each phrased the way that specific person would put it (the adaptive-preferences attack as she would frame it, the Buddhist scholar's charge in his idiom), each with a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Flag explicitly which objections I should concede parts of up front to buy credibility, and which I must not concede anything on. Finally, include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that, in your judgment, my argument as sketched has no good answer to at all — and tell me whether I should pre-empt it or pray.
