My old labmate, who took a philosophy degree after leaving the oil industry, tried to save me some time by explaining Putnam's famous argument over coffee, and now I can't tell if I learned it right or wrong. Here's more or less what he told me, as best I can reproduce it:

"So Putnam's move is this: assume a causal theory of reference, meaning words hook onto the world through causal chains, not through mental pictures. Now suppose you really are a brain in a vat, hooked up to a supercomputer. Your word 'vat' was learned entirely from vat-images fed in by the computer, so it never got causally connected to actual vats at all -- it refers to the computer's simulated vats, or to features of the program itself. Same for 'brain' and for every other word. So when an envatted brain thinks or says that it is a brain in a vat, it's saying something false, because in its language 'brain' and 'vat' pick out things in the simulation. The sentence defeats itself. That's why Putnam says the hypothesis that I am a brain in a vat can never be true, for anyone. It's basically the same trick as Descartes' evil demon, just done with semantics instead of doubt, and it's also the forerunner of the simulation argument people go on about -- Putnam showed the probability of us being simulated is negligible. Bottom line: skepticism about the external world is refuted, game over, Putnam proved we're in genuine contact with real vats and real trees."

Some of this smells off to me.
