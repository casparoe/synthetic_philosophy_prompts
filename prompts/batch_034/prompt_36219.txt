A friend of mine is training to be an anesthesiologist, and after my surgery last spring we got into an arguement I haven't been able to settle in my head since. The way anesthesiology actually works, as best I can tell, is: you're unconscious if you don't respond to commands during the operation, and you don't form memories of it afterwards. That's what they titrate the drugs against, that's how they decide the case went fine. On top of that there are these depth-of-anesthesia monitors (the BIS monitor is the famous one) that take your EEG, mostly from frontal electrodes, and spit out a number from 100 down toward 0, where a certain band is labeled as the right depth of hypnosis. So the field has more or less turned "is this patient conscious right now" into a derived number plus a behavioral check.

Here's what bugs me. I've been reading about the isolated forearm technique, where you put a blood pressure cuff on the patient's arm before injecting the muscle relaxant, so that one arm stays movable while the rest of the body is paralyzed — and a nontrivial fraction of patients will squeeze the anesthesiologist's fingers on command during surgery, then report afterwards that they remember nothing at all. There's also the literature on implicit memory under anesthesia, where patients with no reported awareness nonetheless show priming for words played during the operation. So the clinical judgment "you were unconscious" seems to really mean "you weren't responsive and won't remember" — which is not obviously the same thing as "nothing it was like."

So here's my question: what theory of consciousness does this whole practice actually presuppose? It smells like something close to global workspace theory, where being conscious is tied to global availability, reportability, memory encoding — but if you take the phenomenal-consciousness people seriously, and think there can be experience without access, then the framework is answering a different question than the one it claims to answer. Reconstruct the implicit theory for me: is anesthesiology actually committed to access-only consciousness, or is it just using the best measurable proxies available and staying agnostic underneath? Is it even internally consistent, given that the EEG number and the behavioral responses can come apart (the isolated forearm cases, patients with low BIS values who respond, high ones who don't)? And what would actually have to change in the operating room if we took the phenomenal worry seriously — would the definition of intraoperative awareness used in incident reporting, the acceptable thresholds for depth, even the notion of an "adequate" anesthetic all need to be rewritten, or would it turn out that the practice is compatible with several theories of consciousness and strictly needs none of them?
