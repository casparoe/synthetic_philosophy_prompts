I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and after some years of reporting on life-extension research, cryonics, palliative medicine, and the psychology of mortality, I've settled into something like the Epicurean position: that death is not bad for the person who dies, because death leaves no subject for the misfortune -- when it arrives, I am not there to be harmed, and a harm requires someone it is a harm to. Lucretius's addition of the symmetry with our prenatal nonexistence struck me early on as the final nail: I don't regard the time before my birth as a harm, so why the time after my death? I'd like a serious map of what would count against this view, and I'd like you to play it straight rather than protect me from my own conclusions.

Specifically: which arguments, cases, or considerations should genuinely move an Epicurean, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward? I know the deprivation account is the standard rival -- that death is bad because it cuts off goods one would otherwise have had -- so I want to know where it bites hardest, and also whether the timing puzzles cut against both sides or only against my side. But I'd also like the less familiar pressure points: whether the view can explain our concern with posthumous events like being betrayed or slandered after death; whether rational agents can coherently prefer more future goods without death counting as a loss; whether vagueness about when the subject goes out of existence embarrasses the view; and whether anything empirical could ever touch it -- if, say, work on the psychology of dread or the phenomenology of dying turned out to show that people are rational to fear annihilation itself rather than the process of dying, would that be a defeater or just an explanation of why the view is unpopular? I'd also like you to address the suspicion that the Epicurean wins by definition: that "harm requires an existing subject" is built in at the start, so no possible discovery could refute the position. If that's what's happening, say so plainly, and tell me whether it makes the view trivial, or whether a view of this kind is entitled to that insulation. And tell me which of these defeaters an actual committed Epicurean would concede as counting against them, as opposed to waving away. I'd find a demonstration that my view has no exposure at all less comforting than it sounds, since I suspect I hold it partly because I want it to be true.

Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables -- the way one would write a long, careful letter.
