The essay question was:

"Taurek argues that in a choice between saving one person and saving several, the fact that several would be saved is not by itself a reason to save the several. Explain his argument, and assess whether it succeeds."

And here's the student's answer:

"Taurek asks us to imagine we can save either David or five other people, but not both. Most philosophers before him assumed the right thing to do is obvious: save the five. Taurek's argument starts from a claim about what makes outcomes bad. An outcome can only be bad for someone — there is no further fact that the outcome itself is bad, over and above its being bad for particular people. So in the David case, if the six people each face the same loss (death), then no one is worse off than anyone else, and, crucially, there is no respect in which the outcome where five die is worse than the outcome where David dies. It would be worse for David, but not worse for the others, and there is no standpoint from which it is simply worse.

From here Taurek draws his conclusion: since neither outcome is worse, he is entitled to flip a coin between saving David and saving the five. The coin flip is justified directly by the fact that neither outcome is worse — once we see that, the rest follows. Each of the six ends up with a one-in-two chance of being saved, and this seems fair in a way that simply saving the greater number is not.

It's worth noting what Taurek is not saying. He is not saying we should be indifferent between saving and not saving, and he is not recommending selfishness — he would deny that. His point is about aggregation: the losses of the many cannot be summed into a bigger loss that then outweighs the loss of the one.

The argument depends on denying that we can compare the welfare of different people. If welfare cannot be compared across persons, then we literally cannot say that five deaths are worse than one, and Taurek's conclusion follows immediately. But this is a very strong premise. Most of us think we can make at least rough interpersonal comparisons — we say things like 'she's worse off than he is' all the time, and often we're right. If interpersonal comparisons are available, then the outcome in which five die is worse, and the coin flip is a mistake: it gives David a chance of being saved that he has no claim to, since saving him comes at the cost of five lives. So Taurek's argument succeeds only if we accept a controversial thesis about the incommensurability of welfare, and I don't think we should."
