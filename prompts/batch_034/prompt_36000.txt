Been reading LessWrong since the days of livejournal-adjacent rationality blogs, still work through the Sequences every couple years, so I'm comfortable with the "all models are wrong" talk. But this is a practical problem and I want it treated practically, so here's my situation.

I lead a small data science team at a fintech doing fraud and credit risk. Every few weeks we decide whether to ship a model or a feature, and the arguments are always the same: the pattern held beautifully in training and backtest, and then some pattern that "worked" turns out to have latched onto something that flips in the wild. Classic case from our own history: a fraud model that learned "transactions late at night are riskier," and the real story was that our call center was closed overnight so nobody could call to verify — the model was reading an artifact of our own operations. We caught that one. We didn't catch the credit model that used a merchant category code as a poverty proxy and quietly degraded for two years while the economy shifted underneath it. Nobody got hurt, but we lost alot of money and a chunk of internal trust.

The frame I want you to use, because it's clicked for me more than the usual "distribution shift" vocabulary: distribution shift is descriptive, it explains failures after the fact. What I need on a Thursday morning before a ship decision is closer to Goodman's new riddle of induction — given a regularity that held over all our data so far, which regularities are projectible, which are like "emeralds are green," and which are grue: defined so as to fit everything we've seen and flip the moment we deploy. Every spurious feature is a grue-predicate — perfectly consistent with the training set, false in the world. The one piece of real theory I want baked in is Goodman's own answer, roughly entrenchment: a hypothesis projects better if its predicate has a track record of surviving past projections. And the wrinkle I keep hitting, which I want the procedure to handle honestly, is that entrenchment earned on our own validation sets is circular — a feature that's "worked" in five of our backtests within the same regime has just been grue'd relative to our internal cutoff, not to the world. So the procedure has to be able to tell entrenched-because-the-world from entrenched-because-our-slice-of-it. If the answer is "you can't always tell," say so as an output of the procedure, not a disclaimer at the end.

Constraints, so you don't hand me something unusable: fraud labels take ~90 days to mature, so no quick experiments; we can rarely A/B test credit models for legal reasons; my analysts are sharp with stats but have zero philosophy and about an hour per model; the risk officer has to sign off, so outputs need to be explainable to a non-technical person.

What I want: a decision procedure my analysts can run in order — the questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns "not decidable, ship with monitoring plus human review" instead of pretending, and the points where it hands the call to me because it's a judgment call about values or appetite, not a fact. Then run it on three of our actual cases: (1) the late-night fraud feature, in the version where we suspected the call-center story but hadn't confirmed it; (2) a feature using the borrower's email domain (free provider vs. corporate), which correlates with default in our data and nobody can say why; (3) a claims-flagging model for our insurance arm, trained on two years of data from one country, being asked to deploy into a country we've never operated in. For each, give the honest output, and then tell me which of the three the procedure gets wrong or can't see at all, and what that says about whether entrenchment-as-track-record can actually be operationalized or is just a nice story we tell ourselves about why the last model failed.
