I am a novelist, four years into a book that follows an apprentice scribe in the decades around 1200 BCE, as the world of the Late Bronze Age palaces comes apart — the Hittite capital abandoned, Ugarit burned mid-correspondence, the Mycenaean palaces toppled, Egypt reduced to defending its own delta. My working conviction, and the novel's structural spine, is that this was a genuine collapse: a rapid, systemic, cascading failure in which interconnected states, each weakened by drought, famine, earthquake and unrest, dragged one another down — trade routes severed, tin and copper no longer moving, the palace scripts dying with the bureaucracies that needed them — rather than a slower reorganisation that hindsight dresses up as apocalypse. My editor, who has been reading the archaeology, keeps sending me essays arguing that at village level life went on much as before, that some regions were barely touched, and that collapse is a narrative category that suits novelists rather better than archaeologists. I want to take that seriously rather than defensively.

What would count against my view? Please give me the defeaters — the evidence, the arguments, the interpretive points — ranked from what would genuinely trouble it to what is merely awkward. I am particularly interested in three things: what a committed proponent of the systemic-collapse picture would concede as counting against them rather than explain away; whether the case depends so heavily on elites and their archives as to be circular, in the sense that we call it a collapse because the people who could write called it one; and whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — whether collapse has quietly come to mean nothing more than dramatic change, and whether that matters for someone trying to be historically honest in fiction. For my part, I would be most moved by evidence that ordinary households experienced continuity rather than catastrophe, since my protagonist is precisely nobody.

Do not soft-pedal: if you think my editor is right and the novel's premise is a period piece twice over, say so plainly. Please write no more than 600 words, in British English, and rank rather than merely list — I want to know what should actually move me, not an even-handed survey.
