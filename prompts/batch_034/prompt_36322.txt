so my department has a little reading group on scientific realism, and last week someone brought an argument that's been bugging me since. context: we'd been discussing structural realism, the view that what physics gives us knowledge of is the world's structure — the network of relations between things — and not the intrinsic natures of the things themselves. apparently this is meant to be the great middle path between plain scientific realism and instrumentalism: you get to keep the no-miracles argument for realism while dodging pessimistic induction, because structure is what survives theory change (the something like "kinetic theory got the relations right even if caloric got the natures wrong" story).

anyway here's the argument. the person swears he came up with it himself though he admitted it "probably reinvents something":

(1) Structural realism: all scientific knowledge of the unobservable world is knowledge of structure — how things are related — with nothing left over about the relata themselves.
(2) A structure is exhausted by a domain of objects together with the relations defined on it.
(3) Now take ANY collection of stuff you like with the same cardinality as the physical domain — say the natural numbers, or a set of idealized angels, whatever. Fix a bijection f from your collection to the physical domain.
(4) For every physical relation R, define a corresponding relation R' on your collection by: xR'y if and only if f(x)Rf(y). These defined relations automatically satisfy every structural claim physics makes.
(5) So a pile of numbers carrying these defined relations is structurally isomorphic to the physical world. Structure-wise it is a perfect replica — it satisfies all the same structural sentences.
(6) But then the thesis "we know the world's structure" cannot distinguish the actual physical world from the number-pile replica. If all we know is structure, and the replica has all the same structure, then everything we know is equally satisfied by the replica.
(7) So the structural realist's claim is either trivially true of anything whatever with the right cardinality, or else it covertly smuggles in non-structural facts (about which relations are "really there" vs merely definable, or about what the relata are). Either way the middle path collapses, and the no-miracles argument gets no distinctive structuralist reading.

I have this sinking feeling the argument is basically right and someone in the room should have conceded something big, but the structuralism fans in the group just kept saying "that's cheating, those aren't real relations" without explaining why that reply isn't itself a non-structural commitment. which is maybe fine? maybe the view can just bite the bullet and say knowledge of structure includes knowing the cardinality of the world and which relations are natural, and that's still a substantive middle path? I genuinely can't tell how much of the view survives.

so, help me out: walk through the steps and tell me which one you'd reject (or whether the argument is valid and the conclusion just has to be absorbed). if you reject the move in (3)-(4), say exactly what that costs — is the structuralist then committed to a primitive distinction between natural and merely-defined relations, and is that distinction itself expressible in structural terms or not? and if you'd rather give up something earlier, what does the view look like afterwards? I'd like to keep the middle-path story if possible but I want to know what I'm paying for it. I also vaguely remember something like this being raised against Russell ages ago, if that helps you see what the standard escape routes are supposed to be.
