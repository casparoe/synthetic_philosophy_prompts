I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on value theory, and last week a student brought in a passage from a trade book on decision-making that she'd found persuasive. The book summarizes the debate over incommensurable values roughly as follows: 'Philosophers say values are incommensurable when they cannot be measured on a common scale, the way different lengths can be converted into inches. If two options cannot be so measured, then neither is better than the other — there is simply no fact of the matter about which to choose. Ruth Chang's contribution was to add a third possibility besides better and worse: her notion of parity, where two options are on a par, meaning they are roughly equal in value. The upshot is that when facing a hard choice between, say, two careers, reason runs out. Whatever you pick, you pick arbitrarily, though afterwards you can invest your choice with meaning by coming to endorse it. This is why hard choices show that we are not really rational maximizers at all.'

My instinct is that this compresses a genuinely technical debate into something of a caricature, but I would rather not simply tell her so without being able to say exactly where. Would you go through it and separate what a theorist in this literature would accept, what is a tendentious but defensible gloss, and what is a straightforward misreading? I'd particularly like you to attend to three things: whether incommensurability implies that no betterness facts hold between the options, whether parity is or is not the same as approximate equality, and whether the conclusion that choices between such options are arbitrary actually follows from anything in the positions the passage describes — including whether the book's closing remark about rational maximizers is earned by what precedes it. Please be explicit about where a defender of the views being summarized would insist the passage has conflated two claims that the literature keeps apart.
