A couple of friends and I have been going down the decision theory rabbit hole lately, and we've run into a wall. Everyone online seems to argue about which decision theory "wins" on particular puzzle cases, but nobody ever states what "winning" is supposed to mean. Before we keep ranking causal decision theory, evidential decision theory and the upstart functional decision theory against each other, I want the spec they're being graded on. So: write the desiderata for an adequate decision theory -- the list of things it has to get right, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

To make it concrete, here are the puzzles we've mostly been fighting about. In counterfactual mugging, a perfect predictor flips a fair coin without showing you; if it came up tails, it threatens to destroy your £1000 unless it predicted in advance that you would pay £100 in the heads-world when asked later, and if it came up heads, it asks you for the £100. Paying loses you £100 in the world you're actually in, but you were more likely to receive the offer at all (on the tails branch) if you're the kind of agent who pays. In the XOR blackmail case, an honest predictor sends you a message if and only if either your house has termites (which would cost you £1,000,000 to fix) or it predicts you'll send it £1000, but not both. So if you pay, the message must mean the termites already exist; if you stiff it, receiving the message is evidence your house is fine. Evidential decision theory pays in both cases; causal decision theory refuses in both; the functional crowd claim to refuse the mugging payoff but somehow pay in counterfactual mugging. At least that's my possibly-garbled reconstruction, so correct me if I've mangled the setups.

Anyway, the desiderata. I'd want things like: not being money-pumped or exploited by agents who can model you; not turning news about the world into levers you pretend to control; doing the commonsense thing in medical-style cases where an action is merely correlated with bad news rather than a cause of it; and maybe one-shot versus repeated-encounter robustness, since a theory that only looks good in exotic single encounters with perfect predictors is suspicious. For each requirement, say what case motivates it and whether it's a genuine demand or just a prejudice of one camp (I suspect "don't pay the blackmail" and "the past is fixed" smuggle in causal-decision-theory intuitions as if they were neutral standards). Most importantly: flag any pair of desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied -- if any adequate theory must both resist exploitation and honour evidential correlations, that tension is the actual result I want to see. Then very briefly run the three theories against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard. Also, please answer in British English, and no reading lists or citations -- just the reasoning.
