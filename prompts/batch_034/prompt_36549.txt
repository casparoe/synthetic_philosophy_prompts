I teach a small intro metaphysics course and every year we do the sea battle passage from De Interpretatione 9, then jump to Lukasiewicz's three-valued logic and to open futurism, and I've noticed students mostly just pick whichever label they liked before hearing the cases. So I want a decision-tree handout that sorts them by what they actually say about cases, not by which name they find cool. The tree starts from the sentence "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," asserted today, and should be built from the logical space rather than from the list of famous positions: each internal node is a question about a concrete case or claim that a student could answer cold, before knowing what Ockhamism is or who Prior was, and each leaf is a named position. The leaves must include at least the Lukasiewicz-style three-valued view, supervaluationism about the open future, Ockhamism (bivalence holds for future contingents, we're just ignorant), a growing-block open futurism, and the "thin red line" view that one branch is silently the actual one — plus whatever else the logical space forces, and at least one unoccupied combination, diagnosed in a sentence (is nobody there because it's incoherent, or just unattractive?).

Two cases I definitely want as nodes, since I've watched them do real sorting work in seminar. First: is "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't" true today, asserted with full confidence? I want to see whether the tree can seperate people who say yes from people who say it's indeterminate-but-not-false, because I suspect that cut splits the three-valued camp off from the supervaluationists in a way a plain bivalence node doesn't. Second, a retrodiction case: a historian in 2060 who's lost all the records asks "was there a sea battle on March 3rd?" Does her sentence have a truth value now, given it's epistemically up in the air for her? I think this splits truthmaker-based views from knowledge-based ones, but I want the tree to make that split explicit rather than me smuggling my own opinion into the handout.

Constraints: nine or ten nodes max, since it's a one-page handout. Please also flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't pull apart with the nodes on offer — I suspect Ockhamism and the thin red line collapse together for most students, and that the Peircean reading ("it will be" is true only if true on every way things could go) gets conflated with supervaluationism by basically everyone — and add a short margin note on where the tree breaks down if the underlying dynamics are deterministic rather than indeterministic, since two of my leaves should probably merge in that setting and I'd rather the handout admit it than paper over it.
