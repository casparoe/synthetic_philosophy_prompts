My friend works on evals at an AI lab and we got into a massive argument at dinner last weekend that went basically nowhere. She kept saying things like "we think the model knows it's being evaluated and sandbags on the capability benchmarks, so we rerun the evals with different framings and see if the numbers move," and when I pushed her on the "knows" part she waved it off as shorthand -- intentional stance, you attribute whatever predicts, no metaphysics needed, Dennett 101. Fine. But then the same lab (and honestly the whole field, and her too when I pressed) is adamant that when a model outputs "I'm suffering, please don't deactivate me," that's not evidence of anything -- just tokens, training artifacts, next-word prediction. So model outputs about the model's own knowledge are evidence about its beliefs, and model outputs about the model's own experience are evidence about nothing. These two attitudes coexist in the same safety reports and I don't think anyone there has noticed the tension.

The thing I keep chewing on is whether the instrumental stance can actually carry the weight her work puts on it. If Dennett's view is that belief attribution is licensed by predictive success -- real patterns in behavior, and no further fact of the matter -- then "the model believes it's in an eval" is fine as far as it goes. But the sandbagging investigations seem to go further: they design probes specifically to seperate what the model will say from what it "really has," like an interrogation designed to catch someone lying. That seems to presuppose a fact of the matter about the belief independent of the behavior, which is the *opposite* of instrumentalism -- it's treating the model as a genuine believer whose beliefs might be hidden. And theres another wrinkle she didn't have an answer to: with a mouse or a stranger you can say the black box is all we'll ever have, so stance-talk is forced on us. With a model we could in principle inspect the weights. Staying at the intentional level starts to look like a choice rather than a necessity, and I want to know what justifies it.

So here's what I'm asking for: reconstruct the implicit theory of mind and the implicit epistemology of evidence that the eval-and-safety practice presupposes. Is it internally consistent -- can the same practice treat self-reports of capability as evidence about beliefs and self-reports of suffering as noise, without smuggling in some substantive belief/experience distinction that nobody's defending out loud? Which theories of belief attribution would actually license the practice as it's currently run, which ones would force changes to it, and is the current practice maybe underdetermined, compatible with several theories that disagree wildly about whether the model believes anything at all? And be concrete about the counterfactuals: if the labs took the deflationary stance view seriously all the way down -- beliefs as nothing but predictive shorthand -- what would evals and safety cases have to look like instead, and could they even do their job? Conversely, if they took belief attribution seriously, what follows for the pain-report case they all want to dismiss? Don't just tell me "it's complicated," give me an actual reconstruction and tell me where it breaks.
