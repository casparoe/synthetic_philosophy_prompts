I teach an undergraduate course that ends with a fortnight on the old question of whether culture can be a cause, and every year the seminar dissolves into exactly the same impasse, which has me wondering whether the impasse is the real subject. The case everyone keeps returning to is Weber's thesis that certain Protestant doctrines -- predestination, the calling, the anxiety about one's election -- produced an inner-worldly asceticism that channelled effort into labour and accumulation and helped make capitalism psychologically possible. The students split in a way I can predict by week three. One side insists this is a causal claim like any other: there is a measurable correlate, there is a plausible mechanism running through self-understanding, and if the numbers hold up after you control for literacy, urbanisation and access to trade routes, culture earned its place in the regression. The other side insists the whole enterprise is confused: values are not the kind of thing that can nudge an economy the way a harvest failure can, or if they can, they were themselves caused by material conditions and so are at best a way station in the explanation rather than its engine; and in any case the correlate is a mess, since plenty of Catholic and Confucian and Jewish commercial communities behaved in the relevant ways without the doctrine, and Protestant regions were often ahead on literacy for reasons having nothing to do with soteriology. What strikes me is that the two sides never actually disagree about any historical fact. They disagree about what kind of thing an explanation is allowed to cite.

My own hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the stalled debate is not empirical at all but turns on an unexamined choice: either you think explanations must cite only state variables of the world, in which case a value counts only insofar as it is measurable as, say, a savings rate, and the cultural vocabulary is decoration; or you think explanations of human action are constitutively interpretive, in which case citing what people thought their labour meant is not a proxy for some deeper cause but is the cause, and demanding that it be reduced to something else is a category error. If that is right, then the demand for better identification -- the natural-experiment studies comparing descendants of migrants from different religious traditions and so on -- cannot settle it, because each side will read those results as evidence for its own conception of what is being identified. But I am not certain, because there is a third possibility: the dispute is verbal, resting on two senses of 'cause' -- 'whatever makes a difference to the outcome' versus 'whatever initiates a difference' -- which ought to be dissolved by a distinction rather than won by either side.

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Weber. State your diagnosis in the first paragraph, then defend it against the alternatives by showing what each side's favourite move actually commits them to. Tell me whether, on your diagnosis, anything would count as progress: a sharpened definition of cultural causation, a class of studies that would surprise both sides, a distinction that splits the question into two answerable ones -- or whether this is a case where the disagreement is in good health precisely because it is a standing dispute over the aim of social explanation, and what I should teach my students is the disagreement itself. If it helps you place me: I have no patience for the sociological literature that settles Weber by counting books on thrift, and equally none for the econometric refutations that control for every channel through which the culture could plausibly have acted and then announce they have found nothing. Please write in continuous prose only -- no headings, no bullet points, no tables, nothing in bold -- as though you were writing me a long and rather serious letter.
