ok so, novelist here, and i'm stuck on something that turns out to be a philosophy problem wearing a plot problem's clothes. My next book is narrated by a coroner in a decaying coastal town, and the whole novel turns on whether she can bring herself to sign a death certificate for something that might not have ever been alive, or might still be. For it to work, the thing cant just be mysterious — the coroner (and the reader) needs to be able to fully understand what the thing is and what it does, and STILL not know if it counts as alive. The uncertainty has to come from the concept, not from ignorance of the facts. So here's what I want.

Construct me the scenario. One paragraph, written in a literary register I could basically drop into the book, not textbook voice. It has to meet these specs:

1. Once all the facts about the thing are on the table, at least two mainstream definitions of life give opposite verdicts. Not "unclear what it does" — full information, still a split. I'm imagining the NASA-flavored definition (self-sustaining chemistry plus the capacity for Darwinian evolution) on one side and an autopoiesis-style definition (a system that produces and maintains its own boundary and its own parts) on the other, but if you have a better pairing, go with yours — just tell me what you're using.
2. It has to split along an interesting line, not the tired ones. No viruses, no prions, no fire, no crystals, no mules or worker bees, no computer programs, and none of that Gaia move where the whole biosphere gets to be the organism. Every essay on this goes to one of those and my eyes glaze over.
3. It has to be placeable in a literary novel set roughly now — no space opera, no physics we don't have. And the coroner has to plausibly be the person who has to deal with it, so give it some connection to remains, decay, bodies, or an institution like a hospital or a morgue.

Then after the paragraph, do the walkthrough for me as if I'm smart but not a biologist. State each definition, give its verdict on your thing, and explain the verdict in terms of the specific features you built into the case — not in generalities about how definitions differ. Then be honest with me about the engineering: which single feature of the scenario is doing all the work, what happens to the split if that feature gets removed (does it collapse back into a boring virus-style borderline case, or does something else hold it apart), and how a defender of the definition that comes out "dead" would say I rigged it.

Last thing, and this one matters: tell me which verdict the coroner should come to in the end — not because it's the philosophically right answer, but because of what each choice does to the novel. Some context that might help: she's a narrator who needs the dead to stay dead, professionally and maybe personally, and I haven't decided whether that need is her flaw or her spine. If you can tell me what a death certificate means when it's signed on something the definitions can't agree on, that's the sentence I actually need for the book.
