I work as a program officer at a mid-sized foundation that funds global health and development interventions, and part of my job is defending the cost-effectiveness model we use to compare grants — a model that, like most, aggregates wellbeing gains across people into a single number and is therefore implicitly totalist about population. A trustee recently pressed me on the repugnant conclusion: if our framework is willing to trade one person's large benefit for many people's tiny benefits, and counts the existence of additional barely-positive lives as a gain, doesn't it endorse the world of trillions of barely-worth-living lives that Derek Parfit made famous? I gave the standard deflections, but one thing kept nagging at me, and it has since grown into what I believe is a genuine research question. When people imagine "a life barely worth living," they seem to picture two quite different things: either a life with real highs and real lows whose total barely clears zero, or a life that is uniformly drab, every moment only marginally above neutral. The formal argument is indifferent between these — the total is the same — but my intuitions, and I suspect the trustee's, are not: adding lives of the first kind to the world seems defensible in a way that adding lives of the second kind does not. So here is my half-formed idea: the repugnancy of the repugnant conclusion may be partly an artifact of an equivocation, and a population axiology that is sensitive to the shape of wellbeing within a life — its distribution over time, its variance, the presence or absence of peaks — rather than only its total might dissolve some of the repugnance, or relocate it in instructive ways.

I have a realistic shot at three months of part-time research leave to develop this, and I need a proposal of roughly 1,500 words with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "The question, stated precisely" — one paragraph.
2. "Why the existing debate leaves it open" — about 350 words. Engage the familiar escape routes from the repugnant conclusion — totalism's grim acceptance, averagism, person-affecting views, critical-level and lexical-threshold views, and the impossibility results suggesting that the intuitions behind them cannot all be retained — described in your own terms rather than cited, and explain why none of them isolates the within-life distribution of wellbeing as a variable.
3. "Approach" — about 450 words, including what I would actually do in each of the three months: what would have to be formalized first, what the candidate axiologies look like, and which impossibility-style constraints the shape-sensitive view would have to be tested against.
4. "What a result would look like" — about 250 words, covering both the success case (a principled shape-sensitive axiology that avoids the repugnant conclusion without violating independently compelling adequacy conditions) and the instructive failure case (a new repugnant conclusion of the theory's own, e.g., a world of a few ecstatic spikes).
5. "Failure conditions and scope" — about 200 words, including an honest assessment of whether three months part-time is enough and what would have to be cut.
6. "Why this matters for grantmaking" — about 150 words, connecting the question to real decisions about whether to fund many small-benefit interventions rather than a few large-benefit ones, written so a non-philosopher on a grant committee would find it compelling.

Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply references myself. The proposal should argue both that the question is genuinely open and that the within-life distribution of wellbeing is the right angle on it — not just one more escape hatch alongside the familiar ones — and it should be candid about the strongest objection: that shape-sensitivity is ad hoc unless it can be motivated independently of its convenient conclusions.
