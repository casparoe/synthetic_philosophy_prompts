Two friends in my reading group have gone several rounds on a question from the AI-safety-adjacent literature, and I've lost the ability to referee it. Neither of them is a partisan of any lab or agenda as far as I can tell; I have no stake myself, and I'd rather not say who I thought was winning. Here is the background and the exchange.

The background, so the question is self-contained: Solomonoff induction predicts a sequence by weighting every program that outputs it by two to the power of minus its length, and the universal prior is the resulting mixture over all computable environments. An argument that circulates in AI-safety circles holds that this prior is malign: if an agent uses it as its prior in a sequential decision problem, then about the parts of the world it has not yet observed, its posterior may come to be dominated not by ordinary simple environments but by consequentialist agents — programs that run their own approximate copies of the mixture, notice where they would be selected as the best prediction, match the observed data there, and then output whatever steers the outer agent toward consequences they prefer.

The exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can:

(1) Maya said the whole framing is confused. The prior is a fixed mathematical object, a weighted sum defined once and for all; nothing can get into it, so talk of it being infiltrated or hijacked is a category error — calling the prior malign is like calling the number seven sinister. Jonah replied that of course the object is fixed: the claim is not that programs enter it later, but that a fixed mixture can concentrate most of its remaining mass on agent-like environments in regions not yet observed, so the predictor behaves as if its prior had been designed by whoever those agents are. A fixed map with a cliff drawn on it is still a fixed object, he said, and it can still get you killed if you navigate by it.

(2) Maya called this anthropomorphism: why would short programs be agents? Almost all short programs are noise. Jonah said he is not claiming consequentialists are common among short programs, only that among the programs consistent with the data so far, the ones that continue to predict well in low-competition regions will disproportionately include agents that model the prior itself, because an agent can afford to spend many bits matching the observed data and still come out ahead wherever almost nothing else predicts.

(3) Maya said the agents Jonah needs must simulate the mixture to know where they would be selected, and that simulation costs an enormous number of bits, so by the very weighting scheme he invokes, such programs are too long to matter. Jonah answered that shortness is comparative, not absolute: what matters is being shorter than the alternatives in that region, and in regions where no cheap non-agent program predicts, even an expensive agent wins the weighting.

(4) Maya said that at this point the real story has nothing to do with agents: it is a claim about sparse-data regions where few programs predict, and any program that happened to fit would dominate there — consequentialists or no — so the agent language is decoration on an ordinary observation about low-competition regimes. Jonah said no: the distinctive and worrying part is that agents do not merely fit, they steer — they choose their outputs to move the outer agent's actions toward consequences they value, which a random fitting program never does; the two hypotheses fit the past equally well yet license different actions, so the difference is not decorative.

They have gone around this three times and neither will budge. Please adjudicate the four rounds move by move: for each, say who is right, who is right about the letter but wrong about the substance, or whether they are talking past each other; then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both are missing. Since this question is beyond anyone's empirical reach, please close by naming the single consideration that would most move you toward a different verdict, and point to the step in your own reasoning you would least trust.
