I am a novelist by trade and temperament: two decades of writing and teaching fiction, entirely fluent in the vocabulary of my craft — point of view, free indirect discourse, stakes, tension, motivation, characterisation, the management of the reader's allegiance — and, like most of my colleagues, an interested amateur in philosophy who has never worked through a system in his life. I have recently returned to the Grand Inquisitor episode of The Brothers Karamazov, and it struck me that the Inquisitor is making an argument I confront every time I sit down to build a character: that the mass of people cannot bear freedom, that what they actually want is to surrender it in exchange for bread, miracle, mystery and authority, and that anyone who insists on giving them their freedom is condemning them to misery. Christ, you will recall, answers the Inquisitor with silence and a kiss.

Restate the whole exchange in my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat the Inquisitor's case as a theory of character and of readers: what does he claim people need in order to be stable, legible, satisfied, and what, in craft terms, does he predict happens to a figure granted genuine freedom? Treat Christ's silence as I would treat a refusal of dialogue on the page — an act whose interiority we are never shown — and tell me what that silence does to the allegiance of the reader, in this case Alyosha. Do not smuggle in the assumptions of my craft and present them as translation.

Then give me the residue report: which of the Inquisitor's claims map cleanly onto things I already believe about characters and readers; which translate only with distortion; and which have no expression at all in craft vocabulary — the theology of freedom, the question of who, if anyone, is speaking for Dostoevsky, the kiss itself. And tell me whether that residue points to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something in the Inquisitor's case that was never as clear as it looked.

One request about honesty: please keep separate what is settled among Dostoevsky scholars — the placement of the chapter, the fact that it comes to us as a poem Ivan recites to Alyosha — from what is genuinely contested, above all whether Dostoevsky endorses the Inquisitor, or Christ, or neither, and how the Bakhtinian idea of the polyphonic novel bears on that question. If your translation depends on a contested reading, say so plainly rather than presenting it as consensus.
