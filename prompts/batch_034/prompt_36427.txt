In my philosophy of mind seminar we've been discussing Dennett's idea that the self is like a center of gravity. The picture, as I understand it: a center of gravity is not an extra piece of matter hidden inside an object; it's an abstract point we posit because it lets us predict and explain — how the object will tip, balance, or fall — and we treat it as entirely real for all scientific purposes, even though there's nothing at that exact location you could point to. Likewise, the argument goes, the self is not an extra inner something over and above the body; it's an abstract point we posit to organize and predict a person's behavior and speech, including their reports about their own inner life. So far, so clear. The trouble is what my seminar group does with it, because two of them draw what look like opposite conclusions from the same analogy, and I can't tell who's cheating.

One classmate says the analogy shows that the self is fully real: if a center of gravity is real enough for physics, then being an abstract organizing point rather than a bit of stuff is no demotion, and anyone who concludes that "the self doesn't exist" is making the same mistake as someone who concludes that "centers of gravity don't exist." Our teaching assistant pushes the other way: the analogy shows the self is a theoretical fiction, a bookkeeping device, and therefore personal identity questions about survival, or about who is really suffering, need to be rephrased as questions about continuities in the underlying narrative-and-behavioral pattern — the "I" in my reports refers to a useful posit, not to a sufferer in any further sense. They also disagree about responsibility: the first treats the analogy as securing ordinary accountability (there really is someone answerable, namely the abstractum), the second as deflating it (there's no deep fact about who did it beyond pattern continuity).

Can you audit the analogy so I can referee this? Give me the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the mass distribution, the calculations the physicists do, the object's tipping and balancing, the fact that the center of gravity's location can be defined precisely — and then say where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for this dispute from the ones that don't. In particular, I want to know whether the fact that centers of gravity don't have a point of view, don't care about anything, and are posited entirely from the outside — whereas a self is at least partly posited by the system itself, which goes around saying "I" — matters here or is a distraction. And most importantly: does either classmate's conclusion actually fall out of the analogy, or is the analogy carrying weight in both directions that it can't support? If one of them is closer to the truth, say which and why; and if the analogy is doing real work only for some narrower conclusion, tell me what that narrower conclusion is.
