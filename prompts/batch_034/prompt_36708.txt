I've never studied philosophy formally, but I've spent a lot of time thinking on my own about the ethics of war, and I've ended up with a set of gut verdicts about who it is permissible to kill in war that I can't make sense of as a whole. I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one.

I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of war, and I'd ask you to think carefully and work through this systematically — perhaps starting with some brainstorming about the shape of the problem before you commit to any answer. Please don't just tell me my verdicts are inconsistent; I already suspect some of them are. I want to know which principle or principles best capture the pattern, what those principles say about cases I haven't thought of, and honestly, which of my verdicts I probably need to give up.

Here are my verdicts, stated as plainly as I can:

1. A conscripted eighteen-year-old on the clearly unjust side of a war of aggression, who was told he'd be imprisoned if he refused to serve, and who mostly wants to go home: killing him in battle seems wrong, or at least it seems he isn't to blame. It feels closer to killing a hostage than killing a murderer.

2. A volunteer professional soldier on the clearly just side, bravely defending his country from invasion: it still seems permissible for the invader's soldiers to shoot him in combat. He's a legitimate target even though his cause is just. (This is the one I have the most trouble squaring with #1.)

3. A mercenary hired by the just side, fighting only for money and caring nothing about the cause: also a legitimate target, and somehow it feels even less bad to kill him than to kill the patriotic defender in #2.

4. A soldier on the unjust side who genuinely believes his country's propaganda — he volunteered because he sincerely thinks the war is defensive, and his belief isn't crazy given what his government and press told him: he still seems killable in combat, but it feels like the person actually at fault is someone back in the capital, not him.

5. A civilian worker in a munitions factory on the unjust side: bombing the factory at night when it's empty seems fine, but deliberately timing the strike to kill the workers does not, even though the workers are arguably contributing more to the unjust war than the conscript in #1.

6. A civilian columnist who agitated for the unjust war and continues to cheer it on, and who bears more responsibility for it than almost any individual soldier: still not a legitimate target.

7. A frontline soldier on the unjust side who deliberately aims to miss: he's doing the minimum his conscience allows while avoiding prison, and I think killing him would be wrong in a way that killing his squadmate who aims to kill isn't.

8. The general who planned the unjust invasion: killable if he can be targeted, obviously — but I notice I'd say that even if he never personally fires a shot, which seems to make "being a combatant" the wrong basis for my judgments.

My tentative thought is that what matters is something like how responsible a person is for the unjust threat they pose, and that "wearing a uniform" is just a rough proxy for that — a proxy that misfires in cases #1, #5, and #6. But I can see this leads somewhere I'm not comfortable with: if the conscript in #1 poses little culpable threat and the cheerleading civilian in #6 poses more, why is the conscript a legitimate target and the civilian not? Maybe there's a difference between being liable to be killed and being culpable that I'm not tracking. Or maybe my attachment to the idea that all soldiers are equal targets is just inertia.

So my questions are: Is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures most or all of my verdicts? If so, please state it as precisely as you can, with the case that motivates each part of it. If not, which of my verdicts should go, and why those? And please tell me what the winning principle predicts about two cases I haven't considered: a civilian contractor remotely piloting a drone for the unjust side from a facility in a neutral country, and a soldier on the just side who uses prohibited weapons, making his own side's conduct unjust even though the cause is just. I'd rather have an answer that overturns one of my verdicts with a clear argument than one that keeps them all by being vague.
