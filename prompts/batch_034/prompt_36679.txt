I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and my dissertation keeps circling the separateness of persons objection without ever landing on it. My advisor's line is that the objection is a family of complaints wearing one coat, and that most responses in the literature answer only one member of the family. I half believe her, but I also can't find anywhere that just lays out what the family members are. So before I read yet another paper I want the spec first: what does any adequate theory of moral aggregation have to deliver in order to count as respecting the separateness of persons? For my purposes the theory needs to be good enough for institutional choice -- how a state may trade off harms and benefits across its citizens -- not necessarily for private ethics, though say so if you think that restriction is doing suspicious work.

Some of the cases I'd want on the list, though I'm sure I'm missing some: why I can accept a headache now to avoid my own agony later but can't impose your headache to relieve my agony (the intra- vs interpersonal tradeoff asymmetry); one person's torture versus tiny benefits spread over millions (transplant-adjacent but the budget version: a public health program that foreseeably kills a few to spare many small burdens); the utility monster; the fact that we're allowed to favor our kids over strangers, which pure aggregation seems to deny; whether aggregation is fine among strangers but not when a single person's life gets used up, or whether that's just a threshold effect and not a difference in kind; and whether there's any real distinction left between separateness of persons and the separateness of agents that anyone has to respect (states aggregate the choices of citizens all the time and nobody objects on these grounds). For each requirement I want the case or consideration that motivates it, and I want you to mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just preferences of the anti-aggregation camp dressed up as constraints -- I suspect "no aggregation ever" and "aggregation is fine but not across life prospects" get conflated constantly.

The part I most want to see: which pairs of requirements, if any, can't be jointly satisfied, because if the list itself is unsatisfiable that's actually my thesis chapter and not a problem. And which single requirement knocks out the most candidates -- my bet is it's something about interpersonal compensation, the idea that gains to one person can't count as compensating losses to another, and that it kills not just utilitarianism but prioritarianism too, which is awkward for people who present prioritarianism as the separateness-respecting reform of utilitarianism. At the end, briefly, score utilitarianism with diminishing marginal utility, prioritarianism, Rawlsian maximin, and Scanlon-style contractualism against your own list -- but keep that part short, the scoring is the least interesting bit and I can do it myself; the criteria are where the thinking is. A table is fine if it helps.
