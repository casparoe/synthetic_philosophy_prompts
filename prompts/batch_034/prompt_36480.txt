So I help run a small interdisciplinary seminar -- its sort of a evolution-meets-economics-meets-philosophy thing, third year undergrads, about 16 of them, and I have one 80-minute session to fill on signaling. The theme for the session is basically the handicap principle / costly signaling: the idea that signals are credible *because* they're costly, and that a cheap-to-fake signal is worthless. Peacock tails, stotting gazelles, expensive degrees, long engagement rings, that whole family of ideas.

I want a game, not a lecture. The dream is that the students discover the core idea themselves before I ever name it. Something like: round one where a signal is free to send, so everyone sends it, and trust in the signal collapses in front of their eyes. Then a second round where the signal costs something, and the cost bites differently depending on who you are (the honest/strong/high type pays less to send it than the low type does, or can afford more of it), and suddenly the signal means something again. And ideally a third condition where the cost is the same for everyone -- pure burned money -- so they can fight about whether that kind of cost is doing real work or is just waste, which is the philosophically juicy part (it gets at the intuition that peacock tails are "wasteful" and whether that waste is load-bearing).

Constraints and what I need from you:

- 16ish students, 80 minutes total including debrief, no real money (we use points or tokens or candy, something I can hand out), minimal prep and materials. It has to be runnable by me plus one other TA, and it should work even if a couple students are checked out.
- Payoff scheme needs to be concrete: actual point numbers, what a high type and a low type each walk away with under each choice, so the incentives are real rather than vibes. Please actually check your own numbers -- trace through what a rational-but-lazy student would do at each decision point and confirm they'd want to play along rather than troll. Trolling is a real risk with this crowd.
- A timeline, minute by minute, with the rules for each round written out as I'd read them aloud.
- A debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the concepts (cheap talk, pooling vs separating, why cost is what anchors credibility), and that finally names the handicap principle and connects it to the biology and the economics. I want them to have the "oh THAT'S why the tail is huge" moment.
- An honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- students colluding, someone spite-sending signals to break the game, the numbers being confusing, whatever you can foresee -- with fixes.
- And a frank note on what the game *cannot* reproduce about real costly signaling. My worry is that in the game, the cost structure that makes the signal honest is stipulated by me, the designer -- whereas in nature nobody stipulates it, it's just the equilibrium that got selected. I'd like you to think about whether that gap matters and what I have to supply by talking that the game itself never shows.

You're basically a game designer who also has a serious background in behavioral ecology and experimental economics -- someone who's run classroom games with real incentives before and knows where they fall apart. Before you commit to a design, brainstorm 2-3 candidate game concepts briefly (signal markets, predator-prey role play, etc.), tell me which one you're picking and why the others are worse, then give me the full design for the winner. Think carefully about the incentive structure at every step -- the whole thing dies if the students don't actually feel the collapse of cheap talk in round one, so that round especially has to be airtight. Don't water it down, and don't hide the hard parts.
