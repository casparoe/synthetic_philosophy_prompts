I've been hanging around LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since back when the Agent Foundations agenda was the big thing everyone argued about, and one issue I keep circling back to is what Paul Christiano called inaccessible information. As I understand it, the idea is this: a capable model will learn to use lots of information about the world that we humans can't directly observe or verify ourselves — a detailed implicit model of a particular person's future behavior, or of the long-run consequences of some intervention, say — and since we can only train and test on what we ourselves can check, the model ends up knowing a great deal more than we can extract from it. The scary version is that we'd like our AI to act on its high-quality knowledge of things we can't see (that's much of the point of having one), but ordinary training on accessible examples doesn't obviously teach it to report or act on the inaccessible part correctly rather than just plausibly.

Suppose a well-funded alignment lab has to choose which of the following research directions to bet on, with the goal of eventually making inaccessible information accessible enough that we can safely rely on models acting on it. The candidates I have in mind are:

1. Amplification-style setups — iteratively decompose tasks so that humans, recursively consulted, can in principle check claims they couldn't check unaided;
2. Debate-style setups — two models argue opposing positions in front of a human judge, with the hope that cross-examination surfaces the inaccessible part;
3. Mechanistic interpretability — open up the model and try to read the inaccessible information directly out of its internals;
4. Ontology identification — figure out which structures in the model's internal world-model correspond to the human concepts we actually care about, so that "what the model believes about X" becomes stateable in our terms;
5. Accepting the gap — don't try to extract inaccessible information at all; train honesty on what's checkable, restrict the model to provably defensible claims, and eat the performance loss.

Please rank these five by how promising you find them for this purpose, assigning each a tier (something like "worth a lab's core effort" / "worth a dedicated side team" / "worth one person monitoring the literature") or a rough probability that it works, and give a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. I'd especially appreciate it if you said something about how the approaches interact — e.g. whether interpretability and ontology identification are really separable bets, or whether amplification and debate are better seen as one family.

Lead with the bottom line: state your full ranking in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
