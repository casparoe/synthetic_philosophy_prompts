A friend in my historiography seminar has been pressing an argument on me that historical explanation is incapable of doing what it claims to do, and the more I examine it, the less able I am to identify the defective step without sacrificing something I want to keep.

His argument runs as follows. (1) Hindsight bias is a robust experimental finding: when people are told that a particular outcome occurred, they judge that outcome as having been substantially more likely in advance than people without that knowledge do, and they even misremember their own prior predictions as having been closer to what happened. (2) To explain a historical outcome is to show that it was to be expected — that, given the antecedent conditions, it was more probable than its alternatives; an account that leaves the outcome looking like a fluke has not explained it. (3) Every historian judges with outcome knowledge, and the bias is stubborn: being told to ignore what one knows does not restore foresight. (4) A judgment of probability formed under the influence of the very outcome being assessed cannot serve as evidence that the outcome was probable in advance — that would be bootstrapping. (5) Therefore no historian can ever show that an outcome was to be expected. (6) So no historical explanation succeeds, and the sense we have that, say, the July Crisis of 1914 made a general European war likely is an artifact of knowing how it came out. His conclusion: for all anyone can legitimately claim, the past was as open as it looked from the inside, and historians' confidence in "overdetermined" outcomes is confected after the fact.

To make this vivid, he proposes what he calls an audit test. Take a serious historical explanation, redact the outcome, and hand it to a reader who knows the period but not the ending. If the reader cannot reconstruct what happened from the account, then the account was not doing the work it pretended to; it was hindsight narrating itself. He claims every actual monograph fails this test.

Here is what bothers me. Substitute "geologist" for "historian" and "the end-Cretaceous extinction" for "the July Crisis," and every premise still seems to hold: geologists reason retrospectively, know the outcome, would fail the audit test (no one reading a redacted stratigraphic column predicts an asteroid impact), and yet paleobiology plainly explains that extinction. The same substitution works for accident investigation, forensics, and evolutionary biology generally. So the argument proves far too much — unless he simply embraces that conclusion too, which, worryingly, he shows some signs of doing.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep (1) and (5); the first looks like experimental fact and the second like its honest consequence. My suspicion is that the trouble lies in the neighborhood of (2), (4), or the audit test itself — perhaps an equivocation on "to be expected," or an illicit demand that explanation amount to prediction — but I cannot tell which. Please name the step you would give up, explain why it looked fine in the first place, and price the alternatives; if you think the argument is in fact valid and the geology conclusion is simply true, say so and tell me what that costs.
