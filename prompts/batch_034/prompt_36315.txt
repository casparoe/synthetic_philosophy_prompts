I'm an MA student writing a term paper of roughly 7,000 words, due at the end of a one-semester seminar in metaphysics, and I need to turn my half-formed idea into a proper proposal for my supervisor by next week. The idea concerns the statue and the clay. It has struck me that nearly all the standard responses to the puzzle of material constitution — denying that one of the coincident objects really exists, insisting that the statue and the clay differ in their sortal-relative persistence conditions or their modal properties, treating identity as relative to a sortal, or deflating the whole thing by saying we simply count objects under different concepts — are formulated as answers to a purely synchronic question: how can two things share all their matter and most of their properties at a moment without being identical? My suspicion is that the synchronic framing flatters these views, and that their real costs only show up diachronically: if the sculptor chips away at the statue and replaces the clay piece by piece, the views come apart in what they say about when, if ever, the statue ceases and the clay survives, or whether both survive under different descriptions, and the ordinary judgements we're inclined to make about such gradual replacement seem to pull against each of the standard views in different ways. I want the paper to be a test case: not "which solution to the coincidence puzzle is right?", but "which solutions survive being stretched over time?"

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing positions leave it open (in particular, why "statue" being a phase sortal of "lump of clay" doesn't by itself tell us what happens during replacement, and why the four-dimensionalist's appeal to temporal parts may not dissolve the diachronic tension the way it dissolves the synchronic one), and lay out the method — what I would actually do, month by month over the semester, including which replacement scenarios I would construct and how I would keep them from collapsing into the familiar Ship of Theseus literature rather than illuminating the constitution debate. It should say what a successful result would look like — ideally a ranked verdict on the standard views — and, just as importantly, what would count as the project failing: for instance, if the diachronic cases turn out to reproduce the synchronic disagreement exactly, with no new discriminating power. Please also give an honest assessment of whether this is too big for one semester and, if so, which part of it you would carve off as the term paper proper.
