So I'm teaching intro philosophy and putting together a discussion unit on Kavka's toxin puzzle. The original has a billionaire with a brain scanner, and my students immediately file it under sci-fi and stop caring. Build me a realistic version: a scenario where someone gets a genuine, non-trivial benefit now for forming an intention that will be pointless by the time they'd have to act on it — but no mind-reading machines and no perfect lie detecters; the thing that verifies the intention has to be an ordinary human's fallible read of you (a handshake, a solemn promise in front of witnesses, whatever). One paragraph, and it can't secretly be a normal promise case — ordinary promise-keeping has a point at execution time (reputation, the promisee relying on you), and that crutch is exactly what dissolves the puzzle, so design around it instead of stipulating it away with something cheap like "and then everyone forgets". After the scenario, show it's genuinely a toxin case: say which structural feature is doing the work (the reward tracks the intention, not the act), then walk through the two standard diagnoses — the "you can't just decide to intend" response, and the Bratman-style line that rationality applies to intention-action clusters rather than to slices — and say whether your case survives each one. Finish by telling me honestly where the case is weakest and how a skeptic would attack it.
