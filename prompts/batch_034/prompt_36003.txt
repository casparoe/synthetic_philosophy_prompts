I'm taking a philosophy of action course this term, and we just did Buridan's ass — the donkey that starves between two identical haystacks because it has no reason to go left rather than right. Our professor mentioned in passing that real institutions face this situation all the time and just solve it by randomizing, and now I keep seeing it everywhere. When an election ends in an exact tie, a bunch of US states literally settle it with a coin flip or by drawing names out of a container; FIFA breaks tied World Cup groups by drawing lots; jury pools, audits, and lots of lottery-based allocations work the same way even when you could imagine criteria for picking instead. But here's what bugs me: the very same legal culture treats "arbitrary" as an accusation. Agency decisions can be overturned for being arbitrary and capricious, and calling a judge arbitrary is about the worst thing you can say about them. So the same system that condemns arbitrariness is perfectly happy to let a coin decide who sits in a legislature.

What I want to figure out is what view of choice and reasons these randomization practices actually presuppose. One option is that they assume genuine indifference: when reasons run out, the options really are equally good, so flipping a coin costs nothing and picking either way is fully rational. Another option is that the coin flip manufactures a reason — once it lands heads, "the coin came up heads" is now a reason to take the heads option. But if it's the second one, is that a reason in the same sense that "this candidate got more votes" is a reason to seat them, or is it some weird second-order thing that just gets to piggyback on the structure of choice without being a real reason at all? Like, it explains the choice without really justifying it, or maybe it justifies it in a totally different way than normal reasons do.

So basically: reconstruct the implicit theory of rational choice behind institutional tie-breaking for me. Say whether the coin flip strictly needs one particular theory (the indifference one, the manufactured-reason one, or something else), or whether several theories of choice could all license the same practice — i.e., maybe the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Also, can it genuinely be reconciled with the legal system's hostility to arbitrariness, or is there a hidden distinction doing the work, like arbitrariness in how you pick versus arbitrariness in how you justify the pick? And finally, what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a strict "every choice needs a reason" principle — would tied elections just become undecidable, actual Buridan cases where the seat stays empty? Sorry if this is a bit rambly, I've been going back and forth with a friend about it for a week and we keep talking past each other.
