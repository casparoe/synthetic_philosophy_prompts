I record a small philosophy podcast out of my university's grad common room — 35 minutes an episode, and the listeners are mostly people who've actually read Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature, so I don't need the guest to re-explain the basics from scratch. Next episode my guest is a philosopher who's spent his whole career defending a Rortyan kind of neopragmatism: the antirepresentationalist line that beliefs don't represent the world and language doesn't mirror it, that truth-talk is better replaced with justification-talk relative to an audience of peers, and that the whole epistemology project since Descartes was an artefact of a picture we can just drop. He's charming, extremely practised, and his signature deflection when pressed is to say that pragmatism isn't offering a rival theory of truth at all — it's a recommendation to change the subject — so any objection that presupposes theories of truth competing like pictures supposedly fails to land. I've had him on once before informally and he ran circles around me with that move, plus the one where he diagnoses the questioner as still trapped inside the Kantian scheme/content picture.

Write me a sequence of maybe ten interview questions, ordered so the change-the-subject deflection gets progressively harder to use, ending with a question about what would actually count against his view — whether there's a falsifier for a position that seems built to absorb every objection as just more vocabulary we happen to be stuck with. Requirements: at least one question on the self-refutation worry (if his story about justification being audience-relative is just what his peers let him get away with saying, why prefer it to, say, the Thomist story the colleagues in the building next door let each other get away with); one on whether coping better — prediction, control, bridges standing up — can do all the work that the truth of beliefs was doing, or whether it quietly smuggles representation back in; and one on whether he really wants science and politics to be on all fours as far as being in touch with reality goes, given that aeroplanes fly and parliaments mostly don't produce consensus. For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give, especially the deflections I mentioned. No generic stuff like what drew him to philosophy — the content of the view should do the work. And please write everything in British English, including the question wording itself since I read them out verbatim on air.
