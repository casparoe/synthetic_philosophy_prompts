Wikipedia's core editorial rule is that content must be verifiable: every claim in an article must be supported by published, independent, reliable sources, and the policy states that "Even if you are sure something is true, it must have been published in a reliable source before you can add it." So an editor who personally witnessed an event may not report it, and a claim that happens to be true but appears in no source must be removed — while, oddly, a second-hand newspaper report of dubious accuracy is perfectly admissible. Compare this with the courtroom, where firsthand knowledge licenses testimony and second-hand reports are excluded as hearsay, or with ordinary conversation, where we assert what we ourselves know. What norm of assertion does the Wikipedia practice presuppose? Is it best reconstructed as a knowledge norm, a truth norm, a justification norm, or something like a purely institutional norm of trackability — and does the same norm explain why hearsay is welcome on Wikipedia but barred from the witness stand? Please reconstruct the implicit norm, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e., which rival norms of assertion would license it equally well.
