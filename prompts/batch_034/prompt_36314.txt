I've been reading an econ-adjacent blog for geopolitical forecasting stuff, and in the comments under a post last week someone shut down a whole argument with what I gather is a famous line: rational agents cannot agree to disagree. His move went like this: it's literally a theorem, so if we've read the same sources and neither of us is an idiot, we should end up with the same probability, and since we obviously don't agree, one of us is being irrational and should just update towards the other. He then carried on as if the other guy had been mathematically humiliated.

I know this traces back to some proper economics literature (Aumann, I think? sometime in the seventies?) and that it's turned into a bit of a slogan in forecasting and rationalist circles -- sometimes as you can't disagree with someone you respect, sometimes as two rationalists can't stay in disagreement, sometimes as the guy put it, which sounded the strongest of all. But the theorem must have hypotheses, and I suspect the comment thread used the slogan as if it had none at all. Also, the people invoking it never say whether they take it to be a claim about the actual world -- where it looks plainly false, since I know loads of smart, well-informed people who disagree about loads of things -- or about some idealised model whose conditions never actually hold between two humans in a comment section.

So here's what I'd like: take the sentence rational agents cannot agree to disagree apart and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, as an actual menu, from readings so weak they're trivially true, through readings that are substantive but defensible, to any that are outright false. For each one, tell me whether it's plausible and, crucially, whether it actually gets the commenter his conclusion that one party to a real-world disagreement must be irrational and should move towards the other. I'd especially like to understand what work the hidden assumptions are doing -- I gather the setup needs things like the two parties sharing a prior and somehow knowing each other's posteriors, and I want to see how much of the cannot survives once you drop those, or once you interpret agreeing loosely (same credences? no longer betting against each other? something weaker?).

One more thing if it's not too much: the same circles treat having a common prior as basically a requirement of rationality, which seems like a separate and much bigger claim. If the menu has room for it, flag where that fits and whether it's something the theorem itself needs or just what people say in the pub afterwards.

Please end with a single line starting "Verdict:" giving, in one sentence, the reading of the slogan the commenter actually needed for his move and whether he gets it.
