You are to act as an expert philosopher of physics with a specialty in the interpretation of quantum mechanics and the foundations of probability. Please think carefully and reason step by step; before delivering your final assessment, briefly brainstorm the possible pairwise tensions among the commitments below so that you can then order them properly.

I am an Everettian and have been asked to present my overall stance on probability in the many-worlds interpretation at a workshop, where it will be scrutinized. Before that, I want a consistency check. Here is the package of views I plan to defend:

1. I accept Everettian quantum mechanics: the universal wavefunction evolving unitarily is all that fundamentally exists; there is no collapse and no hidden variables.
2. Since the dynamics is unitary, fundamental physics is fully deterministic — there are no objective dynamical chances anywhere in nature.
3. I find the decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule (in the style of Deutsch and Wallace) convincing: given only that an agent has coherent preferences over outcomes defined on branches, rationality uniquely forces them to weight those outcomes by squared amplitude. An Everettian agent has no rational option but to bet in accordance with the Born rule.
4. Nevertheless, I insist that the probabilities delivered by those derivations are credences — subjective, agent-relative degrees of belief — and not objective chances, since a deterministic theory leaves nothing objective for chance to be.
5. I hold that counting branches is illegitimate: branch structure is emergent and coarse-grained, there is no well-defined number of branches produced by a given measurement, and so locutions like "half of the future worlds" are not even false but meaningless.
6. I also maintain that there is genuine self-locating uncertainty: before a measurement with two equiprobable-by-Born outcomes, I am really, from the inside, uncertain which future successor I will experience, and this uncertainty is phenomenologically just like ordinary chance.

My question: does this package hang together? Go through the commitments, identify which pairs or larger subsets are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally. For each genuine tension, explain what would have to give and what that revision would cost me dialectically — for instance, whether it would force me to give up the claim that Everettians can explain the empirical success of the Born rule. For each merely apparent tension, show me how it dissolves. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and note explicitly whether I can keep the decision-theoretic derivation as the centerpiece of my view if I make it. Please be an auditor, not a lecturer: the object of assessment is my position set, and I want to know exactly what each commitment commits me to.
