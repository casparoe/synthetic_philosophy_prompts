Critics of effective altruism in my circles keep reaching for the streetlight analogy to press what they call the measurability bias objection. You know the shape of it: a man loses his keys in a dark alley but searches for them under the lamppost, because that is where the light is. The critics say this is exactly how EA selects causes: the movement directs billions toward malaria nets and deworming pills not because those are the world's most important problems, but because randomized trials can be run on them, so impact can be quantified, charted, and ranked; meanwhile the things that might matter most -- institutional reform, political influence, growth-enhancing policy, cultural change -- sit in the dark, and EA, having looked only under the lamp, reports with confidence where the keys are. Defenders answer that this gets the story backwards: the lamp was placed where the keys were likely to be all along, since the framework's criterion is tractability, not measurability, and tractability just is a fact about where influence over outcomes is actually available.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a rerun of the debate. Please give me the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the alley, the lamppost, the keys, and -- this is the part I most want unpacked -- the drunk's motivations, i.e., whether EA's behavior is better modeled as someone who wants to find the keys but searches where it's convenient, or someone who mainly wants to be seen searching, or someone who quietly redefines "the keys" as whatever the lamppost illuminates. Then lay out the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the argument from the ones that don't. In particular, I want you to confront whether the analogy smuggles in an unargued premise: that the keys exist in the dark at all, that is, that there are high-expected-value interventions in the systemic-change territory that EA is missing. And close with the verdict: does the conclusion the analogy is being used to carry -- that EA's cause rankings track what can be measured rather than what does the most good -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If a closer analogy is available for EA's actual epistemic situation, propose it and say what it would predict differently.

One discipline throughout: please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. For instance, it may be common ground that EA funding skews heavily toward measurable health interventions, while it is contested whether that skew reflects bias or a defensible response to evidence about leverage; and it is contested whether tractability and measurability can even be pried apart in practice. Flag which claims fall on which side as you go, and do not present a contested position as the consensus view.
