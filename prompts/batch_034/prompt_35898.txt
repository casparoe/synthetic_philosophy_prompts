A member of my decision theory reading group — call her D. — claims to have an argument showing that if updatelessness is a genuine requirement of rationality, then no rational agent can ever act on a medical test, a weather forecast, or the evidence of its own eyes. I think there's a slip somewhere, but I can't isolate it, and the more I stare at the argument the more I worry that the slip is located exactly where the interesting part of updatelessness lives. I want you to diagnose it.

Here is D.'s argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) In the counterfactual mugging, a predictor has already flipped a fair coin. If it came up heads, it deposited $1,000,000 in an account for you if and only if it predicted that you would pay $1,000 if asked on tails; if it came up tails, you are now being asked to pay the $1,000. Paying looks terrible from inside the tails world — the million is settled and gone — but from the pre-flip vantage point the policy of paying if asked nets +$499,500 in expectation, against $0 for refusing. Updatelessness is the claim that the pre-flip evaluation is the one that counts, so you pay. Everyone in our group, including me, accepts this verdict.

(2) D. extracts the principle: when you act at a decision point, the evaluation that counts is the one made from the earlier vantage point — the one that had not yet learned which situation you would face. Rational choice at a node follows the assessment available before the observation that told you which node you're at.

(3) Now consider an ordinary medical decision. There is a condition with prior prevalence 10%; if you have it and leave it untreated, your expected loss is $200,000. Treatment costs $30,000 and fully removes the loss, with no side effects or other downside. Before testing, treating has negative expected value (0.1 × $200,000 − $30,000 = −$10,000), so the pre-test evaluation says don't treat. You then take a highly reliable test, and a positive result moves your credence that you have the condition from 10% to 90%. At this point treating is worth roughly +$150,000 by the post-test lights.

(4) By (2), the updateless agent must act on the pre-test evaluation, since that is the vantage point that had not yet learned which situation it faced. So after a near-certain positive, it declines treatment.

(5) Nothing in the argument turns on medicine. Any observation that tells you which situation you're in — the forecast that tells you whether it will rain, the coin you watched land, the invoice in your mailbox — is subject to the same principle, and the earlier vantage point never had it. An agent that must act on pre-observation evaluations across the board is evidentially paralyzed.

(6) So either updatelessness is false, in which case we lose the mugging verdict we all accept, or rational agents cannot use evidence at all. Both disjuncts are absurd, so the argument fails somewhere. D. says that if my diagnosis is just that updatelessness concerns policies and policies can depend on observations, she has a follow-up ready: which observations may a policy depend on, and which must it average over? If the answer is that you may condition on observations except when they tell you something about worlds other than the one you're acting in, she'll ask why 'I am in the tails world' doesn't get to count as ordinary evidence exactly the way a positive test does, and she'll add a stress test she says collapses my distinction: a case where an observation is straightforward evidence about a factor your action cannot causally affect — something in the style of a gene that independently both predisposes you to want a certain activity and causes the illness the activity is wrongly blamed for — and where she says the whole updateless apparatus seems to be built to tell you not to condition on it, even though it looks like exactly the kind of evidence (4) says you may use.

Tell me which step of the argument fails, if one does, and why that step looked fine. Then take D.'s follow-up seriously rather than waving it off: state where the principled boundary lies between observations a rational agent may condition on and observations it must average over, and check that the boundary vindicates paying in the mugging without also vindicating (4). If you think the argument is in fact valid, say which premise is cheapest to give up and what giving it up costs. For what it's worth, my own suspicion is that the trouble is in how (2) glosses (1) — that the pre-flip evaluation in the mugging is an evaluation of something other than the action I'm now performing, and that D. has quietly changed the object being evaluated — but every time I try to make that precise, D. insists that in the mugging the two evaluations coincide, so the distinction must be doing no work there; and I haven't found a reply to that I believe. I'd like to know whether I should.
