Here's the question (worth 20 points):

"Dennett claims that natural selection gives us 'free-floating rationales' — reasons without reasoners, rationales that are real but were never thought by anybody. (a) Explain what a free-floating rationale is, using one biological example. (b) Explain what makes such a rationale 'free-floating' as opposed to a garden-variety reason that some mind actually has. (c) Two complications: (i) does a free-floating rationale attach only to effects that natural selection actually favored, or to any effect of a trait that turns out to be useful? (ii) when a mind — say, a biologist — finally works out what the rationale was, does the rationale stop being free-floating?"

And here's the student's answer:

"A free-floating rationale is a reason why something exists or is built the way it is, where nobody ever had to think the reason. Take the eyespots on the wings of certain moths and butterflies — they look like vertebrate eyes and startle small birds into hesitating or aborting the attack. The reason for the spots is to scare off predators. But the moth has no idea its spots look like an owl, the bird doesn't know why it flinches, and no designer drew them up. The rationale is real — if the spots didn't reduce predation, they wouldn't have spread — but it's free-floating because there's no reasoner anywhere in the picture.

(b) A garden-variety reason is represented: I put the kettle on because I want tea, and I could tell you so. Free-floating rationales are different — they're explanations of design without any mind holding them. But they're not just 'as-if' reasons either. Natural selection did the design work of testing variations, so these are real reasons in the world, not a metaphor we project onto nature.

(c)(i) It doesn't have to be the trait's original function — a free-floating rationale attaches to whatever the trait is good for. For example, the heart makes a thumping sound, and those sounds are very useful to a doctor with a stethoscope, so 'telling the doctor about the heart's condition' is a free-floating rationale of the heart sound. (ii) Once a biologist works out the eyespot rationale, the reason is no longer free-floating — it's been captured by a mind, and now it's just a regular reason, like my reason for driving on the right. Of course the moths keep getting the benefit either way; evolution doesn't care whether anyone has figured it out."
