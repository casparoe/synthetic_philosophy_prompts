I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of religion, and next week I'm giving the presentation that counts for most of my grade. My thesis is that Hume's argument against believing miracle reports in the Enquiry is not primarily the anti-religious polemic it's often taken to be, but rather a special case of his general account of testimony: testimony is a channel that can legitimately carry belief to things we have never experienced — I want to lean on his own example of the Indian prince who reportedly wouldn't credit accounts of frost, which Hume treats as a defect in the prince's judgment, showing that he is not saying testimony can never outrun experience. My reading is that the real principle is comparative and proportional: the strength of testimony for an event must be weighed against the strength of our uniform experience against it, and Hume's notorious claim that no testimony could ever establish a miracle is best understood as his conclusion about the *actual historical record*, not as an a priori bar that even ideal testimony could never clear. As a stretch, I also want to argue that this proportional reading turns Hume's principle into a live tool outside religion — for instance, for how a jury should weigh surprising forensic evidence, or how we should treat a drug-safety signal that contradicts everything we believe about a well-studied medication — precisely because these cases show we take the same trade-off seriously when theology is not on the table.

The audience is twelve classmates plus the professor, and I've been told to expect real pushback. The professor is an early modern specialist who thinks Hume quietly equivocates between testimony being *outweighed* by experience and testimony being *silenced* by it, and she likes catching students who haven't distinguished the two. Three of my classmates are evangelical and have clearly read the standard apologetic replies — the argument from the cumulative force of many independent witnesses, the point that people don't suffer persecution for fabrications they know to be false, and the charge that Hume's standard would make us reject any genuinely novel discovery if we applied it consistently. One classmate is a physics major who seems convinced the whole dispute collapses into whether laws of nature are exceptionless, and another is fond of pointing out that we routinely believe individual people about highly improbable events, like lottery winners describing their wins, without anything like Hume's caution. I have about fifteen minutes of Q&A.
