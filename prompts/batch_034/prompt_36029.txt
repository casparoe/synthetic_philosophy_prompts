I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been asked to build the ranking module for an IVF clinic's embryo-selection tool. The tool will take polygenic risk scores and related data for a handful of viable embryos and present them to prospective parents, and I have to decide what the software actually does: filter out embryos above a threshold for serious disease risk and stay silent about everything else, rank embryos on a composite index of predicted outcomes, present raw numbers without any ranking, or something else entirely. Before I commit to a design, I want to understand which open questions in the debate over "procreative beneficence" — the claim that parents have a moral reason (or obligation) to choose the child expected to have the best life, among the children they could have — actually matter for this decision, and in what order of priority.

From what I've been able to piece together, the unresolved issues include at least these: (a) whether the non-identity problem makes harm-based reasoning unavailable, since the child who exists because of a given selection can't be said to have been harmed by it — the alternative was a different child, or none; (b) the expressivist objection that screening against disability conveys a message about the worth of existing disabled people, whether or not anyone is harmed; (c) whether there is a morally significant line between selecting against disease and selecting for traits the child would merely benefit from having; (d) whether the child's own good and the parents' preferences can come apart in ways the software should register, and if so, whose interests the ranking should track when they diverge; and (e) whether turning embryo choice into a continuous optimization problem — which is literally my job here — changes the character of the parent-child relationship, independent of any particular choice made.

Rank these for me and defend the ranking. Specifically: which of them is upstream of the others, in the sense that its answer constrains how the rest get resolved; which are tractable as philosophical questions rather than empirical ones about predictive validity or political ones about regulation; and which, if resolved, would actually change what I should build versus leaving the design untouched. I'd also like you to identify which of these are overrated — where the answer, whatever it turns out to be, wouldn't move the design decision. And where the ranking hinges on assumptions I can't evaluate from the outside (for instance, about whether the underlying scores are predictive enough for the ranking to carry information at all), please flag that dependency explicitly rather than burying it. I care more about the reasoning behind the ordering than about the list itself.
