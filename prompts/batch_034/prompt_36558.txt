I teach a general-education elective on language and translation, and I'm designing the centerpiece workshop around an obsession I've had since reading Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* — the book in which he translates, over and over, a twenty-eight-line, three-syllables-to-a-line get-well poem by the sixteenth-century French poet Clément Marot, each version honoring a different hierarchy of constraints, all to argue that form is not packaging around meaning but part of the meaning itself. My students won't have read the book, and I don't want them to: I want them to generate its thesis with their own hands before they ever hear it named. The session is twelve students, one 150-minute block, no laptops, and mixed language backgrounds — roughly a third have a strong second language, the rest are effectively monolingual English speakers.

The centerpiece is a nonce poem I wrote so that nobody can look it up and no canon gets in the way:

Look, love, the light is leaning low,
even gold evenings have to end;
as all the leaves we love leave, so
vainly vowing we shan't pretend —
even though I'm going away,
stay, love: it's all I have to say.

I've tried to bury several layers in it. Every line is exactly eight syllables with a four-beat lilt; the rhyme scheme is ABABCC; the first and last lines both open with a two-word address to the same person ("Look, love" / "stay, love"); line three turns on the pun that the leaves *leave*; and — the payload I want to arrive as a shock — the initial letters of the six lines spell LEAVES, so the form of the poem says what the speaker won't: that he is going even while the content begs the beloved to stay. My hunch is that most students will not find the acrostic, and that their translations will therefore quietly destroy a message they never knew existed, which is the whole lesson in miniature.

Please design the session. No theory up front: they should start believing translation is basically word substitution and then discover, round by round, that every choice is a trade — I'd like at least one round where rhyme starts dictating content instead of serving it, and one brutal compression round (the poem must fit some punishing length) that forces them to decide what was essential. The core rounds must work for the monolinguals, and I'm prepared to count translation into a dialect, register, or style — corporate memo, text message from a fifteen-year-old, nineteenth-century sentimental verse — as full translation, if you can make that feel legitimate rather than like a consolation prize, with a genuine cross-language round for the bilinguals if it fits. I also have a suspicion the judging criteria should themselves be part of the lesson — that whatever rubric we score translations by embodies a theory of what translation is for, and that switching rubrics mid-workshop should change which translation wins — but I haven't worked out how to operationalize that. Beyond rules, materials, and handouts (including what a stripped, formless prose version of the poem should look like), give me minute-by-minute timing and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the book's ideas: the acrostic reveal, the compression triage, the moment form started overriding sense. And be honest about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and which features of real translation under constraint — the centuries of cultural weight behind a form, the native reader's feeling that a constraint is natural rather than imposed — no classroom exercise can reproduce and I'll simply have to supply by fiat.
