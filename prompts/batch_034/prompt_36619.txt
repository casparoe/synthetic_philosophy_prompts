I belong to a university puzzle society, and at a recent meeting two other members — I'll call them Anton and Marta — spent the better part of an hour arguing over a famous exercise from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. I was a bystander and could not referee it, so I am bringing the exchange here. First, the exercise, so that the dispute is self-contained.

The MIU system works on strings made from the letters M, I, and U. You begin with the string MI and may apply any of four rules, in any order, as often as you like:
(R1) If a string ends in I, you may append U to it. (MI → MIU)
(R2) If a string has the form Mx, you may extend it to Mxx, where x is the whole tail after the M. (MIU → MIUIU)
(R3) If the string contains III anywhere, you may replace that III with a single U. (MIIII → MUI)
(R4) If the string contains UU anywhere, you may delete it. (MUUU → MU)
The puzzle asks whether MU can be reached from MI.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Anton set out to find a derivation and filled a page with strings — MI, MII, MIIU, MIIII, MIUIU, and so on — getting no closer to MU but visibly enjoying the search.

(2) Marta, who had read the book, told him to stop: the puzzle is famous precisely because it cannot be solved, and the point of the chapter is that one must stop reasoning inside the rule system and start reasoning about it.

(3) Anton refused to accept this on her authority. That MU is unreachable is, he said, a mathematical claim, and it needs a proof rather than a spoiler; either she should show him why, or she should let him search.

(4) Marta admitted she could not reconstruct the proof off the top of her head — she remembered only the conclusion — but insisted the conclusion was certain, and that the intended lesson concerned the kind of reasoning one would need, not the answer.

(5) Anton then made an observation and immediately walked past it. He noticed that the only rules that change the number of I's are the doubling rule and the III-to-U rule, so that as he doubles, the count runs 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — never a multiple of 3. But, he said, the rules can be interleaved: with four I's on the table, one application of R3 burns a triple and leaves a single I, so the sequence of counts is not confined to powers of two, and he could discern no pattern in the interleaved cases. Counting, he concluded, gets him nothing, and he went back to searching.

(6) Marta, provoked, took up the meta-move herself: stop asking which strings are reachable, she said, and ask what every rule application, no matter which, has in common — look for a quantity that no rule can change. She found some invariants of that kind — every reachable string begins with M, contains exactly one M, and never introduces a letter outside M, I, and U — but not the one that settles the puzzle, and she conceded as much.

(7) Anton then raised the objection he plainly considered his best. Even granting that her counting idea works, notice what it amounts to: ordinary mathematical reasoning. The system was never a cage; he was always free to think about the strings rather than merely manipulate them. So "jumping out of the system" is nothing more than a change of subject, from derivations to proofs about derivations, and the reasoning one jumps into is itself rule-governed if anything is. The celebrated escape, he said, is a trivial topic-switch.

(8) Marta answered that it is a genuine epistemic difference, not a topic-switch. No sequence of rule applications, however long, could ever inform anyone that MU is unreachable: the system generates theorems and nothing else, and underivability is not a fact the system can state or supply. On her view, the counting argument yields something the game could never in principle yield, and the fact that it can be formalized somewhere else does not put it back inside the MIU game.

(9) Anton's last word: then say what the system is. If it is the four rules, he escaped it the moment he wrote the first string in his own notebook rather than in the book. If it is "derivation as opposed to reasoning," then jumping out of the system is just a phrase for doing mathematics. He did not see a third option.

Please adjudicate this move by move — who is right about what, not merely who wins overall. In particular:

(a) Settle the mathematical question itself. Is MU reachable? Give the argument in full, and explain exactly where Anton's step (5) went astray: what he had in hand, and why the interleaving of R3 with doubling does not spoil it. I would like the modular arithmetic spelled out carefully rather than gestured at.

(b) Was Anton right in (3) that Marta's appeal to the puzzle's fame was mere testimony? Does her position — remembering the conclusion without being able to reconstruct the proof — constitute knowledge worth acting on, and does the answer matter for how the rest of the dispute should be scored?

(c) Assess Anton's deflationary charge in (7) and (9). Is jumping out of the system a genuine epistemic distinction or a trivial relabeling, and if genuine, what exactly is the system one jumps out of? Marta's step (8) gestures at "something the game could never in principle yield" — can that thought be made precise, and does it survive Anton's demand for a third option?

(d) One further fact I can supply, since it bears on (c): Hofstadter later shows that the MIU system can itself be mapped onto arithmetic — each string is recoded as a number, and each rule becomes an arithmetic operation, so that MIU-derivations show up as ordinary number-theoretic facts. Does this arithmetization vindicate Anton's deflation, support Marta's claim that the jump was real, or neither?

Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them missed.
