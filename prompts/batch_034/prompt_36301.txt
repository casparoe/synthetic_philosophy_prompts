I'm a third-year economics PhD student working on cheap-talk and disclosure models — signaling games with costless messages, Grossman–Milgrom unravelling of voluntary disclosure, equilibrium refinements, that sort of thing — and a dispute over my recently sold used car has turned into a running argument with my roommate, who is a philosophy PhD. When the buyer asked whether the car had ever been in an accident, I said truthfully that it hadn't been in one while I owned it, knowing full well she would hear "never." The roommate's view is that what I did, while not innocent, was categorically different from lying: a lie violates the norm of assertion directly, whereas misleading works through the hearer's own inference — I exploited her cooperative interpretation rather than feeding her falsehood. My instinct, trained on models where a sender is just a choice of message given private information, is that there's no interesting difference: both moves deliberately induce a wrong posterior, and the only real asymmetry is that my kind of move is harder to verify and penalize ex post. We've been talking past each other for two weeks, and I've concluded the problem is vocabulary.

So here's the request. Restate the main positions in the lying-versus-misleading debate in the vocabulary I actually use — senders, receivers, message spaces, literal content as the set of states consistent with a message, equilibrium inferences, pooling and separating behavior, incentive compatibility, verifiable versus costless messages — as faithfully as you can. Include both the side that says the moral difference is principled (roughly: the liar does something with the message itself, the misleader lets the hearer's own Bayesian updating do the damage) and the side that says the difference is superficial or at least not morally load-bearing. Please translate honestly: don't quietly build the philosophers' premises into the model and call it a translation, and don't collapse their distinctions just because the formalism finds them awkward.

Then give me the residue report, because that's what I actually care about. Which of their claims survive the translation cleanly — does lying map onto sending a message that excludes the true state, and misleading onto a true-content message that still induces a wrong posterior in equilibrium? What translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — the distinction between what is said and what is implicated, the speaker's intentions, blame for trading on someone's cooperativeness, whether the hearer's own inference partially exculpates the speaker? Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: is it a defect of my vocabulary that it can't represent the moral difference my roommate insists on, or is it evidence that the difference is less substantive than it looks — that both moves are posterior manipulation, differing only in who bears the verification costs? I want to know whether my intuition is right or whether the model is missing something the philosophy can see.
