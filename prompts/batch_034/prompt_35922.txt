I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and there's a pattern in my own field that I've never been able to justify to myself in clean terms. I'd like you to reconstruct what view of knowledge it presupposes.

Here is the pattern. On one hand, applied cryptography rests on a doctrine, usually attributed to Kerckhoffs, that a system should remain secure even if everything about its design is public. Treating secrecy of the algorithm itself as protection — "security through obscurity" — is regarded as a rookie mistake, almost a moral failing, and we design on the assumption that the adversary has the full specification in hand. On the other hand, the public-key cryptography that secures nearly all internet commerce — RSA, Diffie–Hellman, elliptic curve schemes — rests on assumptions like "there is no efficient algorithm for factoring large integers," and nobody has ever proven any such thing. P versus NP is open, the hardness of factoring is open, the discrete logarithm problem is open. In effect, the world's payment infrastructure runs on unproven conjectures, and the profession treats this as prudent engineering.

So the field trusts one kind of "no one has found a way in" and distrusts another. Thousands of people have failed for decades to break RSA, and we take that as strong evidence of safety; equally many people have failed to discover some vendor's secret design, and we take that as no evidence at all. What theory of evidence, or of knowledge, does that split presuppose?

My own first stab at it, for what it's worth: the asymmetry may have something to do with facts that already exist versus facts that must be created. A secret design exists somewhere — in a head, a repository, a filing cabinet — so its not being known is a contingent state that one act of espionage can reverse, whereas an efficient factoring algorithm, if there is one, exists nowhere until someone invents it, so decades of not finding it track the difficulty of creation rather than the integrity of storage. But I can see the objection to my own story: mathematical discoveries do happen abruptly, at the hands of single individuals, and when one happens the hardness assumption collapses overnight, just as a leak collapses a secret. Perhaps the practice rests on something else entirely, or is quietly incoherent, or the two stances are underwritten by different views that only look like the same kind of trust.

Please do the following. Reconstruct, in plain language, the implicit epistemology of the practice: what it presupposes about mathematical knowledge as opposed to knowledge of secrets, and about what a long history of failed attempts is evidence of. Then say whether that presupposition is coherent, or whether the practice is actually relying on something it doesn't acknowledge — for instance, a bet on the rarity of mathematical invention, which is a sociological or psychological wager rather than a fact about knowledge. Next, identify where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, that is, where several quite different theories of evidence would license exactly the same behavior, so that the practice cannot be said to presuppose any one of them. Finally, tell me what would have to change in engineering if the implicit view were replaced by a demand for proof: would we be left with nothing but information-theoretically secure schemes like one-time pads and honest few-time authenticators, or does the current migration to post-quantum algorithms — which merely swaps one set of unproven hardness assumptions for another — show that the profession has already concluded the presupposition is all it has, and has decided to live with it? Keep the philosophy accessible; I can follow the mathematics, but I have never read a page of epistemology.
