I'm setting the final for an upper-level philosophy of science course on prediction and confirmation, and I want one essay question built around the Malthus case — his population principle predicted that population growth would outrun food supply and press wages down to subsistence, yet the two centuries after 1798 saw living standards rise and, eventually, birth rates fall. The question I want the students to wrestle with is whether it is legitimate to call such a theory "right but early." To answer, they should have to say what exactly was predicted (a dated point prediction? an open-ended mechanism claim?), which auxiliary assumptions failed (constant agricultural technology, absence of fertility decline), how the Duhem–Quine problem licenses rescuing the core, and at what point a rescue with no deadline becomes unfalsifiable in practice. They should also apply whatever criteria they develop to a present-day analog — I plan to give them a 1972-style Club of Rome resource-exhaustion model whose dates have passed but whose mechanism its defenders still uphold.

Please act as an experienced examiner and think through the crux before drafting: the question should not be answerable by reciting Popper and Lakatos labels; it should separate students who can *build* a criterion for distinguishing a legitimately deferred prediction from a degenerating one. Deliverables: the exam question (for a 90-minute answer, 25 marks), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong, and a sentence on what separates an A from a B. Finally, append a short examiner's note on where your own model answer's verdict is most contestable — which argument or consideration, if pressed harder, would pull the answer toward the opposite conclusion about Malthus.
