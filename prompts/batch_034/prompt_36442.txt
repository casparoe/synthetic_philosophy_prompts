I write a long-read column on science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on whether history is "locked in" — whether the world we inhabit is a fragile result of accidents that could easily have gone another way, or whether something broadly like it would have arrived regardless. The standard telling, popularised by the QWERTY keyboard story and by Stephen Jay Gould's image of replaying the tape of life, is that small early advantages compound into permanent lock-in. Critics respond that geography, resource endowments and human needs push events back onto convergent tracks. I could write a balanced "on the one hand, on the other" piece, but I'd rather find out where the honest weight of evidence actually lies, and I've found that asking for a number forces the issue better than any amount of prose.

So let me state the proposition as precisely as I can. Consider replaying history from, say, 1400 CE with a handful of small perturbations — a monarch dies five years earlier, a decisive battle goes the other way, a key inventor takes up his father's trade instead. By 1900, would the world be recognisably similar to ours: industrialisation achieved in broadly the same regions, comparable geopolitical powers, broadly similar dominant belief systems and constitutional forms? My credence that the answer is yes — recognisably similar rather than merely resembling ours in thin, generic ways — sits around 0.45. My editor, who studied economic history, puts it nearer 0.8 and thinks I romanticise contingency.

Please give me your own credence, and decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each. I'd suggest at least the following terms, though you should add or refine as you see fit: (1) that the counterfactual is even well-posed — that "the same initial conditions plus small perturbations" picks out a determinate enough thing to have a truth value; (2) that geographical and ecological constraints dominate over historical accident at the timescale of centuries; (3) that lock-in of the kind economists describe — increasing returns, switching costs, network effects — is strong enough in human institutions that once anything resembling the modern state or the factory system emerges, much of the rest follows; (4) that what I'll call convergence of function operates independently of lock-in of equilibrium — that is, that whatever contingent details obtain, similar functional niches get filled (some script becomes standard, some cheap transport of goods and people emerges, some system of mass production takes hold) even if it's not the particular script, fuel or institutional form we ended up with. I'm especially interested in whether terms (3) and (4) really are independent, since it seems to me that a world can be convergent in function yet radically divergent in equilibrium — and that would partly dissolve the usual debate, because both the contingency camp and the convergence camp could claim the same world as vindication.

Then combine your terms into an overall number, tell me which sub-question the final figure is most sensitive to, and say what sort of evidence would genuinely move it — I'd rather be able to tell readers what would change our minds than merely declare our priors. Finally, given my 0.45 and my editor's 0.8, tell me where you think the disagreement between us most likely lives.
