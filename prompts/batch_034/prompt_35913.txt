I've sat on the clinical ethics committee of our NHS trust for six years, and Thursday's caselist finally contains the case I've been dreading, because it will almost certainly be taken as precedent by the ward managers afterwards.

The patient, Mr H, is 74 and has moderate Alzheimer's disease. At 58, shortly after watching his mother die in the late stages of the same condition, he completed an advance decision refusing treatment. It is formally valid under the 2005 Act — signed, dated, specific — and it refuses antibiotics, artificial nutrition and hospital admission once he reaches the stage of requiring assistance with dressing and toileting, which he now does. But it also contains a rider he apparently insisted on: that if he ever pleads to be treated, the plea should be discounted as the disease speaking, since he had watched his mother do exactly that and found it unbearable to witness. He is now admitted from his care home with a chest infection that would likely resolve with a routine course of antibiotics. He is cheerful, oriented to his surroundings, greets staff by name, and when the question is put to him in simple terms he says he wants to get better and would like the medicine. His daughter, who has power of attorney for welfare, says her father is visibly content, that the man who wrote the directive was depressed by his mother's decline and cannot have meant this, and that letting him die of a treatable infection over his own present objection would be a betrayal. His son, who witnessed the directive being written, says his father thought about nothing else for a year and would regard treatment as the betrayal. The treating consultant wants our written opinion before deciding, and wants it within the week.

For what it's worth, my own instinct going in, which you should feel free to demolish: I came to this committee after years reading the rationalist blogs, and the frame I keep reaching for is that the earlier self is the only one who ever saw the whole trajectory — the later self is like an agent whose value function has been perturbed after training, and you don't update on outputs from a corrupted process just because they're current. But I've read enough of the Parfit-adjacent pushback to know that the two-selves picture can't simply be assumed: if there aren't literally two patients here, then someone is being overridden, and the rider instructing us to discount Mr H's pleas in advance strikes even me as uncomfortably like rigging the epistemics so the argument can't lose. The palliative care consultant on the committee, meanwhile, holds that current experiential welfare is the only thing with any moral weight at this point, and the lawyer will only tell us the document is valid, which settles the law and not the ethics.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this admission and say something about how the committee should handle the recurring category, since whatever we write will be photocopied and used again. It should engage the daughter's position as a serious philosophical claim about whose welfare and whose values are actually at stake, not merely restate the statutory presumption in favour of valid advance refusals. And it should tell me honestly whether my 'corrupted process' framing is doing real work or just flattering my prior.
