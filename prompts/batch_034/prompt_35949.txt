Please describe the most important implications of superrationality — Hofstadter's idea that a rational agent facing a symmetric decision problem should expect other rational agents to arrive at the same reasoning, and should therefore choose the option that is best if chosen by everyone, rather than the option that is best taking the others' choices as fixed — for everyday collective-action situations: whether I should vote in a large national election, whether I should return a lost wallet anonymously, whether I should cooperate in one-shot anonymous exchanges online, and whether I should contribute to a public good where my own contribution is negligible. I am especially interested in how the implications hinge on how similar the other agents' reasoning must be to mine for the argument to apply: must my counterparts literally run the same deliberation, or is it enough that we reach the same conclusion by different routes, and what happens as the similarity becomes imperfect? I would also like to know whether superrationality ever recommends something that no combination of ordinary causal reasoning and moral concern could justify on its own — or whether, once the agents are similar enough for the argument to work, the ordinary reasons would have sufficed anyway.
