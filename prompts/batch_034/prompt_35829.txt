I'm a journalist who writes a long-running column on science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I'm preparing a piece on how formal theories of confirmation occasionally vindicate patterns of reasoning that look, from the armchair, like an obvious joke. The centrepiece I have in mind is Hempel's raven paradox, but I don't want to rehash the white shoe in its stock form yet again; I want a case that runs the paradox in an unusual direction. Ordinarily the paradox is staged so that we feel the pull of denying that a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black. I want the opposite: a thought experiment in which a sensible character, reasoning about a real generalisation, is right — by the lights of a Bayesian treatment of confirmation — to test that generalisation almost entirely by examining things that are not the subject-term: things that, were they counterexamples, would falsify the claim, but which are, in the familiar idiom, 'non-ravens'. The case should be one in which this practice is not merely permissible but actually the optimal design, and in which readers' intuitions will side with the character rather than with the paradox's usual sting.

Some constraints. The scenario should be realistic and professional in register — think a quality-control engineer, an epidemiologist, or a customs officer; no philosophers appear in the story, no laboratory contrivances, and the scenario itself should fit in a single paragraph. The generalisation should have the form 'All F are G', and the character's evidence should consist overwhelmingly of observed not-G things that turn out to be not-F — contrapositive instances — while direct observations of F are scarce, slow, or expensive to obtain. Crucially, the scenario must not stipulate that this evidence is informative; it has to earn that status, since the point of my column is that the informativeness follows from ordinary background knowledge, not from a philosopher's fiat.

After the scenario, do the audit. First, show that the case is genuinely raven-structured: state the hypothesis, its contrapositive, and which observations count as confirming instances under Hempel's instance condition and equivalence condition. Second, explain in Bayesian terms why, in your scenario, the contrapositive observations carry real confirmational weight — why they are diagnostic here in a way a white shoe is not — and sketch the alternative hypotheses between which the evidence discriminates. Third, tell me which single feature of the case is doing that work, and what happens when you remove it: I would expect the case to collapse back into the ordinary white-shoe situation, where the paradox's sting returns, and I'd like you to confirm or deny that. Finally, close with how a strict Hempelian — someone who insists that confirmation must be symmetric across logically equivalent formulations and that any felt asymmetry is a psychological illusion — would attack your case. Please write the whole thing in British English, and keep the tone of a well-edited magazine: precise, a little wry, but no dumbing down.
