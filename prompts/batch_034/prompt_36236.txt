My friend and I got into a long argument about modal realism over dinner last night and it's still bugging me, so I want to think out loud about it with someone who knows the literature.

The setup: I've basically come to accept Lewis's version — all the possible worlds are real concrete things, just as real as ours, differing from ours in how things are distributed over their parts and not in any metaphysical status; "actual" is just an indexical like "here" or "now"; and modal truth is just truth at some world or other. My friend thinks it's absurd on its face, and their whole case amounted to the incredulous stare plus Ockham's razor. I kept saying that's not an argument, it's a refusal to do cost-benefit analysis, and that the view pays its way by letting you quantify over possibilia, give reductive analyses of counterfactuals, closeness of worlds, de re modality via counterpart theory, etc.

But walking home it hit me that I couldn't actually say what WOULD count against the view. It makes no distinct predictions about how our world behaves — the ersatzist agrees on all of that — so in what sense does it risk anything? So here's what I want:

Give me the map of the view's exposure. What observations, arguments, formal results, or consequences in other areas of philosophy would genuinely count against modal realism, and which are just the objections Lewis already priced in? Specifically I want to know:

1. Does the view stand or fall with counterpart theory? If counterpart theory collapsed — say the arguments about the failure of substitutivity or about how counterparts underwrite de re claims turned out not to work — could a transworld-identity version of modal realism survive, or is that combo incoherent in a way that matters?

2. Is the "plenitude" of worlds a real commitment that could fail? My friend threw out something at 1am that I couldn't answer: if it turned out that reality is finitely decomposable — the universe is discrete, there are only finitely many concrete things, no unrestricted composition, whatever — then the pluriverse itself is finite, and maybe too impoverished to deliver all the modal truths we're confident in. Is that a live defeater, does Lewis have wiggle room (is the "no gaps, no repetitions" characterization of worlds doing enough work?), or does this secretly rest on assumptions about mereology and physics that no one's in a position to settle?

3. The relocation worry: is the analysis of "possibly P" as "P at some world" circular or empty? Does the modal realist just presuppose modal notions in saying how worlds differ (different distributions of natural properties over individuals) or in saying what it takes for something to be a world rather than a proper part of one? If so, is that fatal for a view whose whole selling point was being modalist without spookiness?

4. Which of these would Lewis himself, or a smart present-day defender, concede as counting against the view, versus dismiss as a cost already paid? Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious through merely awkward. And I want an honest verdict on the unfalsifiability question: if the answer is basically "nothing could count against it," is that a fatal problem for a metaphysical thesis, or is that just what metaphysical theses are like and the incredulous stare really is all the opposition has?

For what it's worth, what would move ME is something in the neighborhood of (3): if it turned out the view is committed to something it can't say, or that the story about naturalness/duplication quietly can't do the work of individuating worlds. But tell me if I'm weighting that wrong.

(and if you could avoid the whole "Lewis believed in dinosaurs with purple spots" register of ridicule, i've heard it, my friend tried it, it's not why we were arguing)
