I'm a software engineer with no legal training beyond what I've picked up from arguments at family dinners, and I'd like you to audit an analogy I've been leaning on. At work I maintain a large legacy codebase — most of it written a decade before I arrived, no comments, the original developers long gone. My working method there is completely settled: I don't try to reconstruct what the departed developer "intended"; I read what the code actually does, treat behavior that frustrates any reasonable purpose as a bug, and fix it through the normal patching process rather than by reinterpreting it into behaving differently. Over the holidays I argued to my sister-in-law, a litigation attorney, that courts should read statutes the same way: the legislature is the departed developer, the statute is the code, the judge is the maintenance engineer, and if the plain text produces an absurd or obsolete result, that's a bug for the legislature to patch through amendment — not something judges should paper over by consulting unobservable "legislative intent." This seemed obviously right to me, and I said so with some confidence, mostly by analogy to how impossible it would be to do my job if I had to reverse-engineer intent instead of reading the code.

She pushed back on several fronts, and I want to know whether her objections break the analogy or merely bruise it. First, she said my analogy assumes code is "self-executing" — the behavior just is what the program does — whereas a statute is a message read by millions of citizens and agencies who adjust their conduct based on what they think it says, so "what the code does" and "what the text says" are not the same kind of thing at all. Second, she said the legislature, unlike my departed developers, is not dead: it can be petitioned, lobbied, and asked to amend, and pretending it's a ghost is itself a substantive choice about who should fix what. Third — and this one bothered me — she pointed out that I can only call something a "bug" in my codebase because I already know what the program is supposed to do; "unintended behavior" is unintelligible without an intended function, so the bug/patch vocabulary I was using to defend strict textualism quietly presupposes the purposivist's starting point. She capped it off with a case from 1892, Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, where the Supreme Court read a statute literally barring contracts for "labor or service of any kind" as not covering a New York church that had hired an Anglican rector from England, because Congress had plainly been after cheap industrial labor, not clergy — a case textualists like Scalia have grumbled about for decades.

So here is what I'd like from you. Lay out the analogy explicitly — the correspondence table, if you like: statute to code, legislature to the original dev team, judge to the maintenance engineer, amendment to a patch, an absurd literal application to a bug, ordinary meaning to documented behavior — and then assess each of my sister-in-law's three objections against it, plus any other disanalogies you think matter more than hers. Distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the use I was putting the analogy to (supporting textualist-style statutory interpretation) from the ones that don't. I'd also like to know whether the twist she identified is as devastating as it felt: does the fact that engineers need purposes to identify bugs mean the analogy actually supports purposivism instead, or is there a coherent textualist rejoinder — say, a distinction between using purpose to identify a problem versus using it to rewrite the text? Finally, tell me whether a better analogy from my world exists at all: if not legacy code, then perhaps a public API contract with many downstream consumers, or a specification that clients have relied on and can't be silently changed — something that captures what statutes are that code isn't. I'd appreciate a clear verdict on where my dinner-table argument actually lands once the audit is done.
