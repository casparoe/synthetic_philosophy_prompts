I spent thirty-five years in isotope geochemistry before retiring, and since then I have taken up reading in the philosophy of science, which has left me less certain than I ever was on the job about which of our disputes were genuinely empirical. Permit me to describe one such dispute and ask for your help with it.

At a departmental reunion dinner last month, two former colleagues of mine argued about the Anthropocene — specifically, whether it should be formalized as an epoch in the geological timescale and, if so, when it began. The first, a stratigrapher, held that the question is purely empirical and that its answer is already available: a formal unit of the timescale must be anchored to a physical reference horizon in strata, and the best candidate is the layer of plutonium isotopes deposited worldwide by nuclear weapons testing in the early 1950s. He added that when the relevant subcommission voted a few years ago against formalizing precisely that proposal, the episode showed the machinery working as designed — a contested hypothesis scrutinized and rejected — not that something unscientific was afoot.

The second, an environmental historian, replied that choosing 1950 as a start date is itself a value-laden act with moral consequences: it makes the new epoch begin with nuclear weapons rather than with colonial land seizure, the epidemics that emptied the Americas, or industrial fossil-fuel capitalism, thereby quietly absolving the earlier drivers of planetary change. Dates of this kind are not discovered, she said; they are chosen, and the choice distributes blame.

The stratigrapher called this a category error: the units of the timescale carry no moral freight — nobody supposes the Jurassic lionizes the dinosaurs — and the stratigraphic criterion of a globally synchronous marker in strata is not smuggled in to suit anyone's politics; it is forced by the practical requirement that a boundary be identifiable in the field by any competent worker, anywhere.

The historian answered that identifiability is not neutrality but a decision about whose evidence counts: atmospheric carbon dioxide rise and centuries of land-use change leave real stratigraphic signals too, merely diffuse and diachronous ones, so the crisp plutonium layer wins not because it is more important but because it is more convenient — convenience dressed up as rigor.

His last word was that diachronous signals would yield boundaries that no two labs could place alike, handing the decision over to committees and rhetoric — exactly the politicization she claims to fear. Hers was that the committee vote he himself invoked showed the committees were deciding all along, and at least her account of the choice explains why anyone cared so much.

I found each of them persuasive at different moments of the evening, and I could not referee — a failing that in my working life I would never have tolerated in a data set. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: state who is right about which specific points, where each overreaches, and whether there is something both are missing about what kind of question "When did the Anthropocene begin?" actually is. I ask in earnest, because I suspect the answer bears on a number of boundary-drawing exercises I participated in without ever examining their logic.
