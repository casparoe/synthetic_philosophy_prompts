I'm a doctoral student in formal epistemology, and I've been asked to take a fifty-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates on the idea that credences can be assessed by accuracy alone — that there is a purely epistemic sense in which one set of degrees of belief is better or worse than another, quite apart from whether holding them pays off in any decision. The students have done a term of intro logic and know basic probability, but no decision theory and no formal epistemology. Don't explain the material to me; plan how I should teach it. Specifically: what has to be established first before the notion of an accuracy measure can even be introduced without seeming arbitrary (I suspect they need to see why the naive idea of just counting how many beliefs came true fails for degrees of belief, but you tell me what the real dependency is); the single worked example that should carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must avoid — I'm inclined towards a weather forecaster issuing probabilities over a season, but flag if that imports problems the students will trip over; the misconceptions they will predictably form and exactly where in the sequence each should be pre-empted — I'm expecting at least some of them thinking that accuracy permits being certain of everything, some thinking the whole thing is covertly about betting and bookmakers, and some thinking that because different scoring rules rank different credence assignments differently, the framework is arbitrary in a way that undermines it; how I should handle the prudential/epistemic distinction when someone raises the gambler who profits from overconfidence; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level, saying so to them. Finish with one question I could pose in the last five minutes whose answer would show me whether the core idea — that accuracy is an expectation over possible worlds rather than a count of hits — has actually landed rather than having been nodded along to.
