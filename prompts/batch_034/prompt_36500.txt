You are a professional philosopher working on social choice theory and collective rationality, and I want you to think carefully and brainstorm a bit before committing to an answer — I've gotten too many hedged surveys from people who won't plant a flag. Some background on me: I'm a retired pharmacologist, sat on grant review panels for something like twenty years, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy, partly off my grandson's reading list. Recently I worked through the judgment aggregation stuff — the discursive dilemma, the three-judge court from the law literature (Kornhauser and Sager's, I believe), the impossibility results by List and Pettit from around 2002 — and I've constructed an argument I can't get out of. It seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the loose board.

The setup is a panel I could have actually served on. Three reviewers have to decide whether a grant proposal is fundable, and everyone accepts the rule: fundable only if it's novel AND feasible AND impactful. The reviewers' individual judgments: Alvarez says novel yes, feasible yes, impactful no, therefore not fundable; Bianchi says novel yes, feasible no, impactful yes, therefore not fundable; Chen says novel no, feasible yes, impactful yes, therefore not fundable. Every single reviewer is perfectly consistent. But take majorities on each proposition and "the panel" believes the proposal is novel, believes it's feasible, believes it's impactful — and believes it's not fundable. Okay, that's the famous paradox. Now the argument that worries me. (1) The panel's collective judgments should be determined by the members' judgments on those very propositions, with propositions playing the same logical role treated the same way — novelty, feasibility and impact shouldn't get different kinds of aggregation. (2) The panel, qua panel, should be consistent: if it accepts the criteria and accepts the funding rule, it must accept fundability. (3) Any profile of individually consistent member judgments has to be admissible — you can't toss out ballots just because they'll cause trouble downstream. (4) No member's judgment counts for more by name. (5) The case above shows that with (1)-(4) in place, a panel can end up collectively inconsistent. (6) And the same trap can be built with any interlocked set of propositions, so every aggregation procedure honoring (1)-(4) has some profiles that break (2) — as best I understand, that's the gist of the impossibility results. (7) Therefore no committee, court, jury, legislature, or — here's where it stings — scientific consensus body (think FDA advisory panels, the big climate assessments) can ever be a rational agent. (8) But that's absurd: the panels I sat on were obviously rational most of the time, and I refuse to believe the climate reports are irrational in principle.

So tell me, which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If your answer is that the rot is in the slide from (6) to (7) — "some profile breaks it" versus "it's broken" — then say so and explain exactly what changes in that step, because I keep staring at it and it looks innocent to me. But don't stop at diagnosis: plant the flag. Take a definite position on which premise is least costly to abandon and defend it against the two most attractive alternatives. I can see the standard escape hatches involve dropping something — letting the panel vote directly on the conclusion instead of the premises, letting one designated member's judgment trump the others, restricting which profiles count as admissible, or giving up entirely on the group being a rational agent (my grandson says some philosophers genuinely take that last route; it sounds like it costs a fortune, but I can't articulate why).
