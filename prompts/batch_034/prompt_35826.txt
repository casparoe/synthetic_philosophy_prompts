Three weeks ago I switched my thesis topic to formal epistemology (coming from stats/ML), and yesterday a senior grad student in my reading group told me my whole framework is broken before I've even written chapter one. I can't find the flaw but I also can't accept where the argument lands. Help me out.

Context: I want to write about how agents should update when the evidence itself is uncertain — like a radiologist squinting at a borderline X-ray, or the temperature sensors in my old lab that were right maybe 80% of the time. The obvious tool is Jeffrey's rule: when you can't become certain of E, you just directly adopt a new credence in E, and your credences in everything else follow along through your old conditional probabilities. That's exactly the kind of case I care about.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) An update is rational only if every ingredient of it that isn't forced by probability theory is rationally permitted on its own.
(2) In a Jeffrey update there are only two ingredients: (a) the new value you adopt for your credence in E, and (b) the redistribution of all your other credences through your old conditional probabilities.
(3) Ingredient (b) is forced by probability theory — once the new credence in E is fixed, preserving your conditionals fixes everything else.
(4) So the rationality of the whole update stands or falls with ingredient (a): whether adopting that particular new credence in E was permitted.
(5) Jeffrey's framework says nothing about when adopting a new credence in E is permitted — the input just gets fed in from outside the theory. He dramatized this with a pill that makes you 90% sure the coin is loaded: the rule spreads that change through your web of belief just as obediently as it spreads the change produced by a good look at the coin.
(6) So Jeffrey's rule isn't a theory of rational updating at all, it's bookkeeping — it tells you how to propagate a change once it has happened, never whether the change should happen.
(7) So either rational updating requires certainty, in which case the blurry X-ray, the unreliable sensor, and basically all of actual perception can never rationally move you — or else there's a separate theory of when uncertain inputs are warranted, and that theory is the real epistemology, with Jeffrey's rule relegated to an appendix. He says it's obviously the second, and that my thesis is therefore about the appendix.

The thing that bugs me: he seems to slide from "the rule doesn't evaluate its inputs" to "there's no such thing as rational updating on uncertain evidence," and those don't sound like the same claim to me. But he insists they amount to the same thing, because strict conditionalization (the certainty case) is just a Jeffrey update with input 1, so it inherits the exact same hole — meaning even the classical Bayesian story never answered the input question either, and it's wierd to hold Jeffrey's version to a standard the original never met.

Which step, if any, should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd honestly rather keep the idea that uncertain evidence can rationally move you — that's the entire reason I switched topics — but I want to know what I'm signing up for before I commit a chapter to defending it.
