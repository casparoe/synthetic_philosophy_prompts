I've just started volunteering one afternoon a week at my county historical society, and it has unexpectedly radicalized the way I think about archives: last Tuesday my main job was helping decide which boxes from a defunct fraternal lodge we should accession and which we should let the family throw away, and I realized I was standing inside the machine that decides what the future will be allowed to know. This coincided with finishing Michel-Rolph Trouillot's 1995 book Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History in a course I'm auditing, whose central claim, as I understand it, is that silences are not accidents of forgetting but are produced at every stage of history-making — when sources are created in the first place, when archives assemble and select among them, when narratives are built from what survives, and when significance is assigned in retrospect. I'd like you to act as a historian of archives and colonial knowledge, think carefully before answering, and work through one concrete episode with me using that framework: the Franciscan friar Diego de Landa's campaign in Yucatán, culminating in the auto-da-fé at Maní on July 12, 1562, where, by his own account, twenty-seven Maya books and some five thousand sacred images were burned, after interrogations conducted by hoisting suspects into the air with their hands bound. Only three pre-Columbian Maya codices (plus fragments of a fourth) are known to survive today.

Here are the complications that make the episode feel tailor-made and yet somehow ill-fitting for the framework, and I want you to take them seriously rather than smoothing them over. First, the person who ordered the destruction is also the author of the Relación de las cosas de Yucatán, a detailed account of Maya religion, calendar, and writing written a few years later, which survived only as an abridgment of later copies and was itself lost to scholarship for centuries before resurfacing in the nineteenth century — and which became one of the key Rosetta stones for the twentieth-century decipherment of Maya glyphs. So the primary channel through which we know anything about what was burned is the arsonist's own archive, partly produced under torture, and the silencing was accompanied by an act of preservation that itself proved decisive. Second, and this is what keeps nagging me: Trouillot's silences seem to work by quiet omission, by things never quite making it into the record, whereas Maní was a loud, ceremonial, deliberate destruction, openly justified in Landa's writing as the removal of superstition. Is an announced bonfire a "silence" in Trouillot's sense at all, or is it a limiting case that shows where the framework needs a distinction between records that are missing and records that were made to go missing — and does that distinction actually change what the framework predicts about who can now speak in the sources and who cannot? Third, the three surviving codices survived largely by ending up in European collections — Dresden, Madrid, Paris — so even the scraps of pre-Columbian voice that exist exist on the conquerors' shelf.

Please run the analysis in both directions, as my instructor keeps demanding. Going one way: map the episode onto the stages of Trouillot's framework — what happened at the level of source creation (Maya literate culture, the interrogations, the Relación itself), archive assembly (what the Franciscans kept, copied, and shipped to Spain versus what they burned), narrative construction (how the episode has been told since, including the recent tendency to cast Landa as either monster or inadvertent preserver), and retrospective significance (what it means to us now, in an era of digital archives and my own little accession decisions). Going the other way: say honestly where the framework strains or goes silent on this episode, and whether some other lens — the destruction of books as a technology of conversion, or the archive as war booty — does better work. Where you draw on interpretive debates (about Landa's motives, about how many books there really were, about how much the surviving Relación reflects Landa versus his later copyists), please flag what is reasonably settled among specialists and what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. If it helps you think, start by brainstorming the possible mappings before committing to one, but I'd like the final analysis to be disciplined about who and what plays each role in the framework, and to end with what the episode teaches someone like me, standing over a cardboard box deciding what gets a folder and an accession number and what gets a dumpster.
