I'm a retired entomologist -- thirty-some years working on parasitoid wasps and other hymenoptera, and now that I have time on my hands I've been reading philosophy, mostly badly and out of order. Recently I went back to Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I'd bounced off in the eighties, and the part that stuck with me this time was the digger wasp, Sphex. You probably know the story: the female drags her paralyzed cricket to the burrow entrance, leaves it outside, goes down to check the burrow, then comes back up to haul the cricket in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the entrance... and goes down to check again. Do it forty times and she'll check forty times. Hofstadter apparently coined the word "sphexishness" for this kind of mindless, stereotyped triggering, and I have to admit it hit close to home, because anyone who has actually watched wasps work has seen something like it.

Here's my problem. My philosophy reading group (all of us retired science types) had a long argument about whether sphexishness is just an amusing fact about insects or whether it's an argument against free will generally. One camp says: the wasp's behavior looks purposeful, intelligent even, but it's really a fixed subroutine that fires when triggered, and the moral is that human deliberation is the same kind of thing one level up -- our "choosing" is a more elaborate triggered routine, and the felt sense of deliberating is decoration. The other camp, roughly following Hofstadter, says humans are "antisphexish": we can notice when a routine has hijacked us (I keep checking the fridge even though I know there's no beer), step outside it, and interrupt it, and that capacity is what free will amounts to -- not some spooky soul-stuff, just the ability to jump out of the loop. And to complicate things, a former colleague of mine used to grumble that the Sphex story is old ethology folklore and that wasps in the field are often a lot more flexible than the textbook anecdote lets on, though I never checked how seriously to take that.

So: write an essay on whether sphexishness tells us anything about human free will. Weigh both camps -- the "it's wasps all the way up" pessimists and the "jumping out of the loop is the real difference" side -- and pay some attention to the middle positions, like the view that antisphexishness comes in degrees and humans are only partially above their subroutines (I'm honestly not sure where my own fridge-checking habit leaves me on that scale). Whatever you do, don't end on a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" note -- I want a verdict, and I want it defended. If your verdict is that the wasp is a bad analogy for humans, say exactly why and what would count as the relevant difference. If it's that the pessimists are right, tell me what my sense of deliberating is really doing while it thinks it's running the show.
