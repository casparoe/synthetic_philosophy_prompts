Here's my situation: upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, 14 students, one 90-minute session. The topic is whether qualia are an incoherent posit. Background, in case you need it: there's a line of argument -- Dennett's old quining qualia move, and a later paper by Chase and Sanborn pushing the idea harder -- which says that when you force someone to say what they mean by "the intrinsic, ineffable character of this particular experience," the thing they point at either turns out to be something specifiable after all (a discrimination, a comparison, a memory, an expectation, a reaction) or it turns out to be nothing. The intuition survives because people almost never have to actually specify the residue -- they just gesture at it. The whole game is making someone state the residue explicitly, case by case, and watching it evaporate (or watching it fight back, which is also philosophically interesting).

I want an activity where the students run into this themselves, before I name any view or hand out any reading. My rough idea: real taste stimuli (jelly beans, cola brands, cheap coffee, whatever), with a few stages -- plain discrimination tasks, a memory-with-interference task (taste something, do other stuff, describe it again), and at least one misinformation moment where a flavor gets mislabeled so they can catch themselves confabulating. Then a final stage where each student has to write down what the quale of one specific taste was *over and above* everything they've already been able to say about it, and defend that against structured cross-examination by their classmates. The cross-exam should have constraints: questioners are only allowed to ask about specifiable things (would you recognize it again, how does it compare to X, what did you expect before it hit, what would you have said if I'd told you it was Y), so that the "purely intrinsic" part can't be gestured at but also can't be run down a rabbit hole. I want it to feel like the residue either gets specified or gets exposed as hand-waving.
