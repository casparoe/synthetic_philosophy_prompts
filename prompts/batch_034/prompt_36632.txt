I am assembling the final unit of my graduate seminar in the metaphysics of science, on the realization relation, and I want my students to come to the papers holding a fixed standard of adequacy rather than a blank slate. Please draft that standard for me.

The question behind the unit: what must an account of realization deliver if it is to do the work functionalists want done — underwrite the autonomy of the special sciences, block any blanket identification of psychological or biological properties with physical ones, and still remain honest about composition and causation? I would like the requirements list itself, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. Some ingredients I have in mind, though you should feel free to add, prune, or reframe:

- It must handle the cross-classification cases without collapsing: a single physical structure that realizes several distinct higher-level properties, and a single higher-level property realized by heterogeneous structures, so that realization can be neither a one-one mapping nor a route back to type identity.
- It must respect the causal inheritance intuition — the sense that the powers of a realized property are exhausted by the powers of its realizers — without letting that intuition quietly convert realization into identity and destroy the autonomy the relation was supposed to secure.
- It must distinguish realization from its neighbors: causation, supervenience, token identity, and mechanistic constitution, since much of the literature slides between them to different effect.
- It must take a stance on the size and shape of realization bases: whether a realizer must itself be a property of an individual, or whether relations, individual-level-and-below properties, or ensemble features of many individuals can realize; and what follows for whether realization is always "same-level or below."
- It must disambiguate what multiple realizability claims amount to at the token versus the type level, because I suspect half the disagreements in this literature are disagreements about which claim was made.

Once the list is on the table, do the philosophical work on it: tell me which entries are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp dressed as constraints — for instance, whether the demand for no emergent powers is a requirement of an adequate account or just a physicalist's wish — and flag any pair or subset of the requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if that is right, the list itself is the interesting result. Finally, identify the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise-plausible accounts of realization, since that is the one I will spend class time on.

You needn't provide a bibliography or attribute arguments to named authors; describe positions by their content and I will match them to the literature when I write the syllabus. And keep any scorecard of existing contenders to a paragraph at most — the students will do that themselves in class, and I want the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them.
