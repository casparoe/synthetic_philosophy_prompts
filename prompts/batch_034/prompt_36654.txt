I work in multi-objective optimization — Pareto fronts, scalarization weights, hypervolume indicators, that whole toolbox. Lately I've been reading philosophy about "incommensurable values," like when you have to choose between moving abroad for a career you love and staying near your aging parents, and neither option is better, but they're also not a tie. I gather there's a whole debate about what's going on there, including a philosopher (Ruth Chang, I think?) who says the options can be "on a par" — not better, not worse, not equal, but still comparable — and that in these cases you sort of create the value by committing to one option rather than discovering it was there all along.

Translate that debate into my vocabulary, as faithful as you can, and then give me the residue report. Because here's my issue: in my world, when objectives conflict you either scalarize (pick weights, which always felt like the user hiding their preferences inside an arbitrary-looking vector) or you hand back the entire Pareto front and let someone else choose — and choosing among Pareto-optimal points isn't optimization anymore, it's a preference you're declining to model. So my instinct is that "incommensurability" just means "I won't commit to a scalarization function," and the "creating value by committing" bit is a dressed-up description of the arbitrary pick among non-dominated options. Is that right, or am I flattening something?

Where I suspect I'm flattening: my setup assumes the objectives are given as fixed numbers you can always query, and that a "hard choice" is just a choice where the gradient is zero. But the Chang view seems to say that at that zero-gradient point, what you do actually changes what matters — like the choice itself is part of the objective function. Does anything like that exist in my vocabulary? Does it have a counterpart anywhere (interactive optimization? preference elicitation where the queries change the preference?) or is it precisely the thing that cannot be said? And if it can't be said — is that a gap in my vocabulary, or is the philosophers' claim less clear than it looks? Be honest about where the translation smuggles in assumptions, e.g. that my treating values as fixed scalar quantities is already taking a side in their debate.
