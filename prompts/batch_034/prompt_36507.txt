Analyze the Terri Schiavo case (1990–2005) as a problem of decision-making under moral uncertainty, and then tell me where the framework breaks. The facts, so we're on the same page: Terri Schiavo suffered cardiac arrest in 1990 at twenty-six, leaving her in what the courts eventually accepted was a persistent vegetative state. Her husband Michael petitioned to remove her feeding tube, testifying that she had said she would not want to be kept alive artificially; her parents fought him for years, insisting she was more conscious than the diagnosis allowed and that removing the tube would be killing her. The tube was removed and re-inserted more than once, with the Florida legislature and then Congress intervening along the way; in 2005, after the courts finally sided with the husband and a last-ditch federal law went nowhere, the tube was removed for the last time and she died thirteen days later. The autopsy showed a brain roughly half the normal weight, damage consistent with blindness, and nothing that supported the parents' picture of a responsive patient.

The moral uncertainty literature offers two main tools: maximizing expected choiceworthiness (weight each theory's verdict by your credence in it) and the moral parliament (let representatives of the theories you partly believe negotiate a compromise). The parties here were uncertain not just about facts but about moral questions: whether a patient in a persistent vegetative state has welfare interests at all; whether removing a feeding tube is an omission with foreseen consequences or an act of killing; whether substituted judgment (what she would have wanted) or a best-interest standard governs when the patient never spoke clearly enough; whether the sanctity of innocent human life has a lexical weight that no quality-of-life consideration can touch.

I want a careful mapping. For each of three decision points — Michael's initial decision to petition, the trial court's ruling that authorized removal, and the final removal in 2005 — say what expected choiceworthiness and what a moral parliament would recommend, using the credences the relevant actor plausibly held. Then the harder parts:

1. Notice that some of the theories at issue deny that Terri is a moral patient at all. In the parliament metaphor, is that a vote for removal or an abstention — and if it's an abstention, does the framework quietly hand the decision to whichever theory attributes the most interests to the most beings? Does expected choiceworthiness face a measure problem when the very population whose welfare is being summed is theory-relative: if one theory says there are no experiential interests at stake and another says there is a massive interest in continued life, is the credence-weighted verdict a genuine aggregation, or is something double-counted or incommensurable in the mix?

2. Lexical prohibitions. If one holds, with some credence, that deliberately ending an innocent life is lexically worse than anything on the other side, does expected choiceworthiness collapse into that theory whenever the credence is nonzero? The parliament was invented partly to work around exactly this. Does it work here, or does it merely relabel the problem?

3. Substituted judgment is itself a moral theory about how to decide, not a first-order verdict — the parliament has to elect its own chair. What happens to this two-level structure when the choice among decision procedures (substituted judgment versus best interests versus a presumption for life) is itself the object of the uncertainty?

Finally, tell me honestly what moral uncertainty cannot explain about the case: why the parties could not even converge on a description of what was being decided, what fifteen years of litigation did to the idea that this was a decision problem at all, and whether the episode shows that moral uncertainty theory captures the epistemics of moral disagreement but not its politics — and, if so, whether some other framework handles both.
