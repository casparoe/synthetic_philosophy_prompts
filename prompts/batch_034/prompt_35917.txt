I've been trying to work out from my gut reactions when everyday conventions are actual moral obligations, and especially when visitors have to follow local ones as opposed to their own. Here's my set of verdicts. Cutting in at the bakery when there's a long queue: not fine, even though nothing in the law says anything about queues. An American dining in the US and leaving no tip: not fine, even though the menu price is technically the "real" price and tipping is officially voluntary. That same tourist tipping a server in Japan: not an obligation at all — I've been told it can even cause embarrassment — so for the visitor the obligation seems to flip with the local convention, whatever it happens to be. Standing on the wrong side of a Tokyo escalator (I've read they stand left there, and in Osaka it's the reverse, which nobody can explain to me): fine, a bit clueless, but I wouldn't call it wrong. A German visitor in Boston at 2am standing at a don't-walk signal with no car in sight while locals stream past: not wrong, just odd — no obligation to break a rule just to fit in. But the same kind of person crossing against the light in Germany while schoolkids look on: wrong, actually wrong, not merely embarrassing. Taking your shoes off in a Japanese home even though at home you never do: obligatory for the visitor. At a market stall in a country where there is no queue, just a general crush of people pushing forward, the visitor who politely hangs back and never gets served is not a sucker, and the locals pushing are not being unfair — so local practice sometimes seems to set what's allowed rather than what's required, but the American in Germany had to respect the light even though plenty of locals cross on amber, which sure looks like the same thing. Also: I think the US tipping system is a bad system, and I still think stiffing the server is wrong — but I'm not sure whether a local who sincerely thinks a convention is unjust is allowed to unilaterally opt out of it, or only to complain while complying.

So my question: is there a principle that gets all or nearly all of these? The pattern I half see is that conventions bind when they're doing cooperative work like dividing a burden or supporting somebody's livelihood (the queue, the tip) and merely coordinate when they're not (escalator side), but then the traffic-light pair bothers me, because both lights look like pure coordination to me and yet the verdicts come out different. And there's a case I keep going back and forth on, so tell me what the principle says about it: a vegetarian visiting somewhere as a guest where refusing offered meat would be a serious affront to the host — can they decline, or does the local convention override the home one there the way it did with the shoes? If you can't save every verdict, tell me which ones to drop, and please state whatever principle you end up with precisely enough to say what it'd rule on a case I haven't listed. (Also, jsut to be clear, I don't want "when in Rome" as the answer — I already know that one, and I can't get it to match my own verdicts.)
