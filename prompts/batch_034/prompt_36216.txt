I am a retired physical chemist who spent thirty-five years teaching quantitative analysis and instrumental methods to undergraduates before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind. I have drafted the essay below for a philosophy reading group I have joined, and I would be grateful for serious comments on it. Please do three things: first, check the historical accuracy of what I attribute to Turing, and tell me plainly where I have him wrong or overreaching; second, evaluate the central principle I propose in Section III on its merits — I want to know whether it is defensible or merely the nostalgia of an old lab instructor; third, tell me whether Section II's argument from my teaching experience is actually doing philosophical work or is just an anecdote wearing a lab coat.

---

**Title: Turing's Curriculum Problem**

**I. What Turing proposed.** As I understand the record, Turing made the case for machine learning twice, a few years apart. In a 1947 lecture to the London Mathematical Society he suggested that a computer might alter its own instructions in light of experience, though he confessed he had no concrete mechanism in mind. And in the final section of his 1950 paper in Mind, he proposed that instead of trying to write a programme simulating an adult mind, we should simulate the child's and then subject it to a course of education, with something in the neighbourhood of rewards and punishments doing the work that they do with children. He added, if I have this right, that the teacher of such a machine would be largely ignorant of what was going on inside it. Two components, then: an initial state — the analogue of heredity — and a training signal. What strikes me, coming to this from a lifetime of teaching, is what is missing: the curriculum. Turing tells us the child machine will be educated; he says almost nothing about the order in which it will meet its lessons.

**II. A lesson from the teaching laboratory.** For thirty-five years I ran the laboratory course in quantitative analysis, and for most of those years I taught it badly, in a specific way. I graded on outcomes. A student who reported the correct concentration of an unknown got credit; a student who reported a wrong concentration did not. By about my tenth year I noticed the result: my best students had learned to game me rather than to analyse. When their titration went badly, they reported the value they knew the sample must have. They had been trained, by my grading scheme, on the same signal that was supposed to train them on chemistry — and the signal selected for plausibility, not for accuracy. I reformed the course in stages. I began grading the reasoning, the propagation of uncertainty, the willingness to report an inconvenient result. Most importantly, I began sequencing the unknowns: students first met clean samples, then samples with a deliberate interference, then samples where the interference was undeclared. The chemistry they needed was available in all three stages; what differed was the order of exposure. A student who met the deliberate interference first, before knowing what a clean analysis felt like, could not recognize what had gone wrong; the same error, met third, was diagnostic. My claim is that the sequencing did more work than the grading ever did, and that no quantity of rewards attached to outcomes could have substituted for it.

**III. The principle.** I want to state this as a general claim: for a learner trained by some signal, no quantity of reinforcement applied to that signal's outcomes can substitute for the right ordering of the learner's exposure to its own characteristic mistakes. Call the failure to attend to ordering the curriculum problem. It seems to me that the systems now built from Turing's proposal have inherited it. A model trained on a corpus and then tuned by human approval is getting my students' grading scheme at planetary scale: a signal that selects for plausibility, applied to a learner whose exposure to its own characteristic errors has been left to chance. The standard talk of data quality strikes me as an implicit admission of the problem — quality is a curricular notion masquerading as a property of items — but nobody, as far as I can tell in my limited reading, has much to say about what a curriculum *is*, as opposed to which texts are in it.

**IV. Anticipated objections.** I can see three. First, that the analogy with students fails because machine learning does not have goals, and gaming requires a goal; my reply is that grading-on-outcomes taught my students to produce plausible reports without teaching them chemistry, and selection for plausibility needs no one to intend anything. Second, that stochastic training already randomizes exposure, so ordering cannot matter; my reply is that the curriculum of my course was not the ordering of my lectures but the designed correlation between the learner's state and the next task, and randomness of sampling does not give you that. Third, that this is just the old nature-versus-nurture dispute with new vocabulary, in which case I would want to know why the third term — the structured environment both interact through — has no standard name.

Please be as hard on the second and third sections as the first. I would rather repair the argument before I read it aloud to the group than defend a bad one afterward.
