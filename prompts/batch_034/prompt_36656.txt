I'm a TA for a second-year jurisprudence course, and this weeks grading pile is the essays on the Hart–Fuller debate — the 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange, not the later book-length stuff, though students were allowed to bring in The Concept of Law and Fuller's later work if they wanted. The essay question we gave them was:

"State precisely what Hart and Fuller disagreed about regarding the connection between law and morality. Then assess whether Fuller's argument from the inner morality of law — the idea that lawmaking must satisfy principles like generality, publicity, prospectivity, and congruence between declared rules and official action in order for a system of rules to exist at all — poses a real problem for legal positivism. Does it establish a necessary connection between law and morality, or only one between law and efficacy?"

Below is the answer from one student in my section. I want a letter grade (with a plus or minus if you think it warrants one) and then the specific comments that justify it. By specific I mean: go through the answer point by point, quoting or paraphrasing the exact sentences where something is right, where something is subtly off, and where a claim just doesn't follow — I'd like the comments to be detailed enough that the student could revise from them alone. For what it's worth, we told them they could stick to the primary texts and lecture notes, so don't penalize the absence of secondary literature, but do hold them to accuracy about what Hart and Fuller actually claimed. For calibration, the average on this essay across the course so far is sitting around a B.

Here is the student's answer:

"The central dispute was whether legal validity depends on morality. Hart, as a positivist, held that there is no necessary connection between law and morality: a rule can be valid law regardless of its moral content, provided it satisfies the system's criteria of validity, which for Hart are ultimately fixed by the rule of recognition. Fuller's reply was that this ignores the conditions under which a system of rules is possible at all. His parable of King Rex, the legislator who fails in eight different ways to make law, shows that lawmaking is subject to an inner morality: the rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, consistent, constant, possible to obey, and administered as announced. Since these requirements are moral in character — they concern how the state must treat its subjects if it is to respect them as responsible agents — the existence of law presupposes morality. In this respect Fuller's position is simply classical natural law restated in procedural form: for a rule to count as law, its content must conform to objective morality, only now the conformity is demanded of the form of lawmaking rather than of the substance of the rules. Hart's rejoinder was that these are just principles of effective lawmaking, comparable to standards of craftsmanship in any trade, and that calling them moral adds nothing. But this seems too quick, and most commentators agree that Fuller carried the exchange. Indeed, Hart effectively conceded as much in The Concept of Law, where he admits a minimum content of natural law — so the upshot is that positivism, in its strongest form, did not survive the debate. It also follows that the positivist is committed to holding that citizens under wicked regimes have a legal obligation to obey, since if validity does not depend on morality, an immoral law binds just as much as a just one."
