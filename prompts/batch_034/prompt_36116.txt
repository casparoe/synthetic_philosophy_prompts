I'm marking finals scripts for a second-year paper in social choice theory and political philosophy. One of the questions set was as follows:

**Question (20 marks).** Sen's so-called liberal paradox. (a) State the weak Pareto condition and the condition Sen calls minimal liberalism, and explain how the latter differs from the stronger liberal condition. (b) Give a two-person, three-alternative example of a preference profile under which the two conditions cannot both be respected by any collective choice rule, and derive the inconsistency. (c) Say what the result establishes about the relation between respect for individual rights and Pareto efficiency, and what it does not establish.

One candidate's answer is reproduced below. Please award it a mark out of 20 and write the marker's comments that justify that mark: go through the script point by point, indicating what earns credit, where it loses marks, and precisely why. I would particularly like you to catch anything a quick marker might let pass. A bare mark with a general impression will not do; the comments should be specific enough that the candidate could learn from them.

**Candidate's answer.** (a) The weak Pareto condition says that whenever every individual strictly prefers an alternative x to an alternative y, society must also strictly prefer x to y. Minimal liberalism says that there are at least two distinct individuals such that each is strictly decisive over at least one pair of alternatives: if such an individual prefers x to y within her pair, society must prefer x to y, whatever anyone else may prefer. It differs from the stronger liberal condition in requiring only two such individuals rather than all of them. The pairs concern matters within each individual's personal sphere, which is what makes the condition one about rights.

(b) Consider individuals 1 and 2 and alternatives x, y, z. Let 1 be decisive over the pair {x, y} and 2 over the pair {y, z}. Suppose 1 ranks z > x > y and 2 ranks y > z > x. Decisiveness of 1 forces the social ranking x > y; decisiveness of 2 forces y > z; and since both individuals rank z above x, weak Pareto forces z > x. The social relation therefore contains the strict cycle x > y > z > x, which no transitive social ordering can contain, so for this profile the two conditions are jointly unsatisfiable. Since a collective choice rule must cope with every possible profile, no such rule can satisfy both conditions. The failure is the same transitivity failure that drives Arrow's theorem; indeed Sen's result is best seen as a special case of Arrow's, with the cycle doing the work that Arrow's argument does there.

(c) What the result establishes is that no collective decision procedure can combine respect for individual rights with Pareto efficiency: every procedure must, on some profiles, either override an individual's right or set aside a unanimous preference. As unanimity cannot reasonably be overridden, the moral is that individual rights, as ordinarily understood, have no place in a theory of collective rationality. A society must choose between liberty and efficiency, and on pain of irrationality it ought to choose efficiency.
