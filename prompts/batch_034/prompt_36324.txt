A friend who has been reading about panpsychism walked me into an argument I can't get out of, and it ends somewhere I can't accept. (1) If panpsychism is true, every physical entity has some experience, however rudimentary. (2) Valence — the felt badness or goodness of a state — is as basic and ubiquitous on this picture as experience itself: matter's states are not all evaluatively flat. (3) So wherever matter undergoes change of the kind that, in a nervous system, we would call damaging, there is bad experience — suffering in miniature. (4) But ordinary physics is nothing but such change: friction, fracture, and combustion are, at the micro level, the same events that in us count as noxious. (5) So every physical process contains some amount of suffering. (6) Moral status comes in degrees with no threshold: any nonzero amount counts. (7) Then the difference between breaking a rock and injuring a person is a difference of degree only, and a difference of degree cannot ground a difference in kind in how we may treat things. His conclusion: either panpsychists must hold that torturing a person is wrong only in the way, and to the degree, that chipping a rock is, or the view collapses under its own ethics — so panpsychism is refuted by its moral consequences. I would like to keep (1), and I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot locate the bad step. Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. Also say what would change your mind: which step, if it turned out to be defensible after all, would most alter your diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
