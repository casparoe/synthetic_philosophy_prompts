I chair the publications review board at a mid-sized research institute — not a journal, we vet our own staff's papers before they go anywhere. We flag maybe twenty submissions a year, mostly mild dual-use worries, and the current process is basically whoever feels strongest wins: if one reviewer thinks a result is too risky, we agonise for weeks and usually publish anyway; if nobody feels strongly, it sails through. Someone told me this is the unilateralist's curse running in both directions — the optimists publish unilaterally by default, but any single pessimist can also stall everything indefinitely. I'd like an actual decision procedure my board can run in a ninety-minute meeting: the questions to ask in order, how to agregate five reviewers' risk judgements without just deferring to whoever is loudest or most senior, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back because we're looking at values rather than facts. Please also say which steps are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and where the whole thing will misfire. Then run it on three plausible cases: a method paper that speeds up vaccine development but could speed up pathogen engineering just as easily; a dataset mapping the locations of critical infrastructure; and a negative result showing a popular DIY biohacking supplement protocol is dangerous. At the very end, give one machine-readable verdict line per case, like \verdict{publish}, \verdict{withhold}, or \verdict{escalate}, plus a single line saying what the procedure could not settle.
