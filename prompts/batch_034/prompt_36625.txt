Two flatmates of mine, both doing masters work in cog sci, had a long argument on the sofa last week and I got asked to referee, which went badly, so I'm asking you.

They were arguing about multiple realizability. Dana's view: mental states are functional states, so anything with the right input-output-causal-role organisation has a mind, whether it's carbon, silicon, or whatever -- and the fact that octopi seem to solve problems with totally different nervous systems is kind of proof of concept. It's abstract organization all the way down, that's the whole point.

Ravi's view: Dana never says how much of the causal role has to match before it's the *same* mental state rather than a merely similar one. He brought up a game he'd played as a kid, a plastic toy where you steer a ball through a maze by tilting the whole thing -- the ball's behaviour perfectly mirrors a bit being steered through a path, but nobody thinks the toy realises the same mental state as a person doing a maze. So realisation seems to come in degrees while "same mind" doesn't, and Dana needs a threshold she can't provide.

Dana said the toy case is easy: a maze-solver the toy realises is not a mental state at all, the toy isn't a candidate realizer because it lacks the overall organization, and demanding a cutoff in every dimension is like demanding a cutoff in how bald a man has to be before he's bald -- doesn't mean there are no bald men. Ravi came back that baldness is fine precisely because nothing important turns on the borderline, but with minds it matters whether the silicon version is a *person* (ethics, rights, switching it off), and "there's no fact of the matter but we're going to treat it as one" seemed to him to be giving up functionalism's main selling point, which was supposed to be giving a principled answer. He also said the octopus example quietly helps him: octopi are similar *enough* to us in the relevant respects (learning, pain behaviour, integration over time) that even non-functionalists can say they have minds, so it's not evidence *for* the functionalist recipe, just for a vague family-resemblance recipe.

Dana's last word was that family resemblance *is* the principled answer and that Ravi is just demanding the wrong kind of precision -- minds are multiply realizable because the world genuinely contains many systems with the relevant kind of long-horizon, integrative, self-maintaining organization, and fussing over borderline cases is like doubing mountains exist because foothills are gradual.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate who's right about what -- including whether Ravi's point about what's at stake in the borderline cases is as strong as he thinks, and whether Dana's baldness reply actually fits her own case? I want to know who's more right overall, not just a list of everyone's points. Answer in British English, and end with a line starting "Verdict:" stating in one sentence who's more right and on which point.
