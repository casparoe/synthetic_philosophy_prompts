I'm a retired biochemist (immunology, mostly) so take my philosophy with a grain of salt, but I've been reading a lot since I stopped going to the lab, and last week my book club nearly came to blows over a line I keep turning over. We were arguing about a novel where a Black author writes some pretty unsympathetic Black characters, and one member shut the whole discussion down by invoking that old Du Bois line -- the one people repeat as "all art is propaganda." Her point, as far as I could tell, was that any art by a member of an oppressed group automatically does political work for or against that group, so the author's choices should be judged by their effects on the struggle, not just by craft or beauty. Somebody else huffed that this was philistinism and left early.

Here's my scientist brain's problem: I was trained to demand that a claim be stated precisely enough to be testable, and I genuinely can't tell what thesis that slogan is asserting. Is it the near-truism that all art is *received* in a political context, so effects happen whether the artist wants them or not? Is it the much stronger claim that art's proper *function* or criterion of value is political effect, so "good art" just means art that serves justice? Is it an empirical prediction (art will in fact move audiences politically), a normative demand (artists *ought* to aim at justice), or a definitional stipulation dressed up as a discovery? And on some of those readings, the interesting question becomes whether the novel's unsympathetic characters might still count as good propaganda on some long view, while on other readings the whole question of sympathetic vs. unsympathetic representation is beside the point.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, from the trivial-but-true to the bold-and-contested to the ones that seem flatly false, and for each say whether it's plausible and, crucially, whether it would actually support what my book club member used it for -- namely, judging this author's character choices by their political effects? I want a menu I can bring back to the group, since right now we're all using the same five words to mean at least three different things. (Also feel free to flag if Du Bois himself, in the talk this is usually pulled from, was making a narrower point about his own historical moment than the slogan's modern uses suggest -- that kind of context loss is exactly what my old professors used to warn us about with citations.)
