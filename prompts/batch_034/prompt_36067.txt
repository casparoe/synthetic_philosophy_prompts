Ok so I just had the longest argument with my friend about Dr. Strangelove and I need to hash it out with someone who actually knows deterrence theory. Background: we watched it together friday night and afterwards she said the film's point is obvious -- deterrence doctrine is insane, it's run by paranoid men, and Kubrick is just showing the whole nuclear edifice is a farce, end of story. And I kept saying no, the film is way weirder than that, because it's arguably the most Schelling-friendly movie ever made, and she hasn't read the strategy stuff so she thinks I'm making it up. Apparently Kubrick actually consulted Schelling while making it, and the doomsday machine comes straight out of the strategic literature of the day (Kahn and all that), which is kind of my trump card in the argument.

Here's where I've gotten to, and where I want help: the core paradox of deterrence, as I understand it, is that a rational actor can't make a threat it would be irrational to carry out, so credible deterrence seems to require convincing the other side you're not fully rational -- or better, that you've genuinely locked yourself in and can't back down even if you want to. The doomsday machine is exactly that: a perfect commitment device, the one thing in the film that actually *works* as advertised. And it only fails because the Soviets kept it a secret -- Strangelove's exasperated bit about how a doomsday machine nobody knows about deters nobody is, as far as I can tell, a completely sincere technical point about deterrence, played for laughs. So is the film refuting deterrence theory or dramatizing it? My friend says those come to the same thing, I think they really don't.

What I'd love is a reading of the film that takes both it and the deterrence/brinkmanship literature seriously, scene by scene where it matters:

- Ripper's rogue launch. Does the film think this shows the doctrine is incoherent, or does it show something more specific -- that the doctrine might be fine and the *implementation* (pre-delegation, command and control, one general with a fluoridation obsession and the recall codes) is the thing that can't be made safe? I want someone to actually distinguish "the theory is self-contradictory" from "the theory is coherent but no human institution can carry it out," and say which one the film is claiming.
- Brinkmanship, the "threat that leaves something to chance." Is Major Kong riding the bomb down literally the image of that idea, chance made visible and cheerful? Or is that me overreading a great shot?
- Mandrake's whole subplot -- getting the recall code out of a dead man's memory, then fighting a payphone and a vending machine to reach the White House. Is the joke that deterrence, the most rational project ever attempted, rests on Coca-Cola machines and office phones? What's the philosophical content of bureaucracy-as-friction here?
- The ending in the war room, the mineshaft plan, the ten women to every man. Does the film cheat there -- collapse into absurdist fatalism in a way the deterrence debate doesn't deserve -- or is that where it earns the right to say something the treatises can't?
- And the thing that bugs me most: the recall code *works*. Almost every plane comes back. The film shows the system almost catching its own error and failing by one bomb. Is that Kubrick saying deterrence is a comedy that only has to fail once, or is it genuinely a story about luck, and what would a deterrence theorist say about a strategy whose success rate is "all but one"?

Also happy to be told where the film trades on confusions the actual theory doesn't have -- like whether it blurs "we can't control our weapons" with "irrationality is required for credibility," which I suspect are different problems that the film runs together.

For context, I'm putting together a talk for a film-and-philosophy series at our local cinema, mixed audience, some philosophy grad students. So it should be a real reading, argued, not just vibes -- and I'd like to be able to end by saying what the film contributes to the deterrence debate that the strategic literature on its own doesn't, assuming it contributes anything at all. And if you think my friend is basically right and I'm the one overreading, say that plainly, I can take it.
