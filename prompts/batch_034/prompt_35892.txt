I'm grading a take-home midterm for an upper-level undergraduate course in moral psychology, and I'd like a second opinion on one answer. The exam question was:

"Distinguish guilt and shame as moral emotions. Is guilt the better of the two for regulating moral conduct? And could a culture deliberately shift its own emotional mix — from shame-dominant to guilt-dominant, or vice versa — through childrening, education, and institutions? Defend your answers."

Below is one student's answer, reproduced in full. Please assign a letter grade (A–F, with pluses and minuses as needed) and give the specific comments that justify it: a point-by-point assessment that identifies what the answer gets right, where it goes wrong, and how serious each error is relative to the standards of an upper-level course — I want to be able to hand the student these comments more or less verbatim. End your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the letter grade.

The student's answer:

"Philosophers and psychologists generally agree on the central contrast between the two emotions: guilt is about what one has done, shame is about what one is. As Bernard Williams argues in Shame and Necessity, guilt responds to a specific act, while shame is a global judgment of the whole self. If I betray a friend's confidence, guilt says the betrayal was wrong; shame says I am a person who betrays confidences. Because guilt attaches to the act, it motivates repair — apology, confession, restitution — while shame motivates hiding and withdrawal, which is useless to the wronged party.

Empirical work bears this out. June Tangney and colleagues found that shame-proneness is correlated with anger and defensiveness, while guilt-proneness is correlated with empathy and a tendency to make amends. So guilt is the better moral emotion: it keeps the response proportionate to the wrong and oriented toward repair.

Guilt is also inherently other-directed: to feel guilt, one must have a specific victim whom one has wronged. By contrast, shame requires only an audience, real or imagined. This maps onto Ruth Benedict's famous typology in The Chrysanthemum and the Sword: in a guilt culture, the sanction is internalized — one feels bad about wrongdoing even when nobody is watching — whereas in a shame culture the sanction is public exposure. A shame culture therefore cannot really produce conscience, only fear of detection.

Since guilt motivates repair and shame motivates withdrawal, a culture that succeeded in shifting from shame-dominance to guilt-dominance would see less wrongdoing overall. And such a shift is possible: parents who discipline by pointing to the harm done to the other person, rather than by scolding publicly, tend to raise guilt-dominant children. Institutions can do the same by emphasizing victims and repair rather than exposure and disgrace. A culture can choose, gradually, through these channels — and it should choose guilt."
