A universal basic income, as its advocates specify it, is a regular cash payment from the state that is (i) uniform in amount across recipients, (ii) paid to every individual adult as an individual, rather than assessed per household, (iii) not reduced or withdrawn as the recipient's earnings rise, and (iv) attached to no conditions at all — no job search, no work requirement, no demonstration of need. Each of these design choices contrasts with the social assistance systems most countries actually run, which are means-tested, conditional, often provided in kind (housing support, food assistance, direct services), and typically assessed at the household level. So the question I want to put to you is: what does this package of design features presuppose? What theory of welfare, of entitlement, and of the person does it only make sense under?

Consider the features one at a time. Uniformity in amount: the same payment to the healthy and the chronically ill, to residents of cheap towns and expensive cities. Since UBI advocates usually justify the payment's *level* by appeal to what a person needs — enough to live on — a uniform amount for people with very different needs looks odd, and suggests the payment is not really aimed at need-satisfaction but at something else: a floor of security or status, an unconditional standing as a member of the society. What conception of what the payment is for makes uniformity a feature rather than a bug? Cash rather than in-kind provision appears to presuppose a preference-satisfaction view of welfare together with a strong anti-paternalism: the recipient is a better judge of what improves her life than the state is. Unconditionality in the strong sense — no work requirement whatsoever — seems to rule out reciprocity-based theories of distributive justice, on which one's claim to support is grounded in contribution or willingness to contribute. And individual rather than household assessment quietly takes a side in an old dispute: it presupposes that claims attach to persons and not to family units, and hence that one spouse's income should not reduce the other's claim.

I'd like a committed answer rather than a survey. Pick the reconstruction you think best rationalizes the design — the one under which the most features come out as consequences of the theory rather than as political accommodations — and defend that choice. Say explicitly what your favored reconstruction rules out (for instance, whether any desert-based or reciprocity-based theory of entitlement could license unconditional cash at all), and identify honestly where the design is genuinely underdetermined, so that two quite different theories would license the same features and the features presuppose neither. Please also address the internal tension I flagged: can a level justified in terms of needs sit consistently with a uniform amount across people with different needs, or is one of those two considerations really doing the justifying while the other is rhetoric? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change if your implicit theory were swapped for a rival — what would a basic income designed by a thoroughgoing reciprocity theorist look like, and would it still deserve the name?
