I teach moral philosophy, so philosophy of medicine is a bit outside my lane, but a student in office hours gave me a rundown of the health/disease debate and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it. Her summary went roughly like this: naturalism, going back to Boorse, says disease is nothing but statistical deviation from species-typical biological functioning, so on the naturalist view an elite athlete or someone with an unusually high IQ would literally count as diseased, since those are deviations from the norm too. Normativism, meanwhile, is the view that disease is just whatever a society judges undesirable, so a normativist has to accept that baldness or shyness or aging could become diseases by consensus, with no fact of the matter beyond that. And since naturalism supposedly can't make any sense of why doctors treat things and patients complain about things — that being the whole evaluative point of medicine — she concluded the debate is basically settled and normativism won. My instinct is that several steps here are off, but I dont know the literature well enough to say where. Go claim by claim: which parts would a defender of the biostatistical theory accept, where is a modest thesis being inflated into something the view doesn't say, what do normativists actually commit themselves to (is "whatever society decides" their view or a caricature of it), and does the conclusion follow from whatever parts survive? She also made a point about how the naturalist is forced to treat a beneficial deviation — say, unusual resistance to a local pathogen — as pathological, and I'm curious whether that's a real problem for the view or just a confused example.
