At a public lecture on the origin of the universe, the speaker brushed off the question of why there is anything at all with the remark that 'nothing is unstable'. He spoke as though the one line did a great deal of work: it was meant to explain why there is something rather than nothing, and perhaps even to show that the question needs no further answer. I have since come across the phrase repeatedly in popular physics writing, always offered as if it were self-explanatory. Please separate out the distinct precise claims 'nothing is unstable' could be making — from the comparatively trivial (an already existing quantum vacuum produces fluctuations, so the 'nothing' was never really nothing), through the more ambitious (some physical theory entails that a state with no particles, no fields, and perhaps not even space or time is unstable and gives rise to something), to the frankly metaphysical (that absolute nothingness is somehow impossible or self-undermining). For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is backed by physics as opposed to philosophy dressed up as physics, and, crucially, whether it actually answers why there is something rather than nothing, or merely relocates the question to whatever 'nothing' was left standing. Please do not leave me with a menu and no verdict: commit to the reading the lecturer was most probably relying on, and say plainly whether, on that reading, the slogan succeeds in dissolving the question or merely masks it.
