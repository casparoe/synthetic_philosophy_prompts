I'm a graduate student coordinating a year-long reading group in value theory, and our two most senior members have spent that year locked in a disagreement about hard choices that everyone else thinks has become partly verbal. One of them is a comparativist: she holds that the better-than relation over options is complete, so for any two alternatives in a choice, one is strictly better, strictly worse, or exactly as good as the other — and that apparent cases to the contrary are really imprecision in our value concepts, uncertainty about the facts, or disguised ties. The other holds the parity view associated with Ruth Chang's work on hard choices: some alternatives are neither better, worse, nor equal, but stand in a fourth relation of being "on a par," which is why choices between, say, two careers or two lives of comparable but differently shaped value can be hard in a way that no amount of information or conceptual cleanup will dissolve. They have agreed to let me draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration, and I want to get it right rather than produce something bland they'd both sign because it says nothing.

Please write the document. It should have a numbered section of claims both parties can sign — I'd expect at least some of these: that hard choices are not merely cases of evidential uncertainty; that a chooser facing one can act rationally without it being true that she chose the better option; that the phenomenology of struggle in such choices is real but not by itself evidence for any meta-view about comparability; and that "on a par" and "exactly equal" come apart in at least some descriptive sense, since equal options are interchangeable while par options resist arbitrage-like treatment in a way I'd want them to make precise. Then a disagreements section, ideally as a table, where each row states a single point of residual disagreement in wording its own side would accept — this is the part they'll fight over, so the formulations must be neither vacuous nor slanted.

Finally, the cruxes, which I suspect include at least these: whether the small improvement argument for parity actually works or only shows something weaker; whether the comparativist's appeal to vagueness or imprecision in value can reproduce all the phenomena without a fourth relation; and whether the parity theorist's claim that in hard choices we can create reasons by putting ourselves behind an option presupposes incomparability or is equally available in cases of exact equality. For each crux, give the argument, case, or test both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance — for example, what each predicts about a case where a small improvement is made to one of two alternatives that seemed tied, and whether the improved option must thereby come out better. If you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — say, a disagreement about whether "as good as" is being used to mean precise equality — identify which part and why, and separate it cleanly from what remains substantive.
