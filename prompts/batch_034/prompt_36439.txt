I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been in and around the "taxation is theft" argument for years — comment threads, arguments with libertarian coworkers, more than one family dinner. What strikes me is that it never moves. Both sides concede the same core facts — taxation is compulsory, the money would otherwise be yours, governments spend it on things at least some taxpayers would not buy voluntarily — and yet the theft camp and the consent camp each treat their conclusion as obvious. My working hypothesis, from watching the arguments closely, is that a verbal dispute is doing a lot of the work: one side uses "theft" as a legal term (unlawful taking), under which the claim is trivially false because taxation is lawful, while the other side uses it as a moralized term (taking what one has no right to take), under which the slogan just restates the substantive question and smuggles in the answer. But I suspect something deeper is layered underneath: the sides pick different baselines. One treats pre-tax income as the natural starting point, so any confiscation needs justification; the other treats the whole legal and fiscal framework as prior, so there is no fact of the matter about what "your" pre-tax share even is without it. It reminds me of arguing about whether a diff is an addition or a deletion when the two parties have different base commits in mind. So: is this disagreement ultimately verbal, substantive-but-hidden, or both stacked on top of each other? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a definitional cleanup, a substantive argument about whether property rights are prior to the state, or nothing short of one side's bedrock intuitions changing.
