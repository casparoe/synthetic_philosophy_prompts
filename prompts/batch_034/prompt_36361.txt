Been reading rationalist blogs since maybe 2010 and one thing I never fully sorted out for myself is when to actually trust the aggregate. The Condorcet jury theorem says: if each voter is better than 50% accurate and votes independently, a big enough majority is right with probability as close to 1 as you want. Fine, the math is fine. But when I go by my actual gut on cases, I'm not sure the theorem is tracking what I care about. Here are my verdicts, tell me if there's a principle that captures them:

- 500 strangers guessing the weight of an ox: trust the median over any single guess, including mine. Easy.
- 500 Yelp reviews saying a taqueria is great, vs one foodie friend who's never been there: I go with the crowd. The crowd at least ate the food, my friend is going off vibes.
- Same 500 reviewers saying a philosophy book is badly written: I trust that way less than the taqueria case. Feels like one person's bias ('philosophy = pretentious') copied 500 times, not 500 separate biases.
- 10,000 people each right 51% of the time on some hard binary call, fully independent. The math says the majority is right with probability ~1, and I'll grant the math, but in any real version of this I refuse to update much, because 'barely better than chance AND independent' basically doesn't happen in the wild — if 10k people all lean the same way it's almost always because they share an upstream source.
- A poll average before an election: 20 polls, but with correlated error (same methodologies, same likely-voter screens, shared house effects). I trust the average more than the best single poll, but not 20-polls-worth more — like maybe the equivalent of 5.
- A prediction market with tons of volume but a few whales doing most of the trading: I discount it hard, even though on paper thousands of participants traded.
- A Wikipedia deletion debate where 30 editors vote keep but they all belong to the same wikiproject: don't trust it, maybe even update toward delete.
- Voters each slightly worse than chance (49%), independent: adding voters makes the majority *worse*, so whatever principle I want can't just say 'more opinions = better,' competence has to enter somewhere.

My tentative principle is something like: trust the aggregate insofar as the errors are independent and each voter is at least a bit better than chance, and scale your trust by the *effective* number of independent opinions, not the raw headcount. But here's the pair I can't square: I trust the crowd on the ox's weight and the taqueria without having any real evidence those people are above chance, while I'd take one mechanic with a great track record on my car over a survey of 500 drivers without hesitating. So maybe the real variable isn't 'competence + independence' at all, it's 'is the error idiosyncratic noise or is there a common cause' — but if it's the common-cause thing, I'm not sure what happens to the competence condition at all, since a shared bias makes everyone's track record look fine until it doesn't.

So: give me the principle (or a couple of candidates), what each clause is doing for which verdict, what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on — two subcultures, each internally super-correlated, with biases in opposite directions; is averaging the two group verdicts good (biases cancel) or bad (you've really only got two data points) — and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle just cannot save.
