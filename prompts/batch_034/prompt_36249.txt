I'm a philosophy PhD student, and for the past year my philosophy of mind reading group keeps ending up in the same fight about epiphenomenalism. It's become pretty obvious that nobody's changing their mind, and I'd like someone to step back and tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. The setup, in case it matters for the diagnosis: epiphenomenalism says phenomenal experiences are caused by physical brain states but have no causal power of their own, which is supposed to let you keep both the causal closure of physics and the reality of consciousness. The recurring fight is about the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment. If my qualia don't cause anything, then my saying "I'm seeing red right now" is a physical event with entirely physical causes — the qualia themselves contribute nothing to producing the judgment or the utterance. So why do my judgments about my experience line up so neatly with the experience itself? One guy in the group (the skeptic) thinks this is fatal self-undermining: a theory on which our reports about consciousness don't track consciousness can't itself be believed on the basis of anyone's reports or judgments, including the epiphenomenalist's own belief in epiphenomenalism. The defender in the group keeps making two moves: first, that the correlation between experience and report is guaranteed by the psychophysical laws, which is a lawlike regularity and arguably all the "tracking" anyone needs; second, that every theory of mind faces a cousin of this problem — the physicalist also says introspective judgments are just brain events that happen to be about other brain events, and everyone has brute laws somewhere. Neither move budges the skeptic, and the skeptic's complaint that this is mere re-description doesn't budge the defender. They have now had essentially this same exchange, I swear, six or seven times. Someone once tried to bring in James's old thought experiment about the beloved who turns out to be a soulless automaton, and that just added noise.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who has watched this debate run in circles in the literature for decades, and actually think it through carefully before answering — feel free to brainstorm out loud for a few sentences before settling on a diagnosis. What I want is not a verdict on who wins, but a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement. Here is my working hypothesis, treat it as a hypothesis and push back on it if it's off: I suspect the two of them are operating with different demands on what counts as explaining a judgment — the skeptic thinks a judgment's being "about" or "accurate" requires counterfactual sensitivity (if the qualia had been different, the report would have been different), which epiphenomenalism flatly cannot deliver, while the defender only requires lawful correlation, which it delivers as well as anyone does. If that's right, the "paradox" might just be the sound of a counterfactual theory of aboutness colliding with a regularity theory, with both sides taking their own theory as the obvious reading of "reliable." Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden substantive question underneath it — say, about how surprising fine-grained psychophysical correlation is on plain naturalism versus something like pre-established harmony — that would make the dispute empirical or probabilistic rather than verbal? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress: some sharper notion of tracking, a distinction between the reliability of the judgments and the reliability of our reports of the judgments, or genuinely nothing, because the two sides just weigh the cost of brute laws differently and there is no fact of the matter to converge on. Engage with what each side is actually saying rather than giving me a survey of the positions, and be honest if you think the question is just ill-posed.
