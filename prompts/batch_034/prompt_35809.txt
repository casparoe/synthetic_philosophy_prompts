Something I keep chewing on in my day job: ML engineers routinely "distill" a model into a much smaller one, or port a capability from one architecture to a totally different one, and then report in papers that the two models "have the same capability" -- even though every weight differs, the layers differ, sometimes the whole architecture differs. Nobody thinks this is a category error. What view of what a model *is* does that practice presuppose? Is it basically functionalism plus multiple realizability imported into engineering -- the capability exists at some level of abstraction from the substrate, and the substrate can be swapped -- or is it a weaker, merely practical claim about benchmark interchangeability that a realist about mechanisms could also sign off on? And where does the analogy to minds actually get shaky, e.g. is there anything in a neural net that plays the role of a pain-state, something that individuates across substrates the way functional states are supposed to?

Here's the twist I actually want your read on: mechanistic interpretability research points the other way. Its whole pitch is that you can't understand a model at the computational level alone, you have to open it up and find the actual circuits and features, the way you'd study a brain -- which sounds almost anti-functionalist, like insisting real understanding is substrate-bound. So do these two widespread practices, distillation and interpretability, presuppose compatible things, or is the field quietly running on two inconsistent views of the same object? If the mechanistic people are right that the circuit-level story can come apart from the capability-level story, what does that do to the presupposition the distillation results rest on? And flip it around: if a hardcore functionalist is right, in what sense is mechanistic interpretability doing anything more than convenient bookkeeping?
