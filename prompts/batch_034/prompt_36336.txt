I'm a program officer at a mid-sized foundation that funds a peculiar mix: medical research, animal welfare initiatives, and global health projects. Our board includes a utilitarian economist, a devout Catholic, a contractualist lawyer, and someone who refuses to be pinned down but has strong views about future generations. In practice, our funding decisions often turn on disagreements among these moral views rather than on empirical questions, and the current method for handling this is shouting, followed by whatever the loudest board member remembers from dinner. I want better: a written decision procedure I can actually run on each funding round, drawing on whatever serious work exists on decision-making under moral uncertainty — expected choiceworthiness across theories, the different ways of pooling theories' rankings (averaging versus bargaining versus majority rule among views), the problem that theories don't come with a common unit of value, and what to do with views that contain absolute constraints rather than tradeoffs.

The procedure should cover: how to enumerate the relevant theories for a decision without the list ballooning; how to tell whether theories genuinely disagree about an option or merely reach the same ranking for different reasons; how to handle a view with a lexical constraint (a theory that forbids an act outright, where no size of benefit to others compensates) when other theories assign the act a huge positive value; what to do when intertheoretic value comparisons can't be made even approximately; and when the procedure should stop computing and hand the decision back to the board as a matter of moral judgment that no procedure can absorb. It should say which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and it needs to be runnable in an afternoon by someone without philosophy training, since we decide on roughly twenty proposals per funding cycle with two weeks per cycle.

Then run it on three cases. First: a proposal to fund a campaign that accelerates the transition away from conventional cage systems in poultry farming, versus a proposal to expand a crisis intervention service, where the theories agree on the ordering but for wildly different reasons — does the agreement matter or should the procedure treat it as fragile? Second: a proposal to fund a wildlife intervention that kills a small number of wild animals to prevent a disease that would cause a much larger number to die slowly — here a rights-based constraint plausibly forbids the killing while the aggregative views strongly favor it. Third: a proposal aimed at reducing catastrophic risks to humanity's long-term future, where the disagreement turns on whether future people who don't yet exist can be harmed — and where the officer running the procedure (me) finds the long-termist case persuasive and should learn whether the procedure shares my bias. I want the procedure applied honestly, including wherever it yields a result I won't like.
