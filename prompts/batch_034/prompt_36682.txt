I am a retired chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up the study of philosophy, and this term I am leading a seminar on decision theory at a local lifelong-learning institute. Having no formal training as a philosopher, I find myself unsure how to grade one of my student's essays fairly, and I would be grateful for a careful assessment. The assignment was as follows:

"Good's theorem states that for a Bayesian agent facing a fixed decision problem, the expected value of acquiring costless but noisy information about the state of the world is never negative. Explain why the theorem holds, and then assess the following claim: 'It follows that a rational agent can never have any reason to decline freely offered evidence.'"

Here is my student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

"The theorem holds because, whatever signal arrives, the agent conditions on it and then chooses the act that maximizes expected utility under the resulting posterior. For each particular signal, that posterior-optimal act must be at least as good, in posterior expectation, as sticking with the act that was optimal under the prior. Averaging over all possible signals, the expected payoff of acting with the information is therefore at least the payoff of acting without it, so the information's expected value is never negative. It follows that a rational agent should always accept free evidence. For instance, if a clinic offers you a genetic test at no charge, you should take it: if the result changes which option is best for you, you can act on it, and if it does not, you can simply ignore it. The same reasoning applies in any setting whatsoever. In a salary negotiation, or even an interrogation, more information can only improve your position, since you can always disregard what you learn. The only genuine exceptions arise when the information is costly to obtain, or when the source is unreliable, in which case the message may mislead you. Apart from those cases, the claim is correct: declining free evidence is irrational."

Could you assign a letter grade and provide the specific, point-by-point comments that would justify it? I want to be certain that I neither penalize the student for steps that are in fact sound nor credit passages that merely sound plausible.
