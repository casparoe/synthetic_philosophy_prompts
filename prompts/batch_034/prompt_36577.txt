I run a little "weird ideas" meetup at my local library — about 14 adults, very mixed backgrounds, nobody has any psych or philosophy training, we get 90 minutes once a month. Next month I want to do split-brain stuff, the patients who had the band of nerves between their hemispheres cut and then (as I understand it) one hand would do things the person couldn't explain — like one hand trying to button a shirt while the other keeps unbuttoning it, or they get shown a chicken claw in one visual field and a snow shovel in the other, pick a shovel picture with the "unseen" hand, and then confidently explain the shovel choice by talking about the chicken. I might be garbling the details, feel free to correct me — I've never studied this formally, I just got obsessed after a documentary and then way too many evenings down a rabbit hole.

Here's what I'm asking for. I don't want a lecture, I want an activity where people actually FEEL how creepy it is that the talking part of you may have no idea what the rest of you is doing, and just makes up a story afterward. My half-baked plan so far: pair people up. One is "the talker" — sits facing the group and only gets information through a card the facilitator holds up or reads out, maybe on one side of the room only, I'm not sure how literal to be about the left-field right-field stuff. The other is "the left hand" — they get different instructions (put the red card in the box, move the lamp, whatever) through notes only they read. Then the group interrogates the talker: why did the hand do that? And here's the rule I really want: the talker is not allowed to say "I don't know." They have to give a confident answer, because that's what I read the real talking hemisphere did — it just smoothly made up reasons, no embarrassment.

Please design the whole thing properly: exact setup, rules, how many rounds and what each round should test, materials I can print or grab from a dollar store, timing, and a debrief at the end that connects what happened back to the real cases. Also, two specific things I need you to deal with:

1. My pet theory, which I keep coming back to at 2am: maybe a normal intact brain is like this all the time — the talking part of me is basically the guy who writes the minutes of a meeting he only half attended, and he's been handing those minutes off as "me" my whole life, and the surgery just makes the trick visible. I want the activity to set that up so the participants arrive at something like it themselves, without me preaching it. Can you build that in, or tell me straight if I'm overreaching and the actual research doesn't support stretching it to normal brains?

2. I also ran into the claim that the actual patients mostly reported feeling like one unified person after the surgery, no drama, which honestly confuses me more than the cases do. If that's right, how should the debrief handle it without the whole evening collapsing into "welp, guess it was nothing"? And be honest about which parts of the real phenomenon an activity like mine just cannot reproduce no matter how well it's run — I'd rather know now than find out mid-debrief.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what do I do about it? I've never run anything with this much structure before, so write it so I can print it out and follow it, not an essay about consciousness.
