I work in people analytics at a large employer, and lately I've been responsible for the "adverse impact testing" we do on hiring screens — including algorithmic ones. The practice, for anyone unfamiliar: under the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, US employers treat a selection procedure as showing evidence of adverse impact whenever the selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group falls below four-fifths (80%) of the rate for the highest-selected group. So if we pass 60% of applicants from group A and 40% from group B, the impact ratio is 0.67 and the screen is flagged; the practical workflow is then to either drop the procedure or show it is job-related and consistent with business necessity. With algorithmic screens, the common pattern is to test the model on a held-out dataset, and if the ratio dips below 0.8, re-tune or adjust thresholds until it passes. It's a purely distributional test: nothing about the mechanism, the inputs, or the causal path from application to outcome enters at the checking stage.

I've been trying to figure out what theory of fairness this practice actually presupposes, and I keep finding it slippery. On its face the four-fifths rule presupposes that fairness is a fact about outcome ratios across groups — something in the neighborhood of demographic parity, which is striking, because demographic parity is one of the fairness criteria that formal work treats as most problematic: when base rates of the underlying trait differ between groups, satisfying it seems to require passing over higher-scoring members of one group in favor of lower-scoring members of another, which conflicts with any "rank by score" view of merit and can lower overall utility. And there are results showing that when base rates differ, you cannot simultaneously have a calibrated score and equal error rates across groups, so any single threshold-based test has to give something up. Meanwhile, the business-necessity defense points in a completely different direction: it says that outcome disparities are acceptable if they trace to job-relevant traits, which sounds like fairness is about the process and the inputs, not the outcome distribution at all — closer to "the score measures what it claims to measure, apply it evenhandedly, and let the rates fall where they may." So the same regulatory apparatus seems to presuppose an outcome-based fairness conception at the screening stage and a process-based one at the defense stage, and I can't tell whether these are genuinely in tension or whether the practice is deliberately layered so that only the conjunction of the two commitments is real — a rebuttable presumption rather than a criterion. There's also the awkward fact that the four-fifths threshold is a rule of thumb with no statistical grounding: it ignores sampling error badly at small n, so the same underlying procedure can be "fair" or "unfair" depending on applicant-pool size, which seems like it shouldn't follow from any serious theory of fairness.

So here's what I'd like from you. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of fairness the adverse-impact testing practice presupposes — does it commit us to demographic parity, or to something weaker like a presumption against unexplained disparities, and what exactly separates those? Second, say which rival fairness conceptions the practice rules out (calibration-within-groups, equalized error rates, a purely causal or process-focused view) and which it is merely compatible with. Third, be honest about whether the layered structure — a distributional screen plus a process-validity defense — is a coherent single theory or an unstable compromise, and whether the impossibility results make the incoherence deep rather than incidental. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in day-to-day practice if the presupposed conception were replaced with, say, an equalized-odds standard: what would we test, what would we tune, and what kinds of screens that currently pass would fail (and vice versa). Please lead with the reconstruction rather than a survey of fairness metrics, and end your response with a single final line of the form "Verdict: " followed by one sentence stating whether the four-fifths rule, as actually practiced, presupposes one coherent conception of fairness — and if so, which one.
