I've come around to something like Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, mostly via rationalist-adjacent extinction-risk discussions — the claim that human extinction is a distinctive bad over and above the deaths it involves, because a lot of what makes our lives go as they do depends on the assumption that humanity will continue after we die. Projects that take longer than a life, saving for grandchildren, even caring about who wins the World Cup — Scheffler thinks these would deflate if we learned the world ends shortly after our own natural deaths, even for people whose individual lives go on exactly as long as expected. I like it because it justifies caring about extinction without having to argue that future people have moral status that outweighs ours, which is the part of the standard longtermist package I never fully bought.

So: what would count against it? I don't want just "here's an intuition pump," I want the stuff that should actually move me. Obvious candidates that occur to me: empirical evidence that most people do NOT in fact structure their lives around an assumed collective afterlife (or that they'd report no change at all in a doomsday-forecast condition); cases where people already live with certain apocalyptic expectations — religious communities expecting an imminent end — and their lives don't look deflated in the relevant way; or the suspicion that "many of our values depend on the assumption" quietly slides between "depends causally" and "depends for its point." And the standard worry about views like this: has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? Scheffler is famously careful about whether he's making a moral claim, an anthropological one, or just describing what we're like, and I've never been sure whether that carefulness is wisdom or a load-bearing hedge.

Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, say which a committed Schefflerian would concede and which they'd shrug off, and end with an honest verdict on how exposed the view really is — including whether, if it's mostly insulated, that's a problem for a claim of this kind or just what you get with philosophical anthropology. FWIW what would personally move me most is a well-documented real-world case of people living well with a foreseen, near-term certain end, so tell me if you know of any that dont just cut the other way.
