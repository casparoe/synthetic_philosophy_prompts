I'm a program officer at a small regional foundation that disburses about $3 million a year in grants of roughly $20,000 to $250,000, across a messy portfolio: youth programs, a food bank, a river cleanup coalition, some workforce training pilots. Quarterly cycles, a board of twelve that has to sign off on anything above $100,000, and no research staff — it's me, a half-time analyst, and whatever the applicants themselves send in. I've been reading philosophy in the evenings (I took a couple of courses in college) and I've become obsessed with the problem philosophers call consequentialist cluelessness: the observation that essentially every action sets off causal ripples we cannot trace — a childhood vaccination in 2010 changes who exists in 2080; a well-run literacy program displaces some other program that would have run instead; the counterfactual effects of what we *don't* fund are as unknowable as the effects of what we do. Proponents of act-utilitarian-style reasoning often respond with things like expected-value estimation or fall-back heuristics, and critics reply that when you're guessing at the sign of the second-order effects, the arithmetic confidence is fake. I don't need the philosophy settled. I need it usable.

Please write a decision procedure my office could actually run on a funding proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, which answers should stop the process with a no or escalate it to the board, and — this is the part I care most about — where the procedure has to give up and hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values or unquantifiable judgment rather than facts. The procedure should be honest about the difference between an intervention with traceable local effects and one whose case rests on long untraceable chains, and it should tell me what to do when both kinds are on the table at once (as they always are at allocation time). It has to be runnable in a day per proposal by someone with no statistics training beyond a spreadsheet.

Then run it on three hypotheticals of the kind that actually cross my desk: (1) $80,000 for a food bank to expand weekend meal backpacks to two more school districts, where the main claimed benefit is direct and local but the applicant's own letter admits it will pull volunteers from a neighboring program; (2) $150,000 for a workforce training pilot whose proponents claim a plausible chain from better training to better jobs to reduced incarceration decades out, where every link past the first is a guess; and (3) $30,000 to keep a small, beloved, demonstrably mediocre tutoring program alive, whose strongest argument is that if it dies the school will redirect the space to a study hall. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where cluelessness genuinely bottoms out and the honest output is not a number but a choice someone has to own.
