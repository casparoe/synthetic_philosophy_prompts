I direct the field operations of a humanitarian aid organization in a country where a rebel commander controls the only viable supply route into a drought-stricken region of roughly 150,000 people. He has offered a deal: our convoys will get safe passage, and in exchange we must take on a number of his relatives as paid local staff who will do essentially nothing, and include in our next public situation report a sentence crediting his administration for ensuring humanitarian access — which is false, since the aid exists only because of us and our donors. My board is split. One faction argues that refusing means letting people starve over a matter of principle, and that anyone who chooses this line of work should have accepted long ago that effectiveness sometimes requires a sullied conscience. The other faction replies that once we publish the false sentence we stop being an honest organization and become part of his propaganda apparatus, and that there is no principled stopping point from there. Is there a principled way to decide this, or is the choice between doing wrong and allowing a worse wrong simply built into the job? And if I do go ahead, does it matter whether I treat it as a grim necessity that I will have to answer for, rather than as ordinary business?
