I keep going back and forth on whether to be a realist about the stuff interpretability researchers find in neural nets, so let me just lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle that captures them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Case 1: a linear probe on an image classifier finds a direction that decodes the species of the animal in the photo with 97% accuracy. But intervening along that direction barely changes the output — the model's actual decisions run through some other, messier subspace. My verdict: no representation there. The information is in the activations, but the model isn't using it, so calling it a "species representation" is our projection.

Case 2: a sparse autoencoder feature that fires on the Golden Gate Bridge across tons of different contexts, and when you clamp it to a high value the model starts bringing up the bridge in totally unrelated outputs. Verdict: obviously a representation.

Case 3: a transformer trained on chess move prediction, and there are directions that track which pieces are on which squares — steering them changes which moves the model treats as legal. Verdict: representation, yes.

Case 4: a "zebra direction" that decodes zebras great on the training distribution, except it turns out to be keyed to dry savanna backgrounds — zebras on a beach or at a zoo get misclassified, and it also fires on photos of oryx. Verdict: not a zebra representation. But then what is it, a savanna representation? A "zebra-context" representation? I don't know, I just feel strongly it's not a zebra representation.

Case 5: a direction that predicts whether a language model will comply with a request, and ablating it flips compliance to refusal. Works beautifully on the distribution where it was found, falls apart on new prompt types. Verdict: genuinely torn.

Case 6: a unit that tracks some legible concept, but the network is redundent — ablate it and a backup copy elsewhere takes over, behavior identical. Verdict: I still want to say it's a representation. Feels wrong to deny the model represents the concept just because it's robust to damage.

So the obvious tension: cases 1 and 6 both involve "ablation changes nothing," but I say no representation in 1 and representation in 6. And cases 2 and 4 are both clean decoders, but only one of them feels real. Is there a principle here — something like causal efficacy plus content stability plus the content being what's actually doing the work? — that gets all six, or close to it? If not, which of my verdicts should go, and why that one? Then tell me what your principle predicts for the case I honestly can't decide: a direction that decodes whether a story's narrator is lying, in a model that was only ever trained on next-token prediction, never anything about lying, and the feature is causally efficacious — ablating it makes the model lose track of the lie in the story. Representation of lying, or just predictive structure we've named after the fact? And separately: does whatever principle you land on make you more or less of a realist about interpretability in general?
