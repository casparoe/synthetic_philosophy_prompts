In my philosophy of biology reading group we have been arguing about adaptationism for two years, and the debate has stalled in a peculiar way. After Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper, everyone in the room concedes that constraints, pleiotropy, linkage, drift, and exaptation are real and important; everyone also concedes that many traits are genuine adaptations shaped by selection. Nobody defends the naive "Panglossian" position the original critique attacked, yet the adaptationists and the pluralists in the group have not moved an inch. My working hypothesis is that the residue is a dispute about burden of proof and research aesthetics: whether testing adaptive hypotheses is a virtuous default research strategy, on the ground that a risky-but-checkable adaptive story generates more empirical work than an appeal to constraint, or whether it is a standing risk of just-so storytelling that biases the field toward adaptive explanations. On this reading the disagreement is not about biology at all. A friend disagrees: she thinks there is a genuine empirical question here — roughly, what fraction of phenotypic structure selection actually explains as opposed to merely reshuffles — and the debate is stalled only because methods for quantifying constraint and distinguishing adaptation from exaptation are immature. Diagnose the disagreement. Is it substantive, verbal (perhaps over how the word "adaptation" carves up trait history), a disagreement about which questions are worth funding, or my friend's immature-but-real empirical question? Support your diagnosis with what the two sides actually say and do, and consider the precedent of the units-of-selection debate, which arguably was not won by either side but dissolved once formal tools like the Price equation and contextual analysis let the parties state exactly what they were claiming. Would something analogous — a formal decomposition of a trait's selective history, a methodological convention about null models, or something else — count as progress here, or is there a principled reason to expect this dispute to persist?
