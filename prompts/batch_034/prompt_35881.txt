I am a high school student working on an extended essay about science and explanation, and I have gotten hold of Daniel Dennett's book Darwin's Dangerous Idea. In it he distinguishes cranes from skyhooks: a skyhook is an explanation suspended from nothing—an irreducible mind, a miracle, an unexplained first cause—while a crane is an explanation assembled step by step out of simpler mechanisms that are already understood, and Dennett argues that every good explanation, especially in biology, has to be built out of cranes. He also attacks what he calls greedy reductionism, the urge to explain something in one leap by pointing at its smallest parts, and he treats this as a serious intellectual vice. Putting these together, my understanding is that Dennett demonstrated that reductionism is a false doctrine: that explaining a whole by its parts is exactly the mistake he warns against.

What puzzles me is that practicing scientists talk this way all the time, as if the doctrine were true. Chemists describe chemical bonding as nothing but electromagnetism; biologists describe an organism as nothing but chemistry plus natural selection; and I have read popular pieces in which neuroscientists describe a person's decision as nothing but a burst of electrical activity in certain circuits. If a prominent living philosopher has shown that this style of reasoning is mistaken, why does it persist throughout the sciences? The possibilities I can think of are that scientists are behind on the philosophy, that they are using a looser sense of the phrase that is harmless, or that Dennett's argument does not really apply the way I think it does.
