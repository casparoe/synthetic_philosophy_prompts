Next month I'm on a conference panel where I've been assigned the job of defending illusionism, and the organizers asked for a one-page statement of what I actually believe, to circulate to the respondents in advance. I drafted it, and on rereading I can't tell whether it's one coherent position or several views stapled together. Before I send it, I want an audit. Here is the draft, condensed but faithful:

(1) I am an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness. There are no irreducibly phenomenal properties: nothing in my mental life has an intrinsic qualitative character over and above its physical, functional, and representational properties. The familiar hard problem is a mistake to be diagnosed, and what replaces it is the illusion problem: explaining why our introspective systems represent our mental lives as having such characters at all.

(2) The illusion problem is a problem for cognitive science. It will be addressed by describing the mechanisms of introspection and self-monitoring and showing how those mechanisms output the judgment that experience is phenomenally qualitative. It is not a conceptual problem to be settled from the armchair.

(3) Introspection is, in the ordinary run, reliable. When I sincerely judge that I am in pain, that judgment is normally true, and I trust judgments of this kind enough to let them guide my behavior, ground my learning of mental vocabulary, and settle what I report to my doctor.

(4) Suffering matters. That suffering is bad, and that its badness is what makes it matter, is not something I am prepared to treat as an illusion or a projection; it is a datum that any theory of consciousness owes an account of. I do not officially exempt suffering from my illusionism, but I also cannot bring myself to be an illusionist about it in the way I am about the taste of coffee.

(5) Whether a sophisticated AI system could acquire moral standing turns on whether there is something it is like to be it. I take that to be a genuine question with a fact of the matter, not a verbal dispute that dissolves once we drop a certain vocabulary.

(6) The knowledge argument fails because Mary, having every physical fact, thereby has every fact there is. Whatever she acquires on leaving the room — recognitional abilities, imaginative dispositions — is not knowledge of a further fact, because there is no further fact.

Tell me whether this hangs together. For each pair you find in tension, say how severe the tension is, what would have to give, and what the revision costs elsewhere in the set. Do not treat everything that smells funny as a contradiction: I suspect some of these pairs are only apparently inconsistent, and the respondents will not be careful about that, so I need to know in advance which flags are real. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and close by saying which single change would clean up the most. As you go, please state how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
