The student's answer:

The demandingness objection says that utilitarianism is too demanding: since we could always do somewhat more good by giving more to charity, working longer hours, and so on, act utilitarianism implies that almost all of our ordinary activities — leisure, spending on ourselves, time with friends — are wrong. Bernard Williams made this the centerpiece of his famous attack on utilitarianism: he argued that consequentialism destroys a person's integrity because it makes her a mere channel for the maximization of value, so that no project of her own can matter. That is why commonsense morality looks more plausible: it builds in permissions to favor ourselves and those close to us. Kantian ethics is also less demanding, since the categorical imperative requires us only to treat persons as ends in themselves and never merely as means, which leaves plenty of space for pursuing our own ends.

The best-known consequentialist reply is Liam Murphy's fair-share principle. Murphy argues that utilitarianism is correct as a collective principle: the total amount of good we are required to produce is the utilitarian optimum. But each individual's share is only her fair share of that total. So when others shirk, your own obligation rises to compensate: you must make up their shortfall, or the collective target will not be met. Critics object that this is demanding in exactly the same way as before, and Murphy concedes that his principle only works when compliance is high.

Either way, the demandingness objection shows that any agent-neutral theory that counts everyone's welfare equally cannot give agents any space of their own. So the objection is decisive against utilitarianism as a theory of what we ought to do, even if utilitarianism survives as a standard for evaluating institutions and public policy.
