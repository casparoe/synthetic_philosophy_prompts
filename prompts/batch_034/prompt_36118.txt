kind of a weird question but here goes. i'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and i've been trying to understand this thing epistemologists call the bootstrapping problem, or "easy knowledge". here's my best reconstruction, tell me if i'm off: you want to confirm that some method of forming beliefs is reliable — say, your color vision. so you go through life checking: i judged that thing was red, and (as far as i can tell) it was red. i judged that one was blue, and it was blue. after enough of these, you conclude: hey, my color vision tracks facts pretty well. the accusation is that this is somehow cheating — you used the very faculty under test to score the test. the thing I don't get is why exactly. it's obviously circular in some sense, but plenty of reasoning looks circular at that granularity and seems fine.

the closest thing we have in my world is self-hosting compilers: the compiler that builds our next release was itself built by the previous release. nobody thinks that bootstrap gives you evidence the compiler is correct — it shows at best that it reproduces. if we actually want to know something about the toolchain, we cross-check against something independent (diff against a different vendor's output, hand-inspect the assembly for a few programs). is the epistemology case like that (intra-method checks carry no weight at all, and only independent validation would), or is it subtler? I've seen hints that the subtlety is something like: the individual observations were already "in the bank" before you ever asked about reliability — you didn't need the reliability assumption to earn them, so maybe using them isn't circular in the deadly way. does that distinction actually do any work, or is it a dodge?

so basically: explain what the bootstrapping/easy knowledge problem is, what the alleged fallacy is supposed to be beyond ordinary circularity, and whether the self-hosting compiler analogy is fair or whether epistemologists are working with a stronger standard than engineers. feel free to use programming analogies, they land well with me.
