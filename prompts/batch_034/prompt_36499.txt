The essay question was:

"The Historikerstreit of 1986 turned in part on whether and how the Holocaust may be compared with other atrocities. Separately, Zygmunt Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust (1989) argued that the Holocaust should be understood as an outcome of modernity rather than a relapse into barbarism. Answer both parts: (i) Distinguish the claim that the Holocaust is unique from the claim that it is comparable to other mass atrocities, and explain how the two could consistently be held together. (ii) State Bauman's central thesis, and say whether, on his account, the Holocaust flowed from modernity as such or from a contingent configuration within modernity."

The student's answer reads:

"(i) To say the Holocaust is unique is to say that it differs from all other atrocities in kind, not merely in degree; to say it is comparable is to say that it belongs to a broader type — genocide, state-directed mass killing — about which shared questions can be asked. The two are compatible: an event can be without precedent in kind while still being one instance of a wider class, since we compare events along some dimensions while acknowledging that along others nothing resembles them. In the Historikerstreit, Ernst Nolte argued that there was no moral difference between the crimes of the Soviet Union and those of Nazi Germany, and that the Nazis acted as they did out of fear of what the Soviet Union might do to Germany, so that the Nazi crimes were in substance a response to, and an imitation of, the earlier Bolshevik terror. Jürgen Habermas, writing in Die Zeit, attacked this as an attempt to relativize the Nazi past and thereby normalize it. But this exchange shows that the two claims cannot in fact be combined: if the Holocaust is unique in kind, then there is no kind to which it and other atrocities jointly belong, so comparisons of the sort Nolte drew are impermissible in principle, and Habermas's objection to Nolte is simply the denial of comparability as such.

(ii) Bauman's central thesis is that the Holocaust is to be understood as an outcome of modernity: it was made possible by the distinctive apparatus of the modern state — bureaucratic hierarchy, the fragmentation of tasks, instrumental rationality, and the substitution of technical for moral responsibility. Most of those involved did not need to be antisemites; it was enough that they perform their function competently, at a distance from its human object, which is why ordinary, decent people could contribute to the destruction without ever confronting the moral meaning of the whole — a point Bauman presses home with the Milgram obedience experiments. Bauman's deeper lesson, then, is that this apparatus is only a thin veneer laid over human nature: once the restraints of civilization are removed, what surfaces is an old barbarism, and the Holocaust is therefore best described not as a product of modernity at all but as a relapse into a premodern pattern of savagery that modernity had merely kept suppressed. Moreover, since Bauman shows that modernity supplied the necessary conditions, it follows that once a society reaches a comparable level of bureaucratic rationalization, something like the Holocaust is to be expected of it: the more modern a society becomes, the closer it stands to its own Auschwitz. So on Bauman's account, the Historikerstreit's focus on specifically German guilt is ultimately beside the point — if the causes were general features of modernity rather than anything specifically German, ascribing special responsibility to Germans confuses a moral question with a causal one."
