In two weeks I defend the central chapter of my dissertation at my department's metaphysics of science seminar, and I need help preparing for the question period. The chapter argues that special relativity settles the ontology of time in favor of the block universe. The argument is essentially the Rietdijk–Putnam line, patched in two places. First, against the charge that simultaneity is conventional, I let the argument run on an arbitrarily chosen but then fixed inertial frame rather than claiming frame-invariant conclusions, and I argue that whichever frame you pick, every event ends up fixed or "real" — determinism about what exists follows from any frame, so the conventionality doesn't matter. Second, against the objection made famous by Howard Stein — that in relativity the present is a strictly local notion that cannot be extended to global hypersurfaces, so all we are entitled to is becoming relative to a spacetime point — I concede the locality but argue that point-relative becoming cannot do the work presentists and openness-of-the-future theorists want it to do, since it carves out no global distinction between a settled past and an open future, and the openness we care about when we deliberate seems to be global or nothing.

The audience is about a dozen faculty and graduate students with very different commitments. There is one card-carrying presentist who works on Newtonian spacetime and thinks physicists have been allowed to bully metaphysicists into "physicalist" views of time; a philosopher of physics who knows the Rietdijk and Putnam papers and Stein's replies inside out and has zero tolerance for loose talk about simultaneity; a graduate student in philosophy of quantum foundations who keeps insisting that I am ignoring the fact that our best cosmological models come with a preferred cosmic time, which would apparently let the presentist back in through the front door; and several colleagues who are officially neutral but share a standing suspicion that any argument from physics to metaphysics is moving too fast to be trusted.

Please prepare a full Q&A prep sheet: the objections I should actually expect from this room, in roughly the order they will come and phrased the way each person would phrase it, ranked by how much damage they would do to the chapter. For each objection, give a reply I could realistically deliver in a seminar setting in two or three sentences, plus a note on where the reply is soft and what I should concede up front rather than bluff about. Please make sure to cover the transitivity worry about Putnam's "determines what is real" relation — I have never fully understood whether it is fatal or merely pedantic, and I suspect the philosopher of physics will raise it. Also include at least one objection about whether the argument's conclusion is really eternalism or merely that the future is determined, since a determinist presentist might try to accept my premises while denying my ontology. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what to say if someone asks it anyway.
