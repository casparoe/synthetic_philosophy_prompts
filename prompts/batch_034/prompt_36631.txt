I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training -- my decision theory comes entirely from arguments I've encountered in online discussions about how agents ought to choose, not from coursework. I want to sharpen my objections to updateless reasoning by arguing with someone who actually believes it, so please play a committed updateless decision theorist: intelligent, well-armed, willing to grant me minor points, but you never give up the core commitment that an agent should choose as if deciding the output of its decision procedure everywhere that output is consulted, including in situations it can see it isn't in. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to two paragraphs or fewer so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. In the counterfactual mugging, Omega flipped a fair coin and predicted whether I would pay $100 if asked. On tails, it set aside $10,000 for me if and only if it predicted I would pay; on heads, it simply asks me for the $100. The coin has come up heads, I can see that it came up heads, and the $10,000 either exists or doesn't independently of anything I do now -- yet you tell me to pay, on the grounds that my decision procedure's output was also consulted in the tails branch, where refusing leaves my counterpart empty-handed. Now put alongside this the XOR blackmail case: a predictor knows whether my house has termites, and it sent me a threatening letter if and only if it predicted I would pay $1,000 on receiving it. If I have termites, the letter is a signal I'd rather not have received, but paying changes nothing; your theory refuses to pay. From where I sit the two cases have the same structure: my current act is causally isolated from everything of value, and the only channel connecting my choice to outcomes runs through a predictor's model of me. The mugging's other side is a coin; the blackmailer's other side is an infestation. So spell out the actual difference that makes paying rational in one case and irrational in the other -- and if your answer amounts to checking which policy pattern-matches to intuitively good outcomes, admit that, because then the theory is doing no work I couldn't do with my gut.
