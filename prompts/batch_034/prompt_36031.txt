I am a retired condensed-matter physicist — thirty-five years at a national laboratory, most of it spent on neutron scattering and the endless chore of instrument calibration — and for the past two years I have been auditing lectures on philosophy of mind and working through the literature on consciousness with a reading group of fellow retirees. I have become increasingly fascinated by the view, associated with Daniel Dennett, that consciousness is a kind of user illusion: that the stream of experiences I take myself to be directly acquainted with is less like a window onto the brain's workings and more like the icons on a computer desktop — a simplified, purpose-built interface the brain presents to itself. Before I decide whether I accept it, I want to test my objections against the strongest available version of that position rather than a caricature of it.

Please play a committed and sophisticated defender of the user-illusion view — someone in the spirit of Dennett, speaking in your own voice: sharp, patient with an honest interlocutor, willing to concede minor points but never the central claim, and never breaking character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to at most two paragraphs so that this feels like a live exchange; if you think one of my questions rests on a misunderstanding, say so plainly rather than tiptoeing around it, and then press the objection you think I should have raised instead.

Here is my opening move. In my working life I thought a great deal about instrumentation errors, and every misleading instrument reading I ever encountered was misleading for someone: the ghost peak on the spectrum looked like a real signal to me, or to the graduate student watching over my shoulder, and the illusion lived in that encounter, not in the trace itself. When you tell me that the phenomenal character of my experience — the look of the Cherenkov blue in a reactor pool, which I can still summon up vividly — is an icon on a desktop, a useful fiction, I want to know: a fiction for whom? If you reply that the user is just another icon, I confess I can no longer tell what your view denies. It cannot be that nothing at all went on during the decades I spent squinting at oscilloscope traces; so was there something it was like to be me at that bench, or not? And if there was, what exactly is left of the claim that it was an illusion? I await your response in character.
