I write about science and health policy for a regional paper, and I've spent two years sitting through our county health board's treatment-funding committee meetings for a piece on how these people *actually* make tradeoffs when the values don't line up. The dirty secret is that nobody on the board would ever say out loud that lives, mobility, sight, and mental health are incommensurable -- they all talk the cost-per-QALY language -- but watching their votes, I can't find a single currency they're actually using. I want your read on what rule they're really running before I commit to a thesis for the article.

Here's their track record on the decisions I've dug into:

1. They rejected a moderately priced drug that would add about a year of life on average for a large group of older heart-failure patients, and took the cheaper alternative that adds maybe eight months. Clear cost-effectiveness vote, or so I thought.

2. Then they approved an insanely expensive gene therapy for a rare fatal childhood disease -- extends life maybe four years, at a per-patient cost that would've made them laugh the heart-failure drug out of the room. Their stated reason was something about the severity of the condition, but severity of a fatal condition was already priced into case 1 and they voted no there.

3. They turned down a moderately priced surgery that would fully restore mobility for a small group of adults with a chronic degenerative condition, while approving a somewhat *more* expensive surgery restoring full vision for a similar-sized group. If they're trading everything off into one scale, this vote makes no sense -- vision won even though it cost more per person and affected the same number of people.

4. Last spring they kept funding a long-running home dialysis program for elderly patients that's way past any cost-effectiveness threshold they've ever applied to new stuff, while cutting a perinatal mental health program that by every outcome measure they track was doing more measurable good for less money -- just for fewer, younger people.

Next month's docket: a proposal for a costly therapy that restores partial hearing in adolescents with a congenital condition that's moderately common (nowhere near orphan-drug rare), not life-threatening in any way. Per-patient cost is in the same ballpark as the mobility surgery they rejected in case 3.

I can see at least three or four rules that fit their record so far: cost-effectiveness with a carve-out for severe/rare/fatal stuff; some kind of lexical ranking where certain capabilities (sight? life?) just outrank others regardless of cost; a priority for the young or worst-off that keeps overriding the ledger; or maybe just plain status-quo bias where existing programs are protected and new ones face a much higher bar. What I need from you is a committed call: which rule do you think is actually driving these people, what's your prediction on the hearing therapy vote and why, and -- since I can't sit through their meetings forever -- what one future case would most cleanly seperate the front-runner rule from its closest rival? I'm writing a thesis-driven piece, so "the rules all fit, could go either way" is useless to me. Pick a horse, defend it, and flag where you think I could be embarrassing myself if I print this. (Also, if you think I'm wrong that these decisions are hard to fit on one scale -- that there's some obvious commensurating rule I've missed that explains all four votes -- tell me that instead, bluntly.)
