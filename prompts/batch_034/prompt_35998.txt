I'm a PhD student in philosophy, finishing a dissertation on Sartre, and my advisor — who has spent his entire career defending the strictest reading of radical freedom from *Being and Nothingness*, against every softening amendment and every compatibilist peace offering — is retiring this June. I've been asked to give the toast at his departmental retirement dinner, and I'm dreading it. The room will be split: half philosophers, several of whom have published against him on freedom and determinism and will catch any slip; and half family, including his two adult children, who have heard the phrase bad faith deployed at Thanksgivings for thirty years and do not want one more lecture on their father's big night.

Write me the toast, around four minutes when read aloud, subject to two constraints that I'd like you to treat as real structural requirements rather than decoration:

First, it must not commit the very fault he spent his career diagnosing. No treating him as a finished essence — no "he was always," no "it was just who he was," no "knowing him" — and no determinist-flavored praise of the "the field shaped him" or "he was a product of his era" variety. His view, which the toast should enact rather than argue for, is that a person is nothing over and above what they make of what's made of them, up to and including the moment of retirement. The hard part is that praise itself tends toward ossification: a retirement toast is exactly the genre that turns a person into a settled set of qualities. I'd like the toast to solve that problem rather than quietly ignore it.

Second, it has to land emotionally for the family without any technical vocabulary, while giving the colleagues two or three moments where they recognize the view being performed rather than stated — done through what the toast refrains from saying, or through how it attributes things to him. At least one of these moments should be a genuinely funny line about bad faith or about philosophers retiring that a non-philosopher can enjoy for entirely different reasons.

Begin with the toast itself, no preamble, no commentary before or after.
