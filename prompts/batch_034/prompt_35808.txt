quick context: i'm a postdoc with a pretty mathy background (graph theory, dynamical systems, a bit of information theory, basically zero neuroscience beyond what i picked up from pop books and reading papers here and there). I just got a three-year fellowship to work on consciousness science, and after a lot of dithering I'm seriously considering putting most of it into work connected to Integrated Information Theory -- not necessarily as a true believer, more because it seems like the one big theory where my skillset could actually matter and where the math is underdeveloped. But I keep going back and forth on whether IIT is a research program worth joining or a beautifully engineered dead end, so before I commit I want to think hard about what's actually worth working on inside or around it.

so here's my ask: give me a prioritized agenda. The candidate open questions as I see them (feel free to add or kill some):

1. The tractability problem. Phi, in basically any of its versions, is computationally intractable for anything beyond toy systems -- small grids of logic gates, toy Markov chains. Is developing principled approximations or bounds on Phi a legitimate research program, or does approximating it risk throwing away exactly the thing that made the theory distinctive? And is there any version of this where a math person can make real progress in ~3 years?

2. The exclusion postulate and the grain problem. IIT says only the maximum of integrated information "exists" from the intrinsic perspective, which is supposed to explain why there's one unified experience rather than overlapping many. Is this a well-posed claim? Can it be sharpened into something a mathematician could actually work on (e.g., conditions under which the maximally irreducible subsystem is unique vs ill-defined)?

3. The methodology of deriving the axioms. The theory starts from axioms meant to be self-evident features of phenomenology (intrinsic existence, composition, information, exclusion, etc.) and translates them into postulates about what physical systems must have. Is this derivation doing real work or is it just codifying intuitions? And if the axioms are contestable, does everything downstream inherit that fragility?

4. The weird commitments -- feedforward networks with a huge number of elements can supposedly have high Phi, pure grid structures having enormous Phi, etc. Are these genuinely testable predictions or just bug reports? Where's the line between "the theory says counterintuitive things, good for it" and "the theory misidentifies what has experience"?

5. The sociological fight, i.e., the recent mess where a big group of scientists signed a letter calling IIT pseudoscience and IIT people cried foul about scientific gatekeeping. I don't want to spend my fellowship litigating this, but it does affect whether work on Phi is publishable and fundable -- so how much should it factor into what I take on?

For each: why it matters relative to the others, whether it's upstream of the others (e.g., if the axioms are shaky, does anyone need tractable approximations of Phi?), whether it's better suited to a math person like me or really a philosophy problem, and what finding would move it way up or down your list. Also tell me honestly if you think the right call is that none of these deserve three years of a mathematician's time and I should be doing something else entirely -- i'd rather hear that now than in year 2. and if some questions should be done in parallel because they're independent, say so. thanks!!
