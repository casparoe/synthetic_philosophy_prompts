I've ended up pretty convinced by poverty-of-the-stimulus arguments for innate language structure — the idea that the linguistic data a child actually receives is too thin to determine the grammar they acquire, so something domain-specific and innate has to be closing the gap. The case that sold me is structure dependence: when kids learn to form questions, they seem to know to move the auxiliary of the main clause rather than the nearest auxiliary ("Is the boy who is crying hungry?", never "Is the boy who crying is hungry?"), even though the input arguably never tells them which of the two rules is the right one — every question they've ever heard is compatible with both. Add that parents supposedly don't reliably correct their children's grammar (when they do push back, it's usually about truth, not form), and it looked airtight to me when I first worked through it in a course a couple years ago.

But I keep hearing this is less settled than it looks, and I'd rather stress-test my own view than defend it. What would actually count against it? Not the generic "children are great statistical learners" hand-wave — I want the specific findings and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal through merely awkward. Some candidates I've come across and want assessed honestly: corpus work finding more usable signal in child-directed speech than the argument assumes (indirect negative evidence, distributional cues that actually discriminate structure-dependent rules from linear ones); modeling results where a learner with no built-in syntax gets the auxiliary-inversion rule right from realistic input; cross-cultural cases where kids acquire language fine on far less child-directed speech than the argument implicitly assumes, which seems like it could cut both ways; and the claim I've heard that when you go back and chase down the original sources cited for "parents don't correct grammar," they don't quite say what they're supposed to say. For each one: would a committed innatist concede it counts against them, or wave it off, and on what grounds? And is the view still risking anything at all, or has it been qualified — say, retreating from a spelled-out universal grammar to "some innate bias, unspecified" — into something unfalsifiable? If so, tell me whether that's a fatal flaw for a claim of this kind, or just the honest end state for a big-picture nativism. Also happy to hear about defeaters I havent listed, especially ones the view's own defenders would admit to rather than just the ones critics push.
