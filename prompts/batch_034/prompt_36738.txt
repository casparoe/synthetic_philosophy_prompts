I'm preparing a seminar session on the meaning of life that pairs absurdism with contemporary accounts of meaning, and I want to build the session around Groundhog Day, since the film stages the debate more neatly than any of the stock examples in the literature. I'd like a reading of the film that takes both sides seriously, and I have a specific puzzle I want addressed.

I should say up front that I'm deliberately not framing this through Camus. The move where you call Phil a Sisyphus who befriends his boulder is the one everybody makes, and I don't want to spend the session on it. The interesting friction, as I see it, is between Nagel's version of the absurd and Susan Wolf's account of meaning as the fit between subjective attraction and objective attractiveness — and the film is a genuine problem for that pairing, in a way I'll try to spell out.

Here is how I see the structure. The film divides cleanly into phases. Phil first exploits the loop — the theft, the seductions built on memorized details about strangers — then collapses into despair (the run of suicides), and finally reconstructs himself: piano, ice carving, French poetry, the small daily rescues, until the loop breaks. Read through Nagel, the middle phase looks like the absurd made visible: the collision between taking one's days seriously and the standing ability to view them from outside as arbitrary and inconsequential — a view Phil can take quite literally, since tomorrow the world will be exactly as if none of it happened. Read through Wolf, the final phase looks like the fit view in action: meaning returns precisely when his subjective engagement (he comes to love the town and the day) meets objective attractiveness (skill, kindness, things in the day worth engaging with). So far, so tidy.

The first puzzle is the reset. Nothing accumulates outside Phil. The old man dies no matter what he does; the boy falls out of the tree every morning and will again tomorrow; the piano teacher starts each day never having met him. Everything that persists, persists only in Phil. So either the film holds that the objective pole of meaning can be supplied entirely by what a person becomes, with no worldly residue — which requires Wolf's view to say something it does not obviously say — or the film is quietly counting the value of the days themselves, one at a time, rather than their products. Which is it? And whichever answer you give, can Wolf's view actually accommodate it, or does the film force a revision?

The second puzzle concerns the ending and Nagel. Nagel holds that the absurd cannot be escaped: we cannot stop taking our lives seriously, and we cannot, from inside, make the external view go away; the collision is permanent, and irony or defiance are the only stances available. But the film appears to dramatize an escape: the last Phil we see wakes on February third delighted with ordinary life, wanting to stay in the town he spent the film despising. Is that a refutation of Nagel — evidence that the external view, once fully inhabited, can be dissolved by transformation rather than merely endured? Or does the ending in fact concede Nagel's point, since the resolution comes not from the external view losing its force but from Phil re-investing his seriousness in the very day he had learned to survey with detachment — that is, by going back to taking it seriously, which is exactly what Nagel says we cannot help doing? I genuinely cannot tell whether the film disagrees with Nagel or agrees with him in the most vivid way available, and I'd like this settled.

So, three deliverables. First, adjudicate the reset puzzle: say where the film locates the residue of meaning — in the world, in the person, or in the days themselves — and be explicit about whether that answer fits Wolf's view, revises it, or breaks it. Second, resolve the ending question: does the film escape Nagel's absurdity or re-enact it, and point to what in the earlier phases should lead us to your answer. Third, tell me where the film resists both readings — scenes that fit neither the absurdist frame nor the fit frame — and what an honest critic has to concede there. And if there is something the film shows about meaning that neither Nagel's nor Wolf's arguments manage to say on their own, I'd like that stated plainly at the end; it would make a good closing note for the session.
