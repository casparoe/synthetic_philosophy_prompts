I'm a third-year PhD student in machine learning theory, and this term I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar out of curiosity. The thing that keeps surprising me is how the no-free-lunch theorems I know from optimization and statistical learning theory — roughly, that averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm outperforms any other, so any advantage a method has on some problems is exactly balanced by its disadvantage on the rest — keep brushing up against the problem of induction without either room quite connecting them. Both are, at bottom, about whether past performance is any guide to future performance, and the theorem looks like a formalized, general version of Hume's point. Before I go evaluating any actual answer to Hume — the straight rule, Bayesian priors over model classes, Popperian falsificationism, reliabilism, whatever comes up in seminar — I want the specification first, because I suspect half the disagreements I'm seeing are people optimizing against different unstated requirements. So: write me the desiderata. What must an adequate response to the problem of induction actually deliver?

Fix the purpose as follows: the theory needs to be good enough to justify, or at least rationally reconstruct, the working scientist's or ML practitioner's confidence that fitting a model to Wednesday's data will tell her something about Thursday. Not to dissolve Hume in full generality, and not to deliver certainty — relativize the standard of adequacy to that job. Within that purpose, I want the requirements argued for one by one, each with its motivating case. The ones I can already see mattering: non-circularity (the response cannot have uniformity of nature, or future-resembles-past, hidden in its premises); NFL-compatibility (either the response accepts that no method dominates uniformly and so must relativize success to some measure over possible problems — and then either justify that measure non-circularly or admit it can't — or it explains why the theorem is somehow beside the point for induction, e.g. because real worlds are not drawn uniformly over functions, which itself looks like an inductive claim); handling Goodman's grue, since explaining why projecting green rather than grue isn't arbitrary is a microcosm of the whole problem; and a distinction I suspect is under-appreciated in seminar, namely between showing induction is justified, showing it is rationally required, showing it is indispensable, and showing it is merely the best of the available alternatives — these come apart, and a theory should say which one it is claiming.

One more thing to fold in: someone in seminar claimed, and I haven't been able to shake, that there is a result to the effect that even though no fixed method is uniformly best across worlds, a strategy that at each step switches to whichever method has performed best so far is provably almost as good as the best method in the long run, no assumptions about the world at all — performance being defined entirely by past payoffs. Part of me wants to say this finally answers Hume non-circularly; another part suspects the long-run framing smuggles induction back in through the same door it was thrown out of. Treat this as a candidate to be assessed against the desiderata, not as a settled solution. Finally, tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particular camps, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — my instinct says non-circularity, NFL-compatibility, and being action-guiding may not be jointly satisfiable, and if so, that is the part I most want to see worked out — and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. Skip the scorecard against actual inductivist positions; the criteria are the part I can't do myself.
