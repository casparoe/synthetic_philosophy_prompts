I've been in a reading group on Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations for about a year now, going through the secondary literature on what everyone calls Kripkenstein (the stuff from the 1982 book Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language). We've gotten to the point where all the moves are familiar -- the skeptical paradox with plus and quus, the dispositionalist straight solution and the standard complaints that dispositions are finite and, more importantly, that they get normativity backwards (my disposition to make errors can't settle what I meant, since I can be disposed to get it wrong), the "no-fact" conclusion, and then the two big escape routes: Kripke's own skeptical solution where meaning talk gets justified by communal assertibility conditions rather than truth conditions, and the straight solutions in the style of Boghossian or McGinn that insist there are meaning facts but deny they reduce to descriptions of finite behavior or natural properties. And it's become obvious to me that nobody in the literature has really moved in forty years. The dispositionalists keep re-engineering their view to dodge the normativity objection, the fact-asserters keep saying the skeptical solution conflates the conditions for asserting that someone means addition with what makes it true, and the skeptical-solution camp mostly just reiterates that the straight solutions change the subject.

Here's my working hypothesis, and I want you to evaluate it seriously rather than just surveying positions like every online explainer does: I suspect the stalemate isn't really about meaning at all. It's a disguised fight about whether normativity can be naturalized -- whether "I ought to answer '125' now" is the kind of fact that could in principle be identical to, or constituted by, any descriptive fact about my behavioral or neural or Platonic profile -- and the arithmetic examples are just a vivid stage for that older fight. If that's right, then progress on rule-following would look like progress in metaethics and philosophy of mind generally, and no cleverness about quus-functions specifically will budge anyone. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a genuine localized disagreement about, say, whether grasp of a rule is a state (something you're in, which must have truth-constituting conditions) versus an ability or practice (something you can do, which doesn't need metaphysical grounding) -- one of our members keeps pushing this basically Rylean line and I can't decide if it dissolves the paradox or just relabels the problem.
