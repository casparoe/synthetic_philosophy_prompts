I'm on my city's monuments and naming review committee (long story, it's a mid-sized city and the committee is mostly retired professors plus one extremely organized librarian), and we have a case coming up next month that has me genuinely stuck, mostly because I've been trying to figure out what rule we've even been running these past few years. Our track record so far: (1) Two years ago we voted unaminously to remove a bronze statue of a Confederate general that went up in 1962, explicitly as a message during the civil rights era — nobody on the committee even tried to defend keeping it, and it's in the county museum now. (2) Around the same time we kept the statue of our founding-era governor, a slaveholder, and added a contextual plaque, on the reasoning that what we were honoring was his role in founding the state university, not his plantation. (3) Last year we renamed Arndt Street, which had been named back in the 1950s for the state's most prominent eugenicist, a doctor remembered locally for running the forced sterilization program — when we discussed it, nobody could really say what else the name was doing. (4) But we refused to rename Havlicek Street, named for the man who donated the land for the park it borders, even after a historian published his private letters showing he was viciously racist; the neighborhood association fought hard to keep the name, and the committee basically said the street honors the donation and the racism was never part of why he was locally remembered.

The new case: a statue of an early twentieth-century mayor, erected by public subscription in the 1920s, with dedication speeches that were all about the hospital system and the parks he built. But a documentary that came out last year laid out in detail how he was the architect of the city's racially restrictive housing covenants, and the neighborhoods those covenants created are still segregated to this day. The statue stands right at the edge of one of those neighborhoods. Residents there have petitioned for removal, while a citywide nonbinding referendum this spring favored keeping it 58–42.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of our earlier cases is the closest analog? The rules I can see fitting our record so far come apart here: one keyed to what the original dedication actually celebrated (which points toward keeping, like the governor and like Havlicek, since nobody in the 1920s was honoring the covenants); one keyed to whether the subject's principal claim to being honored just is the wrong (which pointed toward removing the general and the eugenicist, and is murkier here since his fame rests on the parks but the covenants were arguably part of the same governing project); and one keyed to whether the commemoration currently does dignitary harm to people still living with the consequences, which seems strongest here but arguably also applied to the Confederate statue we removed. I suspect our precedents underdetermine this one, so tell me what principle we'd be adopting with each possible outcome — remove outright, relocate to the museum, keep with a plaque, or keep as is — and give me your best recommendation with reasons. Also, if you can think of a hypothetical next case that would really cleanly separate those rules (something we might plausibly get next year), I'd love to have it in my back pocket, because I'm fairly sure at least one of my colleagues is running a rule none of us has articulated out loud yet and I want to smoke it out.
