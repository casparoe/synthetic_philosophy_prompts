My department has a long-running seminar on personal identity, and for two years now two of its members have been arguing past each other without either conceding anything. One is a philosopher sympathetic to the Buddhist anatta tradition: she holds that what we call the self is a conceptual construction imposed on a causally connected but impermanent stream of physical and psychological processes, that careful analysis (and, she adds, careful meditation) finds no owner behind the processes, and that nothing of ethical importance is lost by giving up the owner. The other defends a fairly orthodox psychological-continuity account: he holds that a person is constituted by overlapping chains of memory, intention, and character held together by causal dependence, and he insists that this is not merely a useful convention but a genuine relation that grounds prudential concern and moral responsibility over time — though he is happy to concede that no immortal, indivisible Cartesian ego exists. They have now agreed to run a structured adversarial collaboration for the spring term, and I have been asked to prepare the document they will jointly sign at the end.

Please draft that joint statement. It should have three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign — and I want it to be genuinely substantive rather than vacuous: things about what the classical arguments show and don't show, about the fission and teletransporter cases, about what survives the denial of a soul, about the old Buddhist worry that if there is no self, then one person cultivates the path and someone else reaps the benefit, and about whether the Milindapañha-style chariot analysis (naming a conventional person in terms of parts, as one names a chariot in terms of its components) settles anything or merely relabels the dispute. Second, a table of their exact remaining disagreements, with each row's wording phrased so that the camp named in that row would accept the formulation as a fair statement of its own view; for example, over whether psychological continuity is what matters or merely the best available human-friendly summary of a process in which nothing is what matters, and over whether a narrative self is a metaphysical discovery or a practical accomplishment. Third, the cruxes: I suspect one is whether the sense-of-self's reported absence in sustained meditation is evidence about the self's nonexistence or only about the sense, and another is whether the no-branching rider a psychological-continuity theorist needs to handle fission is a principled constraint or an ad hoc patch that reveals the underlying account was never about a real relation. For each crux, give at least one concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both parties have already agreed in advance would bear on it, along with each side's recorded prediction about what the case shows, so that the seminar can check later who called it.

Two further requirements. Somewhere in the document the two of them must explicitly address whether part of their historical dispute is merely verbal — both deny a permanent ego, both accept causal continuity of aggregates, so the drafting should say exactly which residual disagreement survives that clarification and which evaporates, and why. And the agreed-claims section must contain at least one claim that is genuinely uncomfortable for the Buddhist side (she suspects the burden of explaining why long-term self-concern and responsibility-ascription remain coherent falls on her) and one genuinely uncomfortable for the continuity theorist (he suspects fission and teletransporter cases make his relation look like it was never identity-shaped to begin with); the point of the exercise is a document neither could have written alone, not a ceasefire. Keep it to a length that could plausibly be read aloud and signed in a single seminar session, and write in the neutral, slightly stiff register of a jointly authored summary rather than of either party's usual prose.
