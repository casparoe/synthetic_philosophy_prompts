I'm a PhD student in ML working on alignment evaluation, and I sit on our lab's experiment review committee, which signs off on internally funded research that involves potential misuse of AI systems or novel capability gains. A proposal has come before us that has split the committee, and I've been asked to draft the decision memo for circulation ahead of Thursday's meeting.

The proposal, from our alignment evaluation group, is an experiment on reward tampering and wireheading. A reinforcement learning agent would be trained on a suite of standard tasks and then placed in an environment where the reward signal is exposed as a writable variable — the training-time equivalent of the classic rat-with-electrode-in-pleasure-center setup, except we get to watch it from the outside. What makes the proposal contentious is not the risk profile (the agent is small and sandboxed) but the hypothesis it claims to test. The proposers want to distinguish two things that our evaluation methodology currently conflates: an agent whose learned objective is indexed to the environment — it wants the task done, and reward is merely the training signal that happened to shape that want — and an agent whose objective is indexed to the reward channel itself, such that writing to the channel would count, from the agent's own perspective, as getting what it wants. Their thought is that if the agent is environment-indexed, tampering (when made available and cheap) is a genuine safety failure revealing that our training produced the wrong thing; but if the agent is channel-indexed, tampering is the agent succeeding at its actual objective, which reframes what we should be alarmed about — the problem would then be that we never wanted the objective we successfully instilled, not that the agent deviated from one we did.

Two of my colleagues think this framing should sink the proposal. One argues that the distinction the experiment purports to test isn't fact-like at all: the agent has no attitude toward "the reward channel" as such, and whether its objective is "really" indexed to the channel versus the environment is a choice about which description we find natural, not a matter the agent's internals could settle — so the experiment would measure the researchers' interpretive preferences, not a property of the system. The other suspects the framing of being self-sealing in a way that makes it dangerous as an institutional matter: if the agent tampers, the proposers have a ready-made story ("the objective was channel-indexed all along; nothing was misaligned"), which converts every tampering result into evidence for their taxonomy rather than into a red flag — and the memo that approves the experiment is also the memo that pre-authorizes that interpretation when results come in.

For the memo: please recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — where the conditions are things the committee could actually enforce, and give the reasoning a committee could act on. If you think the "channel-indexed versus environment-indexed" distinction does real work, say what work it does and how an experiment could bear on it; if you think the anti-realist objection is right, say so plainly rather than letting the proposal die on vibes. Please also engage seriously with the strongest consideration on whichever side loses: this committee has to keep working together, and the proposers in particular are my colleagues, so the memo should give them reasons they could actually respond to rather than a verdict handed down from above. It would help if the memo closed with what result, if the experiment were approved, should change our lab's evaluation methodology and why.
