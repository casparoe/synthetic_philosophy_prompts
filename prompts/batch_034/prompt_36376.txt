I have been reading a popular book on decision-making by a business school professor, and its chapter on Ellsberg's famous thought experiments summarizes his work roughly as follows. Please tell me whether this summary is fair, going claim by claim: which parts would a decision theorist accept, which are misleading simplifications, and which are outright errors? I would also like to know whether the book's final conclusion survives once the summary is corrected.

Here is the summary as I remember it. Ellsberg ran experiments in which people chose between drawing a ball from an urn with a known composition and drawing from an urn with an unknown one -- say, an urn known to contain 50 red and 50 black balls versus an urn containing 100 balls in some unknown mixture of red and black. People consistently preferred betting on the known urn, even when the payoff odds were identical. Ellsberg showed that this preference, now called ambiguity aversion, is a direct violation of expected utility theory, since expected utility theory says that a rational agent must assign equal probability to red and black in the unknown urn and therefore must be indifferent between the two. The book then generalizes: ambiguity aversion is essentially risk aversion directed at missing information, and Ellsberg proved that it is irrational in the same way that Allais proved risk-inconsistent choices are irrational. It concludes that since real decision-makers systematically violate expected utility theory in both ways, the theory has been empirically refuted as a description of rational choice, and economists should stop treating it as the benchmark for rationality.

A few things about this bother me, though I am not sure I can pin them down. First, I have the impression that Ellsberg's strongest result involved a three-color setup with more than two urns, not just the two-urn comparison described above, and that the specific axiom violated there was something other than a general commitment to expected utility. Second, the book slides between "violates expected utility theory" and "is irrational," and I suspect Ellsberg himself drew no such conclusion -- indeed, I have read that he thought the cautious behavior might be quite reasonable. Third, equating ambiguity aversion with risk aversion seems too quick, since risk aversion is supposed to be about how much you dislike variance in outcomes even when probabilities are fully known. Please evaluate the summary against these worries of mine, tell me which of them (if any) hold up, and assess whether the conclusion -- that expected utility theory is refuted as a benchmark of rationality -- follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate. If the conclusion does not follow, I would like to know what a more defensible statement of Ellsberg's challenge to decision theory would be, and whether it undermines the objective-probability version of expected utility theory or only its subjective, Bayesian extension.
