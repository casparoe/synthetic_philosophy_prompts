I've been an active commenter in the rationalist blogosphere for a long time, and one norm I've absorbed and mostly defend is something like a strict literal-truthfulness policy: never assert what you believe to be false, but regard carefully chosen true statements — even ones crafted to induce a false belief — as the sanctioned escape hatch. This is the position people usually gesture at with the murderer-at-the-door case: the hardline Kantian who refuses to lie looks monstrous, yet the person who says something technically true that predictably misdirects the murderer seems to come out clean, or at least cleaner. The philosophical literature mostly runs in the same direction: lying is treated as the paradigm wrong, and misleading-by-implicature as the lesser wrong because it leaves the hearer's rational capacities in play — they could, in principle, work out what's really going on. I want to stress-test my own norm, and for that I need a case that runs the usual intuition backwards.

Construct me a thought experiment in which an outright lie comes out morally permissible while the technically-true-but-calculatedly-deceptive alternative comes out impermissible — the exact reverse of the conventional ranking. Constraints: one speaker, one hearer, the same false belief ends up induced either way, and the same foreseeable downstream consequences either way, so the only difference between the two acts is the mechanism (asserting a falsehood versus exploiting an implicature to engineer the false belief from true premises). The setting should be realistic — a professional context where misleading speech is institutionally normalized, like an attorney's precisely worded denial, a physician's selective disclosure to family, or a spokesperson's non-answer. And the reversal must be generated by a principled account of the lying/misleading distinction, not stipulated: it should fall out of something like the Kantian thought that misleading preserves the hearer's agency as a reasoner while a bare falsehood rides roughshod over it, or out of a reliance-based account on which the wrong of deception tracks inviting trust-based uptake. Don't let the case cheat by giving the liar better intentions, a lower chance of being caught, or an authority's blessing.

After the scenario itself (one paragraph), walk me through what each act comes to under two accounts of the difference — one on the agency-preservation side and one on the reliance side — and tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work. Then do the two stress tests: first, remove or blunt that feature and show whether the case collapses back into the standard direction where lying comes out worse; second, give me the strongest reply a defender of the conventional ranking could make, whether that's arguing that your misleader was covertly lying all along (asserting something false by implicature) or arguing that the liar's act wasn't permissible either. Finally, tell me whether someone like me — running the literal-truthfulness rule as an exceptionless personal policy rather than deriving verdicts case by case — could accept your verdicts without giving the rule up, or whether the case forces a choice between the rule and the morality. The output is for a long-form blog post arguing that our community's honesty norm has an unexamined soft spot, so a case where the reversal is stipulated rather than earned is exactly what I can't use.
