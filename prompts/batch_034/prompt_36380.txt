I am a retired ecologist — thirty-five years studying population dynamics in freshwater wetlands — and only in the last few years have I taken up moral philosophy seriously. A question I keep circling, and cannot settle to my own satisfaction, is this: when we destroy or degrade a wild habitat, are we reducing suffering in the aggregate, or merely extinguishing the sufferers along with their suffering?

My training left me with two commitments that now strike me as being in tension. The first is empirical: in most ecosystems I have worked in, the overwhelming majority of organisms are r-selected species — animals that produce enormous numbers of offspring, of which the vast majority die before reaching maturity, typically painfully (starvation, predation, parasitism, desiccation). If a single frog's clutch of two thousand eggs yields, on average, two breeding adults, then something on the order of 1,998 of those individuals lived short and, presumably, quite bad lives. Multiply across entire marshes and the arithmetic becomes staggering. My second commitment is the ordinary conservationist's intuition that draining such a marsh is a tragedy, not a mercy — that we ought to preserve the wetland, and that the destruction of the millions of lives within it is part of what makes it tragic.

Philosophers seem to want to force a choice here. If one holds that most wild animals' lives are net-negative, then paving over the marsh resembles unplugging a machine that runs on suffering: the suffering stops, and no one is left worse off, since the counterfactual individuals who never exist cannot be harmed by not existing. Against this, one might insist that death is a harm even to creatures whose lives would have been bad, or that extinction forecloses whatever positive welfare the few survivors would have enjoyed, or that the value of ecosystems is not reducible to the summed experiences of their inhabitants.

I would like your help thinking this through carefully. Please write a formal essay of no more than 700 words that weighs the strongest considerations on each side — the total-view utilitarian logic that habitat destruction can be welfare-improving in aggregate, and the objections to it, including the claim that ceasing to bring bad lives into existence is not the same as improving any life. As someone who spent a career counting egg masses and estimating juvenile mortality, I confess I find the utilitarian arithmetic uncomfortably hard to escape; but as someone who also mourns every wetland I have seen lost, I suspect there is something the arithmetic leaves out. Where exactly does it go wrong, if it does? Do not end on a non-committal note — I want you to defend a definite verdict on whether habitat destruction reduces or increases aggregate wild animal suffering, and, briefly, what that verdict implies for whether conservation is a moral duty toward animals or only toward other values.
