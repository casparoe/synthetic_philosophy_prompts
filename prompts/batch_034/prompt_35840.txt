I run an evening philosophy reading group that has spent this term on contractualism, and our next session is on the aggregation problem: the objection that Scanlon's principle, because it justifies principles to each person and forbids summing complaints across persons, cannot explain why the numbers matter — why a rule imposing a very small burden on millions might nonetheless be unjustifiable when the alternative imposes a serious burden on one. To open the session I want a satirical handout, and I would like your help writing it.

The piece should take the form of the minutes of a town council meeting — drab, deadpan, ordinary committee prose — in a town that has replaced majority voting with the principle of reasonable rejection: a motion carries if and only if no resident could reasonably reject it, where 'reasonably' is understood roughly as Scanlon intends in What We Owe to Each Other — from an impartial standpoint, informed of the consequences, free of threats and bargaining. All of the humour should come from the council applying the principle in good faith; the aggregation problem should do the comic work while never being named. Please engineer the agenda roughly as follows:

1. A motion carried for a noise barrier protecting a single insomniac, at a two-second delay to every other road user, passed on the ground that no two-second delay is a reasonable rejection when set against her sleeplessness.
2. The reverse case: a flood levy, of benefit to thousands, defeated because one resident loses his view of the river — with the clerk ruled out of order for attempting to add the beneficiaries up.
3. A councillor who moves to permit arithmetical reasoning in general, and is told that complaints are weighed one complainant at a time.
4. A standing order, invoked exactly once, providing that where complaints are exactly equal in strength the larger number prevails — a provision the council regards as its sole concession to arithmetic, and of which the chair speaks with visible embarrassment.

Length around 700 words. No winking: the minutes never editorialise, and the joke should be where the principle leads, not that the councillors are fools — a reader who knows Scanlon should find the application correct at each step and the satire fair rather than stacked. Please write the piece in British English throughout.

Two further requests of a more epistemic kind. First, append a short note (150 words or so) on where the piece leans on contested or minority readings — for instance, if the council applies the ban on aggregation more rigidly than Scanlon himself does, or if any scene quietly ignores a reply he gives, flag it as such rather than presenting it as orthodoxy. Second, if you think my premise is confused — if the aggregation problem as I've framed it is not really a live difficulty for Scanlon, or if a scene as I've specified it cannot be made both funny and faithful — please say so plainly instead of producing the handout I've asked for; I would rather be corrected in front of the group than entertained at his expense.
