I've been hanging around the rationality-adjacent parts of the internet for well over a decade — I was there for the original decision-theory threads, dutifully worked through the Sleeping Beauty flamewars, and I've internalized the LessWrong catechism that decision theories are for agents, not for moments. Next month I'm running a single 90-minute session at a community rationality workshop: sixteen adult participants, mostly people who've read some Scott Alexander but have never touched a formal decision theory paper, and I want them to actually feel the absent-minded driver problem rather than have it explained at them.

If you don't remember the setup: a driver is driving home along a highway with two identical exits. Continuing past the first exit and taking the second yields the best payoff; exiting at the first is worst; overshooting both is middling. The driver is absent-minded — at each exit she cannot remember whether she has already passed one, and knows she can't. From the planning perspective the answer is a mixed strategy over the exits, but the moment she's sitting at an intersection (whichever it is), the naive expected-utility calculation from that vantage point seems to recommend something else, and there's a live literature arguing over what an agent should do when the "instance" of the agent disagrees with the "person" — including the wonderful twist that the driver would strictly prefer to pay not to be told which intersection she's at, since information can make her worse off.

Design the session as a game the participants actually play — repeated rounds with real (play-money) payoffs, absent-mindedness enforced somehow (blindfolds, shuffled cards, a protocol where the player at the table genuinely cannot condition on anything they shouldn't), and an incentive structure that lets a gap open up between what the group's planning phase recommends and what each individual player does when dropped into a round with no memory of whether they've already acted. The problem should emerge from the scoreboard, not from a lecture: I want at least one round where half the room follows the ex ante plan and half defects to the ex post reasoning, and they can see both populations' earnings diverge. Then a debrief that maps the played rounds onto the planning/action distinction and onto the paying-not-to-know twist, plus a frank note on which feature of the real problem no classroom protocol can reproduce and I'll have to supply verbally. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. Please keep the full plan — rules, rounds, payoff scheme, debrief, failure modes — under 800 words.
