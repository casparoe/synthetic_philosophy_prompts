My grandfather, a junior official in wartime Bengal's food administration, died last spring, and while sorting his papers I found his letters from 1943 — the year in which, by most estimates, two to three million people in Bengal died of famine-related causes while he stamped requisition notices and wrote home about the panic in the rice trade. I am now writing the family memoir he never did, and it forces a question I cannot dodge: should the book present the Bengal famine of 1943 as a natural disaster he happened to be near, a policy failure he was part of, or a political crime he was complicit in? I would like a disciplined case analysis, run in both directions — the theories over the episode, and then the episode back over the theories.

Two lenses. First, Amartya Sen's entitlement approach, from Poverty and Famines (1981): famine as the collapse of whole classes' ability to command food through production, trade, or labor, rather than a simple shortfall in food supply. Sen's original case was precisely Bengal 1943, where he argued that overall food availability was not dramatically below that of non-famine years, while the exchange entitlements of rural laborers, fishermen, and artisans against rice collapsed. Second, the more recent framing associated with Alex de Waal, in Famine Crimes (1997) and his later work on mass starvation as an atrocity: famine is typically produced by political conduct — censorship, blockade, market manipulation, and the indifference of the powerful — and should be named, and where possible prosecuted, as criminal, in the spirit of the Rome Statute's treatment of starvation as a method of warfare.

The salient facts as I understand them, in case the mapping needs them: the fall of Rangoon in 1942 cut Bengal off from Burmese rice; a cyclone damaged the winter crop in parts of the province; the boat-denial policy, intended to deny landing craft to a feared Japanese invasion, destroyed river transport in coastal districts; provincial trade barriers and wartime censorship suppressed both the movement of grain and the news of distress; and the imperial government declined for many months to authorize large-scale imports or intervene decisively in the market, while the province's elected ministry, answerable to a restricted franchise under a censored press, had weak incentives to act. The relief machinery only lurched into gear once the scale of the deaths was undeniable. My grandfather stood one level down from all of this: not a minister, not a merchant, just a functionary in the supplies directorate.

What I want to know, specifically. Does the entitlement approach explain 1943, and where does it go silent? It is an account of how famine happens economically; does it have anything to say about who is responsible, or does responsibility require an add-on theory that Sen's framework does not itself supply? And does the crime framing, built largely from cases of deliberate siege — Biafra, Darfur, Tigray — have the resources for a famine that emerged from distributed, partly well-intentioned, mostly non-malicious decisions, in which nobody wanted the deaths? Is there a coherent notion of crime that requires no one to intend the outcome, and if so, what is left of it when stretched this far? When Sen observes that functioning democracies with a free press do not have famines, and notes that Bengal had an elected ministry under a restricted franchise with censored newspapers, does that strengthen the crime framing — the missing press freedom being the missing restraint — or does it merely show that the accountability category is doing all the work while the crime category rides along?

Then the practical question the memoir turns on: given what each lens explains, misdescribes, and passes over in silence, what should the book claim — and what does that claim imply for how I write my grandfather's chapter? I would rather be told that none of the three framings fits cleanly than be handed a tidy verdict.
