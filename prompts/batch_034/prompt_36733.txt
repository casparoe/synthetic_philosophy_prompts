I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice theory — 15 students, one 90-minute session — and I want a game that makes them feel, rather than merely accept, why "maximize expected value per round" is bad advice for a repeated bettor, and why the Kelly fraction is something you run into rather than something you paste on. The bet itself I'd like you to build around: each student starts with a bankroll of 100 points and, every round, stakes any fraction of their current bankroll on a biased coin (60% heads) at even odds, with the stake won or lost outright. Requirements on the design: enough rounds for compounding to do its work and a public ledger of everyone's trajectory; no real money; and — this is the piece I most want your ingenuity on — one designated "ensemble" entity, run by the facilitator or a rotating student, that tracks, say, twenty-five independent parallel bettors all using the same stake fraction, so the class can watch the average across many parallel lives keep climbing even while most individual trajectories crater. I want them to collide with the ensemble/time-average distinction before I ever name it. Somewhere in the middle I'd also like a "lending desk" to open that lets students bet more than their bankroll. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the session — the first bust, the all-in lucky survivor, the point where the ensemble line crosses most individuals — onto the formal difference between arithmetic and geometric growth, and onto what the Kelly criterion actually optimizes. In the debrief, take a definite stance on whether to present the lesson as a claim about what to value (logarithmic utility) or a claim about dynamics (time-averages versus ensemble-averages), and defend that framing rather than offering both as equally good. Include your predicted failure modes the first time I run this — especially how to handle the EV-maximizer who gets lucky and finishes near the top — with concrete fixes. Close with an honest note on which feature of the underlying argument no finite classroom game can reproduce. Please make definite choices throughout: exact number of rounds, payoff bookkeeping, materials, timings, and how the ensemble entity is logged and displayed, each with your reasons, rather than a menu of variants for me to pick from.
