I am halfway through drafting a novel set in a residential institution — a boarding school for the children of civil servants in an unnamed republic — and in workshop discussions I have found myself leaning on the panopticon as my governing conceit. The architecture, I tell my editor, will do the character work for me: once the dormitories are ranged around a central tower that no one can confirm is staffed, the children will discipline themselves, and my protagonist's tragedy will be that she polices herself more ruthlessly than any warden could. I am extending the figure outward from the building, following what I understand of Foucault's use of Bentham's design in his book on the prison — the claim that the scheme was not merely a prison but a diagram for hospitals, schools, factories, and barracks, a general machinery for producing docile and useful bodies through timetables, examinations, ranked seating, and files. In the novel, the school stands for the society.

As it happens, I also write occasional criticism, and I have begun to notice that reviewers deploy the same figure far more loosely than I have been using it. Social media platforms are called a panopticon; so are CCTV networks, so is the open-plan office with its analytics dashboard. Every arrangement of observation receives the name, and the name is then used to license a conclusion: that the watched internalize the gaze, become their own wardens, and therefore that no actual observer is required — this being presented as the deepest and most economical form of control, the one my novel's ending also trades on.

Before I commit several hundred pages to it, I would like the conceit audited rather than admired. Please give me the correspondence table first: in the case of my school, and in the platform and dashboard cases the reviewers invoke, what plays the role of the tower, the unseen watcher, the inmate, the unverifiable scrutiny, the internalized gaze. Then lay out where the mapping breaks in each case, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from those that do not. The one I most want your judgment on is a distinction my novel may have quietly ignored: between observation that is unverifiable, as in the tower no one can see into, and observation that is certain but inscrutable, where you know the logs are being kept but cannot know by whom, under what criteria, or toward what end. Does the reviewers' conclusion — that surveillance of this kind needs no watcher and manufactures self-discipline on its own — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the panopticon carrying it the whole way? And if the figure cannot bear that weight, tell me what a novelist who wants self-policing rather than mere compliance should build instead.
