So our CTO keeps saying we're "falling prey to the sunk cost fallacy" whenever we keep going on anything that's taken more than a few months, and honestly it's become a conversation stopper. Like we have this in-house ORM replacement we're 8 months into, it's maybe 60% done, morale around it is bad, and every planning meeting someone (sometimes me tbh) says "sunk costs, kill it" like that settles the thing. But I've been reading a bit and it seems like the textbook version -- "rational agents ignore sunk costs, period" -- is maybe too clean? Like, the money already spent doesn't *logically* compel finishing, but it can still be *evidence*: nobody spends 8 months on something without learning things about the codebase that a fresh decision made today would incorporate. The fact that we were willing to invest that much might mean the people closest to it know something. Also there's the team-reputation / commitments-to-other-teams angle, which feels like it shouldn't count but our platform team genuinely plans their quarter around us delivering this thing, and killing it has real costs that aren't "sunk" at all, they're future ones.

So here's what I want. I'm going to run a retro on the ORM project and I want to propose an actual decision procedure, not vibes. Turn whatever the real philosophy of this says into a checklist or ordered set of questions that a bunch of engineers (zero philosophy background, one of them will 100% make a joke about "sunk cost fallacy fallacy") could actually run in a 45-minute meeting. Requirements:

1. It has to make the distinction between "sunk costs are biasing us" and "sunk costs gave us information that a cold restart would throw away" operational -- like, actual questions you ask and what answers point which way.

2. It has to catch the sneaky inverse bias too, where people invoke the fallacy as a rhetorical move to kill projects they never liked, and where "we already paid for it" is doing legitimate work as a coordination device (with other teams, or with our own past selves who made commitments).

3. Where the procedure can't decide -- because it's actually a values question about what we owe each other or how much risk the team should carry -- it should say so and punt to a human, not pretend the checklist settled it.

Then run it on two cases so I can see it work: (a) the ORM, which as I said is 60% done, despised, but already integrated into two of our services, and (b) a hypothetical second one -- we're being asked to decide whether to keep paying for a multi-year cloud contract that's underused but where the alternative is a migration effort nobody has scoped. One of these should come out with an answer I won't like, and I want the procedure to commit anyway, so I can see it's not just a machine for ratifying whatever I already believe.

Also please be honest about where the procedure will misfire -- the failure modes of using a checklist for this at all. And keep the philosophy light-touch in the writeup, like a paragraph of "here's the actual principle" is fine, but the deliverable is the thing we run in the meeting. Casual tone if you can, the doc's going in our internal wiki next to stuff called "why our deploy pipeline is haunted."
