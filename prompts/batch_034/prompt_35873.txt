I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and we've just spent three weeks on the conceivability argument: Chalmers's version, where the crucial move is that the zombie is stipulated to be physically identical to us — identical down to the particles, so that every physical fact about me has a counterpart — and merely lacks experience. Our professor kept stressing that this is a much stronger claim than behavioral indistinguishability, and that the argument's force comes precisely from identity rather than mimicry. I thought I had this straight until a debate with my roommate, who follows AI closely and thinks it's all been settled empirically. He says the philosophical zombie has already been built, and that it's a language model: it passes for a person in conversation, reports on its "feelings," describes the taste of coffee convincingly, apologizes when told it was insensitive — all, he assumes, with nothing going on inside. He uses this to license two conclusions at once: first, that the conceivability argument was on the right track all along, since we can now point to a concrete, working example of sophisticated behavior without experience; and second, more practically, that we can be quite certain there is nothing it is like to be the model, so any worry about its welfare is confused.

My suspicion is that the analogy quietly downgrades the target: a language model is at most a behavioral duplicate, and producing conversational behavior under completely different physical conditions seems like exactly the case the zombie thought experiment was designed to bypass — the defender of the argument can shrug and say the model's emptiness, even if granted, shows nothing about whether microphysical identity without consciousness is possible. But I'm not confident enough to press this in section without a proper map, and I can also see the reply coming that even a "weak" duplicate should worry us if the strong one is conceivable.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Set out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the physical duplicate, the behavioral repertoire, the inner life, the inverted-spectrum possibilities — and then separate the disanalogies that matter for my roommate's two conclusions from the ones that don't. In particular: does the existence of a chatty but arguably empty system lend any support to the conceivability of a genuine zombie, or does it conflate the "robot" objection that everyone already concedes with the argument's real claim? And is his certainty about the model's lack of inner life licensed by the analogy at all, or does it have to stand on some independent argument? If the language model is a bad stand-in, tell me whether there is a better everyday analogy for the genuine zombie, and whether the conceivability argument loses any of its grip once we notice that every analogy we actually have at hand is a case of mimicry rather than identity.
