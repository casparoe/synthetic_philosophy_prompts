i teach a philosophy of science survey and we just wrapped the Lakatos unit — research programmes, hard core, protective belt, progressive vs degenerating problem shifts. the class went way better than i expected and i want to close the last session with a poem, and i've got a very specific idea for it that i can't execute myself (i am hopeless with fixed forms).

write a sestina about the life and death of the Newtonian research programme: its progressive phase, when the wobble in Uranus's orbit got absorbed by revising the protective belt — a new planet conjured on paper and then actually found in a telescope — and its degenerating phase, when the exact same move (invent a planet, call it Vulcan) tried and failed to patch the drift in Mercury's perihelion, until the whole anomaly got explained from outside the programme by a theory about curved spacetime.

the constraint i actually care about: the six end-words are the programme's own furniture. i'd suggest law, belt, anomaly, prediction, patch, and orbit, but swap any of those out if you've got better ones. the point is that the end-words never change while everything between them keeps getting rewritten to keep them in place — that's the negative heuristic and the belt absorbing shocks, enacted by the form rather than just mentioned. follow the standard rotation exactly.

and here's the hard part: the progressive-to-degenerating shift has to happen in *how* the end-words are reused, not in anything you say outright. in the early stanzas, each return of an end-word should earn genuinely new sense from its new context — that's what a novel prediction is. by the later stanzas the returns should feel increasingly strained, same words now being bent to fit, which is the ad hoc move. the envoi is where the programme gets walked away from. traditionally you'd use all six end-words in it; i genuinely can't decide whether i want that or whether the philosophically right envoi is one where the six words *can't* all live anymore, so some get dropped or left half-broken. your call — but make whichever choice deliberately, and make the final line land on the word that hurts most.

someone who knows Lakatos should be able to read off from the form alone which stanzas are the progressive phase and which are the degenerating one. someone who doesn't know Lakatos should still get a real poem about a theory being loved to death. no terms of art explained inside the poem itself — the envoi especially shouldn't turn into a lecture.

send me the poem first, then a section called craft notes (roughly 150 words) walking through the load-bearing choices — which reuse of which end-word does which theoretical work, and why you made the envoi call you made. no line-by-line, just the moves that matter. if any line is doing double duty (formal and philosophical), flag it there rather than underlining it in the poem.
