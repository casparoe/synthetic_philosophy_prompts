Two of my friends from a political philosophy seminar got into a long argument afterwards about luck egalitarianism, and I've been turning it over since. The trigger was a hypothetical our prof raised: a guy who had a perfectly decent stable career option drops it to spend five years trying to go pro at poker, and it doesn't work out — he's now broke and needs help. Should egalitarians say his poverty is unjust?

Maya went first: yes, injustice tracks luck. The whole point of equality is to neutralize the effects of circumstances people didn't choose — the family, genes, and shocks you were born into. Someone who voluntarily rolls the dice with open eyes and full information hasn't been treated unfairly when the dice come up bad. Compensating him means taking from people who chose safer paths, and there's no injustice that compensation corrects.

Dev said this is exactly the famous "harshness objection": the view abandons the worst off precisely when they're worst off. Egalitarianism, he said, was supposed to be about making sure nobody's life goes badly regardless of why, and Maya's version instead rescues the prudent unlucky while shrugging at the imprudent — which inverts what a humane person would do. He pressed her: would she deny a smoker cancer treatment? Would she cut off the guy's food stamps?

Maya backed off a bit: of course you don't let people starve, but there's a difference between a floor of decency that everyone gets and claims to equal shares — the guy's claim to extra resources is weakened by his choice, even if he keeps the floor. She added that Dev's side has its own problem: if choice never weakens claims, then there's nothing unfair about the person who works hard ending up with more than the one who doesn't, since the hard worker also chose, and apparently choices stop mattering entirely.

Dev answered that responsibility is never that clean — this guy's risk appetite, his confidence, maybe even his bad judgment about his own talent are plausibly products of a chaotic upbringing, so at what point does "choice" become disguised brute luck? If she needs a line between the two, and every actual choice has causes behind it, the line looks arbitrary.

Maya's last move was the hypothetical insurance idea: people who didn't know how their gambles would go would rationally buy insurance against career-gamble failure, so funding a safety net for failed gamblers through taxation is exactly what they would have chosen for themselves — the compensation is itself choice-tracked, so her view gets Dev's result without abandoning the principle. Dev shot back that real people know their own risk appetites just fine, so the ones who'd never take the gamble wouldn't buy that insurance, and she's smuggling solidarity in under a responsibility label.

They left it there. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who's right at each step, what (if anything) each of them is missing, and who comes out more right overall? Feel free to bring in the standard distinctions from the literature (option luck vs brute luck, expensive tastes, etc.) where they actually help settle a step, but I mainly want your own verdict on the arguments as they made them.
