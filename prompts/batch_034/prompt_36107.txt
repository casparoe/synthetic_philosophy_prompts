At a panel discussion on science and religion last month, a theologian closed off discussion of scriptural literalism by invoking the line Galileo quotes in his 1615 letter to Christina of Lorraine — a line he reports having heard from an ecclesiastic of the most eminent degree: "That the intention of the Holy Ghost is to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes." As the panelist used it, the line established that scripture simply does not make claims about the natural world, and that therefore apparent conflicts between science and scripture — over the age of the earth, say, or human origins — are always merely verbal. I left uneasy, and I want help sorting out exactly what that sentence can and cannot establish.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single interpretation. At a minimum I would expect something like the following to be on the list, though you should restructure or extend it as you see fit: (1) an accommodationist claim about authorial intention — that where scripture touches nature, it speaks in the language of common appearance rather than asserting astronomical facts; (2) a scope-of-authority claim — that scriptural authority extends only to matters of salvation, so whatever it says elsewhere binds no one; (3) a hermeneutic-priority claim — that where a physical conclusion has been demonstrated, the burden falls on interpretation to accommodate it; (4) an independence claim of the later "non-overlapping magisteria" sort — that the two domains cannot in principle come into conflict. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial, whether Galileo actually held it in that letter, and — most importantly — whether it would support the panelist's use: does any of them entail that scripture never makes empirical claims, or that conflicts between science and scripture are always apparent rather than real?

Two complications I would like you to weigh in your verdicts. First, Galileo's own argument in the letter is not that scripture makes no claims about nature; it is that where a conclusion has been established by sense experience and necessary demonstration, the literal reading must yield. His handling of Joshua's sun standing still — where he argues, remarkably, that the miracle reads more, not less, naturally on the Copernican system, since halting the sun's rotation would halt the whole celestial machinery — takes himself to be interpreting a natural-historical claim, not setting it aside as outside scripture's remit. That sits awkwardly with the panelist's reading, and I would like to know whether the accommodationist and hermeneutic-priority readings can be pried apart cleanly enough to save it. Second, the letter was written as a defense within, not against, ecclesiastical authority: Galileo conceded that scripture cannot err and disputed only its meaning. So a final question: on its strongest defensible reading, does the slogan describe a stable epistemological position at all, or is it better understood, both in Galileo's mouth and in the panelist's, as a strategic retreat — a way of declining a fight one is not in a position to win? If the latter, please say plainly which reading the panelist would actually need to get his conclusion, and whether that reading is true.
