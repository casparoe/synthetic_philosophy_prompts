quick background: i'm a third-year phd student, i mostly work on kant's aesthetics, so hume was supposed to be the side-gig, and yet somehow i'm now running the jury for our department's annual grad student short film festival. about 25 shorts screened over a weekend, a five-person jury picks the main prize in a single afternoon of deliberation (people have jobs, we can't do more), and there's also an audience-vote award the organizers refuse to give up. last year's jury gave the top prize to a very slow, very serious tarkovsky riff that half the room walked out of, while the audience went insane for a scrappy comedy, and i am still hearing about it at colloquium receptions.

my thought is that hume's of the standard of taste is basically a manual for exactly this mess: the ideal critic with strong sense and delicacy of sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, cleared of prejudice, plus the whole thing about time being the ultimate filter. so what i want from you is to turn that into an actual procedure i can run. concretely:

(1) a screening rubric for picking the five jurors. which of hume's criteria can i realistically check in advance, and how — like, what would "delicacy of taste" even look like as a testable thing in a 15-minute interview or a questionnaire? and be honest about where checking the criteria collapses into circularity, since for hume the joint verdict of good judges is partly what makes them good judges. if some criterion just can't be operationalized without presupposing the verdict, say so and mark it as a judgment call.

(2) rules for the deliberation itself. how should jurors be required to justify their votes? hume's line that every kind of art has its own peculiar excellences suggests comedy and slow art film should be judged by different standards, which is convenient for ranking across genres — but then what stops a juror from saying "it's great *for a comedy*" forever? also: what to do when qualified jurors simply don't converge — hume admits some disagreement survives all his criteria, right? — and whether the audience vote should be allowed to feed back into the jury's deliberation. the organizers WILL ask me why the crowd is wrong and i need an answer that isn't just "they're not qualified," or at least an honest version of it.

(3) what to do about the time criterion, since we obviously can't wait fifty years to see what survives. is there a defensible fast proxy (e.g., what still gets argued about rather than what still gets praised?), or should i just admit this part of the theory doesn't scale to a weekend and cut it?

then run the procedure on two cases. first: my own advisor, who i badly want on the jury — genuinely widely-watched, writes beautifully about film, but has loudly and repeatedly hated comedies since roughly 2009. does the prejudice criterion actually catch that, or is a stable genre aversion different from prejudice in hume's sense? second: a hypothetical entry that's technically the best thing in the festival by a mile but whose whole conceit is, let's say, morally nasty — hume has this thing where moral flaws in the subject matter can't count as aesthetic virtues, and i want the procedure to tell me whether the jury is allowed to dock it, with the reasoning on record so i can defend the decision afterward. ideally one of these two should come out in a way i'll personally find annoying; i'd rather stress-test the procedure now than at the afterparty.

constraints: the rubric part should fit on a page, the whole thing has to be usable by people who've never read a word of hume, and finish by telling me where the procedure is a judgment call wearing a hume costume, and whether what i've built actually gets me anything like objectivity or just a defensible process (and if it's the latter, why that might still be enough).
