I'm part of a small reading group on epistemic game theory — mostly philosophy grad students plus a couple of economists — and after a semester working through common knowledge of rationality, iterated dominance, and the backward induction debates, I've collected a set of gut verdicts about particular scenarios that I can't yet see any single principle behind. Here are the cases and my verdicts; I'd like you to tell me what, if anything, they add up to.

(1) Muddy children: three children, two with muddy foreheads, and a father who publicly announces that at least one child is muddy. I think the two muddy children can each figure out they're muddy after two public rounds of everyone saying "I don't know." (2) Two commanders who must both attack at dawn to win, while attacking alone is a disaster; they can communicate only by couriers, each of which has a small chance — say one in fifty — of being captured, and they keep sending acknowledgments back and forth until one fails to arrive. My verdict: they never attack, no matter how many acknowledgments get through. I hold this even at ninety-nine successful acknowledgments, which my friends call paranoia. (3) The same commanders, but now there is a signal they can observe simultaneously and publicly; there they attack. So the gap between "each knows, and each knows the other knows, ... to depth ninety-nine" and genuine common knowledge strikes me as real and behaviorally decisive. (4) A stag hunt in which each partner receives a private but quite reliable signal of the other's intentions, right nineteen times out of twenty: here I think they do hunt stag. The noise doesn't destroy cooperation — and this is what bothers me, since case (2) looks structurally similar. (5) A centipede game with a hundred nodes: I think it can be rational to pass at early nodes, because the fact that we have both passed a few times is itself evidence against the unbounded rationality that backward induction presupposes. (6) A one-shot prisoner's dilemma under full common knowledge of payoffs and rationality: defect, full stop, no matter how many layers deep the mutual knowledge goes — and this one feels robust to me in a way (2) doesn't.

Is there a principle that delivers all of these, or close to it? My tentative stab is that the payoff structure decides whether a speck of higher-order doubt gets amplified (as with the commanders, where each needs to be sure the other will attack) or washed out (as in the stag hunt, where — somehow — it doesn't), but I can't make that precise, and I can't see what it could say about my centipede verdict, where the doubt isn't about whether a message arrives but about whether the other player is even the type that backward induction assumes. Please state your candidate principle or principles as precisely as you can, explain what each clause is doing there, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts you would overturn and why — I'd rather lose a verdict than keep it by torturing the principle. Then apply it to two cases I haven't listed: a "guess two-thirds of the average" game among players who all know that a small minority of them choose numbers at random, and the commanders' setup again but with the couriers captured only one time in a thousand. Finally — and I mean this seriously — tell me what would change your mind: which of my verdicts, if I refused to give it up, would sink your best principle, and where in the chain of reasoning is the whole thing most likely to break.
