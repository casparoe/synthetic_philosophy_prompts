In Consciousness Explained Dennett has this thought experiment I've been chewing on and can't leave alone: the Careenium. The way I remember it (details might be slightly garbled, doesn't really matter, work with the spirit of the thing): imagine a world where everything is made of paper, and some paper engineers build a room-sized contraption with little ball bearings rolling around ramps and chutes -- the simmballs. The trajectories of these balls, viewed from the right altitude, form stable higher-level patterns, and those patterns are what get called the symbols. And the question the whole setup is built to force is: do the symbols push the substrate around? Or is every causal fact fully settled down at the micro level -- paper shoving paper -- so that the symbol-level description is just a compression trick, a handy way of summarizing the low-level story for creatures too dumb to track every collision, but not an additional goings-on that does anything?

Write an essay on this controversy and actually take a side, don't wimp out at the end. Things I'd want you to grapple with: whether stable, projectible macro-level patterns that support counterfactuals ("if the symbol had been different, the substrate would have moved differently") are enough for genuine causal work, or whether that kind of causal talk is just wearing a disguise; whether the causal exclusion worry (that micro-level fixity crowds out higher-level causes) is a real argument or a confusion between causation and constitution; whether "the symbols push the substrate around" and "the substrate pushes itself around" are even rivals, or two true descriptions at different resolutions that don't compete; and what any of this buys us for the actual brain, where the substrate is neurons and the "symbols" are whatever the intentional level is tracking. If in the end you think the whole dispute dissolves into a verbal issue, say so explicitly and defend that -- but if you think there's a substantive question left over, say exactly what it is. The verdict should be stated in one bolded sentence somewhere near the end. You can use my possibly-off recollection of the thought experiment as the setup, and you can bring in whatever framework helps (Dennett's real patterns stuff, interventionist causation, whatever), just don't make the essay a book report on Dennett -- I've read the chapter, I want the argument.
