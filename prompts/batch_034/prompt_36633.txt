I got myself into a mess at my reading group and can't find the way out, so I need your help diagnosing it. We'd been reading Chalmers's paper on the meta-problem of consciousness — the one where instead of asking why consciousness exists, you ask why we all keep insisting there's a hard problem at all — and afterwards one of the group, a fairly committed illusionist, said to me that the meta-problem is really a trap for people like me: non-illusionists who think they can just stroll through it. I went home and tried to build the trap explicitly, and now I can't tell whether I've constructed a real argument or a cheap trick that I've fallen for myself. It goes like this. (1) My utterances about the hard problem — all my zombie talk, my Mary's-room hand-waving, my saying that I can't see how physical stuff could feel like anything — are themselves ordinary physical events: air vibrations from a mouth, governed by a brain, just like any other behaviour. (2) A completed neuroscience should in principle explain why I produce those utterances, the same way it would explain why I reach for coffee when tired. (3) That explanation will either make essential reference to phenomenal consciousness itself, or it won't. (4) If it does — if the redness of red figures in the causal story of my puzzlement — then consciousness is a non-physical cause producing physical effects, causal closure is false, and physicalism is in trouble. (5) If it doesn't — if the whole story runs on judgements, introspective mechanisms, limits of our imagination, concepts misfiring — then everything I say about the hard problem gets explained with consciousness never doing any work anywhere, which seems to mean my problem-reports never tracked qualia in the first place, only judgements about qualia, which is exactly what the illusionist has been telling me all along. (6) So the meta-problem forces a choice: either physicalism is false, or the hard problem was never really about consciousness and illusionism wins. The position I thought I held — physicalist, anti-illusionist — gets squashed from both directions.

Here's what bugs me. The conclusion looks too strong, since plenty of people (Chalmers himself, as far as I understand him) take the meta-problem seriously without treating it as a proof of illusionism, so some step must be bait. My instinct says the cheat lives in (5), in the jump from "you can explain my reports without mentioning consciousness" to "consciousness did nothing," which feels related to the stuff people say about epiphenomenalism, but I can't pin down why that jump is illegitamate rather than merely unpalatable. And if the jump is actually fine, then maybe I should bite the bullet and become an illusionist — except my conviction that there is something it is like to see red has survived every argument thrown at it so far, and I'm told that conviction is precisely what an illusionist predicts I'd have anyway, which is somehow the most annoying part. So tell me: which premise or step do you reject, why did that step look fine to me (and to my illusionist friend, who is not stupid), and what does rejecting it cost? If there's more than one respectable place to break the argument, lay out the alternatives and what each one pays. For the record, I'd much rather give up (5) than (1), and if I'm honest I'd rather stay a physicalist than keep my qualia — though I'm aware I might not get to choose which of those to sacrifice. Please write your answer in British English.
