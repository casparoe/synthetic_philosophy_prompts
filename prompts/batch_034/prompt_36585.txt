I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on well-being, writing a term paper defending hedonism against the heterogeneity problem, and I'd like your help stress-testing my solution before I commit to it.

The problem, as I understand it: the pleasures of a good meal, of understanding a proof, of listening to a piece of music, and of an act of revenge seem to share no common felt quality — an observation with roots in Sidgwick and pressed more recently by philosophers like Roger Crisp. If nothing phenomenological unites them, the hedonist's claim that they are all one kind of thing (pleasure) differing only in quantity and intensity looks unstable, since the theory seemed to presuppose that a single scale of better and worse lives could be built out of a single kind of item.

My answer takes the pro-attitude route rather than the felt-quality route. I claim that what unites all pleasures is not a distinctive sensation but the favorable attitude the subject takes toward her experience while it occurs: a pleasure just is an experience the subject endorses or enjoys at the time, in a way that can be more or less intense. Heterogeneity of feel is then no objection, because the unifier was never supposed to be phenomenological in the first place. Two pleasures differ in quantity when the favorable attitudes differ in intensity, not when the sensations differ in character.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this. Specifically:

1. Which cases would genuinely embarrass the view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I'm thinking of ones like these, but tell me which actually have force: pleasures that seem clearly present with no accompanying favorable attitude (e.g., a familiar taste one enjoys while mentally elsewhere); a favorable attitude toward an experience that doesn't feel good at all (e.g., being pleased that one is suffering for a cause); and cases where the subject's avowal and her felt response come apart, such as the reluctant enjoyment of schadenfreude one disapproves of while having it.
2. What does the position stand or fall with outside the case literature? In particular, does my proposal quietly collapse into a desire-satisfaction theory of well-being, and if so, is that a fatal concession for a hedonist, or just a relabeling? And should I be worried by the suggestion from affective neuroscience that valence is, after all, a common dimension running through all pleasurable states — i.e., that the felt-quality theorist may be vindicated empirically rather than conceptually? If that suggestion is right, does my view become redundant, or does it answer a different question?
3. Has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability? Whenever a counterexample is produced, it's tempting for me to respond that the attitude in question is the one individuated by the pleasure itself, which starts to look circular. Be honest about whether the pro-attitude answer has this character, and whether that would be a defect for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of conceptual analysis.
4. Finally, tell me which objections a committed defender of my view would concede as counting against it, and which she would dismiss — I want to know how exposed the position actually is, not just how a critic would caricature it. If you think the honest conclusion is that the view is well insulated and that this is a problem, say so.

I have about ten pages and two weeks, so I'd also appreciate a closing judgment: given the map you produce, should I defend this view in the paper, defend it with qualifications (say which), or switch to arguing that the heterogeneity problem is insoluble and hedonism should be abandoned on other grounds?
