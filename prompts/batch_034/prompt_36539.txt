I've been following the arguments around the strategy-stealing assumption since they started showing up in takeoff-model discussions on LessWrong and in the forecasting communities around 2019-2021, and my honest read is that the debate has been running in place for years. To make sure we're talking about the same thing: the assumption, as used in AI-risk and multipolarity arguments, is roughly that a leading AI project cannot obtain a decisive capabilities advantage that rival projects are unable to replicate in time — because algorithmic insights diffuse or get independently re-derived, because model weights and training recipes leak, because whatever the leader does can be copied or stolen, and so the world stays competitive rather than tipping into a singleton. The stakes are real: several arguments for optimism about multipolar outcomes, and several arguments that a careless actor's gains can be matched by careful ones, quietly lean on it. The combinatorial-game ancestor of the argument — Nash's proof that the first player wins Hex — is worth keeping in mind, because that proof rests on a monotonicity premise: having an extra piece on the board can never hurt you. The argument was never airtight even for chess, where zugzwang means an extra move can be a liability, and I suspect the port to AI development is where all the action is.

Here is the stall as I've watched it play out. The pro-diffusion side keeps making the same points: key algorithmic ideas get discovered roughly simultaneously by multiple groups, replication is cheaper than discovery, publication norms and employee mobility spread the important stuff, and historical cases of long-lived proprietary algorithmic moats are hard to find. The skeptics keep making the same counterpoints: the knowledge that matters in frontier ML is largely tacit and embodied in teams and infrastructure rather than in papers; compute and data advantages don't diffuse at all; even if a strategy is stealable in principle, a six-month copy lag is decisive if returns to capability are steep enough; and the monotonicity premise fails outright in the AI setting, since a leader's actions reveal information, invite targeting, and change the environment others act in. Everyone restates these moves, new commenters arrive and restate them again, and the positions never budge. It has the texture of a genuinely stuck disagreement, and I want a diagnosis of its structure rather than another round of the object-level exchange.

My tentative hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is a hidden quantitative dispute — about the ratio of idea-diffusion half-life to compounding capability growth rate, plus the slope of the returns curve — that both sides have dressed up as a qualitative one, so that each side's extreme-but-defensible parameter values make their qualitative claims feel obviously true. But it might instead be that the two sides are answering different questions under one name: "is stealing possible in principle" versus "does stealing happen fast enough given a specified threat model," which nobody ever specifies. Or it could go deeper — a bedrock disagreement about how the world works, roughly "important knowledge is codified and leaky" versus "important knowledge is tacit and sticky," that no evidence short of a full AI race would move. Diagnose which of these it is, or argue for a different diagnosis entirely, using what the participants actually say. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper formalization that forces the timescale and threat model to be stated, case studies of attempted technology-stealing in other domains, some distinction between capability-stealing and alignment-relevant-stealing, or nothing at all — and if nothing, say plainly whether the assumption is too underspecified to be worth arguing about in its current form.
