At the meditation group I attend, someone asked the teacher whether an enlightened person still feels physical pain, and the teacher answered with a line I've since heard a dozen times: "pain is inevitable, suffering is optional." I let it pass then, but the more I turn it over, the less I know what it asserts. It gets used in my circle to mean very different things — sometimes as a mere definitional reminder that "pain" and "suffering" are being used for different states, sometimes as an empirical claim about what meditation training can do, and once, from a friend who studies Buddhist ethics, as the expression of a whole value theory on which only the absence of suffering matters and positive feelings add nothing. Please disentangle for me the precise claims this one-liner could be making, and give a verdict on each. I'd want the menu to include at least: (1) the near-tautological reading, where suffering is stipulated to include resistance to pain, so that pain-without-resistance just doesn't count as suffering; (2) the empirical reading, on which the sensory and distressing components of pain genuinely dissociate — I gather there is a condition, pain asymbolia, in which people report feeling pain but are not bothered by it, and I'd like to know what such cases do and don't establish; (3) the normative-advice reading, where "optional" does real work — including the troubling suggestion that the sufferer is somehow at fault for not opting out; (4) the strong causal reading that craving is the sole generator of suffering, so that its elimination would remove suffering regardless of what else happens to you; and (5) the fully tranquilist reading, on which the slogan isn't advice at all but an axiom of value theory: the absence of suffering is the only thing worth having, and pleasant states are worthless. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and whether it would actually have answered the question the teacher was using it to answer — namely whether the enlightened still feel pain. Then tell me which reading the people who repeat the line probably have in mind, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. Please present the readings as a numbered list, ordered from weakest to strongest, that I can bring back to the group.
