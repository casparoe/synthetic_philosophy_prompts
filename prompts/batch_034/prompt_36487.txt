Write the joint statement that a committed Dennettian compatibilist and a committed Carusonian free will skeptic could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether anyone ever deserves, in a basic, backward-looking sense, to suffer for wrongdoing. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Include at least: that determinism (or its epistemic stand-ins, constitutive luck and early environmental luck) does not, by itself, make prediction or explanation of behavior impossible; that no one is an uncaused cause of themselves, and that the traditional "ultimate sourcehood" condition on responsibility cannot be met by anyone; that retributive attitudes have costs and that some practices of blame are corrosive; and that the criminal justice systems of most actual states are harsher than either party's view would license. Phrase each so that neither side would balk at the wording.

Second, a disagreements table. At minimum, the rows should cover: (1) whether the notion of desert Dennett retains is a genuine form of basic desert or a renamed forward-looking entitlement; (2) whether Caruso's target, "basic desert," is a real presupposition of ordinary practices or a philosopher's invention that ordinary responsibility talk can survive without; (3) whether giving up basic desert "diminishes" people — degrading our sense of authorship, achievement, and love — or liberates them; (4) whether punishment justified purely prospectively, on the quarantine model, can capture what proportionality to the offense is supposed to capture, or whether proportionality is covert desert-talk all the way down. Write each party's position in words that party would accept, and include one row where you record that both sides accept the same sentence but mean different things by a key term — identify the term.

Third, the cruxes. I want three, each stated so that settling it would move at least one side, and each paired with something both accept as bearing on it, with predictions recorded in advance. For the first crux, use the two-luck question: whether constitutive luck (the luck of being the kind of person you are) differs in kind or only in degree from resultant luck (the luck of how things turn out), and whether a responsibility-preserving difference can be drawn without appealing to the very sourcehood condition both reject. For the second, use a reverse-lottery case I'll describe and you should develop: a jurisdiction sentences convicted offenders to severity levels determined by a weighted lottery keyed to predicted deterrent effect, entirely independent of the seriousness of the offense. Both parties would reject this scheme — but record, for each, whether the rejection flows from their stated theory or from a residual desert intuition their theory officially disowns, and let each say what their view predicts they should feel about the scheme in ten years, were it empirically shown to reduce crime. For the third, use the fittingness question: whether an emotion like resentment or gratitude can be *fitting* to its object — appropriate in the way fear is fitting toward danger — even if it licenses no harsh treatment; each side should say whether a "fittingness without deserts" position belongs to the compatibilist's camp, the skeptic's camp, or dissolves the dispute between them.

Finally, a short closing section in which the parties record, honestly, which parts of their disagreement they now believe are substantive and which are verbal, and one thing each learned from the other that they did not expect to concede. Do not include citations; the parties know their own works. The document should read as though both actually signed it, not as a neutral summary written over their heads: where a formulation is delicate, show the compromise in the wording itself.
