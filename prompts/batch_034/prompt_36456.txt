I'm a PhD student in early medieval philosophy preparing an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need a two-page research proposal built out of a half-formed idea I've been circling for a while. My topic is Augustine on lying. Everyone knows the headline position: Augustine holds, against nearly every intuition we have, that lying is always wrong — no exception for the inquisitor at the door, no exception for saving a life. The usual treatment is to present this as an extreme deontological rule and then either admire its consistency or pound it with counterexamples. I think that framing misses where the real interest lies. Augustine's prohibition doesn't float free; in his two treatises on lying and in his general account of signs in the work on Christian teaching, the ban is presented as flowing from a theory of language: speech exists so that one mind can convey to another what is in it, so a lie is the corruption of a divinely instituted sign-system and an assault on the social bond that the system makes possible. If that's the real ground, then the view should have consequences nobody bothers to draw. Notably, the ground should classify cases differently than our intuitive notion of lying does: fictions, jokes, and irony might be non-lies non-arbitrarily (they don't aim at false belief), while some misleading but literally true statements — the paradigm of permissible 'mental reservation' that later Catholic casuistry allowed — should come out looking worse for Augustine than we'd expect, since they seem to weaponize the sign-system in a related way, yet he apparently lets them pass. The prohibition starts to look like a special case of a teleology-of-language doctrine rather than a bare rule.

The idea I want the proposal to develop and test: the interpretive claim that Augustine's absolutism is language-theoretic in its foundations explains his classification of cases better than the competing readings (the ban as divine command, the ban as a claim about corrupting the liar's soul, the ban as proto-Kantian respect for persons), and that the classification of cases — jokes, fiction, misleading truths, war-time deception, equivocation — is the proper evidence base for adjudicating between these readings. The competing readings make different predictions about where the treatises' distinctions should fall, and no one has, to my knowledge, run the comparison systematically. There's also a normative payoff if the interpretation is right: Augustine becomes an early interlocutor for the contemporary debate about whether lying is defined by asserting what you believe false or by intending to deceive, and his teleological apparatus is a resource for that debate rather than a museum piece.

Please write the proposal with: a precise statement of the research question (something answerable in a year, not 'what did Augustine think about lying'); an explanation of why the existing readings leave it open; the method — what I would actually do month by month over twelve months, given that the corpus is large but the relevant texts are manageable; what a positive result looks like and, just as important, what would count as the project failing (e.g., if the classification evidence turns out to be consistent with all the rival readings, or if Augustine's treatment of the sign theory is too thin to bear the weight); and a paragraph on the project's contribution both to Augustine scholarship and to the systematic debate about the definition of lying. Keep it to roughly two pages, formal in register, suitable for a fellowship committee of philosophers and theologians who know who Augustine is but not the finer points. Please do not include a bibliography or citations — I'll add references myself; you can refer to positions, arguments, and works by name where needed, but the proposal should stand on its own as prose.
