I've been trying to work out when cluelessness about consequences actually matters and when it doesn't — you know, the thought that every action has unknowable ripple effects, so maybe you can never really tell which act does more good. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases, and I'd like to know if there's a principle behind them:

1. Buying a latte vs. donating that £4 to an effective charity: the donation is definately better. I can't trace the long-run effects of either, but I don't think that makes them a tie.
2. Hiring one of two equally qualified candidates who interviewed identically: I don't think there's a fact of the matter about which hire is better. Whichever way the ripple effects go, they're so unknowable it seems to neutralise the comparison.
3. Deciding to have a child this year vs. waiting five years: the knowable consequences look pretty similar either way, but I feel sure there's a better and worse choice here — even though I couldn't begin to say which is which.
4. Picking a career partly on salary so I can donate more: I'm comfortable weighing the predictable differences and ignoring the butterfly effects, at least when the foreseeable gap is large.
5. Two charities identical on foreseeable outcomes, where I privately think one has a better long-run theory of change: I lean towards that one, but if my long-run story is pure armchair specualtion, I think it should count for nothing.

Cases 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions, and I can't for the life of me see what seperates them. So: is there one principle that gets all five, or close? If so, what does it predict for voting in a large election, which I honestly have no verdict on? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn, and why those rather than others? I'd rather you be honest about the trade-off than give me a principle so baroque it saves every verdict by cheating.
