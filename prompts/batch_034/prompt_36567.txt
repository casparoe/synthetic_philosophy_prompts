i'm a phd student in phil of science and i'm TAing the confirmation theory section of our intro course next week. my students always glaze over when i present Hempel's raven paradox as a chain of individually plausible steps, so this semester i want to try something dumb enough to actually be memorable: limericks. write me exactly five limericks, numbered 1 through 5, one per step of the paradox: (1) the hypothesis that all ravens are black, (2) Nicod's intuition that observing a black raven confirms it, (3) the logical point that "all ravens are black" is equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens", (4) the punchline that the same criterion then makes a white shoe (or a green apple) confirm that all ravens are black, and (5) what to make of the whole thing. each one should be technically accurate — a philosopher of science should be able to read them without wincing — while still being charming enough that undergrads walk away remembering the shoe.

one constraint on the fifth limerick: don't declare a winner. whether the paradox is a cheap dissolution (say, the bayesian point that the white shoe confirms the hypothesis, but only by a laughably tiny amount), a genuine lesson about what equivalence can do to confirmation, or an artifact of ignoring relevance, is actually contested among people who work on this, so limerick 5 should sit with the discomfort rather than preach one resolution as the consensus view. same for step 2 if you mention Hempel's own take on it — flag it as his, not everyone's.

after the five limericks, add a short note (a few sentences, that's all) telling me which of the five steps are just uncontroversial logic versus which limerick leans hardest on a contested reading, so i know what caveats to give in section. nothing else — no intro, no outro, just the five limericks and the note.
