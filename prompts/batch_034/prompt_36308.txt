At my monthly reading group for non-specialists, a debate about whether the ending of a novel we had just finished was emotionally earned or merely sentimental was cut short when a member declared that beauty is in the eye of the beholder, and that therefore no one in the room was in any position to say the author had botched it. I had the feeling the sentence was carrying more weight than it can bear, but I could not put my finger on where it overloads. Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise theses that one-liner might be asserting — for instance as a mere report about the speaker's own experience, as a semantic claim that aesthetic predicates just express attitudes rather than describe anything, as a claim that putative aesthetic disagreements are not really disagreements at all, or as a normative claim that while there may be facts of the matter, nobody is entitled to correct anybody about them — and say for each whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false, and, crucially, which of those readings would actually justify using the slogan to end the particular dispute we were having, which was not about whether the ending pleased each of us but about whether it was well made? A friend tells me Hume wrestled with something like this by appealing to the verdicts of qualified judges over time; if that strategy amounts to a further, distinct reading of the slogan, or shows one of the readings on my list to be unstable, I would like to know which. Please also state plainly what would change your verdicts: which consideration or argument would most plausibly reorder your assessment of the readings, and where in your reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong, rather than presenting the menu as if it were settled.
