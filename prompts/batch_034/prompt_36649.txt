Got into a proper row with my flatmate over too many beers about rationality, of all things, and there's an argument he walked me into that I can't find my way out of, but which also can't be right. Help me out.

(1) Rational credences obey the probability axioms, including that anything that's logically or mathematically certain gets probability 1.
(2) Now suppose you give some mathematical statement less than full credence -- say you're 50/50 on whether some enormous number-theoretic claim is true.
(3) If the claim is in fact a provable truth, a bookie who can see this can offer you bets on it at odds you'll happily accept, and since the claim is guaranteed to come out true, you lose money with certainty. Classic Dutch book setup.
(4) So any credence below 1 in a mathematical truth is irrational.
(5) But no actual agent assigns 1 to every mathematical truth. There are true statements whose shortest proofs are longer than the age of the universe, and there's no procedure that settles them all, even in principle.
(6) So no actual agent ever satisfies the axioms, and Bayesian rationality turns out to be a set of norms for logically omniscient beings -- who don't exist and possibly can't.

He was quite happy to bite the bullet and say the axioms are a "regulative ideal", like frictionless planes in physics. That seems to prove way too much -- a theory of rationality that only binds beings that can't exist is a strange sort of theory. But I can't find the dodgy step. My instinct is that something's off in (3): the bookie has to already know the claim is true in order to exploit me, so the argument quietly assumes an omniscient adversary and then blames me for not being one. My flatmate says that's special pleading -- not counting a bet as exploitative just because you personally can't see you'll lose is exactly what the axioms are supposed to rule out. And I notice if I try to fix (1) by saying the axioms only bind relative to what I've already deduced, it feels like the problem just gets spread out -- I'm now irrational one un-deduced theorem at a time, which is even more damning.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd quite like to hang on to some usable version of "obey the probability axioms," it's the one bit of rationality theory that's ever actually helped me, but if keeping it means only angels can be rational then I want to know what I'm really paying.
