Two friends in my AI reading group got into a long argument about whether "program equilibrium" actually solves the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and they asked me to referee, but honestly I don't know the terrain well enough. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right at each point, not just who wins overall?

The setup they were arguing about: two agents about to play a one-shot PD who can read each other's source code, where each one's code says roughly "cooperate iff the other's code is functionally equivalent to mine."

Round 1: Dan said this is overhyped -- it doesn't solve the PD at all, it just changes the game. Once you can read each other's code you've added moves and information, so it's a different game, and claiming you solved the original by playing a different one is like claiming you solved chess by switching to checkers. The theorem that rational agents defect is untouched.

Round 2: Mira said the game is unchanged -- payoff matrix identical, choices simaltaneous, code-reading isn't a move, it's common knowledge. And there now exist agents (the matching ones) who, inside the standard PD, walk away with cooperate/cooperate where any pair of defectors gets defect/defect. That's a substantive result about the original game, not a dodge.

Round 3: Dan said if my strategy is fully predictable to you, the situation is effectively sequential, and "cooperate iff you're like me" is doing the same work that repetition and punishment do in the iterated game -- it's the iterated PD with extra steps. Plus the whole thing depends on the verification channel, which gets assumed in from outside.

Round 4: Mira said you don't even need the channel: agents that reason similarly and know it can cooperate in a one-shot PD just off logical correlation -- each one's own choice is evidence about the other's -- and decision theories that take that seriously (she mentioned updatelessness) get mutual cooperation with no code reading at all. So the solution lives in being the right kind of agent, not in the extra information.

Round 5: Dan said then "rational" has been quietly redefined, and the classic lesson of the PD -- individually rational choices producing collective disaster -- is being lost by stipulation rather than refuted. And on Mira's own setup, the matching agents only prosper against each other; against everyone else they do no better than defectors do, so this is kin selection / correlation in CS clothing, which is fine, but call it that.

Mira's last word was that Dan keeps treating "the PD" as the payoff matrix plus a fixed conception of rationality, when the whole point of the literature is that the matrix is fixed but the conception of rationality was never up for grabs by the matrix.

Who's right about what here? I'd especially like to know whether "changing the game vs. solving the game" is even a clean distinction, and whether Mira's round-4 move is as strong as she thinks it is.
