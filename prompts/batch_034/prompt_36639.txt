I teach a bioethics seminar, and every year some student catches a wrinkle in health policy that I wave my hands at, so let me farm it out to you. Pretty much every health system on earth — national health services and market insurers alike — draws a line between treatment and enhancement and only pays for the treatment side. Insurers will buy growth hormone for a kid with a diagnosed pituitary deficiency but not for a kid who is equally short with no diagnosable cause; glasses get covered but LASIK typically doesn't, on the theory that if glasses work, surgery is a preference; a standard prosthetic leg is covered but the fancy running blade is a fight; antidepressants for depression are covered while a hypothetical pill that made a merely content person happier would never be; and yet statins are handed out to people with zero symptoms, purely to lower future risk. So here's what I want. What theory of health, function, or need does that whole bundle of practices presuppose? The obvious candidate is some restore-to-species-normal-functioning view, but I honestly can't tell whether the practice strictly needs that theory or whether it's mostly a rationing device that several different theories would happily license — check, in particular, against the prevention cases, which treat the not-yet-sick and so look like enhancement-by-another-name if the theory is really about restoring lost function. My own hunch is that the line has less to do with health than with what insurance is for: pooling misfortunes that befall you rather than satisfying preferences you choose, which would make the treatment/enhancement distinction a chance/choice distinction wearing a medical costume. Test that hunch against the examples — does it handle the statin case and the LASIK case, or does it break? Then reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, tell me which parts of the practice are doing real philosophical work and which are just budget lines, say where the practice is underdetermined (i.e., where rival theories of what health systems owe people would all converge on the same coverage decisions), and sketch what would actually have to change in coverage practice if a system swapped the presupposed view for, say, a straight welfarist one where anything that reliably improves wellbeing counts as treatment.
