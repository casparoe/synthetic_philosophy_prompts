I'm TA-ing an upper-level seminar called Ethics of Speech and I'm grading finals. There was one question on the lying/misleading distinction, worth 25 points, and I've got a stack of answers to get through tonight. Here's the question I gave them:

A murderer at your door asks where your friend is hiding. You believe she is in your attic. In Case A you reply, "She caught the 6:40 train to Berlin." In Case B you reply, "I saw her heading to the station around 6:30" — this is true (she set out, then turned back), and you say it precisely so that the murderer goes to the station. (a) Explain what, if anything, is supposed to distinguish lying from merely misleading someone, drawing on the definitional debate about what makes something a lie — e.g., whether an intention to deceive is really necessary, and what the liar does that the misleader does not. (b) Evaluate whether the distinction carries any moral weight here: can the verdicts on A and B come apart, and if so, what would have to be true for them to?

And here's one student's answer, typed out exactly as recieved:

"In Case A I make a statement I believe to be false with the intention that the murderer believes it, so it's a lie. In Case B everything I say is true, but I intend exactly the same false belief. The traditional idea is that the difference lies in assertion: the liar asserts what she believes false, while the misleader gets the same result with statements that are all true. Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated explains how Case B works: the murderer draws the false conclusion himself, through conversational implicature. In fact, all cases of misleading work through conversational implicature. And Grice's theory already explains why lying is worse: the liar violates the maxim of quality and breaks the cooperative contract, while the misleader merely exploits the assumption that he is being cooperative. So lying is a breach of trust in a way that misleading never is.

Kant's position is well known: Constant raised the murderer case against him, and Kant replied in 1797 that you may not lie even to the murderer, since a lie is always wrong and wrongs humanity in general. But he never says anything against misleading, and in the Doctrine of Virtue he says somewhere that when you cannot avoid answering a question you may give a true but misleading answer. So Case B is permissible even on Kant's own system: only lying is forbidden.

On the definition of lying, most contemporary philosophers follow Carson, who defines lying as making a statement you believe false with the intention to deceive the addressee. On this definition Case A is a lie and Case B is not, which is the right result.

Finally, notice that the consequences of A and B are the same, and my intention is the same in both, namely that the murderer form a false belief. A consequentialist therefore cannot distinguish them. Since the consequentialist can't tell them apart and the Gricean point is purely linguistic, no plausible theory can ground a moral difference between A and B. The intuition that lying is worse is just a confusion of a semantic distinction with a moral one. There may still be something weaselly about Case B — the misleader keeps his hands clean by letting the audience do the misleading to himself — but that's a point about style, not morality, and it could just as well make B worse than A."
