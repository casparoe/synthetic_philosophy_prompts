Next month I defend my dissertation chapter to a department that is heavy on formal epistemology: four or five card-carrying Bayesians who believe every rational update is ordinary conditionalization and are suspicious of anything that looks like a new update rule, two of whom are committed halfers about Sleeping Beauty and treat that commitment as a mark of seriousness generally, and one metaphysician who works on personal identity and persistence and will be alert to any premise that quietly takes a stand on those issues. There are no decision theorists and no anthropic reasoners in the room. The chapter defends a view I call phenomenally individuated SIA: the self-indication assumption is correct, but the entities whose numbers it weights must be individuated by total phenomenal state. When evaluating hypotheses about how many observers exist, you weight a world by the expected number of exact phenomenal duplicates of your current total experience — not by the number of observers, not by observer-moments sharing your evidence, and not by anything involving a selectable reference class. I claim this dissolves the presumptuous philosopher objection (in outline: two empirically equivalent cosmological theories, one with vastly more observers than the other, and the objection that SIA lets you infer the larger-observer theory with enormous confidence; on my view the surplus observers in the larger theory are mostly not phenomenal duplicates of you, so the likelihood ratio collapses toward one), and it deflates the Doomsday argument, but at the price that self-locating evidence almost never does much work.
