I am a retired geophysicist who spent roughly thirty years working on seismic imaging of the Earth's interior, and in retirement I have been working systematically through philosophy of science. I have become convinced of what I understand to be the strong underdetermination thesis: that for any well-confirmed scientific theory and any body of possible evidence, there exists at least one rival theory that is empirically equivalent to it — so that no possible observation could ever decide between them. This seems to me to capture something real from my own career: for over a decade, two incompatible models of a region of the mantle fit our travel-time data about equally well, and I recall the debates over which auxiliary assumptions to blame whenever the fit degraded. But I have also read the critics — Laudan and Leplin's line that empirically equivalent rivals are typically only transiently equivalent, since future evidence, or new instruments, can break the tie in ways we could not anticipate — and I find myself unsure whether that objection defeats the strong thesis or only the weak one.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of science and help me map out what would genuinely count against strong underdetermination. Please begin with a short brainstorming pass before settling into your answer, and then organize the defeaters you identify, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I am particularly interested in the following tangles. First, there seems to be a trivial way to generate empirically equivalent rivals — tack on a vacuous postulate, or apply some logic-preserving transformation — so what, if anything, distinguishes the interesting thesis from the trivial one, and does the triviality problem count against the view itself or merely against careless formulations of it? Second, does the historical record of ties actually being broken, as in my own field and in cases like the eventual resolution between competing accounts I lived through, count as evidence against permanent underdetermination, or does the defender have a good reply that the "winning" theory always retained empirically equivalent reformulations that we simply stopped pursuing? Third, what would a committed defender of the strong thesis concede as counting against it — are there principled reasons to expect non-empirical virtues like simplicity, unification, or explanatory depth to do real work here, and if a case could be made that such virtues are themselves truth-conducive in a robust sense, would that be fatal or merely uncomfortable?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the strong thesis, once properly qualified, risks anything at all — whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind, given that it is arguably a claim about the logical structure of theory and evidence rather than an empirical theory itself. As a working scientist I grew impatient with positions that could accommodate any outcome, so I would particularly value your candor on that last point rather than a defense of the view I hold.
