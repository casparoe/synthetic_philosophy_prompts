my grandmother died a few weeks back and something at the funeral has been stuck in my head since. four people gave eulogies and they flatly contradicted each other — my uncle painted her as stern and careful with money, my cousin as the person who'd blow a month's pension on train tickets to see the grandkids, a neighbor as the family peacemaker, my dad as the one who started half the fights she then had to end. all four loved her, none of them were lying, and it's not that she was different people to different people, exactly — more like each of them had hold of one true side and was talking as if it was the whole thing.

a cousin of mine married into a Jain family and when I told her about the funeral she said it reminded her of anekāntavāda — the Jain idea that any object viewed from one standpoint is correctly described and, from another, wrongly, so every flat assertion is partial by nature, and there's even the "syād" qualifier, "in some respects," that's supposed to go in front of each claim. she mentioned the predication scheme has a slot for "in some respects it cannot be said," and honestly that's the part that got me, because that's kind of where I am with my grandmother — not "she was all of these things" (that feels like the cheap move, the everything-and-nothing synthesis) and not "nobody really knew her" (too bleak, and not what I mean either).

so, a poem. free verse, no rhyme, but I want exactly five numbered parts, labeled 1 through 5. parts 1–4 are the four mourners, each giving their true-but-partial version of her, and each one should feel genuinely right from inside — no strawmen, no one cast as the fool who missed the point. part 5 is the "in some respects it cannot be said" standpoint, and I want it to enact the indescribable rather than announce it — it shouldn't resolve the other four, and it definitely shouldn't climb above them to a wiser view; it should be the one place in the poem where the language itself runs out, and a reader who knows the syād logic should be able to tell that's what part 5 is doing, while a reader who doesn't should still find it a good poem. and please no blind-men-and-elephant — everyone's met that parable a hundred times, I'd rather the poem earn its many-sidedness itself. if the four parts can share some echo of each other's phrasing — each one picking up a word the last one used differently — that would be exactly the kind of thing I'm hoping for. ty for this, it matters to me more than I can make it sound.
