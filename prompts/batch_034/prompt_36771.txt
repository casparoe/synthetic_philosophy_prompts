I've been hanging around the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, so I've probably read every "Aumann's agreement theorem" post ever written -- the ones explaining that if two Bayesians with common priors have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, they can't disagree, the ones about whether that means you should update toward the person you're arguing with at dinner, all of it. It's a theorem I've taken mostly on faith as "true under assumptions that never hold." But I've been rereading Keynes's Treatise on Probability lately (the 1921 one, the philosophy book he wrote before he was the Keynes) after someone in a comment thread brought it up, and I keep wondering how a genuine technical-philosophical mind from that era would have reacted to the agreement theorem if you sat him down and explained the whole setup carefully.

Here's what makes me think Keynes specifically is an interesting test case. He had this notion of the "weight of argument" -- the idea that two probabilities can be identical while one is backed by lots of relevant evidence and the other by very little, and that this difference matters practically even though the probability number doesn't change. He also thought most real probabilities just aren't numerical, and he was deeply suspicious of what you get when you force a precise number out of thin evidence. It seems to me like all of that is pointed straight at the agreement theorem's weak spots: the theorem tells agents to converge once they've exchanged posteriors, but exchanging a number doesn't obviously exchange the *weight* behind the number, and Keynes might say two people who report the same credence but hold it with wildly different weights are still not in the epistemic state the theorem requires. And then there's the common prior assumption, which Keynes, who disliked uniform priors derived from ignorance, might greet with a raised eyebrow. But maybe I'm forcing it -- maybe he'd just say fine, this is a neat logical result about ideal agents, consistent with his whole "probability is a logical relation between propositions" picture, and the folk conclusion people draw from it online is the thing he'd actually mock.

So my question: suppose Keynes could be briefed on modern Bayesian epistemology and Aumann's 1976 result, including the later stuff about what happens when you relax the assumptions (someone once explained to me a version where agents just average their opinions back and forth until they meet in the middle, no full posteriors ever exchanged -- I only half remember the details, don't judge me). Would Keynes see the agreement theorem as a friendly formalization of his own view, or as exactly the kind of spurious precision he wrote the Treatise to push back against? Where exactly would his weight-of-argument concept bite against the convergence results, and where would it fail to bite? And secondarily -- is the rationalist community's use of the theorem (the whole "rational agents can't agree to disagree" move) closer to the rigorous result or to the folk thing Keynes would have hated? I'd love a real answer and not just "the assumptions don't hold in practice," which is the vibe everyone retreats to when pushed on this.
