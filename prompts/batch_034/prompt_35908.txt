Design a pair of matched thought experiments about Hilbert's Grand Hotel, built to isolate one question: is our discomfort with the hotel actually about infinite cardinality, or only about the fact that in the standard case "full" stops implying "no room for more"?

The pair must share everything: the same fully occupied, countably infinite hotel, the same capable manager, the same administrative setup. They may differ in exactly one feature. In the first scenario, that feature is set so the manager's standard trick works — the hotel is full, yet infinitely many new guests are accommodated without evicting anyone. In the second, that same feature is changed so that the hotel genuinely cannot take even one additional guest, and — this is the hard part — the impossibility must be demonstrated rather than stipulated: you may not write that the rooms mysteriously refuse or that the doors will not open; you must show, at a level an undergraduate who knows what a bijection is could follow, that every reassignment scheme the manager might run leaves at least one arriving guest in the lobby. A diagonal construction is the natural tool, but any rigorous route is acceptable.

Constraints: keep each scenario to roughly one paragraph of setup; introduce no physics beyond the premise of infinitely many rooms (no supertask check-ins, no demons, nothing about the speed of the front desk); and do not stipulate any verdict in the scenario's wording — the verdicts must fall out of the mathematics.

After presenting the pair, walk through the following:

1. The demonstration for the second scenario: show, do not assert, that no scheme works.
2. Which single feature is doing the work, and what happens to the pair if you restore that feature — does the second scenario collapse back into the first?
3. What the pair shows about the original Hilbert case: was the fact that a full hotel can still absorb infinitely many guests ever a fact about fullness, or only an accident of the arriving party being countable? Say what your answer does to the case's persuasive power as an argument against finitism — does vindicating our finite-intuition in the second scenario cost the infinitist anything?
4. The finitist's best attack on the pair as a whole (for instance, an argument that the two scenarios are equally incoherent, so the contrast between them is worthless), and one thing the Cantorian can say in reply.

The deliverable is the pair plus the analysis; the point of the exercise is the isolation, so if you find you need more than the one feature to generate the contrast, say so and explain — a pair that needs two moving parts is a different, and less interesting, thought experiment than the one I have asked for.
