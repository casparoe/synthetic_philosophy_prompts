I'm a third-year student taking a course on collective action problems, and we just did the unit on multipolar traps and races to the bottom — the "Moloch" idea, where everybody races to the bottom because nobody can afford to unilaterally stop, and the result is worse for all concerned even though nobody actually wanted it. For my term paper I want to run the EPO era of professional cycling, roughly the mid-90s through the late 2000s, through that framework. My professor approved the idea but warned me the mapping has to be disciplined, so I want to think it through properly before I write, and figure out both what the framework explains and where it honestly breaks.

The facts I'm working with, which I can state in the paper: recombinant EPO spread through the peloton in the early 90s and there was no test for it until around 2000, so a banned substance was effectively undetectable; the UCI's response in the mid-90s was a 50% hematocrit ceiling, where a rider over it was pulled from racing for a couple of weeks as a "health" measure rather than treated as a doping case, and everyone in the sport read that as the real rule being stay just under 50; in 1998 the Festina soigneur Willy Voet was stopped at the French-Belgian border with a car full of EPO and the whole thing blew up — police raids on team hotels, riders in custody, confessions — and it came out that doping was systematic, organised at the team level, and close to universal among the riders; guys who wouldn't participate, like Christophe Bassons, got frozen out and basically hounded out of the sport; and even after all that the doping carried on through the Armstrong years until detection actually caught up (the EPO test, later the biological passport).

So here's what I want to work through. Who are the players and what are the payoffs — riders, teams, sponsors, the governing body, maybe even fans who want faster races — and is staying clean strictly dominated for a rider whose contract depends on results, such that an all-doping peloton is an equilibrium nobody chose? Should the 50% rule be read as an attempted escape from the trap that instead turned into a coordination point that stabilised it (dope to 49.9)? What does Festina show, given that a massive public scandal didn't dissolve the trap — is that evidence for the Moloch view that exposure and moralising don't matter, only changing the payoff structure does? And above all, where does the model break? The thing that keeps bothering me is the omertà: a multipolar trap is supposed to be a situation where coordination fails, but the pelicot obviously did coordinate — to protect the doping, through a code of silence enforced against defectors. It looks like they solved a coordination problem in order to keep the arms race going, and I can't find a place for that in the framework as I understand it. Is that a genuine anomaly, or does it work out if you set the units differently, say teams rather than individual riders? And where else should I be honest that the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak?

Please write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — like a long email to a friend who knows this stuff. And don't hold back on telling me where my framing is off, I'd much rather hear it from you than from the grader.
