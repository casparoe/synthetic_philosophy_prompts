I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar that pairs the ethics of speech with works of fiction, and my next unit is on the question of whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading. The core materials are the familiar ones: Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated, the robust intuition that asserting a falsehood is somehow worse than crafting your words so that a listener draws a false conclusion on their own, and the revisionist position that this asymmetry is an illusion — that what matters is the deception, not the linguistic route by which it's achieved. A structural wrinkle I want the unit to confront: misleading via implicature seems parasitic on a background norm of sincerity, a kind of free-riding on other people's honesty that might itself carry independent moral weight.

For the literary pairing I've chosen Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, because Stevens is arguably the great laboratory specimen for this question: he almost never asserts a falsehood outright. He evades, withholds, changes the subject, and lets others construct false beliefs from his silences — most obviously in the scenes where he allows strangers to take him for a gentleman and declines to correct them about his history with Lord Darlington, and in his interchanges with Miss Kenton, where his non-answers do the work that lies would do in a more forthright man. And there is a further layer I find philosophically interesting but am unsure how to handle: the novel is narrated by Stevens, so his misdirections are also directed at himself. Whether the lying/misleading distinction — designed for speech acts with an audience other than the speaker — even applies to self-directed discourse is something I'd like the reading to address rather than assume.

Please produce a reading of the novel through this lens. Does Ishiguro dramatize the moral asymmetry between lying and misleading, or expose it as a loophole Stevens has engineered to preserve his precious "dignity" while still getting the deniability he wants? Distinguish carefully between the episodes where Stevens merely misleads, the ones where he arguably crosses into outright assertion of falsehood, and the ones where he is simply silent — and say whether those categories carry different moral weights in the novel's own economy. Take both the novel and the philosophy seriously in both directions: don't reduce Stevens to an illustration of Grice, and don't bend the philosophy to fit the book. Where the novel resists the reading — where its moral center arguably lies elsewhere, in complicity or self-deception rather than in the lying/misleading distinction — say so plainly rather than forcing the fit.

Since this is criticism rather than argument, I don't need a verdict you'd change your mind about, but I do want the nearest equivalent: end with a short note identifying the interpretive claim the reading most depends on, and which specific scene or rival reading would most threaten the thesis if pressed. And since I'll be teaching this to students, flag any point where the reading leans on a contested interpretation of Grice or of the novel rather than something defensible from common ground.
