PhD student in ML here, working on folding expert judgments into probabilistic models, and I keep hitting a wall I suspect has a real literature behind it. The situation: someone hands me information that's purely conditional. Like a security analyst telling me "if the attacker already has admin access, then the breach is about three times as likely to have come through the vendor portal as through the VPN" — while being explicit that she has no view at all on whether the attacker has admin access. Or an elicitation session where a geologist will commit to "given the fault is active, the chance of rupture within 30 years is 0.9" but refuses to say anything about whether the fault is active. So I've got these three-way partitions (antecedent; one way the antecedent-world could go; another way), a constraint on the ratios inside the antecedent branch, and literally nothing else. Plain Bayes doesn't help because there's no likelihood to plug in — the constraint is compatible with a whole family of posteriors, and different members of that family give different overall probabilities for the thing I actually care about ("breach came through the vendor portal", full stop, no antecedent attached).

What got me thinking there's a named puzzle here is that everyone on my team has a different reflex and none of them can justify theirs. One guy refuses to move at all ("it's not evidence until you learn something about the antecedent"). Another keeps the antecedent's probability exactly where it was and only shuffles things around inside the branches. A third picks whatever posterior is in some sense closest to the prior, which sounds natural until you ask what "closest" means and people start fighting about measures. And a fourth just says ask the analyst for a full joint distribution, which dodges the question rather than answering it — the puzzle is what to do when you can't get more than the conditional.

So: does this configuration have a name in philosophy of probability or the Bayesian literature? If there's a canonical version I'd like to know what it's called, what the main proposed answers are, and where they genuinely disagree — but please be explicit with me about which parts are just math (settled, everyone who works on this agrees) and which parts are actually contested, e.g. whether any of these update rules is required by rationality or is just a convention some people like. My strong suspicion is that at least one popular rule gets presented like a theorem when it's really a choice, and I'd much rather know where that line sits than get a confident answer that papers over it.
