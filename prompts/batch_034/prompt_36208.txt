The answer I have to assess is:

"Both views start from the same datum: when the depositor stops by on Friday with plenty of time, we say she knows the bank is open on Saturdays; when she's there on Saturday and her rent check would bounce if it weren't, we hesitate, even though her evidence is unchanged. Contextualism explains this by treating 'knows' like an indexical such as 'I' or 'tall.' The truth of a knowledge attribution depends on the subject's practical stakes: when the stakes are high, more is required for 'S knows p' to be true, so the high-stakes attribution comes out false while the low-stakes one comes out true. The contextualist can add that in high-stakes situations, error possibilities that we could otherwise ignore — that the bank changed its hours — become relevant alternatives that must be ruled out. Interest-relative invariantism (Stanley) agrees that 'knows' is context-sensitive in this way, but locates the sensitivity in the subject's practical situation rather than the speaker's. Since the two views agree about every case — high stakes or low, each view makes the attribution true or false in just the same way — the dispute between them is merely verbal: the invariantist describes the variation as variation in whether the subject knows, while the contextualist describes it as variation in the standards for the word 'knows,' but nothing substantive divides them. And this is the right result, since whether you know can't really depend on your interests: knowledge is a relation between you and the world, and the world doesn't change when your interests do."
