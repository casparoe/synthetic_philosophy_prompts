been arguing with a friend about whether philosophy ever actually goes anywhere, and I realized my own gut verdicts on specific cases are a mess, so help me figure out what principle (if any) is behind them. here's roughly where I land on a bunch of cases:

- logic from Frege through Gödel: clear progress. nobody teaches the syllogistic as the last word anymore, and we now know things (incompleteness, compactness) that no amount of pre-Frege thinking was ever going to find.
- the Euthyphro dilemma: zero progress? it's 2400 years old, both horns still have defenders, and I cant name anything we know now that Plato didn't. though a friend pointed out we at least now know which questions in that neighborhood are load-bearing, so maybe partial credit.
- Gettier: I keep flip flopping. everyone agrees justified true belief isn't knowledge, and the counterexample style invented there is now standard equipment everywhere in philosophy, but after 60 years there is no accepted replacement analysis. feels like progress in tools, zero progress in answers.
- Kant's claim that euclidean geometry is the a priori form of intuition: refuted by non-euclidean geometry plus relativity. I want to say that counts as progress somehow — philosophy got corrected by something outside philosophy — but I can't explain why being wrong counts as progress.
- the absolute space fight between Leibniz and Newton (via Clarke): the puzzle basically dissolved once general relativity arrived, nobody won in 1716. but I DO count this one as progress, and I'm not sure how it differs from the Kant case except that it feels different.
- mind-body problem: more positions on the table now than in 1650, none of them refuted to anyone's satisfaction. honestly feels like regress.
- personal identity: after Parfit's fission cases I think most working philosophers agree psychological continuity is what matters and identity per se isn't — not settled, but converging. I'd call that progress even though it's not resolved.

and then the case that really worries me: philosophy of mind slowly spawning psychology as its own discipline in the 1800s. I want to count that as philosophy making progress — the questions got answered, just by a discipline that stopped calling itself philosophy. but my friend says thats cheating, that's philosophy losing its questions, not answering them. and by the same move you could say ethics made progress whenever society morally improved (abolition, etc.), which I absolutely do NOT want to say — that feels like taking credit for work other people did.

so: is there a single notion of philosophical progress that saves all or most of these verdicts? does it treat the Kant case and the absolute space case the same way, and if not, which of those two verdicts should I give up? does it have anything principled to say about the mind-body one, because if progress is supposed to mean "narrowing the option space," mind-body looks like regress times ten. and what does the principle predict for a case I haven't listed — I'm thinking of free will after the neuroscience stuff (Libet and so on), where the science arrived but philosophers mostly just reorganized around it. happy to hear which of my verdicts you'd just flat out overturn.
