I'm a hardware engineer working on power electronics and functional safety: fuses, TVS diodes, crowbar circuits, protective relaying, conformal coatings, derating curves, fault-tree analysis, highly accelerated life testing, "no-fire" thresholds for energetic devices. I'm fluent in that vocabulary and have no philosophy training. I recently stumbled into the metaphysics literature on dispositions and was struck by how familiar it felt — as I understand it, one of the stock counterexamples in that debate is literally an electro-fragile wire hooked to a device that would disconnect or protect it before it could break. In my world that isn't a thought experiment; it's an ordinary Tuesday of design review.

Here is the debate as I've pieced it together, and I'd like you to correct me where I've misread it before doing the main task. Philosophers once hoped to analyze disposition ascriptions with a conditional: a thing is fragile iff it would break if struck, soluble iff it would dissolve if immersed. The counterexamples come in families. Finks: something that would remove the very disposition at the moment the stimulus arrives, so the conditional is false but the disposition is present. Masks: something that blocks the manifestation without removing the disposition at all. Antidotes: the trigger fires and the manifestation begins, but an intervening process intercepts it. The conditional analysis keeps dying, and the repair attempts get increasingly baroque.

The task: restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report the residue. My own first pass suggests rough mappings — masks as conformal coating or potting compound, finks as protection circuits that race the fault (the breaker that clears before the fuse can blow, the crowbar that clamps the transient before the junction avalanches), antidotes as interlocks that trip mid-sequence — but I don't trust my first pass, and I want you to work through the counterexample families one at a time. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then address two candidates for the residue that I've spotted in my own reading:

First, the intrinsicality question. The philosophers seem to care whether a disposition belongs to the object "considered in itself," so that a finked or masked object still has the disposition even though, in situ, it would never manifest. In engineering practice this question never arises: a fuse behind a breaker is not "a fragile thing whose fragility is masked," it's a correctly designed subsystem, and the spec is written at the assembly level. "Is the fuse still fragile, taken alone?" is not a question my discipline can even pose operationally. Is that because my vocabulary only ever takes a system-level stance, or because the question is confused?

Second, the question of what a disposition is over and above its physical basis. I have failure mechanisms (the metallurgy and geometry of the fuse element) and failure modes (it opens the circuit), but nothing in my practice corresponds to asking whether the fuse's rated interrupting capacity is an additional property over and above that metallurgy, or just a shorthand for how the metallurgy behaves under specified test conditions. Philosophers, I gather, argue about whether dispositions are identical to their categorical bases, or irreducible, or the fundamental things themselves. Translate that dispute too, as far as it goes.

Then give me the verdict, and I want honesty in both directions. Is the residue a limitation of an engineering vocabulary that only ever takes the design-for-purpose, system-level view — something my vocabulary is missing — or is it a sign that the metaphysical questions are artifacts of a way of talking my field was wise never to adopt? Don't flatter me by pretending engineering dissolves the puzzle, and don't smuggle my framework's assumptions into the translation and present them as results. One constraint in particular: most of my vocabulary is shot through with function — a breaker is *for* protecting, a coating is *for* isolating — whereas the philosophical notion of a disposition is supposed to apply to purposeless objects like glaciers and gall bladders. Flag explicitly where that difference does or doesn't matter to the translation, because I suspect it does more work than it first appears to.
