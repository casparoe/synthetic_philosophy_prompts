Got into a long argument with my friend Marcus last night that ran way past dinner. It started because I ordered the veggie burger and he accused me of being preachy, and it turned into a two-hour fight about whether my individual food choices do anything at all. He kept hitting me with the standard line: one person going vegetarian doesn't reduce the number of animals raised and slaughtered — production gets set way in advance, my one burger doesn't tip any threshhold, and if I abstain someone else just eats what I don't. The annoying thing is I think he's right about single acts, and I'm tired of defending them with expected-value gymnastics about tiny chances of crossing a batch boundary (which he, rightly I think, called spooky). So I stayed up and wrote notes for a short essay defending vegetarianism anyway on a different basis. Can you comment on whether it works?

The core idea is a distinction between what I'm calling spot inefficacy and trajectory inefficacy. Spot inefficacy: my individual act today makes no difference to production — I concede this wholesale. My claim is that the inefficacy objection only ever establishes spot inefficacy, and the morally relevant unit isn't the act but the pattern: a stable years-long practice of abstaining, unlike a single skipped burger, is the kind of thing that shows up in the demand signals retailers and forecasters actually aggregate over. The act is invisible; the pattern is visible. But then my own worry is that the pattern is nothing over and above many acts, each invisible on its own — so maybe I've just relabeled the problem instead of solving it. That's the main thing I want your comments on: does the trajectory move actually answer the inefficacy objection, or does it just change the subject?

Two smaller things as well. (1) Is my claim about demand aggregation empirically plausible, or am I hand-waving about how supply chains actually work? (2) At one point I compare the pattern to a pledge — I say abstaining over years is like a promise because it commits future acts — and Marcus said that's a category error, since only persons promise, not behaviors. Is he right, and if so does it matter for the essay?

One more thing since I may send this somewhere: for each verdict you give, say roughly how confident you are in it, and single out the point in your comments you're least sure of.
