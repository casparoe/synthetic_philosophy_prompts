After a career in psychophysics — mostly contrast adaptation and aftereffects, in the tradition of the vision laboratories of the 1980s and 90s — I have lately been reading philosophy of perception, and I keep encountering the claim that belief, desire, or conceptual know-how penetrates experience itself: the oenologist tasting the vintage that a novice cannot, the racially primed subject seeing a face as angrier than it is, the fisherman seeing a catch where I see weeds. Before I evaluate any theory of this, I want the specification first. State the desiderata that any adequate theory of cognitive penetrability must meet: which empirical phenomena it must accommodate without reclassifying every top-down effect as attention, adaptation, or response bias; which distinctions it must respect — penetration proper versus pre-perceptual tuning of the inputs versus post-perceptual judgment, and effects on perceptual content versus effects on phenomenology alone; what it must say about the epistemic consequence, since an experience that has been shaped by a belief seems unable to serve as independent testimony for that very belief; and how it must handle the stubbornness of illusions, which refuse to shrink no matter how often I have measured the lines and know them to be equal. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of one camp. Then say which pair of the desiderata, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied — my suspicion, from the laboratory, is that a theory that both captures the expert's genuine perceptual advantage and preserves the epistemic constraint is impossible, but I would like to see the argument. Close with a brief scorecard of predictive-coding accounts, where priors reach down into perception itself, against your list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate my question.
