I've been wrestling with this thing in ethics about when an action counts as having moral worth, and specifically what happens when an agent's motives are all mixed together. Maybe you can untangle it for me.

The background idea (roughly Kantian but also just common in the "acting for the right reasons" literature) is that an action has moral worth only if the agent does it *because* it's the right thing to do -- the moral feature of the action has to be doing the motivational work, not just hanging around in the background. Fine. But now consider cases where someone has several motives at once, and they're all actually causally sufficient for the action on their own.

My example: a woman volunteers at a soup kitchen. She genuinely cares about hungry people, that's real. But she also enjoys the social status of being seen as charitable, and she feels guilty about her comfortable life, and there's a guy she likes who volunteers there too. Suppose -- and here's the part I want to focus on -- each of these motives alone would have been enough to get her to show up. So in some sense the moral motive isn't *necessary* for the action, since the vanity or the crush would have produced it anyway. Does the overdetermination ruin the moral worth? Or is the right test something counterfactual, like: would she have acted *differently* (slower, more grudgingly, less carefully) if the moral motive were absent? I've also seen the suggestion that you should ask not whether the action would have occurred, but whether the *manner* of the action tracks the moral motive -- how attentive she is to the diners' dignity, say, versus just being seen near them.

Please explain this conceptual issue: the problem of assigning moral worth when motives are overdetermined or mixed, what the different candidate tests are (necessity, counterfactual sensitivity of the manner of action, whether the agent would endorse the act from a moral point of view), and where each test gives weird results. Feel free to invent your own variations on my soup kitchen case to stress-test them -- I don't need any names of philosophers or papers, just the ideas, explained causually. Also curious whether moral worth could come in degrees instead of being all-or-nothing, which would maybe take some of the pressure off.
