I am a novelist at work on a scene set at Down House in the late 1860s, in which a fictional guest — a clever, somewhat impertinent young freethinking journalist — cross-examines Darwin over the port about the implications his own theory carries for religion and morality. My training is in fiction rather than the history of science, and my problem is dramaturgical rather than scholarly: the confrontation never took place, so no source records it, and I must decide what my Darwin says in a way that is true to the man rather than to a modern character wearing his beard. Rather than ask you to invent his dialogue, I want you to reason from his pattern of verdicts, which I have assembled below.

Here is the record I am working from. On natural theology: he found the traditional argument from design unpersuasive — the clumsy contrivances, the endless gradations, the wasp that lays its eggs in a living caterpillar strike him as the work of something other than a beneficent designer — though he says such things mainly in private correspondence, never in the books. On conscience: he treats it as an outgrowth of the social instincts, memory, and habit, not a divine implant, yet nowhere suggests that explaining its origin discredits it; and when the vivisection debates came, he landed on the side of experiment while working quietly for humane regulation. On religion personally: his faith eroded gradually and privately, he described himself in later years as agnostic rather than atheist, he refused to attack Christianity in print or from any platform, and he sat his whole married life respectfully alongside his devout wife's belief. On the public use of his theory: he disliked seeing it fired as artillery at the clergy and kept the more aggressive philosophical materialists at arm's length. And once, in a private note, he confessed that when his reasoning arrived at certain conclusions, a horrid doubt would arise about whether a mind developed from that of the lower animals could be trusted to reach true conclusions at all — a doubt he applied to his own convictions and left unresolved.

Here is the scene I must write. The journalist's challenge: if conscience is nothing but inherited herd habit, then when it inconveniences me its authority over me is spent — your own theory cuts the ground from under your beloved moral sense. And if you retreat to the horrid doubt, doubting the minds of evolved creatures, then your theory falls with the rest, for it is the product of just such a mind. At that point my scene requires Darwin's reply, and I confess I can see him answering in more than one register. So tell me, please: which consistent rules actually fit this track record — the private skeptic who declines public combat on principle; the naturalist for whom explaining how a sentiment arose is not the same as debunking it; the gentleman who simply partitions science, faith, and morals into separate rooms of the house; something else again. For each rule, what does it predict Darwin says to the journalist in that moment — does he draw the line between the origin of a belief and its truth, does he concede the doubt but refuse to generalize it beyond the religious convictions where he first felt it, does he take refuge in manner and change the subject? Give me your best guess at the reply most true to the man and your reasons for it, and then one further thing — another verdict from his life I should add to my file, or one further question the journalist could put — that would best distinguish the candidate readings from one another, since the shape of my second scene depends on which one I commit to.
