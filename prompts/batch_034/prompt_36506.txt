In Dennett's "Quining Qualia," Chase and Sanborn are two coffee tasters: Chase has drunk the same coffee for years and now dislikes it, and cannot tell whether his preference changed, his quality-detectors changed, or his memory of how much he used to enjoy it is inflated; Sanborn moved from mass-market coffee to fancier stuff and wonders, in reverse, whether he now tastes defects he used to ignore or merely misremembers his earlier enjoyment. Dennett takes cases like these to show that qualia, as standardly conceived — ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately given to introspection — are an incoherent posit rather than real but merely absent. My credence that Dennett's conclusion is correct is about 0.35. Please give me your own number, but the number matters less than the decomposition. I see at least these sub-questions: (1) whether the classical marks of qualia are jointly inconsistent, so the posit is contradictory, or merely jointly unsatisfiable, so it is coherent but empty; (2) whether the Chase–Sanborn indeterminacy is genuine — whether any fact, even in principle, settles for a single person which of experience, preference, and memory shifted — and whether, if the indeterminacy holds only for slow drift over years, that limits how far the argument generalizes to the apparently instantaneous comparisons that seem to ground the marks; (3) whether a coherent residue survives if one or two marks are dropped, for example by conceding that experiences are relational rather than intrinsic, and whether that residue is still a quale in any sense that matters; and (4) whether the whole dispute is substantive rather than verbal — a fight over the word rather than the facts. Assign a probability to each sub-question, show how they combine into your overall credence, tell me which sub-question your number is most sensitive to and what argument or kind of case would move it most, and — since I sit at 0.35 — which sub-question most likely holds the disagreement between us.
