I'm writing up my views on decision theory for a seminar where everyone gets their position statement torn apart, and I want a consistency check before mine goes under the knife. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the independence axiom is a genuine requirement of rationality, at least in its coin-flip form: if I prefer gamble A to gamble B, then a coin flip that pays A on heads must be preferred to the same coin flip paying B on heads — mixing both options with a third prospect can't flip the ranking.

(2) In Allais' famous pair, I'd take the guaranteed £1 million over the gamble that pays £1m with 89% chance, £5m with 10% chance, and nothing with 1% chance.

(3) In the companion choice I'd take the 10% shot at £5m (nothing otherwise) over the 11% shot at £1m.

(4) I'm not remotely worried about (2) and (3), because that's just risk aversion, and risk aversion is perfectly rational — it's nothing more than the diminishing marginal utility of money.

(5) I also accept the reduction of compound lotteries: a two-stage gamble is worth exactly the simple gamble you get when you multiply out all the probabilities, and a rational agent shouldn't care whether a risk is resolved in one throw or several.

(6) Expected utility theory is a theory of ideal rationality, not a prediction of what people actually do, so the fact that most experimental subjects make the same choices I do in both rounds doesn't bother me at all.

Do these hang together? If something has to give, tell me which commitment is cheapest to drop and what dropping it costs me. Please rank any tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally fatal — and I genuinely don't know where the problem is, or if there even is one. One thing I keep going back and forth on: whether (1) as I've stated it even applies to the Allais situation, or whether it only bites once you bring (5) in, so I'd appriciate some care on that specifically.
