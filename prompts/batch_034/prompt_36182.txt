I help advise a small philanthropic fund that can support around eight researchers for three years, and we're considering committing the whole of that window to conceptual and normative work on moral status — specifically the assumption, which most of us on the committee accept but none of us has worked out, that sentience comes in degrees and that moral status may inherit that structure. The easy cases are easy; what we keep circling back to is the region between "clearly counts for a lot" (a dog, say) and "clearly counts for almost nothing" (a thermostat), and we've noticed the field's energy is scattered across very different kinds of questions in that region. Some are conceptual: is moral status itself smooth or step-like even if sentience is graded — is there a threshold in moral status even where there is none in the underlying capacity? Some are aggregative: if a being's suffering counts for very little, how do we weigh billions of such beings against a smaller number of more richly sentient ones, and does "counts for very little" behave like a small weight or like a discount that changes the arithmetic? Some are decision-theoretic: what should an actor do under severe uncertainty near the boundary — can expected-value reasoning be trusted where the moral difference between "zero" and "a tiny bit" is the whole question, or does uncertainty of that shape push toward thresholds and fallback rules? And some are what I'd call criteria-refinement: if our best functional and behavioral indicators of sentience would, taken at face value, be satisfied by alarm systems and simple control loops, does that show the indicators are too permissive, or that permissiveness at the bottom is the price of objectivity anywhere in the scale?
