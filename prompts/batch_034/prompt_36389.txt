I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a literary novel set in eleventh-century Persia whose narrator is a scholar quietly wrecked by the suspicion that nothing causes anything, and I need your help with something distracting that keeps pulling me out of the book. While researching him I kept tripping over Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I noticed it sounds uncannily like my day job. When I write that she struck the match and the flame caught, nothing inside the story makes the striking do anything -- I wrote both events, one sentence after the other, and the causal glue between them is supplied by my readers habits, not by any power resident in the match. So here's the ask: take on the role of a philosopher of religion who genuinely understands narrative craft (not one who name-drops it), and restate Ghazālī's core position in my working vocabulary. That means translating his claims -- God as the only true agent, created things having no causal power over each other, fire doing nothing to cotton, the regularities we naively call causation being just His settled custom in the order of events, miracles being possible precisely because there are no necessary connections to suspend -- into terms like drafts and revisions, scene versus summary, continuity, retcons, Chekhov's gun, point of view, and the distinction between what's literally on the page and what's true "in the story." Assume I know the outlines of his position from secondary reading but have never touched the Arabic; don't pad it with history-of-philosophy background I could get anywhere.

The translation is only half of it though, and honestly the less interesting half. I want a residue report, done properly: which of his claims map cleanly onto the novelist's picture, which ones have to be bent or fudged to fit at all, and which ones my vocabulary simply cannot express. I already suspect a few pressure points, and I want you to check me on them rather than politely agree. For instance: I finished my draft once, and the novel now sits there existing whether or not I keep writing, but the occasionalist world seems to need continuous re-creation at every instant, which is closer to a book that only exists while its being composed, sentence by sentence, forever. My characters have no causal power but also no being except on the page. And my sense that I, the author, am the villain's knife's true killer -- is that genuinely Ghazālī's point about God's agency, or a seductive distortion I should be embarrassed by? Think carefully, maybe sketch two or three candidate translations before commiting to the best one, and then give me a verdict on the residue: does what my craft vocabulary can't carry point to something real in his metaphysics that narrative can't articulate, or does it show the original position is less clear than it looked, or is the whole parallel just a nice essay waiting for someone else to write?

Start your reply with the bottom line in the very first sentence -- something like which parts of the translation survive and which do not, in one line -- and then give me the reasoning and the full report, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
