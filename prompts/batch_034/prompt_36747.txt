I teach philosophy of physics, and for the past two years a colleage in the physics department and I have been stuck in the same argument every time we get coffee: he's what you'd call psi-ontic, he thinks the quantum state is a real thing out there in the world, and I keep insisting it's closer to a state of information or a bookkeeping device, which makes me roughly psi-epistemic. We've decided to stop sniping and do this as a proper adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement we could both actually put our names to.

Here's what has to be in it. First, the claims we can both sign — and I mean the technical ones, not platitudes: that the formalism generates probabilities through the Born rule, that nonorthogonal states can never be perfectly distinguished in a single shot, that different preparations can produce the same statistics while the formalism still assigns them different states, that neither of us doubts quantum mechanics as a predictive machine. Second, the disagreement itself, stated twice: once in wording my friend would accept for his side, once in wording I'd accept for mine. I am tired of him summarizing my view as "you think the theory is about nothing at all."

Then the cruxes. I have a list and I want your judgment on it. One is surely what the no-go theorems actually rule out — he waves the 2012 Pusey-Barrett-Rudolph result at me, and I keep replying that it leans on preparation independence, which is more or less the thing we're fighting about, so I don't think it settles anything; that exchange needs to be written down carefully, since we'd both agree it bears on the crux even while predicting different things about how damaging it is. A second is whether the state of a single, individual system means anything without reference to ensembles or to an agent holding expectations — he says yes, I say that's smuggling the conclusion in. A third, I think, is the Wigner's-friend kind of setup, where two agents in good standing assign different states to the same system, and the question is whether that embarrasses his view or mine more. For each crux, write down what we would jointly count as evidence or argument bearing on it, and record both predictions in advance, so whichever of us loses can't wriggle out of it later. And if some stretch of our dispute is really just verbal — I have a growing suspicion we mean incompatible things by the word "real" — say exactly which part and why.

One demand about format: I want this as continuous prose, like a long letter or memo. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know the house style for these joint statements is a numbered claims section and a disagreements table, but I've read too many of those and they read like slides; if the structure can't survive being carried by plain sentences, that's a sign the structure wasn't doing real work in the first place. Keep it serious, but write it like a person who has had this argument for two years and wants it nailed down at last.
