"The doctrine of double effect has been invoked to explain why we judge the driver who diverts a runaway tram from a track with five workers onto a track with one differently from the judge who frames and executes an innocent person in order to appease a rioting mob and prevent a larger massacre. Explain how the doctrine is supposed to draw this distinction, discuss at least one trolley-style case that has been thought to cause trouble for the doctrine, and assess whether the doctrine ultimately offers the best explanation of our judgements about these cases. You should situate the cases in the literature that gave rise to them."

The student's answer reads:

"The doctrine of double effect originates with Aquinas, who argued in his discussion of self-defence that an act may have two effects, one intended and one merely foreseen, and that the foreseen harm of killing one's attacker is permissible so long as the saving of one's own life is what is aimed at, provided the response is proportionate. In its modern form, the doctrine says that it can be permissible to bring about a foreseen but unintended harm as a side effect of pursuing a good, while it is impermissible to bring about that same harm as a means to the good.

Philippa Foot introduced the tram case in 1967 in order to defend this doctrine. She observed that the driver who steers from the track with five workers onto the track with one does not intend the death of the single worker; his aim is simply to save the five, and the one death is a side effect of the redirection. By contrast, the judge who frames an innocent person does intend the innocent man's death, since executing him is the means by which the riot is prevented. The doctrine of double effect therefore explains exactly why the driver may proceed and the judge may not.

Judith Jarvis Thomson later built on this. She argued that the doctrine also explains the contrast between the bystander at the switch, who may divert the trolley onto the track with one, and the bystander on the footbridge, who may not topple the large man into the trolley's path. In the switch case the death of the one is a side effect of redirecting the trolley, whereas in the footbridge case the man's body is used as the means of stopping the trolley, so his death is intended. Since the two cases are otherwise consequential parallels, the only difference that can account for our divergent judgements is the intended/foreseen distinction, which shows that it is morally significant.

Thomson also devised the loop case as a further test. Here the single worker is on a loop of track such that diverting the trolley towards him will cause it to strike and kill him, and his body will then prevent the trolley from continuing around the loop and reaching the five. Since the bystander foresees the one's death with certainty, and since that death is needed to save the five, the doctrine implies that diverting is impermissible here just as on the footbridge. But most people's intuitions remain permissive in the loop case, which suggests that the doctrine of double effect is extensionally inadequate: it classifies cases together that we judge differently and, more importantly, it classifies cases apart that we judge alike. Thomson concluded from this that the doctrine should be abandoned not only in the trolley literature but across applied ethics, for instance in the medical cases, such as the administration of painkillers that foreseeably hasten death, where the doctrine was traditionally supposed to do useful work.

A further mark against the doctrine is that the intended/foreseen distinction is simply the killing/letting-die distinction under another description. The driver kills the one but lets the five die; the judge kills the one rather than letting the five hostages die; the footbridge bystander kills the man rather than letting the five die. Since every case the doctrine handles can be redescribed in these terms, and since the doctrine offers no account of why intention should matter independently of the act/ommission contrast, the doctrine is explanatorily redundant.

In conclusion, the doctrine of double effect correctly distinguishes the driver from the judge and the switch from the footbridge, but the loop case and the redundancy of the intention distinction show that our judgements are better explained by Thomson's own preferred theory, which grounds the asymmetries in the distinction between negative duties not to harm and positive duties to aid: the driver and the switch bystander infringe no negative duty, whereas the judge and the footbridge bystander violate a stringent negative duty not to kill. This account explains all the cases without recourse to the intended/foreseen distinction."
