I train LLMs for a living, and I've been keeping a running list of my gut verdicts about when process-based supervision (grading the steps / reasoning) beats outcome-based supervision (grading the final product), and I want to know whether there's an actual principle under all of them or whether I'm just vibes-ing. Here are the verdicts:

- Math word problems: grading each step is clearly better. When I train on final answers only, the model learns to leap to answers with subtly broken reasoning, and step-grading catches the rot early. Verdict: process.
- A legal research assistant: outcome check only — did the cited cases actually support the proposition. Checking the proccess would mean me redoing the research, at which point why have the model. Verdict: outcome, even though it's superficially the same "grade the reasoning?" question.
- Go: if you'd graded AlphaGo's moves against "what strong human players consider sound," move 37 never happens. Outcome-only (win/loss) was right, and it's not close. Verdict: outcome, even though a human process-grader would have flagged exactly the moves that made it work.
- A coding model whose spaghetti passes all the tests: I still want to penalize the ugly code, even though the outcome check passed. And this feels in tension with Go — both are "unorthodox route to a verified win."
- A writing assistant: grading its "reasoning about the essay" feels wrong even when I could do it — good essays get written in weird ways, and the model's stated plan is often post-hoc anyway. Verdict: outcome (human preference on the essay itself).
- A model that reaches a benign, correct answer through a chain of paranoid or rationalizing justifications: I'd penalize the chain even with a fine outcome. Verdict: process.

The case I keep going back and forth on: interpretability people keep finding that a written-out chain of thought isn't always doing the causal work — sometimes the answer gets computed elsewhere and the "reasoning" is a plausible-sounding press release. If that's right, isn't process grading just grading the press release and hoping it becomes load-bearing? Do you penalize a correct answer with confabulated reasoning, and if the goal is to make the stated process be the actual process, is that still "supervision" or is it something else?

And the thing that bugs me most: math says process, Go says outcome, coding says process-but-only-sort-of. My tentative principle is just "grade whatever you can verify cheaply and honestly" — i.e., process grading is what outcome grading looks like when the outcome decomposes into checkable pieces. But that principle seems to tell me to leave the spaghetti code alone (the tests passed!), and I really don't want to leave the spaghetti code alone.

So: is there one principle that captures all or most of these, and if so, what does it predict for the confabulated-reasoning case and for the Go-vs-coding tension? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? I'd take a small set of candidate principles over one bloated one. Please answer in American English.
