I've been appointed to a residents' reference group that advises our city council, and I need your help drafting the written recommendation we'll eventually submit. Let me explain the situation properly, because the difficulty is not political but, I think, genuinely conceptual.

In 1968, our council demolished an area everyone called the Winnow Street quarter -- about four hundred homes, several chapels, a swimming baths, dozens of small shops -- to build a stretch of ring road and a lorry depot. Families were rehoused on estates at opposite ends of the city; the community simply scattered. It was legal, it had majority support at the time, and the paperwork described the district as "unfit for habitation", which the residents always disputed. An association of descendants has now asked the council for a formal apology, and my group must recommend a course of action within the next two months: a full formal apology in the council chamber, an apology tied to some material gesture (a plaque, a memorial, funding for a local history project), a mere statement of historical regret, or declining altogether.

I've thought about this a great deal, and I keep snagging on things I can't resolve.

First, no one who voted for the clearance is alive, and none of the councillors who would deliver the apology had any hand in it. Yet it doesn't feel right to say the apology would be insincere, because it's the council apologising, not those individuals. My own clumsy way of putting it: the council is like a very old ship whose crew has been replaced a hundred times over -- is the ship the thing that wronged these people, and can the ship apologise without pretending the current crew is guilty?

Second, most of the displaced residents are dead, and the descendants asking for the apology mostly never lived in the quarter. So who is the apology actually addressed to? The dead? People to whom the wrong was not directly done? The city itself? I notice that the answer might change what the apology can honestly say.

Third, the descendants' association is itself split. Some say an apology without money or material measures is a photo opportunity and an insult. Others say attaching compensation turns it into a transaction -- once you've paid, you're square, and that's precisely not what an apology should convey. I can see force in both.

Fourth, one councillor argues it's flatly unjust to apologise, because an apology implies the people of 1968 did wrong, when they acted within the law and by their own lights in good conscience. Is that right, or can an institution apologise for something without condemning the individuals who carried it out?

And fifth, I have a nagging worry of my own: that whatever we recommend, the actual event will be a ceremony, some speeches, a photograph, and then nothing -- that apology is the cheapest possible form of taking responsibility, which may be exactly why institutions are so fond of it.

I have no philosophical training -- I'm a retired bus inspector and I've simply been chewing on this for months -- so I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on collective responsibility, institutional action over time, and political apology. Please think carefully and take the questions above seriously rather than smoothing them over; a glib "apology aids healing" answer would be worse than useless to me. Before writing the final document, do some genuine brainstorming about the different ways of framing what an institutional apology is, and let the best framing drive the recommendation rather than the politics. Then draft the memo itself: it should be suitable for circulation to the reference group, recommend one concrete course of action, explain its reasoning in plain language a mixed group of residents and officials could follow, and -- this matters to me -- give an honest account of the strongest argument on the losing side. Please write everything in British English.
