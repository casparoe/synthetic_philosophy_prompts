I spent thirty-odd years as a hydrologist, mostly modelling how contaminants move through groundwater, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy and also, somehow, serving on a citizen's advisory panel that reviews the benefit-cost analyses our state agencies put together for flood-control and water projects. These analyses are remarkable documents: avoided flood damages in dollars, a "value of a statistical life" attached to tiny changes in drowning risk, wetland acres converted to dollars via something called ecosystem services, all discounted back to present value. My scientific instinct is to ask what quantity is actually being measured here, and I honestly can't say. In my old work a number in a table was a measurement of something -- hydraulic conductivity, whatever -- with an instrument and an error bar. What is the instrument here?

A retired economist on the panel likes to needle everyone by claiming the spreadsheet only works because there are real markets out there feeding it: wage data for the life values, property transactions for the flood damages, and so on. He says in a fully planned economy the sheet would be empty, or just numbers copying other numbers. I went and read up and gathered this is basically the old socialist calculation debate -- Mises and Hayek arguing planners could never compute genuine prices because the relevant knowledge is dispersed and tacit, Lange answering with a trial-and-error procedure where planners set prices, watch shortages and gluts, and adjust. So here's what I keep chewing on: what does benefit-cost analysis actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that wetlands, human lives, and flooded houses all sit on one commensurable scale, measurable cardinally and comparable across persons? Does it presuppose a surrounding market economy as its measuring device, in something like the way my flow meters presupposed a calibration standard? And does Marvin -- the economist -- actually have it right, or if Lange were right and a planner could solve for shadow prices by trial and error, would the practice carry on unchanged, just with a different theory underneath it?

The part that really bugs me is that the same agencies treat certain things as strictly off the table: a burial ground, an endangered species' last habitat -- no dollar figure gets assigned, it enters the analysis as a hard constraint instead. So one half of the practice seems to presuppose that everything can be priced and the other half presupposes that some things can't be. Is that a flat contradiction, or is there an implicit theory that can hold both together? Help me out here. Reconstruct what the practice is actually committed to, tell me which rival views of value it quietly rules out, and be honest about where it's underdetermined -- maybe several different theories would license the same spreadsheet. And if you swapped the presupposed view for, say, a doctrine of strict incommensurability, what concretely would have to change in how these agencies go about their work?
