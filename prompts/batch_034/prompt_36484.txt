I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy and governance of AI, and for my final paper I want to defend a research agenda rather than produce another survey. The topic is debate as a mechanism for scalable oversight: the proposal that two AI systems, or two instances of the same system, should argue for opposing answers in front of a human judge (or a weaker AI acting as judge), on the theory that adversarial pressure makes claims easier to evaluate than to produce, so that verification stays cheap even as generation becomes expensive. My sense is that a lot of adjacent work quietly assumes this scheme works, and that which open question gets settled first matters enormously for whether that assumption is earned. What I'd like from you is a prioritized list of open questions, with the ordering defended, not just asserted.

Here are the candidates I've noticed so far, though I'd welcome additions: (1) whether debate presupposes that arguments can be decomposed into steps a bounded judge can check one at a time, given the worry that some falsehoods are obfuscated -- hiding inside long chains whose individual steps look individually acceptable and only mislead jointly; (2) whether the boundedness of the judge is a bug to be engineered around or the entire point of the scheme, since if the judge had to be unboundedly capable to follow the debate, the protocol would have achieved nothing; (3) whether cross-examination between the debaters can substitute for the judge's own capacity to check claims, or whether it merely relocates the verification burden; (4) whether debate requires that at least one debater be able to find the right answer, or whether the adversarial format can extract truth that neither side possesses; (5) how the scheme degrades when debaters can model and exploit the judge's biases, since a persuasive liar with a good model of the judge may beat an honest debater with a poor one; and (6) whether the recurring analogy to adversarial legal systems is load-bearing or misleading, given that legal procedure tolerates a substantial rate of wrong verdicts as the price of other goods.

To fix what the prioritization is for: imagine advising a small theory group — roughly five philosophers and logicians with two years of funding, no empirical ML resources — whose goal is to determine whether debate-style oversight is trustworthy enough to rely on for evaluating proposals that exceed unaided human competence. Rank the questions by that goal, and be explicit about the criteria you're using: how much downstream work depends on the answer, tractability with a priori methods rather than experiments, how neglected each question is relative to its importance, and whether settling it would actually change a deployment decision or merely satisfy curiosity. Where the criteria conflict, say so and explain which you let win and why. Please also flag which of my six candidates is most overrated — which one, if answered tomorrow, would change the least — and whether there's an important question I've missed entirely, particularly ones that arise when the two debaters are copies of the same underlying system rather than independently trained models. Finally, tell me what kind of development over the next few years would move each item up or down your list, so the agenda isn't static. I care more about the reasoning connecting the ranking to the criteria than about the ranking itself, since that connection is what my paper needs to defend.
