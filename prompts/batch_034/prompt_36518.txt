I sit on the allocation committee of our regional health authority, and our meeting this Thursday has to settle a case our written policy was never designed for. During a supply failure we came to hold a single remaining course of a scarce therapy that can be given whole or divided. If the full course goes to Patient A, A lives; if it is divided among Patients B through F, each of the five lives. Either way, everyone not treated dies. Clinically the six are indistinguishable: same prognosis with treatment, same life expectancy, and none has priority under our ordinary criteria — this is not a triage-by-benefit case. The only distinguishing fact is the number of people on each side, and the drug must be administered within seventy-two hours of the case being opened, so we need both a decision and a procedure for reaching it, not just a vote.

I mention the philosophy because I spent most of last weekend arguing about exactly this with my closest friend, and I expect the committee to split along the same fault line. He invoked a paper from the 1970s that asks whether the numbers should count, and took its author's side: he would flip a coin. Each of the six, he says, has an equal claim to the drug, and nothing about the mere existence of additional claimants weakens the lone patient's claim — what exactly would we say to that patient, that his life counts for less because strangers he has never met also happen to be sick? He denies that five deaths are worse than one in any sense that gives us a reason to act: comparisons of better and worse, he insists, must be better or worse for someone, and there is no person for whom five deaths are worse than one. When I proposed a lottery weighted five-to-one as a compromise, he called it a fraud — a way of maximizing the number saved while wearing the costume of fairness. I pushed back that five deaths really are worse than one and that a body empowered to prevent the worse outcome has reason to; he answered that this treats people as vessels of utility rather than as separate persons with separate claims, and that an aggregation nobody experiences is doing all the work in my argument. We got nowhere, and two of our members have already said informally that anything short of an equal-chance lottery treats the lone patient as if his claim were thinner than theirs.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this case, including the procedure and its timing; it should also say whether we ought to adopt a standing policy for future cases in which numbers are the only difference between patient groups, or decide each such case ad hoc, and what turns on that choice — a colleague worries, for instance, that a published policy of equal chances would change how future patients and their families see the fairness of our process in ways a one-off decision would not. Finally, and this is the part I most need, the opinion must engage my friend's strongest point on its own terms rather than waving it away: if it simply asserts that more lives saved is better, full stop, it will lose half the committee on the first page. I would particularly like the reasoning to address two questions he left me with. First, whether a standing institution deciding on behalf of unseen strangers is in a genuinely different position from the lone passer-by rescuing whoever he can, or whether that difference is window dressing. Second, whether there is anything to be said for the weighted lottery from the equal-claims point of view, or whether he is right that proportional odds are just maximization in disguise — and if he is right, whether that should push us toward the coin flip or toward simply saving the five. Assume the committee are clinicians and administrators rather than philosophers: explain any position in plain terms rather than by name-dropping, and keep the whole document short enough to be read closely before Thursday.
