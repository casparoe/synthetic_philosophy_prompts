I head the employee-conduct review team at a mid-sized professional services firm — about a dozen investigators, none of them philosophers, each handling several complaints a week. Our code of conduct forbids "harassing conduct," which our policy glosses as "repeated or severe behavior that a reasonable person would find intimidating, hostile, or offensive, whether or not any single instance would independently violate this code." The words are vague in the way the sorites literature studies: no one can say how many rude remarks make conduct "repeated," how far short of criminal cruelty an episode can fall and still be "severe," or how "hostile" differs from merely "unpleasant." Our investigators currently decide these cases by intuition, and the intuition drifts: whoever handled the last borderline case sets a precedent nobody wrote down.

Please write a decision procedure my team could actually run on a complaint, grounded in what the philosophy of vagueness actually establishes — the tolerance intuition ("one more remark can't make the difference") and its collapse under conjunction, the difference between a borderline case having no determinate truth versus our not knowing it, the way context and comparison class shift as cases accumulate, and whatever epistemicism, supervaluationism, and degree or contextualist theories have to offer that survives contact with practice. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it terminates in a finding, where in a dismissal, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a named human being because the residual question is one of values rather than of vagueness. It must run in under an hour per case, use no jargon beyond what you define in a short preamble, and produce written rationales that will hold up if a decision is appealed.

Three hazards it must explicitly address. First, the sorites trap: an investigator who agrees that no single instance of a behavior crosses the line, and that adding one instance to a non-violation cannot create a violation, ends up dismissing everything; the procedure needs a principled way to break that chain of reasoning without pretending each instance is evaluated in isolation. Second, the ratchet: once we sustain a complaint at, say, five instances over a quarter, every subsequent case gets argued by analogy to that one, so the boundary creeps; the procedure should tell investigators when comparison to prior outcomes is legitimate and when it smuggles in a boundary nobody actually chose. Third, the disagreement problem: two investigators applying the same procedure will sometimes reach opposite verdicts on the same facts, and I need to know whether the procedure should treat that as a defect to be engineered away, a normal feature of penumbral cases to be managed (for instance, by routing them somewhere), or evidence of a higher-order fact about the case.

Then run the procedure on two complaints. In the first, an employee reports that a colleague has, over four months, made roughly a dozen individually trivial remarks about her accent, none of which any witness calls severe, but which she says have made her dread team meetings. In the second, an employee reports exactly two incidents, but one of them — a joke made in front of a client — was, by broad agreement, genuinely vicious. Include at least one worked case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where vagueness theory runs out and the decision belongs entirely to judgment or to institutional values. Finally, tell me which theory of vagueness the procedure, as you have built it, is implicitly committed to, and what a team of ours that instead built the procedure on a rival theory would do differently at which step.
