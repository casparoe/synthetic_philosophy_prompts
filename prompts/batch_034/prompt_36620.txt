My mum spent the better part of two years going back to her GP saying she was constantly exhausted and losing weight without trying, and kept getting told it was probably stress, or her age, or to try more exercise. Last month it turned out to be something a routine blood test would have flagged ages ago, and by then it was alot further along than it needed to be. I was recounting all this at a friend's birthday dinner and it set off an argument between two friends of mine that I was in no position to referee.

Dora said this was basically the textbook case of epistemic injustice — the Fricker kind, where a speaker's credibility gets knocked down because of identity prejudice, in this case the stock figure of the middle-aged woman with vague complaints. Marcus said hang on, the doctor probably treats every patient who walks in with tiredness the same, whether it's a 25-year-old man or my mum, so there's no prejudice against her, just a rushed doctor applying base rates — vague symptoms are usually nothing, and if you take every single one seriously the system grinds to a halt. Dora said that's definately the problem: the "usually nothing" prior isn't neutral, it's built up from decades of not taking women's symptoms seriously, so leaning on it just launders the prejudice. Marcus said by that reasoning no evidence could ever be clean, since everything is downstream of some biased past — which makes the charge unfalsifiable and therefore useless. Dora answered that he was demanding a smoking gun inside the doctor's head, when the injustice lives in the pattern, not the intention. Marcus said then "injustice" is a dressed-up word for misdiagnosis, which happens to everyone and isn't aimed at anyone as a knower specifically. Dora's last word was that the worst part wasn't even the late diagnosis — it's that her mum started doubting her own reports and stopped going back, which is a harm to her as a knower even if the condition had turned out to be nothing.
