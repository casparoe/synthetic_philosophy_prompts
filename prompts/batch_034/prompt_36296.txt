So I'm a novelist, and my new book has gotten me into trouble with my writing group. The setup: a woman in her late fifties donates a kidney to a stranger she met on a donor-matching forum, and it quietly wrecks her relationship with her adult daughter, who thinks she chose a stranger over her own family. I intended her as an ordinary good person who did an extraordinary thing. But my writing group has spent three sessions fighting about her. The ex-lawyer in the group says donating is admirable but skipping it is fine, that's the whole point of calling something superogatory. The guy who reads a lot of Peter Singer says the "beyond the call of duty" talk is exactly how comfortable people let themselves off the hook, and that if the donation does more good than harm there's no coherent sense in which it's optional. And the one who did a philosophy BA keeps mumbling something about latitude and imperfect duties, that the act is good but nothing has a "call" attached to it in the first place. None of them can even agree what they're disagreeing about, and I suspect at least two of them hold the same view under different names.

Here's what I want: a decision tree I can hand out at the next session, built so it sorts them (and me) by what we actually say about cases rather than which label we like the sound of. Start from the shared intuition that the kidney thing was good, then branch on the diagnostic questions where the positions genuinely come apart -- things like: is it blameworthy to decline? does the praiseworthiness depend on the act exceeding a duty, or could an act be praiseworthy while being merely permissible in every sense? is there a cost ceiling above which any duty cancels itself out (and is that a fact about morality or just about human psychology)? would we think differently if the surgery had a 5% death risk versus basically none? does it matter that she picked this stranger rather than responding to the most urgent need? The tree should be built from the logical space first and the named positions located in it after, not the other way around.

Leaves I need, because someone at the table already holds each one: the classic view that supererogation is a real fourth category (optional, good, praiseworthy to do, blameless to omit), the Singer-ish denial that collapses it into duty, the Kantian latitude view where the space of duty is indeterminate and "beyond the call" just means "inside the wide duty's discretion," threshold views where cost limits obligation, and the splitter who thinks my kidney case and, say, a soldier jumping on a grenade are two different phenomena that the single label glues together wrongly. If you think there's a view that says the whole fourth category is a moral illusion -- duties, permissions, and excuses, nothing else -- include that too. And flag explicitly any two leaves the case questions can't actually pull apart, because I'd rather know my tree is blunt in places than discover it mid-argument.

Test every branch on three cases from the book so it stays concrete: the kidney, the grenade (a backstory event), and a moment where my character walks past a panhandler because she's late for a dialysis visit -- I want to know whether anyone's position forces them to say walking past was wrong. Keep the node questions in plain english, answerable by someone who's never read a page of ethics. And wherever a branch turns on something genuinely contested rather than settled -- like whether praiseworthiness requires supererogation or just requires effort or sacrifice -- say so at the node, don't bury it.
