I am a high school student on my school's debate team, and I keep getting drawn into an argument that never resolves. One side says the world has become dramatically less violent over the sweep of human history: fewer deaths per person from war, murder, and cruelty than in any earlier era, with the trend accelerating over the past few decades. The other side says this is statistical sleight of hand: in absolute terms the twentieth century contained the deadliest events in history, the victims of state violence and exploitation may not show up in the murder statistics at all, and if the population keeps growing while per-person risk holds steady, the total amount of suffering still climbs. A third reaction I have encountered, mostly from my philosophy-club friends, is that the whole question is confused: "violence" is not a single measurable property like mass or temperature, so there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is "less violent" until you first stipulate a definition, and different stipulations give different answers.

My own working hypothesis, which I would like you to take seriously but not simply accept, is that the disagreement is at bottom about the choice of denominator and the choice of what counts as a data point. If you measure deaths per person per year, the decline story looks strong; if you measure total deaths, or deaths per year without adjusting for population, it looks weak or false; and if you widen the category to include harms that are not anyone's deliberate act, the two sides are not even using the same concept, so their disagreement is verbal rather than factual. But I also suspect something deeper might be going on: per-capita normalization itself may smuggle in a moral verdict, because saying "things got better" because each individual's risk fell, even as total deaths rose, seems to treat a death as mattering less the more people exist — which is a substantive moral claim, not a statistical one, and neither side ever argues for it explicitly.

Here is what I want from you, and please write your response in American English. First, diagnose the disagreement: is it a factual dispute about data, a conceptual or verbal dispute about what "violence" and "less" should mean, a normative dispute about how we ought to weight individual risk against aggregate suffering, or some combination with one of these doing most of the work? Use what each side actually says — the per-capita trend line, the absolute-numbers objection, the expanded-definition objection — to support the diagnosis rather than giving me a survey of the debate, which I already know. Second, address the fact-of-the-matter question directly: is "the world is becoming less violent" more like "the universe is expanding," where a fixed measurement procedure can make it simply true or false, or more like "food is becoming tastier," where any answer is relative to a scale nobody is obliged to share — and is the relativism disqualifying, or is a chosen scale good enough for the claim to have genuine truth conditions? Third, evaluate my denominator hypothesis honestly: tell me where it captures the disagreement and where it misses something, especially the possibility that the two sides are answering different practical questions (for instance, "how worried should I be?" versus "how much preventable suffering is there?") under a single grammatical form. Finally, tell me what would count as progress here — a precise definition everyone could adopt, a decomposition of the claim into separately answerable empirical and moral parts, some particular kind of data, or a change in the question itself — or whether the impasse is a sign that the question, as normally asked, is ill-posed and should simply be retired. I am asking for a defended diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right about the trend.
