I've been asked to write a short position piece on what heritability does and does not mean — the audience is a mixed group of social scientists who deploy heritability numbers in their arguments without always being careful, and I'd like to be careful. Before drafting it, I want an audit of whether my own working commitments hang together. Here is the set:

1. Heritability is a population-level variance ratio: the fraction of phenotypic variation in a trait, in a specific population living within a specific range of environments, that is attributable to genetic variation in that population. It is therefore undefined without specifying the population and environment, and it can say nothing by itself about the sources of mean differences between populations.

2. The classical twin and adoption literature gets the order of magnitude right: in contemporary Western populations, measured IQ and many behavioral traits have heritabilities of roughly 0.4 to 0.8. I read this as showing that genetic differences between people are a substantial cause of the phenotypic differences between them — that is, I take heritability estimates as evidence about genetic causation at the level of individuals, not merely as a partitioning of variance.

3. The Flynn effect — the large secular gains in measured IQ observed within single generations-spanning-decades in the same populations — is real, is environmental, and does not embarrass commitment (2) at all.

4. Passive gene–environment correlation is pervasive and substantial: parents transmit both genes and home environments, and the two are confounded in ways that classical twin designs have limited power to separate.

5. As a society equalizes the environments its members face, the heritability of a given trait should rise, because environmental variance shrinks while genetic variance does not. I take this to mean that a high heritability estimate from a society with broadly equal environments is itself evidence that environmental unfairness is no longer the dominant driver of inequality in that trait there.

6. Heritability says nothing about malleability. A trait can be highly heritable and still be dramatically altered by the right intervention — phenylketonuria is the stock example, and I believe the stock example is correct.
