Suppose you are a professional philosopher specializing in anthropic reasoning and the logic of self-locating evidence. I am a high-school student in an after-school philosophy club, and I need your help evaluating an argument.

Last week our club advisor defended the view that your own existence is evidence that the universe is populous. His analogy: being you rather than someone else is like holding the winning ticket in a lottery with billions of tickets sold. If you find yourself holding the winning ticket, he said, that should raise your credence that many tickets were sold rather than few; likewise, finding yourself existing at all should raise your credence that the world contains many observers rather than few. He then leaned on this to argue that we should prefer scientific theories on which the universe contains vastly more life, even when those theories fit the observed data no better than their rivals — the move sometimes called the presumptuous philosopher problem.

Please think carefully, and do some brief brainstorming before your main answer. Then audit the analogy for me. Set out what corresponds to what (a table of correspondences is welcome), and identify where the mapping breaks down — for instance: was the "winning" condition fixed in advance, as a lottery prize is? Is everyone who exists thereby a "winner," and if so does that make the observation vacuous? Who or what plays the role of the lottery operator, and does anything play the role of a drawing at all? Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he wanted — that big-world theories get a boost from our existence alone — and whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Finally, if there is a better analogy for reasoning from my existence to claims about the number of observers, tell me what it is and why it is better. Do not give me a reading list; reason it through.
