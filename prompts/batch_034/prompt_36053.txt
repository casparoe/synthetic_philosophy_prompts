I'm a statistician; my day job is combining forecasts — ensemble weather models, expert elicitations, meta-analyses of clinical trials. Lately I've been reading some epistemology about what should happen when two people who are equally informed and equally competent on some question nonetheless disagree, and I have the persistent feeling that the debate is a rediscovery of problems we fight about in model combination, just with the labels filed off. Rather than another explanation pitched at a general reader, I'd like a translation exercise, and I want it done honestly — no smuggling the statistician's assumptions in and calling them translation.

Restate the disagreement between conciliationism and steadfastness in my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. To seed the translation: treat the other party's opinion as data; treat overlap in the underlying evidence as correlated errors and the familiar double-counting problem; treat the "split the difference" instinct as equal-weight pooling; treat staying with your own credence as the question of whether the incumbent model gets a privileged role in the ensemble or whether partial pooling should drag it toward the group mean; and treat the acknowledgment that you might be the less reliable party as uncertainty about a latent reliability parameter. Assume I know hierarchical Bayes, Bayesian model averaging, and the forecast-combination literature well; assume nothing about epistemology beyond what a curious outsider would have absorbed.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. I've spotted two candidate residues on my own. First, whether someone is my peer is itself a judgment I make, and the other party may contest it — in my vocabulary the weights are fixed by the analyst outside the model, but in the philosophical case the weight assignment seems to be part of what is being fought over. Second, the philosophers appear to argue about what I am *permitted* to do with my credence, and my vocabulary only scores outcomes; it has no deontic register at all. Tell me whether these are genuine residues or whether I've overlooked a statistical counterpart, and more generally whether the residue points to something my framework cannot say or to something in the original debate that is less substantive than it looks. Don't flatter my framework: if part of the debate only looks like model combination because statistics quietly assumes, say, that there is a fact of the matter about relative reliability, say so explicitly. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
