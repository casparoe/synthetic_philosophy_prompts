I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about whether retributive justifications of punishment can survive the charge of Barbarism — the worry that it is never intrinsically good that a person suffer, whatever she has done. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters on exactly this question.

The first character should be a sympathetic reconstruction of David Lewis's position on penal substitution, which he sketched in an unpublished manuscript from the 1990s, later printed in his posthumous Papers in Ethics and Social Philosophy. As I understand the view, Lewis treats the would-be offender's choice as a many-person prisoner's dilemma: each person faces a mild temptation to commit a crime, and if everyone yields, everyone is worse off — a Hobbesian trap at the level of the population. Deterrence solves this only if defection is made worse than cooperation, and there is no stable way to threaten the sanction without someone actually undergoing it. Rather than have the guilty person be the one who supplies the needed instance of suffering, Lewis proposes, half-seriously but with a straight face, that an innocent volunteer — or an appropriately compensated one — could bear the punishment instead. Crucially, he does not present this as a way of making punishment cheaper or kinder; his stated point is that this substitution would retain whatever value retributivists think punishment has. His deeper aim is dialectical: the retributivist who recoils at the idea of punishing a substitute thereby reveals, he thinks, that what she really values is not the intrinsic fittingness of suffering-for-guilt but something else — perhaps the expressive condemnation of the guilty, or the fair distribution of a burden — and Lewis thinks those things are available without retribution in the strict sense, so the strict retributivist position is exposed as unstable. That, at least, is how the argument was presented to me; reconstruct the position along these lines, and let the character argue it as well as it can be argued.

The second character should be a retributivist who takes the substitutional proposal seriously as a test case rather than as an absurdity. She should press on points like the following, though not only these: whether the substitutional scenario really preserves the retributivist's object — whether suffering that is not deserved can be intrinsically good, or whether the scenario silently converts the value of punishment into an instrumental one (the deterrent structure doing all the normative work, so the volunteer's suffering is valuable only as a means to deterrence, which concedes the anti-retributivist's point rather than answering it); whether the claim that we could not credibly threaten punishment without occasionally inflicting it smuggles in empirical assumptions about deterrence that, if they failed, would dissolve the rationale for punishment altogether; and whether the retributivist's recoil at the substitute reveals incoherence in her view or instead reveals that her view was never the one Lewis was targeting — a view on which punishment's value is conditional on desert, so undeserved suffering is disvalued and deserved suffering is not specially prized, a combination that can consistently reject substitution while denying that punishment is instrumentally good.

I'd like the two of them to actually engage: the Lewis character should respond to the objections, perhaps by biting bullets about fair burden distribution, and the retributivist should in turn refine her position under pressure rather than repeat her opening statement. Please have the dialogue reach some resolution short of agreement — for instance, a diagnosis of precisely which premise the two of them cannot share, stated in one or two lines by whichever character is in a position to see it. Avoid strawmanning on either side, and keep the exchange at a level a graduate seminar in philosophy of law could follow, with the game-theoretic structure stated plainly where it matters.
