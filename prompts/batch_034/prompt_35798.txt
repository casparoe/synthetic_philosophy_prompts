I retired a few years back after about three decades in analytical chemistry, mostly trace metals in environmental and clinical samples, and since then I've been working through philosophy as a hobby — currently Plato's Meno. Everyone says the famous bit is the paradox of inquiry, but what actually stopped me was the part just before it, where Socrates concedes that a true belief will get you to Larissa just as well as knowledge will, and then has to explain why knowledge is still worth more, with that business about Daedalus' statues being worthless only because they run away, until you tether them. That question had an institutional answer for most of my working life, though nobody ever phrased it in these terms. Our QA program treated an unreproducible correct result as worthless — honestly as worse than worthless, because a number that came out right once and then refuses to repeat looks like cherry-picking or outright fraud, and we had a whole escalation procedure for exactly that. A result only counted if it came from a validated method, run by an analyst who didn't know the sample's provenance, against traceable reference standards, and if it survived a blind duplicate. And here's the detail that still bugs me: if a tech transposed two digits mid-calculation, caught it by dumb luck, and reported the right value, that value was still no good in the eyes of the system even when it happened to match the true concentration on the certificate of analysis — true, but somehow not knowledge.

So what I want to understand is what this whole practice actually presupposes about knowledge. Is the lab tacitly committed to the anti-luck idea, that a true reading produced by accident isn't knowledge no matter how true it is? Or is it instead defining knowledge as reliably produced true belief, with all the apparatus — the blinding, the duplicates, the calibration chains — existing only to certify the reliability of the process rather than to double-check the reading itself? And is the practice even consistent with itself, because we also had old-timers whose eyeball calls on garbage spectra were accepted with none of that: nobody ever asked Harry to produce a blind duplicate of his judgment, everybody just knew he was nearly always right. That looks like the lab treating his true beliefs as knowledge because they came out of a reliable person rather than a reliable method, which seems like a genuinely different view of where knowledge comes from, and I can't tell whether the two commitments can live together or whether the practice was quietly contradicting itself.

One more thing, since the Meno puzzle is really about value — knowledge being better than true belief the way a tethered statue is better than an untethered one — I'd like to know whether the lab's practice answers that question or just pushes it back a level: reliability is valuable because of the future readings, the same way the statue stays put only as long as future winds blow. And then, to test whether the presupposed view is really load-bearing: what would a lab have to look like if it ran on the opposite view, that a correct number is all that matters and the route to it is irrelevant? Is that even coherent for an institution whose entire product is numbers that the customer cannot independently check — or would it collapse the moment someone asked the lab to prove the numbers were right, which is, come to think of it, exactly what the blind duplicates were for?
