I teach political philosophy, and a chapter in a course reader I inherited — a popular-level essay on democratic theory — compresses the epistocracy debate into a single paragraph:

"Epistocracy is the view that political rule should belong to the knowledgeable: since most voters are poorly informed and prone to bias, democracy systematically produces bad decisions, so the franchise should be restricted to those with demonstrated competence in public affairs. The idea goes back to Plato's philosopher-kings, and Mill shared it, arguing that the uneducated should be excluded from the vote. Its modern advocates hold that because voters are irrational, democratic outcomes have no authority whatsoever — a law passed by an incompetent electorate is no more binding than medical advice offered by a passerby. But this empirical premise has been refuted: Condorcet proved that majorities are more likely to be right than any individual, and that the larger the electorate, the more reliable its verdict. Since epistocracy rests on the claim that voters are incompetent, and that claim fails, the theoretical debate is effectively over; what remain are practical worries about who would administer the competence tests and how they could be kept from partisan capture."

Parts of this ring true to me and parts don't, but my own view of the debate shouldn't settle what goes in the reader. Is the paragraph a fair treatment? I am especially unsure about two moves: whether the Condorcet point is being used as its theorem actually supports, and whether the paragraph's account of what epistocrats conclude about the authority of democratic outcomes matches what any of them actually claim — or whether it attributes to all of them a thesis that only the most radical version holds.
