I've been reading a popular neuroscience book about the split-brain patients that Sperry and Gazzaniga studied in the 1960s and 70s, and it closes the chapter with a summary of the philosophical upshot that I want to check before I take it on board. Here is the gist of it: 'Severing the corpus callosum cuts the only channel of communication between the hemispheres, so the patient becomes two isolated systems in one skull. Since speech is controlled almost entirely by the left hemisphere, the right hemisphere is left with no means of reporting anything, and without the capacity for language there is no consciousness there at all — a highly capable but empty automaton that can solve puzzles with the left hand. The left hemisphere, meanwhile, has an interpreter module that confabulates reasons for whatever the body does: in one experiment a patient whose right hemisphere saw a snow scene and whose left saw a chicken claw explained his selection of a shovel by saying he needed one to clean out the chicken shed. Gazzaniga drew from this the conclusion that the interpreter confabulates in healthy brains too, and Nagel's classic paper on brain bisection agreed that the operation leaves literally two streams of experience in one body. It follows that the unity of consciousness each of us takes ourselves to have is an illusion: there is no single self, just a talkative hemisphere narrating a story the rest of the brain never endorsed.'

Some of this sounds right to me, but two things nag at me. First, I have read elsewhere that split-brain patients behave quite normally in everyday life — same personality, no obvious signs of being two people — and I can't square that with the 'two isolated systems' framing, though I don't know whether that's a real objection or just a limitation of the lab tasks. Second, the zombie characterisation of the right hemisphere strikes me as too quick, but I can't articulate why without assuming what's at issue. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a neuropsychologist or philosopher of mind would accept, what they would call a simplification, and what they would flatly reject — including where it is unfair to Sperry's and Nagel's actual views — and then say whether the book's concluding leap, that the unity of the self is generally an illusion, follows from the parts that survive.
