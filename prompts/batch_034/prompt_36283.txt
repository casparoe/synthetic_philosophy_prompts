I've been in a small reading group this year that's half philosophy grad students and half economics/psych people, and we've spent way too long on the connection between akrasia and hyperbolic discounting, and at this point I think we're just talking past each other, but I can't quite put my finger on how. The philosophers keep insisting that weakness of will is a conceptual problem about whether you can freely, knowingly do what you judge worse all things considered -- the classic Socratic/Davidson thing, where the puzzle is how an action can be intentional and yet against your own better judgment. The econ folks keep replying that there's no puzzle at all: with hyperbolic discounting, preference reversals fall straight out of the math. When rewards are discounted more steeply the closer they are in time, an agent can coherently prefer "study tomorrow" over "party tonight" today, and then when tonight arrives, prefer partying -- no irrationality, no failure of knowledge, just a curve. Different selves, effectively, with the earlier one losing to the later one.

So my question is really about the structure of this stalled disagreement, not a verdict on who's right. Is the economists' model actually an explanation of akrasia, or is it a model of a different phenomenon (temporal preference instability) that the philosophers never denied could exist? In other words, is the dispute substantive, or are the two sides answering different questions under one word -- one asking "what is going on psychologically/structurally when someone acts against their better judgment" and the other asking "can a consistent discount function produce choices that look like that"? And if it is substantive, where exactly does the disagreement live: in what counts as an explanation (mathematical prediction vs. rationalizing agency), in whether "better judgment" is even coherent for someone with non-exponential discounting, or in some bedrock intuition about free action that neither side can argue for? My own half-formed hypothesis is that the crux is over whether explaining the behavior pattern is the same thing as explaining the phenomenon, but I genuinely don't know, and our group's philosphy people get visibly annoyed when the econ people say the puzzle has been "solved," which makes me suspect something normative is lurking.

I'd like you to actually diagnose this rather than survey both literatures, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress -- a distinction, a way of carving up the phenomena, maybe some condition under which the hyperbolic story and the philosophical story make different predictions about a case. Also, since I'm aware you could just be pattern-matching here: please say how confident you are in the main claims of your diagnosis, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd rather get an honest "this part is shaky" than a polished-sounding answer.
