I'm a retired materials scientist — thirty-odd years mostly spent on semiconductor metrology, nothing glamorous — and since retiring I've fallen down a rabbit hole of epistemic game theory, of all things. Our little reading group asked everyone to write up "where they stand," and I want a sanity check before I embarrass myself, because I have a feeling not all of this hangs together, but I genuinely can't tell where the problem is. Here's my package:

(1) In the centipede game, if both players are rational and that's common knowledge between them, backward induction says you take the small sure payoff at the very first node, and I accept that — iterated dominance is just logic, it's not optional.

(2) At the same time, I beleive strict common knowledge of rationality — the whole infinite tower of "I know that you know that I know that you know..." — is a mathematical fiction that has never held between two actual human beings, not once, ever. It's an idealization like a frictionless plane, and I'm fine with idealizations, but let's be clear that's what it is.

(3) Even so, I think what the theory says for ideal agents is normatively binding on real ones: in a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma between rational players, defecting from round one through the last round is the right answer, and when human subjects cooperate in the lab that's a bug in the humans, not in the theory.

(4) As a scientist my instinct is continuity: if we all know that we all know that we all know, up to some decent depth — a hundred levels, whatever — that should be operationally as good as the full infinite hierarchy. Taking limits of finite things is how the infinite is used in physics constantly, and it works.

(5) But then there's this email game I read about, and it's been bugging me. Two players want to coordinate on something risky, and they confirm back and forth by automated emails — each message can get lost with some small probability, so after many successful messages each knows the other knows, and the other knows that, up to a very high finite level. Yet the punchline as I understood it is that they play as if they'd never emailed at all: the coordination essentially never happens, no matter how reliable the channel. I find this fascinating and I have no idea whether I'm supposed to give up (4) because of it or whether they're compatible somehow.

(6) Separately, I'm sympathetic to the whole epistemic program — solution concepts should be grounded in what players know about each other. My impression is that Nash equilibrium doesn't actually need the full common-knowledge-of-rationality machinery, that something weaker like mutual knowledge of rationality plus shared priors does the job, but don't hold me to the exact conditions, I'd have to go back to the book.

So: which of these are really in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest fix? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one tell me what would have to give and what it costs me. To be clear, I'm not attached to any particular item and I don't know which one should go — that's the point of asking. Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence (which commitment, if any, has to go), then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
