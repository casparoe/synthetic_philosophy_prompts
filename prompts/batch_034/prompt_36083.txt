I've been haunting rationalist blogs since the original Sequences threads, and for the last couple of years I've run a small side blog that does philosophy-adjacent satire — the sort of thing that only lands if you actually did the reading. Past entries include a mock risk assessment for Newcomb's problem and a startup pitch deck for a Pascal's-mugging-as-a-service venture. My readers are the type who will forgive a joke about the norm of assertion but not a botched statement of it, so accuracy is non-negotiable even in the jokes.

The next entry is about deflationary theories of truth, and I'd like the form to carry a lot of the argument. Specifically, I want a parody of enterprise software release notes plus a short migration guide (the genre is instantly recognisable: "What's Changing", "Breaking Changes", "Deprecations", "Frequently Asked Questions") announcing the launch of Truth 2.0, a leaner, deflationary truth that ships without most legacy features. The changelog should quietly track the real moves in the Ramsey-through-Quine-through-Horwich tradition: the correspondence module deprecated as bloat that never actually ran during assertion; the observation that appending "and that's true" to a claim adds no payload; the retention of the truth predicate as a lightweight device for blind endorsement and for expressing generalisations you couldn't list one by one, since "everything the audit says is true" beats a five-thousand-page conjunction. Sprinkle in closed support tickets standing in for the standard objections — complaints about truth-makers, about truth as the goal of inquiry, about the norm of belief, each answered with the cheerful ticket-closing bureaucratese of a vendor who has decided you were never really using that feature.

Practical constraints: around 600–700 words, numbered sections, deadpan throughout — the vendor should be earnestly proud of the weight reduction, never winking at the reader. Include at least one customer testimonial from a correspondence theorist who is furious and one from a pragmatist who is smug about having predicted the pivot, and make sure the humour comes from real features of the view rather than invented silliness, so that someone who teaches this literature would judge the satire fair rather than cheap. Where you can, let the release notes gesture at the genuine open question: what, if anything, is left for truth to do once everything explainable has been explained away — and whether a predicate this thin can really be the same predicate we were all so worried about all along.
