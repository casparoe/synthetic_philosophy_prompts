I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm preparing a piece on what happens when moral theories refuse to do arithmetic. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications, aimed at readers with some philosophy background but no specialist training.

The case must be one in which Scanlon's contractualism, because it refuses to aggregate complaints, delivers a verdict that even most deontologists would find too demanding: a hospital administrator must choose between funding a very expensive treatment for a single patient facing a severe, long-term burden and funding a far cheaper measure that would spare a thousand patients a moderate but genuinely significant burden—say, chronic pain or a permanent reduction in mobility. The scenario should be realistic, no more than two paragraphs, and it must not stipulate the verdicts: the contractualist and the utilitarian conclusions must be derived from the theories, specifically from the reasonable-rejection test and from straightforward summing of welfare, respectively.

After presenting the scenario, please (a) walk through the reasonable-rejection reasoning that leads contractualism to favor the single patient, showing why the thousand moderate complaints cannot simply be added together; (b) identify precisely which feature of the case does the work, and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed; and (c) give the strongest contractualist reply—drawing on how Scanlon handles stringency of complaints or statistical risks—to soften the counterintuitive result, and say honestly whether it succeeds. Please do not merely restate the transmitter room case; the arithmetic of many moderate burdens versus one severe burden is essential. Please answer in American English.
