I teach a cross-listed seminar on the concept of societal collapse — 16 students, one two-hour session, and they arrive having read about the Late Bronze Age palatial systems and the Maya lowlands only in broad outline. Design an in-class game in which teams run a Late Bronze Age palace economy — managing grain stores, long-distance trade in tin and copper, scribes, and the loyalty of garrison troops — and then suffer a compound shock of my choosing. The crucial design constraint is this: the same run must be honestly scoreable as either a collapse or a successful transformation, depending on which indicators (elite continuity, literacy, population, long-distance trade, monumental building) and which timescale one attends to, and the students must not realise this until the debrief. Provide roles, rules, a scoring sheet with no single aggregate, materials cheap enough for a departmental budget, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the interpretive dispute between those who read the archaeological record as failure and those who read it as reorganisation — and that then asks students which verdict they would defend for their own run, and why. They should come away doubting that 'did this society collapse?' is settled by the evidence alone, rather than by choices about what counts as the society and what counts as collapse. Include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the real scholarly dispute the game cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. Please write your answer in British English.
