I'm TA-ing a philosophy of science course and grading finals, and I want a second opnion on one answer before I commit to a number. The course covered the standard three-way split: frequency interpretations, subjective/Bayesian interpretations, and propensity interpretations. Here's the exam question I wrote:

"A fair-looking coin is about to be flipped once. For each of the three interpretations of probability covered in the course (frequency, subjective/Bayesian, propensity), explain what — if anything — that interpretation lets you say about the probability that this particular flip lands heads. Then explain what is meant by the problem of the single case, say which of the three it is a problem for, and discuss how the reference class problem bears on each interpretation here."

And here's the student's answer, typed out as written (typos theirs):

"On the frequency interpretation, probability just is long-run relative frequency. You need a collective — an infinite sequence of events that is random in the sense that the frequencies are invariant under place selection — and the probability of heads is the limit of the head-frequency in that collective. Since one flip is not a sequence, the single flip has no probability of its own on this view; the best you can do is assign it the frequency of some larger class of flips it resembels. This is where the reference class problem comes in: for the subjectivist, your degree of belief about this flip will depend on which reference class you take it to belong to — all coin flips, all flips of this coin, all flips by this persn on a Tuesday — and nothing in the theory tells you which one, so subjectivism inherits the reference class problem in its most acute form. The problem of the single case is that frequency theory can't ground probability claims about individual events, and it's a problem for the frequency interpretation only, since the other two both assign probabilities to single events. The subjectivist says the probability of this flip is just a coherent degree of belief: what odds you'd accept on a bet on heads. The Dutch book argument, as Carnap showed, proves that if your betting odds are coherent, then as you observe more and more flips your degrees of belief must converge to the true relative frequency, so subjective probabilities eventually line up with the frequencies after all. On the propensity interpretation, probability is a physical disposition of the experimental set-up: the coin plus the flipping conditions. The propensity is just the frequency the set-up would produce if repeated indefinitely, relocated from the sequence to the set-up, so this flip has probability 1/2 because the set-up has a propensity of .5 for heads. This was Popper's solution, and he was partly motivated by quantum mechanics, where experiments genuinely seem to produce single-case probabilities that can't be reduced to anything about our knowledge of the system. So overall the propensity view handles the single case best: it gives an objective probability for this flip, while the frequency view gives none and the subjective view only gives what a particular person happens to believe."
