Background: we're juniors in the same AP Physics class, and our teacher offhandedly said that the law of gravitation doesn't "make" anything happen, it just describes how masses move. That comment is what started everything.

Marcus went first. He said laws of nature have to be more like the engine of a video game than the recap of a match — the recap tells you what happened but nothing in the recap keeps the game running, whereas the engine actually enforces the rules in real time. He said if laws were just descriptions, then nothing makes the pattern continue: the universe would be "coincidentally" obeying F = ma at every single instant, which he compared to flipping heads a trillion times in a row and just shrugging about it. So laws govern, they have actual push, and that's the difference between a law and a lucky streak. Priya answered that this whole picture confuses the map with the territory or something — actually no, her phrase was that laws are like the best summary of a giant novel. The world, she said, is just one enormous collection of events spread out across space and time, all the little local happenings, and a law is the most efficient summary of the patterns in that collection — the one that says the most with the least, like the best compression of a huge file. Asking what "makes" the pattern hold is like asking what makes it hold that all the events in the summary happened — the pattern isn't enforced by anything, it's just there, and the summary describes it.

Marcus had two comebacks. First, he said if laws are summaries then they're about our bookkeeping, not about the world — scientists revise laws all the time, so on her view the "real" laws would be whatever the final perfect summary ends up saying, which seems to make physics the study of an ideal library book rather than of nature. Second, counterfactuals: when we say the earth's orbit would change if the sun suddenly doubled in mass, that's a claim about a situation that never happened, and no summary of what did happen can tell you about what didn't. Priya said the best summary doesn't depend on humans — it exists whether or not anyone ever writes it down, the way the best compression of a file exists even if nobody ever runs the compression program. And for counterfactuals she sketched an idea she'd read about: you compare our world to nearby worlds where things went differently, altering as little as possible while keeping the overall pattern, and you see what the best summary of the nearby world looks like — that's what "would have happened" means. Marcus pounced on that as circular: you use the laws (the summaries) to figure out the counterfactuals, and then you'd use counterfactuals to say what the laws are, so neither one is standing on its own. His closing shot was the trillion heads thing again but sharper: on Priya's view, a universe where literally every coin ever flipped came up heads would just have "all coins come up heads" as one of its laws, and a lucky streak shouldn't get promoted to lawhood like that. Priya's final word was that maybe that's just fine — maybe in that world "all heads" really would be the best summary, and our feeling that it "deserves" some stronger title is just us wanting magic push behind the pattern.
