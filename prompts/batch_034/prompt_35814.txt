I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and in two weeks I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on well-being where the audience is maybe two-thirds empirically-minded philosophers of well-being and one-third psychologists who actually run studies (one of them works on positive illusions, which is both great and terrifying for me). My paper defends a modernized version of Émilie du Châtelet's view from her Discourse on Happiness: roughly, that a happy life centrally depends on what she calls illusions -- selectively maintained flattering or stabilizing ways of seeing oneself and one's prospects -- and that this is not a paradox or an embarrassment but a defensible piece of advice about how to live. My argument, in sketch: I read her as distinguishing illusions from passions (passions are involvements that can go badly and make you miserable, illusions are more like background framing you can cultivate), and then I argue her real insight is about what I call the shock-absorber role of certain tastes and self-conceptions -- they keep functioning through misfortune in a way that accurate global self-assessment doesn't. I then claim this is compatible with being rational because the cultivation operates on attention and occupation rather than on outright false beliefs, so you never have to believe p while knowing not-p. Help me prep for Q&A specifically for this room: what will the empirically-minded philosophers object to, in their own vocabulary (measurement worries, the fit with desire satisfactionism vs hedonism, authenticity-style objections about lives built on things the person would endorse only because of the distortion), what will the illusions psychologist hit me with (I'm bracing for the replication status of the Taylor-and-Brown-style results, depressive realism, whether "illusion" as I use it even maps onto anything they study), and what should I just concede upfront so I don't die the death of a thousand qualifications. Order the objections by how much damage they'd actually do in that room, give me a short reply to each with a note on where the reply is soft, and include the one I'm quietly hoping nobody asks -- for me it's probably something like whether du Châtelet's own biography (the gambling, the catastropic pregnancy at the end) shows the advice doesn't work even on its author, but tell me if you think there's a worse one.
