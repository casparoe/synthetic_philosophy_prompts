I would be grateful for some help with a passage of Aristotle. Since retiring, I have been attending a reading group on ancient philosophy, and our current text is the Metaphysics. My interest has also become personal in another way: at a recent dinner, a friend of mine who enjoys sweeping relativist pronouncements declared that no statement is simply true or false, only "true for" this or that person, and I want to be able to reply to him at our next dinner with something more than an irritated feeling. I had heard that Aristotle confronts views of this general shape in Book IV (Gamma), so I read ahead, and I find myself genuinely stuck on the closing argument of chapter 8. In W. D. Ross's translation, Aristotle writes:

"But in opposing all such theories we must demand, as was said in our discussion above, not that something should be or not be, but some significant statement; and so we must argue from a definition, having first grasped what 'falsehood' or 'truth' means. And if to assert what is true is nothing else than to deny what is false, everything cannot be false; for one part of the contradiction must be true. Further, if everything must be either asserted or denied, both parts cannot be false; for one and only one part of the contradiction is false. Indeed, the consequence follows which is notorious in the case of all such theories, that they destroy themselves; for he who says that everything is true makes the opposite theory true too, and therefore his own untrue (for the opposite theory says that his is not true); and he who says that everything is false makes himself a liar."

There are three things I would like help understanding.

First, what exactly is the structure of the self-refutation argument here? It looks as though Aristotle relies on the premises that every pair of contradictories has exactly one true member and one false one — but surely the person who says that everything is false rejects precisely that. Does Aristotle's argument therefore beg the question against his opponent, or is there something in his insistence on arguing "from a definition" that rescues the argument from circularity?

Second, when Aristotle says that the person who says that everything is false "makes himself a liar," is this an early version of the Liar paradox? It does not seem to have quite the same shape as the sentence "This very sentence is false": from "everything is false" it follows, apparently consistently, only that something else I say is true, not that this very assertion is both true and false. So is Aristotle's claim a genuine paradox or merely a prudential embarrassment, and how does it differ structurally from the classical Liar and from the Epimenides case?

Third, I am curious how a philosopher working with a contemporary theory of truth would evaluate Aristotle's reasoning. Would a Tarskian say that Aristotle's demand for a definition of truth and falsehood, applied to the opponent's own utterance, already gestures at the idea that a language cannot contain its own truth predicate? And would someone who allows truth-value gaps or overdetermination simply reject the assumption that "one and only one part of the contradiction is false," thereby escaping the revenge argument altogether?

I would be especially glad if you could tell me which parts of the argument are doing the real work, so that I can judge how much of my friend's dinner-table relativism the passage genuinely touches.
