I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. A while back I read about Kripke's version of Wittgenstein's rule-following puzzle -- the quus function, which returns the same values as addition whenever both arguments are below 57 and returns 5 otherwise -- and it's been nagging at me ever since, because it looks structurally identical to something I deal with professionally: a finite test suite can never pin down an implementation. Any set of unit tests, however extensive, is passed by infinitely many programs, some of which behave pathologically exactly where your tests don't look. A friend and I turned this into the argument below, and it now seems to prove far too much, but neither of us can locate the faulty step:

(1) To follow a rule R, rather than merely act in ways consistent with R, there must be some fact about you that determines that it is R you are following -- and not any of the rival rules that agree with R on every case you have actually encountered so far. If no fact distinguishes these, then "following R" is like claiming a codebase implements the spec when no test distinguishes the two: the claim has no content.

(2) The candidate facts about me are my computational history (the finitely many sums I have actually produced), my dispositions (what I am inclined to answer if asked), and my internal states (whatever a complete scan of my brain would reveal).

(3) Each of these is finite in the relevant sense: I have produced finitely many answers; my dispositions only cover the cases I could be asked in a finite lifetime, and they include dispositions to err, since I sometimes slip and say 125 where I meant 126; and my brain, however complicated, is one finite physical system among the possible ones -- no more than finitely many rows of a truth table.

(4) For any finite body of facts of these kinds, and for any case I have not yet decided, there are at least two rules that agree with everything in that body and diverge on that case: plus and quus, plus and its infinitely many cousins that agree with everything I have ever done and diverge wherever you please.

(5) Therefore no fact about me determines that I mean addition by "+" rather than quaddition.

(6) What holds of me holds of any rule-follower, and, if anything, more so of artifacts: no fact about my calculator determines that it means addition by "+", since its test suite, its circuit diagram, and its makers' intentions are all equally finite and equally consistent with deviant interpretations.

(7) But my calculator means addition by "+". When it displays 125 after I enter 68 and 57, it is correct, and if it displayed 5 it would be wrong -- wrong, not merely unusual. That normativity is precisely what (5) tells us cannot be grounded in any fact about the device.

(8) So the argument establishes that nothing -- human, calculator, or compiler -- ever means anything determinate by any symbol.

(9) And that conclusion is not merely false but self-undermining: the sentences above are symbols, and if nothing means anything determinate, the argument has not been stated.

Neither of us can accept (9)'s conclusion, so something upstream has to break. My friend wants to deny (7): he says it's cheap, because the calculator is "just" an artifact and whatever normativity it has is borrowed from us, so rejecting (7) costs nothing that (5) hasn't already cost. I'm suspicious of that move -- if the normativity is borrowed, the loan still has to be repaid somewhere, and (5) says we have nothing to repay it with -- but I also can't see how to hold onto (7) without smuggling in exactly the kind of fact (4) says doesn't exist. I'd rather give up (1), which smells most like a hidden assumption, except that giving it up seems to leave "following a rule" as a phrase with nothing behind it, and my entire profession is built on that phrase having content.

Please identify which premise or inference I should reject, explain why that premise looked so plausible to us -- especially given the test-suite analogy I keep leaning on -- and then price the main ways out: what each of the standard responses (whatever they are; my friend mutters about "dispositional" and "skeptical solution" as if I knew what those meant) has to pay to reject it, and whether my friend's suggestion of denying (7) about the calculator is really as cheap as he thinks, or whether it just moves the debt. If some step commits an equivocation -- say, between one sense of "determine" and another -- I'd like that named explicitly rather than left as a vibe.
