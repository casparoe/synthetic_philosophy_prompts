I'm a retired radio astronomer who's spent the last two years catching up on philosophy, and decision theory has become my latest rabbit hole. In my working life expectations were always friendly things — you average enough noisy voltages, the noise converges, that's basically the whole job — so the classic paradoxes here hit me harder than they probably should. I've got a decent handle on St. Petersburg: flip a coin until it lands heads, the payoff doubles each time it lands tails, and the expected value ends up being a sum whose terms don't shrink, so it just diverges to infinity. Fine. I've seen a million divergent series in my life, I know how to not be scared of them. But then I ran into the so-called Pasadena game (I believe this is due to Nover and Hájek, though I might be garbling the details), and I'm struggling to see whether it's a genuinely *different* and deeper problem or just St. Petersburg in a costume. As I understand it, the payoffs alternate in sign with magnitudes something like 2 to the n divided by n, so that when you compute the expectation, the terms end up being basically the alternating harmonic series: summed in the natural order it converges to ln 2 (of all things!), but the positive terms alone sum to +infinity and the negative terms alone sum to -infinity, so the "total expectation" isn't infinite — it's undefined.

So here's what I'd like from you: a proper comparison of the two paradoxes. Is my instinct right that the Pasadena game is basically the Riemann rearrangement theorem doing a cameo in decision theory, or does that analogy break down? (One thing that nags me: nobody actually re-orders the tosses of a single gamble — the order is fixed by the setup — so is the "rearrangement" business even a legitimate move here, or just a metaphor?) And which situation is worse for the expected-utility theorist: a gamble whose expectation is infinite, or a gamble with no expectation at all? My gut says "undefined" is the more corrosive one — with St. Petersburg you can at least mutter about utilities being bounded or real casinos having finite bankrolls, but with Pasadena it's not even clear what the question "what would you pay to play?" means, since different sensible-looking ways of adding up the same terms give different answers. But maybe I'm overreacting, and a divergent series was the deeper embarassment all along. Relatedly: do the standard patches — bounded utility functions, capping payoffs somewhere, whatever else is on offer — fix both paradoxes in one go, or are they genuinely separate repairs? Feel free to get technical with me, sums and convergence talk won't scare me off, I did this for a living.
