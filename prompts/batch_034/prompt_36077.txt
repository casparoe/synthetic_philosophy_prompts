I want to understand a specific pressure point in the causal exclusion argument for the epiphenomenalism of the mental, one that the standard textbook presentations skip past. Let me set the argument up the way I've usually seen it, so we're working from the same text: (i) physical causal closure — every physical event that has a sufficient cause has a sufficient physical cause; (ii) mental events cause physical events (for instance, my pain causes me to withdraw my hand); (iii) the mental is neither identical to nor reducible to the physical — the premise a nonreductive physicalist is committed to; (iv) no systematic overdetermination — it is not the case that physical effects generally have two independent sufficient causes; therefore the nonreductive physicalist's position is unstable, and the mental events either collapse into the physical or lose their causal work.

What I want explained is where, exactly, the argument gets its grip, and specifically whether the answer depends on an unargued conception of causation. In the paradigm overdetermination case — two assassins independently firing, either bullet alone sufficient for death — the two causes are independent in a strong sense: either could have occurred without the other, and neither depends for its existence or its causal contribution on the other. Everyone agrees the mental event and its neural realizer are not independent in that way, since the mental state exists only because the neural state does. But I find the usual move here unsatisfying: it seems to win by re-description ("they're related by realization, not by causation, so no overdetermination") rather than by argument, and it leaves unclear why the realizing state's sufficiency doesn't still crowd out the mental state's relevance.

So here is my question, in three connected parts.

(a) Is the exclusion argument, on reflection, an argument that presupposes a production-based or "transfer of causal power" conception of causation — the picture on which a cause is something that suffices to bring about its effect and so competes with other sufficient producers — such that it has no purchase, or considerably less purchase, on a difference-making conception of causation, on which to cause something is to make a difference to whether it occurs? On the difference-making picture, two states that differ over whether the effect occurs are not competing for a single causal role; but two states both of which suffice to bring the effect about are, on the production picture, in zero-sum competition. If this is right, the exclusion argument becomes an argument that one conception of causation, plus nonreductive physicalism, entails epiphenomenalism — and the natural escape is to deny that production is what causation is, at least in cases like this. Explain whether that is a fair characterization of what the argument needs, and what the nonreductivist is committed to if she takes the escape.

(b) A further wrinkle. It's natural to cash out difference-making counterfactually or interventionistically: the pain caused the withdrawal because, had the pain not occurred, the withdrawal would not have. But the realization relation seems to make the relevant counterfactual awkward in both directions. Intervening on the neural realizer will almost inevitably change the mental state with it, which threatens the claim that the mental variable does independent difference-making work; while an "intervention" that removes the pain while holding its realizer fixed looks metaphysically impossible or at least ill-defined, since the pain is entailed by what it realizes. Walk me through how the nonreductivist should handle this — whether it requires backtracking or interventionist machinery (or something else), and whether the resulting account of mental causation is substantive or merely a way of saying that mental variables are convenient summaries of the real causal story.

(c) Finally, tell me whether there is a residue of the exclusion problem that survives all of this — a version of the worry that is genuinely about causal explanation rather than causal production, to the effect that whenever a mental-state explanation of an action and a neural explanation of the same action are both available, one of them must be doing all the real explanatory work. If such a residue exists, say what it is; if it does not, say why the deflationary response (mental explanations are legitimate difference-making explanations and there is nothing further to argue about) is not just changing the subject away from Kim's original challenge.

I'd like the explanation to be self-contained enough that I don't need to consult the literature to follow it, and I'd rather have an honest assessment of where the deflationary response is strong and where it smells like accounting tricks than a partisan defense of either side.
