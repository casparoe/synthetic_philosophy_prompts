I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and after a departmental seminar on the ethics of war that ended in frustration rather than progress, I've persuaded two faculty members with directly opposed views on the moral equality of combatants to attempt an adversarial collaboration. One holds the Walzerian line: combatants on both sides of a war are equally liable to be attacked and equally entitled to fight, regardless of the justice of their cause. The other is a McMahan-style revisionist: liability to attack tracks individual responsibility for an unjust threat, so a combatant who fights without just cause acts wrongly and is not morally entitled to fight. Please draft the joint statement they should produce. I want (1) a numbered section of claims both could sign — about what soldiers actually do, about the distinction between jus ad bellum and jus in bello, and whatever else they genuinely share; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept as fair to it; and (3) the cruxes between them. For the cruxes, please push past the rehearsed slogans and focus on three specific ones: whether a combatant's epistemic access to the justice of the war can change what they are liable to, and whether a sincerely held false belief at home can excuse; whether the rules of war derive their authority from being fair terms imposed on people who are made to fight regardless of their cause, or from tracking the deeper facts about liability; and what follows for conscripts in a war of aggression that their own society believes is defensive. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Close with a short section in which they identify which parts of their original dispute strike both as having been partly verbal, and what would genuinely move either of them. Do not include citations or a literature review — positions identified by name and argument are sufficient.
