I'm recording an episode with a philosopher who's spent her career defending straight-up utilitarianism, and she specifically asked me to come at her with the Bernard Williams stuff -- she says she's tired of interviewers who think integrity objection just means "utilitarianism is demanding." I want maybe 8-10 questions total that actually bite.

Some constraints on what I want in there. One question has to be about the integrity point proper, the one where Williams says utilitarianism makes a person the mere conduit for the satisfaction of everyone else's preferences, so nothing is ever just his own to do. And another has to be on the drowning-wife case and the one thought too many point -- the guy who saves his wife and, if asked to justify it, reaches for the fact that it's his wife AND that one is generally permitted to save one's spouse rather than a stranger. For that one I want a follow-up keyed to the reply she's almost certainly going to give, which is the standard move that the criterion of rightness and the decision procedure come apart, so the good utilitarian deliberates with the wife-thought and only the theory evaluates from the outside.

Here's the part I actually care most about and where I want the weirder questions. I read somewhere (can't remember where, honestly) that there's a difference between the extra thought being too many in the *justification* versus it being too many in the *motivation* -- like, maybe the utilitarian is fine if the wife-thought merely causes the rescue but in trouble if it's what the rescue is done *for*, or maybe it's the reverse and the problem is only upstream in what licenses the action. I don't totally have my head around that distinction, so one or two questions should press on it directly: does her view say the husband's deliberation is defective, his action is wrong, or just that a utilitarian bystander would assess it differently than he should deliberate?

Also include one question on the version of the case where the wife and a stranger are both drowning, the rescue costs the same, and the outcomes on every utilitarian metric are literally identical -- can she say it's merely permissible, not required, to flip a coin? And if she instead wants to say the husband is required to save the wife, what in her theory does that work, since it's not the utilities? That feels like the spot where the "decision procedure vs criterion" move can't help, because it's about the verdict, not the deliberation.

Oh and she tends to deflect with the line that Williams only ever showed utilitarianism is alienating in the sense that all impartial theories are, so it's not a special problem for her. At least one question should make that deflection harder -- maybe something about whether the surrogate-parent vibes of the indirect strategy (raising kids to be non-utilitarians because that maximizes utility, etc.) show the theory wants people who can't accept it, and whether she thinks that's a cost or just a fact about moral architecture.

Don't worry about questions on the demandingness objection or the trolley stuff, we did those with a previous guest. Also please dont number them rigidly -- a flowing sequence with the follow-ups marked is fine, I basically read straight off the page when we record.
