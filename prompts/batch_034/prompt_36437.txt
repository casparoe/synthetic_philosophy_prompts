I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on anthropic reasoning, and I have hit a wall on the reference-class problem for the doomsday argument. In the standard presentations, the choice of reference class — the set of observers among whom I should reason as if I were a random sample — is treated as a theoretical embarrassment to be managed by compromise: Bostrom, for instance, allows a range of admissible classes and dilutes the doomsday conclusion accordingly. What none of this makes vivid is how much the choice can matter in an ordinary case in which an agent has to act. What I want from you is a concrete case that turns the problem from an academic one into a practical one.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. The setting is everyday and realistic — a restaurant, a wedding, an office, a train — with no urns, no lotteries, no serial-number analogues, and no far-future populations. It should read as a situation an ordinary person could actually be in on an ordinary afternoon.
2. The case must have the doomsday structure: the agent knows her ordinal position in some sequence (her rank) but not the total size of the sequence; the total is decision-relevant; and the scenario itself supplies a defensible prior over totals. The prior need not be sophisticated, but it must be stated, because the updates have to be derivable, at least roughly and in plain prose — I do not need Bayes factors to three decimal places.
3. The scenario must make available at least two reference classes that a reasonable person could choose between, the distinction being supplied by the scenario itself (walk-ins versus reservations, locals versus tourists, one room versus another), and the choice of class must flip what the agent does, not merely what she believes. I want the reference-class choice to be visible in behavior — she orders the slow dish on one resolution of the case and the quick one on the other.
4. The verdicts may not be stipulated. The scenario fixes the evidence and the prior, and the updates under each reference class must fall out of that. A case that announces its conclusions rather than generating them from the structure is of no use to me.
5. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do the following:

(a) Run the reasoning twice — once under the broad reference class and once under the narrow one — showing concretely how the same observed facts yield different estimates of the total and hence different decisions.
(b) Say what the self-indication assumption does here. I expect it to push against the doomsday-style update, but I want you to show exactly where it enters, and whether it changes the agent's decision or merely her credence.
(c) Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the case if that feature is deleted.
(d) Close with the strongest Carter–Leslie-style reply available: if a defender of the doomsday argument were shown your case, how would she argue that it does not impugn the original inference about humanity's future?

Please do not include citations or a bibliography. I am interested in the reasoning, not the references, and I will supply those myself.
