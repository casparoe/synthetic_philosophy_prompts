I recently joined a fifteen-member research group, and because I am its newest member I was given the task everyone else avoided: drafting the section of our code of conduct that governs when a member may go public with a result the group has agreed is sensitive. The section is due Friday, and it is now stalled, because the most senior member of the group has circulated an argument which, if sound, would delete the section altogether. I have checked each premise separately and find it plausible; the conclusion I cannot accept. Here is the situation the argument models: one of us goes public with the result; once public, it cannot be recalled; and no member of the group is better placed than another to judge whether going public is beneficial.

His argument runs as follows. (1) Each of the fifteen of us, judging independently on the same dossier of evidence, puts the probability that going public is beneficial at about one half. (2) Our judgments are independent, so the probability that at least one of us judges the release beneficial is 1 − (1/2)^15, which is essentially one: someone will favor it. (3) The only way any of us learns another's view before the fact is by being told, and the telling is done as a favor. (4) A procedure of mutual notification is therefore one under which, whenever any of us is about to go public, every one of us hears of it beforehand and knows the others' views. (5) When we have all been told, the evidence before each of us is the same as it was, since we all judged the same dossier; so each of us holds the same view after the procedure as before it. (6) The release therefore goes ahead whenever at least one of us favors it — which, by (2), is essentially always — and a procedure whose outcome is always the action that any single member wants is indistinguishable from having no procedure at all. (7) Our section on coordinated releases is pointless: it adds a step and changes nothing, so we should delete it and let each of us publish individually whenever he judges it right.

Another colleague replied that if the argument is sound it applies equally to any committee that votes after talking, which would make every deliberative procedure in existence a piece of theater; she thinks that is too much to conclude, but she could not point to the faulty step, and neither can I. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2), since they are the standard model of our situation and the numbers follow from them by arithmetic anyone can check. Please tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine despite being flawed, and what rejecting it costs the rest of the argument. Conclude with a machine-readable final line of the form \answer{N}, where N is the single integer labeling the premise you give up; if you judge the argument sound, write \answer{sound} instead. Nothing should follow that line.
