So, background: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I run a tiny podcast in the evenings where I interview philosophers about stuff I can't stop thinking about after work. Next week's guest is a political philosopher who has spent basically her whole career defending luck egalitarianism — the view that inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck (genes, family, illness) but are okay when they trace back to choices people made. I've done my homework on this one: Dworkin's brute luck vs option luck distinction, the hypothetical insurance scheme, the big critiques people have thrown at the view over the years. I don't want 40 minutes of her restating the position politely — I want to actually press on the places where it hurts.

Write the interview questions for me, in four sections, in this order. Section one: "Setting the terms" — at most 2 short questions just to pin the core view down for listeners who've never heard of it. Section two: "The abandonment problem" — 3 questions pressing on whether the view really does leave the imprudent person who gambled everything and lost to bleed out on the street, with each one followed by a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply (she'll probably say something about people being able to buy insurance ex ante, so make the follow-ups bite on what happens when someone couldn't afford insurance or misjudged the risk). Section three: "Where 'choice' gets fuzzy" — 3 questions about the cases where the choice/luck line blurs: the person whose expensive tastes arguably grew out of being born into deprivation, the religious person whose costly obligations are technically chosen but don't feel that way to them, and someone who makes a dumb decision partly because of how they were raised. At least one of these needs to make her go-to move — "that's an implementation problem, not a problem with the ideal" — harder to pull off. Section four: "The closer" — a single question asking what would actually make her give the view up, ideally some policy result or evidence that would count against it. Every question needs its follow-up attached, and nothing generic like what drew her to philosophy.

If it helps with flavor: in my day job we do blameless postmortems, which is kind of a cousin idea — separating outcome from process when you assign responsibility — and it'd be cool if one question could gesture at an analogy like that, but only if it doesn't feel forced. Roughly 1200-1500 words total across the four sections is plenty, and I'd say not more than about 250 of those should go on section one since its just setup. Also she's famously quick, so no softballs that give her a free two-minute stump speech — keep the questions pointed and short.
