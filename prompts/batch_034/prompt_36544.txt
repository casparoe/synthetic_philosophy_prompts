I write on science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am preparing a piece on how mass deaths come to be classified as natural rather than political. Reading about the Irish famine of the 1840s, wartime Bengal, and the Great Leap Forward, I keep encountering a rhetorical move that I cannot put a name to. Officials and, later, many historians describe the famine in the grammatical register of weather: the crop failed, the rains failed, food ran out, people died — even though in each case there was food in the country, food was often leaving it under guard, and identifiable officials made identifiable decisions about who could obtain it. The same governments that describe the famine as an accident describe other deaths of the same period as somebody's doing; the famine alone is treated as agentless. It is as if calling the event a natural disaster is not the conclusion of an argument but a substitute for one.

Is there an established concept for this — the conversion of a political event into a natural one through framing, so that agency quietly disappears into the weather? I am aware of Amartya Sen's entitlement approach, but that appears to concern the economics of who can buy food, not the language and moral bookkeeping. If an established term exists, I would like to know it; but I want the carving, not merely the label: how the concept differs from its nearest neighbours, such as structural violence, euphemism, or ordinary understatement, and where its boundary runs — for instance, whether it covers cases in which the natural framing is sincere rather than self-serving. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it precisely, and test it against those boundary cases. Please write your answer in American English.
