I help run a small foundation that funds philosophy-of-mind research, and we're considering a focused grant on epiphenomenalism — specifically the cluster of issues around what is often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then my sincere report "I am having a reddish experience right now" was produced entirely by physical processes that do not depend, causally, on the reddish experience itself. We can support roughly three early-career researchers for three years, and we'd like them working on a coherent agenda rather than scattering across the whole debate. So my question is which problems in this cluster actually deserve those researcher-years, and in what order.

Let me sketch the candidates as we currently see them, so you can prioritize against something concrete. First, there is the question of whether the paradox even arises as stated — some argue that on epiphenomenalism phenomenal judgments still reliably track experiences because both are fixed by a common physical base, and that this kind of non-causal reliability is enough; others reply that this concedes too much, since it seems to make our justification for believing in consciousness rest on a correlation we could have had no access to. Second, there is the self-undermining worry: the epiphenomenalist philosopher's very argument is a physical event, so does she ever reason *from* her evidence about consciousness, and does that undercut epiphenomenalism as a rationally held position or merely as a deliverance of reason? Third, there is the skeptical generalization: whether epiphenomenalism entails that we cannot know anything about the specific character of others' experiences, or even of our own past ones, and whether this is worse than ordinary skeptical scenarios. Fourth, whether "interaction-lite" positions that keep consciousness within the physical causal fabric avoid the paradox or merely relocate it. And fifth, a structural question we find tempting but suspect: whether the paradox is really a special case of a broader problem for any view that severs a domain from causal explanation, and hence whether work on it would generalize.

I'd like a defended agenda, not a survey. For each item you rank highly, tell me what makes it worth the years: how many of the other questions depend on it, whether it's tractable for philosophers working a priori rather than requiring empirical work, how neglected it is relative to the well-worn material, and what developments — in philosophy or in the science of consciousness — would move it up or down the list over the next few years. I'm especially interested in whether the self-undermining worry and the reliability response to the basic paradox are genuinely distinct problems or two presentations of one problem, since that affects how many separate researchers we actually need. Feel free to argue that one of our candidate framings is confused or a dead end.
