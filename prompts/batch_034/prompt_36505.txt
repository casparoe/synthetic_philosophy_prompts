heres the draft (its rough, sorry):

"Everyone knows you shouldn't trust a stranger demanding your wallet. But add weird math and suddenly it's not obvious. A man stops you and says: give me $50 or I will use my powers to simulate a trillion trillion conscious beings and torture each of them for a billion years. You think he's lying, obviously — say there's a one-in-a-quadrillion chance he's not. Even so, when you multiply a quadrillion-quadrillion victims by that tiny probability, the expected harm dwarfs anything $50 could do to you. Pure expected value theory says pay up.

My first thought is that this proves too much. If it works, it works against everyone: any person can walk up to anyone and recite the same threat with a bigger number. There's no step where the math stops. But my real argument is a different one.

Here it is: probabilities that small aren't measurable by us. When I say the mugger has a one-in-a-quadrillion chance of telling the truth, I'm not detecting anything — I made that number up. Below some floor, my estimate of the probability carries no information about the world. And multiplying a made-up number by a giant number isn't math anymore, it's fiction with multiplication signs in it. The problem isn't that tiny probability times huge stakes is hard to compute; it's that the tiny probability was never a probability, just a noise value that i dressed up in decimal notation.

The counter I expect is: but the chance isn't literally zero. My answer is symmetry. For any mugger who threatens torture if I don't pay, I can imagine a second stranger who threatens equal torture if I DO pay. Once both exist, the two infinites cancel and I'm back to the ordinary choice of whether a guy on the street deserves $50. So the rational move isn't to pay; it's to notice that the mugging only works if you listen to one stranger at a time."
