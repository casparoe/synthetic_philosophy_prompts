Before our reading group reads anything else on the split-brain cases, I want us to agree on what an adequate theory of the unity of consciousness has to deliver, because right now the two-minds camp and the one-mind camp are just talking past each other. Write the desiderata: the phenomena any theory must accommodate — the disconnection data from the commissurotomy experiments, but also the patient's steady first-person insistence that nothing about their mental life is divided, the hemispheres' cooperation in ordinary life, cross-cuing and the left hemisphere's fluent confabulations about actions the right hemisphere initiated, the partial-section cases in which only some commissural fibers are cut, and alien-hand behaviors — together with the distinctions the theory must respect, above all unity of consciousness versus unity of the subject of experience versus unity of the person, and lacking interhemispheric access versus there being two streams at all. Add the practical demand that the theory tell the treating neurologist something usable, for instance whether consent voiced by the speaking hemisphere counts as consent for the whole patient. Mark which of these demands are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp — I suspect the insistence that the answer must be a whole number of minds belongs in the second group. Then say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable; if so, that is the part I most want, since the list would itself be the finding. Close with a short paragraph on how the familiar candidates — two-stream views, single-consciousness-with-restricted-access views, and deflationary accounts that deny the surgery bears on unity at all — fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is for us to do in the group.
