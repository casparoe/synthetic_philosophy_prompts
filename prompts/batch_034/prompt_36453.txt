I serve on my department's hiring committee, and our scores for the final dossier of this year's search are due tomorrow at noon. The dossier is genuinely borderline: impressive letters, a publication record thinner than we usually require. It was the last of twelve files I read this week, and I read it close to midnight, after eleven hours at my desk. I came away convinced it deserves a high score, and I can articulate the case for that conviction clause by clause. The trouble is what a colleague on the committee — a social psychologist by training, who follows the judgment and decision-making literature the way I follow philosophy — told me over coffee this morning. She says that late-sequence, end-of-day review of a borderline file is precisely the condition under which evaluators drift toward the favorable default and stop scrutinizing; that the same case, read fresh, reliably receives a lower score; and that order and fatigue effects of this kind are among the most robust findings in her field. I believe her, I believe the dossier is strong, and the former belief now gives me reason to distrust the latter. She has an argument, which I reconstruct as best I can:

(1) It is permissible to believe p only if your evidence, all things considered, supports p; and everything you know about how your belief that p was formed counts as evidence for or against p.
(2) A belief you know to be the predictable output of a process biased in its favor is not one your evidence supports.
(3) My belief that this dossier is strong was the output of such a process: it is exactly what late-night, late-sequence review of a borderline file would produce, and I know that.
(4) So my evidence does not support my belief that the dossier is strong.
(5) Anyone who sees that her evidence does not support p must stop believing p; going on is epistemic akrasia — believing p while believing you should not believe p.
(6) The bias literature now tags nearly every process by which I form judgments — order effects, fatigue, confirmation, motivated reasoning — so by (2) nearly every judgment of mine is unsupported; a reflective person ought therefore to withhold on nearly everything and defer to base rates, and anyone who trusts her own judgment while knowing that literature is akratic by (5).

She draws the practical moral without blinking: tomorrow I must either score the dossier by the actuarial profile of end-of-day reviewers or record no view at all, and any expert who does otherwise, knowing what the literature says about judgments like hers, is irrational. This is where the argument seems to prove too much. Taken seriously, it makes expertise self-defeating — the entire point of training people to read dossiers is lost at the moment they learn what the literature says about people reading dossiers — and it makes akrasia obligatory for anyone with a passing knowledge of the bias literature. Yet when I go back through the steps, each still looks tolerable to me, and I can see why (2) in particular has pull: I would not trust a witness whom I knew always leaned toward the accused. I also notice, though I am not sure what to make of it, that my conviction that her findings apply to me is itself a late-night judgment. If I were forced to choose, I would keep (1) and give up (5), but I want to understand what I am buying before I sign anything.

Please reply with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, and nothing else — no preamble and nothing after the fifth item. In item 1, name the step you reject by its number and state in a clause what is wrong with it. In item 2, say why that step looked acceptable to us despite the flaw. In item 3, state what the argument would oblige me to do tomorrow morning if the step you reject were allowed to stand. In item 4, say what I should do tomorrow morning instead, and why that is not akrasia on your diagnosis. In item 5, name the one thing my colleague must give up to keep her conclusion, and what giving it up costs her.
