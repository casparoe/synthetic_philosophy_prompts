I'd like your credence on a question of origin essentialism, with the reasoning laid out rather than asserted. The proposition, stated precisely: for any person, that person could not have developed from a wholly different sperm and egg — a child conceived from entirely different gametes, even one with a similar temperament and appearance, would simply not be that person. My credence in this is roughly 0.7; my sister's is about 0.15. I want your number, but more importantly the decomposition. At minimum I'd expect terms for the following, each with a rough probability and a line of justification:

1. Whether persons are essentially biological organisms (animalism) rather than beings individuated by psychological continuity — since on most psychological-continuity views, facts about the very early organism seem to matter less to who one is.
2. Given whichever account of persons you favor, whether that thing's identity depends on its particular zygotic origin at all, as opposed to only on some continuous developmental history; note that this sub-question is complicated by the fact that different gametes means a different genome, so it is hard to hold the later organism fixed while varying the origin.
3. Whether our person-referring terms rigidly designate via origin in something like the way Kripke thought names of natural kinds do — that is, whether origin functions as the reference-fixer rather than merely a causal contributor.

To make this concrete rather than textbook, consider the case of mitochondrial replacement: a child conceived with nuclear DNA from two intended parents but mitochondrial DNA from a third donor, a technique used to avoid mitochondrial disease. Suppose two embryos are created with the same intended parents but different mitochondrial donors. The resulting children are genetically distinct, though the difference is confined to a small fraction of the genome. Under origin essentialism, is the mitochondrial donor's contribution part of the child's essential origin, or does 'origin' mean only the zygote that began the organism — so that which donor supplied the mitochondria is an inessential detail? Your answer to this, and how it fits into your decomposition, will tell me whether you treat 'origin' as the whole gametic input or as the starting event of a continuous life.

Please combine your sub-probabilities into your overall credence, tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument or thought experiment would move it most, and finally say where you think the disagreement between my 0.7 and my sister's 0.15 most likely lives: in the metaphysics of persons (term 1), in the dependence of identity on origin (term 2), or in the semantics of person-referring terms (term 3).
