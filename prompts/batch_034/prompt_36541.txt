Quick background before the ask: I sit on the safety review board of a mid-size aerospace components supplier. About three years ago we started running every design through an AI analysis tool before human sign-off — it's a black-box ML model trained on simulation and past failure data, not a physics solver you can trace line by line. Its been great for throughput (typo intended? no, casual register), it's caught at least two stress issues our own engineers missed, and we've had zero incidents since adoption. So this isn't a story about the tool failing.

The problem showed up at last month's internal audit. Two of our senior engineers admitted they could no longer walk through the reasoning on a wing component we shipped two years ago — they could explain what the tool concluded but not why the conclusion was safe. One said, off the record, that he'd basically stopped doing his own calculations entirely, and honestly the workflow never asks him to. Worse, our newest hires have never done a full structural analysis by hand at any point in their careers here; their entire job has been reviewing the tool's output. Nobody's sure they'd know a bad analysis if the tool produced one.

One of our board members, a former stress analyst, is pushing a proposal he calls the "re-derivation rule." As he's sketched it: every quarter, a random sample of the tool's conclusions on shipped designs gets pulled, and an engineer who didn't touch that design redoes the analysis from first principles using standard classical methods. If the hand analysis reproduces the tool's conclusion, fine. If it can't, that case gets flagged to the board and we treat it as a live question about the design, not just a difference of methods. His stated goal, and I quote his memo roughly, is keeping the humans capable of independent judgment rather than just capable of approving.

The board is split and I have to draft the decision memo for thursday's meeting. Half think the rule is exactly right: it keeps genuine understanding somewhere in the pipeline, and a passed re-derivation is real evidence, not paperwork. The other half think it's theater with a cost — the tool is right even when nobody can say why, they argue, and if a hand analysis disagrees with the tool, we won't actually know which one to trust, so the rule mostly generates expensive ambiguity. One of them also pointed out that the rule could backfire: engineers might start treating a passed re-derivation as proof the whole tool is sound, when it's only proof about a handful of cases.

Please draft the memo: recommend adopt as proposed, adopt with modifications (say which — e.g. what happens on a disagreement, who picks the sample, whether failures trigger design action or just monitoring), or reject, with reasoning both camps would recognize as fair to their side, and an honest treatment of the strongest point the losing side is going to make. Don't write it like a philosophy essay — the board wants something they can vote on.
