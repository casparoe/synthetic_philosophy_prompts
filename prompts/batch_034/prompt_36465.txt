Under most endangered-species legislation, the protected unit is the species, and the following pattern is typical. First, protection tracks current taxonomy: when a review committee splits a widespread coastal bird into three species, two of the new species are instantly rare and eligible for listing, and funding follows the change in classification without any change in the field; when the committee later lumps them back together, the protection lapses although not a single animal has died. Second, hybrids receive nothing: an animal descended partly from a protected wild canid and partly from a common one is excluded from the captive-breeding programme and, in some schemes, culled — not for any defect in the individual animal, but purely because of its mixed ancestry. Reconstruct the implicit view of what a species is that these two rules rest on. Does the pattern presuppose that species are natural kinds with essences, so that cross-breeding is a kind of dilution or pollution, or is it equally at home with the view that species are lineages or populations rather than classes, or with outright pluralism about species concepts? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, which rival accounts of the species problem would license exactly the same two rules. Finally, say what concretely would have to change in either rule if the unit of protection were populations or lineages rather than species.

Please answer in British English, as exactly five numbered items of a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion — for instance: one item stating the implicit view of species, one on the essentialism question, one on what is strictly required rather than merely convenient, one on the rival accounts that would license the same rules, and one on what would change under population- or lineage-based protection.
