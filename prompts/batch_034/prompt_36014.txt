I spent thirty-five years as an experimental hydrologist, where I learned that a measurement is a theory in disguise: put a gauge in a river and you have already assumed the river is the sort of thing that has a level. Since retiring I have been working through philosophy of science and ethics more systematically, and I keep circling back to the World Happiness Report and its annual announcement that Finland — again — is the happiest country on earth, with the usual steady procession of Nordic states behind it. The headline quantity is a single question asking respondents to place their lives on a ladder whose top rung is the best possible life for them and whose bottom rung is the worst. I am not asking whether Finland is a pleasant place to live; I am asking a sharper question, and I want your credence on it, with the reasoning laid open.

Specifically: what is your probability that a country's mean score on such a measure tracks a real, interpersonally comparable property that it is not misleading to call happiness — that is, that the rankings are more like thermometry than like asking people to score their favourite films? Please decompose it rather than giving me a bare number. Some of the terms I would want in the decomposition: whether happiness is unitary and cardinal enough, as a matter of conceptual truth, to admit a total ordering of persons and hence of countries; whether self-reports anchored to "the best possible life for you" are comparable across cultures, given that the anchoring rung itself may mean different things in, say, Helsinki and Lagos, so that a person content with a modest life may report a lower number than a striving, restless one; whether the residual national variance is genuinely social (institutions, trust, security) rather than a stylistic artifact of how cultures respond to surveys; and whatever other terms you think belong. Give each a rough probability with a line of justification, combine them, and tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence would most move it.

For calibration: my nephew, an economist, puts it at about 0.85 and treats the question as settled methodology; I put it nearer 0.5, partly because I have seen too many composite indices in my own field reify themselves into policy. I would also like your view on a second, conditional question — assuming the measure does track something real, what is your credence that a government aiming to top the rankings would thereby make its citizens' lives better rather than merely optimizing their reporting — and one line on where, in your decomposition, you suspect my nephew and I most likely disagree.
