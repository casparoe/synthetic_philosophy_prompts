I am a fifth-year graduate student in physics, and this spring I served as the graduate representative on our department's curriculum committee, which was charged with deciding whether to keep the required history-and-philosophy-of-science course in the doctoral program. The argument ran across three meetings, ended in a close vote, and changed no one's mind — and it struck me afterwards that we had re-enacted, in miniature, the long public quarrel between prominent physicists and academic philosophers: Hawking's declaration in a popular book, co-authored with another physicist, that philosophy is dead because philosophers have not kept up with science; the Nobel laureate whose well-known essay takes a title that pointedly parodies Wigner's famous one about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, arguing that philosophy's genuine insights into science have mostly arrived after the fact and come from scientists themselves; the quip, usually attributed to Feynman, comparing philosophy of science to ornithology for birds; and the philosophers' standard rejoinder that all of this targets a caricature nobody holds, since no serious philosopher of science claims the field should hand research pointers to a working physicist. In our committee the same script played out without anyone citing any of it: the experimentalists and the younger theorists said nothing in their actual research would have gone differently with the course, while the senior members pointed to the historical cases — the entanglement of logical positivism with the founders' debates over how to read quantum mechanics, or the way quantum information science eventually grew out of a foundational dispute about hidden variables that most working physicists of the 1970s would have dismissed as philosophy — and neither side moved an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is largely verbal, because the two sides are answering different questions under a single phrase: the skeptics are talking about usefulness to an individual researcher on the timescale of a career, while the defenders are talking about contributions to the conceptual content of science on the timescale of decades or a century, so that each side may be entirely right about the question it is actually answering while appearing obtuse to the other. But I am not confident this diagnosis survives the details, since at least two participants were plainly also making a sociological claim about turf and prestige, and one senior theorist seemed to hold a genuinely substantive view: that conceptual analysis, pursued as a method, cannot yield anything that careful physics would not have produced anyway. So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, a proxy fight for something else, or several distinct disagreements travelling under one heading? Please ground the answer in what the sides actually say, both in the public quarrel and in our committee's version of it. And then tell me, given that diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress — a distinction, a systematic historical accounting of the cases, a reframing of the question — or whether the impasse is one I should simply expect to persist, and why.
