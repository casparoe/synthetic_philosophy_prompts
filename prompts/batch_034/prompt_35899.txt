I am writing a novel set in Cambridge in the years just after 1900, and one of its central characters is a young fellow in moral philosophy — a member of a secretive discussion society modeled on the Apostles, and a disciple, at one remove, of the author of Principia Ethica. I want his table talk and his private agonies to be philosophically accurate to the period without the book turning into a lecture, which means I need to understand the doctrine well enough to depict people half-understanding and selectively using it, as people do in life.

A popular history of the Bloomsbury circle that I picked up for background gives the philosophy like this:

"G. E. Moore demonstrated that 'good' cannot be defined, and having thus secured the foundations of ethics, he asked which things are good in themselves. His answer was that there are exactly two: the appreciation of beauty, in art or in nature, and the love of one's friends. Since both are accomplishments of a cultivated sensibility rather than of ordinary people, the Moorean good life was necessarily the life of a small educated class — a creed his Bloomsbury followers adopted enthusiastically, keeping the aesthetics and quietly discarding the ethics."

I am inclined to distrust this paragraph, partly because it flatters the novelist in me by making the philosophy sound settled, slightly smug, and easy to dramatize, and I have learned to be suspicious of anything that does that. But I cannot locate the errors myself, and I would rather correct my character's views than caricature them. Please audit the summary sentence by sentence, telling me what a scholar of the period would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong.

A few of my own half-thoughts, which you should correct if they are off. First, my recollection is that Moore presented his catalogue of intrinsic goods with conspicuous hedging — as the most probable conjecture about the ideal rather than a demonstrated result — so that "demonstrated" and "secured the foundations" may be doing rhetorical work the book does not earn. Second, I suspect "exactly two" is stronger than anything Moore said; my sense is that other things had some intrinsic value for him and these two merely ranked highest, which is a different claim with different implications for my character's austerity. Third — and this governs a scene I have planned — I want to know precisely where, for Moore, the value sits: in the beautiful object, or in the consciousness that appreciates it? In the scene, my character, standing before a landscape at dusk after everyone else has gone in to dinner, insists that the beauty before him would be no less worth having if no mind ever saw it. His interlocutor replies that he is therefore not a Moorean. Who, on the best reading, is right — and is that verdict already implicit in the summary's phrase "the appreciation of beauty," which my scene might otherwise be read as contradicting? Fourth, the closing inference: granted whatever Moore held, does the elitism charge follow from the doctrine, or is it a fact about reception — about what his admirers did with him rather than what he wrote? If the latter, my paragraph conflates a philosopher with his followers, and I would like to know whether that is its deepest error or a minor liberty.

Please organize your reply under these headings, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated: "What the paragraph gets right" (one short paragraph); "Where it overstates" (two or three short paragraphs, taking the doubtful phrases in turn); "Appreciation and its object" (the longest section, around 250 words, since it governs my scene); "The elitism inference" (distinguishing what follows from the doctrine from what history did with it); and "Notes for the novel" (a few sentences on how my character should speak if I want him philosophically accurate, and whether his landscape-lyrical speech should stand as written). I would rather you argued the philosophy directly than name scholars or books at me; I can chase references myself once I know which claims need checking.
