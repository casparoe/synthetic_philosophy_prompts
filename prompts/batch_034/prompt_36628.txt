In my historiography course we spent a seminar on structural versus individualist explanation of historical outcomes, and it ended with the counterfactual question "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" A classmate closed the discussion by reciting Carlyle's line from On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History that universal history is "at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here," as though that settled the counterfactual. I suspect that one sentence is compressing several claims of very different strength, and I would like them separated carefully. Could you lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing — reading it, for instance, as (a) a near-trivial claim about what our histories and textbooks happen to foreground; (b) a substantive causal claim that certain individuals are, more or less by themselves, sufficient to redirect the course of events; and (c) the stronger quasi-metaphysical reading Carlyle himself seems drawn to in the surrounding text, where accomplished things in the world are described as the "practical realization and embodiment" of thoughts that dwelt in the Great Men — a reading on which the claim concerns the origin of ideas and values rather than the direction of events, and in which the masses merely execute what a few minds supply? For each reading, please say whether it is plausible, whether it is the kind of thing that could ever be tested or supported with evidence, and — most importantly — which of the readings would actually back my classmate's answer on the Hitler counterfactual, as opposed to merely sounding deep when recited. I would like a menu of readings I can bring to the next seminar.
