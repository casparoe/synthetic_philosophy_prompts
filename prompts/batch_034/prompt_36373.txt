I teach composition at a conservatory, and every spring I help judge a student prize. The format works against us: roughly sixty pieces, most of them explicitly framed as theme and variations, reharmonized jazz standards, or arrangements of shared source material, so the question of what makes one variation genuinely creative and another merely different or merely faithful is not academic — it decides who wins, and the losers appeal. I have been drawn to Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion that creativity is essentially variation within a structured space: on his picture, a concept, style, or piece of music is like a piece of audio equipment with a fixed panel of knobs, and a creative act is a meaningful twist of one or more of those knobs, as opposed either to leaving every knob at its factory setting or to replacing the signal with static. What I want from you is a grading procedure built on this idea that a judge can actually run on a score in about ten minutes, without philosophical training, and that will hold up in front of an appeals committee that wants reasons and not just numbers.

The procedure should, in order, do the following. It should get the judge to identify the theme of the piece — the deep structure being varied — and to write that identification down explicitly, since it is an act of interpretation that everything downstream depends on. It should then get the judge to list the knobs available in that space and sort them into surface knobs (tempo, instrumentation, register) and deep knobs (the harmonic scheme, the metric organization, the function each section serves in the whole). It should then classify the piece's deviations from the theme as faithful reproduction, legible variation, or noise, where a twist counts as legible if a competent listener could reconstruct the theme through it. At each step the procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer, and it should mark honestly where it stops with a rule and hands the decision back to the judge's ear and judgment — above all the question of what to conclude when the judge cannot find the theme underneath a wildly original piece: whether there is none, whether she is listening for the wrong theme, or whether the piece has changed the space itself rather than the settings on its panel.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a dazzling orchestration of the theme that twists only surface knobs while the deep structure is left untouched. Second, a piece that alters nearly every parameter at once, so that no single knob seems to have been deliberately chosen; the procedure has to say whether that result is randomness, several legible variations happening at once, or the invention of a new theme. Third, a piece that twists exactly one deep knob — say it treats every phrase-ending as a phrase-beginning — and does nothing else, so that its entire claim to creativity rests on the depth of that single twist. For each case, give the verdicts and the reasons a judge would put in the margin of the score. Please flag the case where the procedure gives a verdict I will not like — I suspect it undervalues the dazzling orchestration, which my colleagues and I find beautiful — and be candid about what the procedure cannot do, including whether the whole knob-twiddling frame breaks down precisely for the pieces that are most creative in the way that matters to me.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were writing me a long letter, with the three worked cases at the end.
