The exam question was:

"In philosophy of mind it's common to distinguish strengths of claim about first-person awareness of one's own mental states: (a) introspective reports of one's occurrent states are infallible — they cannot be wrong; (b) they are incorrigible — no one else is in a position to correct them; (c) they are reliable but fallible, like perception or memory. Explain the difference between infallibility and incorrigibility, and say why the second does not imply the first. Then assess what the confabulation literature — the Nisbett and Wilson tradition, and the choice blindness experiments where people end up defending choices they never made — shows about which of these claims, if any, we should abandon."

Here is the student's answer, typed out as written:

"The three claims differ in strength. An infallible report is one that cannot be false. An incorrigible report is one that no outside observer can correct; and since no one is better placed than I am about my own mind, my incorrigible reports are as good as guaranteed true. So really incorrigibility is just infallibility plus a remark about who has the standing to say so. Reliability is weaker: it means the reports are usually right, as with perception.

The empirical evidence decisively favors treating introspection as fallible. Nisbett and Wilson's classic study is a nice illustration: shoppers in a department store evaluated four identical pairs of stockings, overwhelmingly preferred the pair on the right, and insisted the item's position had nothing to do with their preference — they confabulated the reasons for a choice actually driven by position. Choice blindness shows the same thing more dramatically: participants pick which of two faces they find more attractive, a sleight of hand swaps the photo they are handed, and most of them not only fail to notice the swap but confidently explain why they chose the face they in fact rejected. So introspection does not merely misreport now and then — it actively invents.

This is what we should have expected on conceptual grounds as well. Ryle, in his attack on the Cartesian ghost in the machine, already observed that introspection is literally a form of inner perception, and that like all perception it is subject to illusion. And Wittgenstein's private language argument shows that a language intelligible only to its single speaker is impossible, from which it follows that introspective reports must answer to public criteria, and anything that answers to public criteria can turn out wrong. Taken together, the conceptual and empirical points tell against even the incorrigibility claim: the confabulation results refute it directly.

The upshot: introspection is reliable for a limited range of states, roughly those that are vivid and currently present, and unreliable elsewhere. Infallibility is gone, incorrigibility is gone, and what survives is a bounded reliability."
