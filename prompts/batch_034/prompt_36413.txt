I adjudicate first-party property claims for a mid-sized mutual insurer, and a startling share of my contested files turn on someone's not doing something: the caretaker who didn't shut off the water before the hard freeze, the property manager who never inspected the supply line, the alarm contractor that didn't run the annual test, and sometimes our own insured's failure to do the post-loss mitigation the policy demands. The policy language and state precedent hand me "proximate cause" with no guidance at all about absences, and I've noticed my reasoning drifts in a way that worries me: I call an omission a cause when the omitter had a duty, and dismiss it as mere background condition when they didn't — which means I'm deciding cases by pointing at norm violations rather than by anything causal, and I don't know if that's sound or a bias. Please turn what the philosophy of causation actually knows about omissions into a procedure I can run on a file in twenty to thirty minutes: the questions to ask in order (I suspect something like "cause of what, as opposed to what?" and "who or what counts as a candidate omitted preventer?" will have to appear early), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "not a cause" and where it can't settle the matter and has to hand off to coverage counsel or to my judgment, with the handoff points marked as places where values or law, not causation, are doing the work. It has to handle the cases that make omissions hard: two parties each of whose single omission alone would have prevented the entire loss; the omitted preventer that would itself have failed anyway; the omission that only matters because of a later storm no one had a duty to anticipate; the omission of a bystander with no duty whose act would trivially have saved the property. Then run the procedure on three claims — the caretaker and the burst pipe, a fire loss where both the landlord and the alarm contractor skipped inspections that either alone would have caught the hazard, and a mold claim where our insured's failure to mitigate after a small leak multiplied the loss — including at least one where the procedure yields an outcome I would rather it didn't. Finally, say plainly where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot do, especially whether it really answers my drift worry or only launders it.
