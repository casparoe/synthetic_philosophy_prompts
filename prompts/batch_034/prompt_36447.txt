I'm a final-year undergraduate writing my term paper for a seminar on well-being and rational choice, and my topic is whether idealized or extrapolated preferences can serve as a standard of what is good for a person — the family of views that runs from Brandt's cognitive psychotherapy to the coherent extrapolated volition proposals now discussed in AI alignment. My advisor's comment on my outline was that I was evaluating proposals before I had said what an adequate proposal would even have to do, so that is what I want first. Please write out the desiderata for a theory of extrapolated or idealized preference: what it must deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement, and with a judgment about which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely prejudices of one camp. There are several I want on the list. First, it must handle sour grapes and addiction — cases where a person's actual preference seems to mislead about their good — while not treating every preference formed under bad circumstances as thereby defective. Second, it must distinguish error-correction from maturation: a theory must say whether it is merely removing false beliefs and cognitive limitations or simulating the person's development, because the teenager corrected about the facts and the adult that teenager becomes can want different things, and these are different outputs. Third, path independence: running the extrapolation from the person at eighteen and from the person at forty-five should not terminate in different endpoints, and if it can, the theory owes an account of which one counts. Fourth, fixed-point stability: whatever the procedure outputs must itself survive a second pass through the procedure, since the extrapolated agent is presumably the kind of thing that can be extrapolated again. Fifth, interference between persons: people can prefer that others' values be reshaped to match their own, and the theory must say whether extrapolation sanitizes such preferences or faithfully preserves them — this matters most once extrapolation is run over a population rather than an individual, where aggregation worries of the Arrow sort arise. Add whatever else you think belongs on the list. For each desideratum, give the case that forces it and note what it rules out. Then flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion that fixed-point stability and path independence are the ones that fight, and that is a central thread I want for the paper — and identify the single requirement that excludes the most extant contenders. Keep any scoring of named proposals to a sentence each at most; the criteria are the part I need, not the scorecard.
