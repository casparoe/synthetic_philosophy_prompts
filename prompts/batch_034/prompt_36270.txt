I'm putting together a handout for an intro-level ethics lecture on hard choices — the "should I go to law school or move to Lisbon for the painting" genre, not trolley stuff. What I want is a decision tree that sorts students into a position on incommensurability, built from questions they can answer without knowing any of the literature. Start from the raw logical space: (1) is every alternative pair such that one is better, worse, or equal? (2) if they can be neither, does that just mean the two are equal in value, or is there room for a fourth relation? (3) when neither option is better, is there still a fact about what you ought to choose, or is that where your will comes in? Leaves should include at least the single-scale comparabilist, the brute pluralist, the lexicographic/lexical-priority view, the parity-style fourth-relation view, and the "no fact of the matter, you constitute the reason by committing" view — give them names or "unnamed" where nobody really defends them. Each internal node should be phrased as a case or claim about a case, not "do you believe in value pluralism," and use a couple of the standard probes: the small-improvement test (adding a tiny bit of one good until it tips), a choice between two options where a third is slightly better than one of them but still loses overall, and the question of whether flipping a coin over a hard choice is irrational or just honest. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate and say whether you think that's because the views collapse together or because my node questions are too coarse. Keep the whole tree to at most 10 nodes, and one line per leaf on its most awkward commitment. Audience is first-years, so no untranslated jargon in the node questions — the jargon can live in the leaves.
