I recently took over as enrichment coordinator for the invertebrate and corvid sections of a mid-sized public aquarium — my charges include giant Pacific octopuses, cuttlefish, and a small group of ravens — and my director has asked me to submit a proposal that sorts our candidate enrichment interventions into tiers by the kind of cognitive work each one presupposes, so that budget can follow cognitive demand rather than visual novelty. I'd like to use Daniel Dennett's "tower of generate and test" as the organizing framework: Darwinian creatures (hardwired, selected phylogenetically), Skinnerian creatures (shaped by operant conditioning within a lifetime), Popperian creatures (able to run candidate actions through an internal model and discard bad options in advance), and Gregorian creatures (able to import tools for thinking from the minds of others, including demonstrators and culture).

The candidate interventions, roughly as our team has drafted them:

1. Rotating which jar in a fixed row of anchored jars contains a shrimp, with all jars visually identical.
2. A latch-and-slide puzzle box whose solution mechanism stays constant across weeks, so that success is a matter of practiced habit.
3. A schedule of novel-object presentations — unfamiliar floating toys, changing substrate textures — with no food or task attached.
4. A "rule-switch" task: the animal learns that the opaque cup on the left hides the food, and after a run of trials the rule changes without any signal, so that continuing to persist with the old strategy yields nothing.
5. An arrangement in which a skilled conspecific solves a task in view of a naive individual, who is then given the same task.
6. A mirror-mediated task: the food is visible only via reflection, and the animal must use the mirror to guide its reach.

Please sort these six interventions into tiers by the level of Dennett's tower each is genuinely taxing — where a given item lands should reflect what the animal must be doing to succeed, not what the intervention looks like to visitors. For each intervention, give a sentence on why it lands where it does. Then flag any cases where I risk mistaking mere persistence or habituation for insight, and close by identifying which single intervention would give me the strongest evidence of Popperian-level cognition in an octopus as opposed to a raven, and why the same evidence should be read differently for the two animals. Please answer in American English.
