I co-teach a seminar that pairs Parfit on personal identity with selections from the Pali Canon on not-self, and the discussions have revealed a problem I want an instrument for. By the end of the unit, roughly half the room says "there is no self," but their follow-up arguments show they mean quite different, incompatible things by it: one student apparently means only that there is no immortal, persisting soul; another that there is never any subject of experience at all, not even a fleeting one; a third that the self is a cognitive construction that would be healthier to drop. I want a decision tree that takes a student from the bare commitment "there is no self" to a specific, named position.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: fix the branch questions first, then locate the actual positions in that space. The branches I care about, roughly in this order of priority, though you may reorganize if you tell me why:

1. What exactly is being denied: a soul-like persisting substance of the kind the doctrine is standardly read as targeting, or any subject of experience whatsoever, however momentary? This is the split my students are actually blind to.
2. Is the denial metaphysical — a claim about what exists — or therapeutic: a claim about what it is wise or possible to identify with? The student who says the doctrine's point is to loosen clinging should land somewhere different from the one who says persons literally do not exist.
3. What does introspective search show? "I looked for a self and couldn't find one" — is that evidence, and of what? Split the students who take the failure to find a self to be like failing to find a unicorn from those who take it to be like the eye's failing to see itself.
4. For whoever is left holding a conventional person: does everyday personal language — promises, responsibility, first-person pronouns — remain fully respectable at the end of the analysis, or is it a ladder to be kicked away?

Leaves should include, at minimum: Parfit's claim that the Buddha was a reductionist about persons; Siderits-style two-level reductionism, on which the ultimate description is impersonal and personal idiom is conventionally licensed; the Madhyamaka two-truths conventionalist move; Metzinger-style illusionism, on which the self is a generated model and the doctrine is vindicated rather than replaced by cognitive science; Zahavi's minimal-self objection, under which the denial overreaches because some for-me-ness is still given in experience; Albahari's witness-consciousness defense, on which a witnessing awareness survives the analysis — a leaf that is arguably not a Buddhist position at all and may have to hang off the tree as an exit; and the pragmatic reading on which not-self is an instruction rather than a thesis. If the logical space supports further leaves, or admits combinations nobody occupies, say so, and diagnose each empty cell as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate rather than inventing a distinction to force them apart. I suspect Parfit and Siderits will come out nearly indistinguishable to a student who cannot see the metaphysics behind the idioms; if so, tell me that. Same for the Madhyamaka conventionalist and the deflationist who holds the Buddha denied only a soul: if splitting them would require a question no student without Sanskrit could answer, say that rather than faking a branch.

Every node must be phrased as something a student can answer without knowing where it leads and without any Pali or Sanskrit terminology; the point is to reveal what they believe, not which labels they have read. Nothing of the form "would you call yourself a reductionist?"

Finally, two epistemic requirements. At each leaf, mark whether the interpretation of the canonical texts it rests on is settled or genuinely disputed among people who work on this material: whether the Pali suttas deny a subject of experience, or only a permanent substance, is a live scholarly dispute, and I do not want any leaf presented as "what the Buddha meant" when it is one scholar's reconstruction. And where a leaf attributes a view to a contemporary philosopher, indicate whether that person actually holds the position or whether the leaf is a reading introduced for the tree's symmetry; if any leaf is your own coinage, say so. Don't present a contested reading as the consensus anywhere in the tree.
