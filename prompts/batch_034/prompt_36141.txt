Here's something I keep bumping into in my job (I work adjacent to energy policy, not in philosophy, so go easy on jargon). My jurisdiction recently killed a proposed carbon tax after a public campaign that was basically "it's immoral to let people buy the right to pollute — the rich will just pay and keep emitting, and making ordinary families pay for heating their homes is a tax grab." Fine. But then, within a year, the same government passed a whole regulatory package: no natural gas hookups in new buildings, a sales mandate forcing automakers to hit a rising percentage of EV sales, and a clean electricity standard for the grid. Analysts here (and even some ministry people privately) admit that several of these regulations carry an implied cost per tonne of CO2 well above what the carbon tax would have charged — like, the EV mandate alone is several times the proposed tax rate. And fuel subsidies quietly kept gasoline cheaper than it would otherwise be the whole time. So the "people shouldn't have to pay to emit" government has built a system where people pay much more to emit, just through car prices and utility bills and slower housing construction, without anyone ever seeing a line item called a carbon price.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit commitments here, not a policy recommendation. It can't literally be a commitment to "nobody pays for emissions," since everyone pays, and pays more. Is the real presupposition something like: costs are legitimate when they're hidden inside product mandates and construction codes but illegitimate when they take the form of a visible transfer to the state? That seems to presuppose a view about the moral salience of transparency or of who receives the money rather than who bears it. Or is it a genuinely deontological commitment — pollution is a wrong you prohibit, not a price you set — which would explain rejecting a tax in principle but would then have trouble explaining why the regulations only constrain rather than ban, and why fuel subsidies push in exactly the opposite direction. Or maybe the practice presupposes nothing coherent at all, and the right answer is that the pattern is only explicable by political incentives, in which case I want you to say what specifically a philosophical reconstruction adds or fails to add here. There's also a third strand I'm confused about: the same officials celebrate the green jobs and GDP growth these rules will supposedly create, which seems to presuppose that decarbonization and growth are compatible — does the package quietly rule out the degrowth view that emissions and GDP can't be decoupled, or is that just rhetoric layered on top with no real commitment either way?

Please take a definite position: give me the single best account of what this package of practices presupposes, say which rival readings it actually rules out versus which it's merely compatible with, and identify the point where you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined or flatly incoherent. I'd rather you commit to one reconstruction and defend it, even if you flag the cost of doing so, than lay out four candidate theories and leave me to choose. And if you think the deontological reading ("pollution is a sin, not a fee") is actually doing the most work in public discourse even while the legislation itself behaves consequentialistly, I'd genuinely like to hear that argued rather than hedged.
