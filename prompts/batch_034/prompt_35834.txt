I keep running into the old puzzle of the ship whose planks are replaced one by one, and I've noticed something odd about how the debate over it proceeds: nobody disagrees about the facts. The schedule of replacement, the salvage of the discarded planks, the duplicate ship built from them — all of that is common ground. What the sides disagree about is what a theory of persistence is supposed to do with such cases, and that's the part I want nailed down before I commit to any view. So write me the specification, not the sales pitch: the list of requirements an adequate theory of how objects persist through change and replacement must meet, with the case or consideration behind each.

The list should at least cover:

- The cases any theory must get right, beyond the vanilla gradual-replacement story: the version where the discarded planks are reassembled into a rival ship that coexists with the continuously repaired one; the version where the original is destroyed and a later duplicate is built from new materials to the same plans; the version where the ship is dismantled and left in storage for decades before reassembly; and a borderline I rarely see discussed — a ship that spends half its life beached as a static museum exhibit while its name is transferred to an actively sailing successor.
- The distinctions a theory must not collapse: being the same object versus being an exact continuation of one versus being a faithful replica; the identity of the ship versus the identity of its matter; answers that are relative to a kind — same ship, same collection of planks, same fleet, same business operating it — versus answers that are absolute and kind-independent; and, if the theory distinguishes them, sameness of thing from sameness of matter persisting.
- The constraints it cannot contradict: the formal properties of identity, above all transitivity and Leibniz's law, since those are what generate the sharpest pressure — any story on which the repaired ship, the reassembled ship, and the duplicate all claim a single original had better not make identity non-transitive in the process; whatever one's best account of parthood implies about how much of a thing can be swapped out while it survives; and the verdicts we actually rely on in practice, where money and obligations turn on the answer — a heritage registry deciding whether a heavily restored cathedral counts as the original, a marine insurance contract that pays out only if it is the same vessel that departed, a court deciding whether a business that has since replaced every employee, asset, and location still owes a debt incurred forty years ago.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp, and flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I am especially interested in whether any theory can deliver determinate, absolute, single answers in the reassembly and duplicate cases while preserving transitivity, or whether the requirements of determinacy and of transitivity quietly force a choice between them. Note, too, which single requirement on your list does the most to eliminate otherwise respectable contenders.

Then, briefly, score the main views against the list — the endurance picture on which identity through time is governed by a sortal, the four-dimensional picture on which objects have temporal parts, best-candidate approaches, and the view that there is simply no fact of the matter and the question is settled by convention or by our interests — but keep this short; the criteria are where I want the thinking. Fix the standard of adequacy as follows: a theory counts if it is good enough to decide the courthouse and registry disputes, not merely to classify them. If you think that standard is itself the crux — that no metaphysics of persistence could be what those institutions are implicitly using — say so and explain what would have to be true for the specification to be satisfiable at all.
