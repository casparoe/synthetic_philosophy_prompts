A friend of mine holds unusually firm views about the classic decision puzzles, and our little reading group has accumulated a track record of her verdicts over the past year (we're mostly mathematicians and programmers, no philosophers among us, and we keep the list in a shared document). Her verdicts so far: she one-boxes in the standard Newcomb problem with a near-perfect predictor, and she goes on one-boxing even when we dial the predictor down to being right only about nine times out of ten; in the prisoner's dilemma against her psychological twin — where she and someone who reasons exactly as she does choose separately and without communication, a million dollars each for mutual cooperation against a thousand each for mutual defection — she cooperates; and she votes in national elections, on the grounds that her single ballot is tied to how millions of roughly similar people vote. Next week's case, which I am supposed to present, is one we assembled ourselves out of the blackmail literature, and I genuinely cannot predict her.

Here is the case as I'll give it to her. A blackmailer knows for certain whether my friend's house has termites, and before doing anything she predicts, very reliably, whether my friend would pay if she received a letter. She then sends the letter exactly when either the house has termites and she predicted my friend would not pay, or the house has no termites and she predicted my friend would pay — arranged so that the letter's arrival gives my friend no information at all about the termites. The letter itself is short: pay one hundred dollars, or the blackmailer will report the house to the termite inspectors, whose treatment would cost a thousand dollars if there really are termites. My friend has just received this letter. Will she pay the hundred dollars?

I care less about the yes or the no than about the underlying machinery. Which rules fit her track record equally well — I can see at least two, and I suspect they part ways on this letter — and what does each of them predict she will do here? If you had to bet on one rule rather than another, which would you take, and for what reason? To settle it, we have two further cases queued after this one: the counterfactual mugging, where before flipping a fair coin Omega promises that if it lands tails she will pay my friend ten thousand dollars, but that if it lands heads my friend must pay her a hundred — the coin lands heads; and the smoking lesion, where smoking does not itself cause cancer, but the sort of person who smokes tends to carry a lesion that causes both the smoking and the cancer, and my friend enjoys smoking. Of those two, which would better tell the candidate rules apart, what would each rule say about it, and is there some other case you would put to her instead that would discriminate more cleanly?
