I'm the deputy head of a private secular secondary school in Brussels with about 900 students, and every year we field a steady trickle of requests for religious accommodation that our current practice handles ad hoc, and inconsistently — the same request has been granted by one year head and denied by another, and parents have started noticing. The deeper problem is that our governing board is split along a fault line that I've come to realize is genuinely philosophical: roughly half the board was educated in the French tradition and takes the laïcité view that a school's job is to maintain a neutral common space from which communal identities are set aside, and the other half is Anglo-American and takes the multiculturalist view that refusing accommodations is itself a choice that quietly privileges the majority's holidays, food, and dress as the unmarked default. Every time we try to settle a case, the meeting replays this dispute at full volume and decides nothing. What I want is not for you to referee the board, but to give me something the school can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure, in the form of an ordered list of questions with criteria, that our year heads could apply to any accommodation request in under an hour: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points at which the procedure terminates in a no, and — crucially — the points at which it must stop and hand the decision up, because what remains is a question of values the procedure cannot resolve and only the board can. The conceptual work I'm asking for is in the translation: the procedure should be honest about which parts of the school's stance come from laïcité-type reasoning (uniform treatment, the school as a space of emancipation from inherited community, equality as sameness before the institution) and which from multiculturalist reasoning (recognition, the claim that neutrality is a myth because the default already encodes the majority culture, equality as sometimes requiring differential treatment), and it should mark clearly where those two traditions would give different answers to the same question rather than smuggling one in. It should also state its own failure modes — for instance, where treating all religions identically becomes a rule that only ever bites on minorities, or where case-by-case accommodation drifts into an implicit ranking of which faiths are serious.

Then run the procedure on three requests we actually received last year: a fifteen-year-old who wants to wear a headscarf in PE and in class; a group of parents asking that afternoon exams during Ramadan be moved to the morning; and parents asking that their daughter be excused from co-ed swimming lessons. I'd like to see where the procedure gives a firm answer, where the two traditions embedded in it would diverge, and at least one point where it produces an outcome the board majority will dislike — I suspect that if we take our neutrality rhetoric seriously it commits us to things about crucifix necklaces and birthday cakes that nobody on the board has thought through. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of requests that will come to us and that it was never built to handle, and where it will misfire if we lean on it for a decade. Keep in mind our constraints: year heads are not philosophers, decisions must be explainable in writing to parents in two languages, every ruling sets a precedent we can be held to, and the Ramadan question comes back annually whether we have a framework or not.
