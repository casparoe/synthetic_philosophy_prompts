Compare the moral category of supererogation with the much less discussed category of suberogation, and assess whether the two are genuinely mirror images of one another.

To fix terms: supererogatory acts, such as heroically rescuing a stranger at great personal risk, are commonly said to be permissible, morally good in a praiseworthy way, yet not obligatory — their omission leaves no room for blame. Suberogation, a category introduced into the literature by Julia Driver, is meant to be its mirror image: acts that are permissible in the sense that they violate no duty, yet fall short of some moral ideal and thereby merit a mild, blame-adjacent negative appraisal — think of gratuitously letting a door slam in someone's face when holding it open would cost you nothing. Such acts seem wrong-ish without being wrong.

In your comparison, address the following questions:

1. Structural symmetry. Supererogation involves a normative surplus (going beyond duty), while suberogation involves a normative deficit (falling short of an ideal without breaching duty). Is "deficit below an ideal, yet above the threshold of duty" a coherent position on the moral map, or does admitting suberogation force us to collapse the important distinction between acts that are blameworthy and acts that are simply less than ideal?

2. Asymmetries in defensibility. Some philosophers accept supererogation while denying suberogation, claiming that anything short of the ideal but above the duty-threshold is simply fine. Can this asymmetric position be sustained, or does a principled account of why supererogation exists (e.g., the role of cost to the agent in excusing non-performance of good acts) automatically vindicate suberogation? You may consider whether costs to the agent function differently in the two cases — as an excuse that shields from blame in one direction but not as a justification for doing the less-than-ideal thing in the other.

3. Theoretical uptake. How do different moral frameworks handle the two categories? For instance, does a maximizing consequentialism that recognizes supererogation as a practical impossibility (no one can always maximize) thereby also generate suberogatory acts, since most permissible acts fall short of the best? Does a satisficing view, on which duty requires only "good enough" performance, leave room for either category?

Conclude with a considered judgment on whether suberogation deserves the same standing in ethical theory as supererogation, or whether it is a gerrymandered category that adds nothing beyond ordinary talk of rudeness, unkindness, and callousness.
