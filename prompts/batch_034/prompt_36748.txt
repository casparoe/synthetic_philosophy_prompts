My friend and I watched Hitchcock's Lifeboat (1944) for movie night and then stayed up till like 2am yelling about it, and I need someone to settle this. You remember the setup -- Allied freighter torpedoed, the whole film never leaves the lifeboat, and the guy they fish out of the water turns out to be Willi, the U-boat captain who sank them. My friend is convinced the film is Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics essay in miniature: scarce water, limited seats, hard rationing, and the debate over what you owe the man in the water next to your boat. I think that reading is wrong twice over. First, Hardin's 1974 essay is about the lucky already being IN the boat and the unlucky being in the water -- a distribution between rich and poor worlds -- but the film's boat is just the war dumping everyone into the water indiscriminately, so there's no we-built-this-lifeboat-and-you-didn't claim anywhere. Second, and this is where it got heated, Hardin's swimmer is a stranger to your predicament, but Willi is the guy who CREATED the predicament, which turns a rescue question into a justice question, and Hardin's framework has nothing to say about that. The film seems to know it too -- by the end they pull another frightened young German sailor out of the water, and this time its Rittenhouse, the industrialist who earlier insisted the first German be allowed to stay aboard, who has to be physically held back from killing him.

So give me a reading of the film through Hardin's metaphor that takes the metaphor seriously on its own terms -- carrying capacity, the commons, the drowning-the-boat problem -- and then commits: does the film illustrate Hardin, complicate him, or refute him? Pick one and defend it, no both-sidesing, I'm not trying to grade a paper here. Exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion. One point has to be about the ending with the second German (my friend says the repetition proves the Hardin reading, since the boat keeps facing the same swimmer problem; I think it wrecks the reading, since the film clearly thinks the answer this time is different). One point has to deal with the fact that the story is by John Steinbeck, who was not exactly Hardin's demographic -- think about whether all the collectivist stuff on the boat, like Kovac the crewman rationing the food and the whole crew pitching in to amputate Gus's leg, is doing ethical work Hardin can't account for. And at least one point should be honest about where the film is just a survival melodrama making no point at all -- but still land on one verdict at the end.
