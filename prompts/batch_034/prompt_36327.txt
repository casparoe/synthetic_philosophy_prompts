I've been going pretty deep on Schopenhauer's line that music is different from the other arts because it doesn't go through ideas or concepts at all — where a poem can only get at, say, grief by way of the particular case, music supposedly hands you grief as such, not this grief or that grief. I'm tired of the standard back-and-forth about whether music is a copy of the Will, and I want to write about what instrumental music expresses on its own terms. So before I argue for anything, I want the spec, not the theories.

Write me the desiderata: exactly five numbered items, nothing before item 1 and nothing after item 5. Each item is one or two sentences — state the requirement and the case or consideration behind it. Here are candidates I've been toying with; keep, fix, or drop them as you see fit, and fill out the list yourself:

- the sad-music case — we pay money to hear grief played at us and then voluntarily replay it, which nobody does with, like, a friend's account of their divorce; a theory that gets the expression right shouldn't be predicting we can't enjoy it
- a persona/symptom distinction — whatever the cello line expresses, it's not the composer's actual feeling as a causal symptom, the way a trembling voice is; theories that blur this confuse expression with cause. this one definately belongs somewhere but fix my wording
- Schopenhauer's own non-particularity requirement, that music can never be about a particular person or event — I half suspect this is a camp prejudice dressed up as a desideratum, so tell me inside the list whether it earns a spot at all

Two more things about the list itself. If you think any two of the five are jointly unsatisfiable, flag that in a clause inside the later item rather than saving it for a wrap-up — there shouldn't be a wrap-up. And put an asterisk on the single requirement that does the most work to knock out candidate theories, since that's the part I care most about. Don't score any actual theories against the list, I'll do that myself, and no names of books, papers, or people — just the requirements, stated in plain words.
