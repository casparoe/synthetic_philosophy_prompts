I edit a small metaphysics blog whose readers are philosophy graduate students and well-prepared undergraduates, and I'd like to commission a diagnostic quiz with the working title "Which theory of de re modality do you actually hold?" The target debate is counterpart theory versus transworld identity: whether individuals themselves exist in other possible worlds, or whether other worlds contain only counterparts that resemble them in various respects. The point of the quiz is to sort readers by what they are inclined to say about concrete modal cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question may ask about positions or their proponents directly ("do you accept counterpart theory?") or appeal to authority ("as Kripke would put it"). Kripke and Lewis may be named in the scoring key, where you locate the recognized positions in the logical space your questions generate, but never in the questions themselves.

Please structure the deliverable as follows. First, a set of eight to ten questions, each presenting one concrete case with two or three answer options phrased in ordinary language. Second, a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Third, for each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — for instance, the counterpart theorist's embarrassment that when I say I could have won, I seem to be reporting that someone else won; or the haecceitist's difficulty saying how we could ever know which individual plays which role in a qualitatively symmetric world.

The questions must include transpositions of the following cases, which I want covered regardless of how you organize the rest:

1. A near-miss case: a politician loses an election he nearly won, and we say he could have won. What does that claim concern — him, in another world, doing something; or something in another world that resembles him doing it?
2. A fission-style case transposed to modality: in the nearest world where I win a lottery, there are two candidates for "me," one who shares my memories up to a certain date and one who shares my appearance and mannerisms, and neither resembles me more overall. Does "I would have won" come out true, false, indeterminate, or in need of a tie-breaking convention?
3. A qualitatively symmetric world: a world just like this one in every qualitative respect, but in which I and my closest physical duplicate have swapped histories. Is anything de re different between the two worlds?
4. An origin case: someone conceived from a different pairing of gametes, otherwise as similar to me as you like — could that be me?
5. A degradation case: could I have been a frog? I want to see how the quiz handles the intuition that the answer is obviously no, and what that "no" is tracking — similarity, origin, or something about the thing itself that survives qualitative change.

Beyond the leaves corresponding to Lewisian counterpart theory (with the relative-similarity relation doing the work) and Kripkean transworld identity with something like origin essentialism, the logical space should include at least these further cells: a permissive counterpart theory on which multiple counterparts yield indeterminate truth values; bare haecceitism, on which individuals differ across worlds without any qualitative difference (so that "I could have been a frog" can be true if God so arranged it); and the anti-haecceitist thought that there is no de re difference without a qualitative difference, however it is combined with a view about worlds. Build the tree from the questions first — the nodes should be commitments a reader can express without knowing the labels — and only then locate the named positions in it.

Two further requirements. First, where a combination of answers is occupied by no recognized position, say so and speculate briefly on whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag honestly any pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate: I am particularly worried that a counterpart theorist who weights origin similarity overwhelmingly heavily will answer every question just like a transworld-identity theorist with origin essentialism, and if the quiz cannot drive a wedge between them, I would rather publish a caveat than a false sense of diagnostic precision.
