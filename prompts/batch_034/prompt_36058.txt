I'm TA-ing a mid-level decision theory course and need to grade a homework set on the value of information, and I want a second opinion on one particular answer because I keep going back and forth on how much to deduct. The homework question was:

"State Good's theorem that costless information never has negative expected value, and sketch a proof of it. Then answer two follow-ups: (i) does the theorem show that a rational agent can never be made worse off by receiving information? (ii) does the theorem by itself license the advice 'always gather all the information you can'?"

And here's the student's answer, copied from the submission:

"Good's theorem: if an agent can choose an act after observing some signal X, for free, then the expected utility of the best act given X is at least the expected utility of the best act chosen without X. Proof: let V be the expected utility of choosing optimally after seeing X. Formally V = E over X of [max over acts a of E(u | X)]. Without the observation the agent gets V0 = max over a of E(u). For each value of X the conditional expectation inside the max is an average, and an average of maxima is at least the maximum of the corresponding averages, so V ≥ V0. Since expectations only add and the observation is free, nothing is subtracted, so the value of the observation itself is V − V0 ≥ 0, and this is strict whenever X is not statistically independent of the state of the world — correlated signals always help a little.

(i) Yes. Since the expected value of information is non-negative, a rational agent can never regret receiving information: seeing the signal leaves you at least as well off as not seeing it. The only way information hurts is if you pay for it or if you're irrational and choose badly afterward. (ii) Roughly yes — this is the thought behind the principle of total evidence: since free information never lowers expected utility, collect everything that's free. One caveat: this all assumes the agent is risk-neutral. A risk-averse agent values information differently and could in principle be made worse off by it, so the theorem needs expected utility maximizers specifically, which is fine since that's our framework in this course anyway."
