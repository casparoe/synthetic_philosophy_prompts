At a dinner party last weekend, two friends of mine, Lena and Marcus, got into a long and surprisingly heated argument about Marcus's new system for managing his own bad habits. Over the past few months he has paid for a year of gym classes up front, deleted the food delivery apps from his phone, and started putting his phone into a timed lockbox at 9 p.m., which it won't open until morning. (1) Marcus defended all of this as straightforwardly rational: he says he knows from experience that his 11 p.m. self discounts the future hyperbolically — the value of tomorrow morning's workout, which feels decisive when he plans it on Sunday, collapses once the option of one more episode is right there — so he sets up barriers in advance, the way you'd hire a guard for a warehouse you know gets burgled. (2) Lena said this gets something backwards: if the person who buys the lockbox and the person who gets locked out are the same person, then the rational thing to do is sort out what he actually wants and act on it, and precommitment devices only make sense if you've already decided the evening self's preferences shouldn't count — but whether they should count is exactly the question at issue, so Marcus is smuggling in his conclusion. (3) Marcus replied that the question isn't open at all: with the same information, the same calm mood, and nothing changed but the passage of time, he prefers one thing on Sunday and the opposite on Thursday night, and a chooser whose preferences reverse under mere temporal proximity to temptation is irrational at one moment or the other — so he's entitled to appoint the Sunday self as the referee and treat the Thursday self as the malfunction. (4) Lena answered that 'irrational' is only licensed if the two evaluations purport to answer to a single underlying scale, and she'd read that hyperbolic discounting is better pictured as turning a person into a series of agents, each with its own steep near horizons; on that picture there is no privileged referee at all, and what Marcus calls rational self-correction is just one agent strategically outmaneuvering its successors — possibly clever, possibly even prudent, but the label 'correcting a bias' versus 'winning an internal power struggle' is being imported from outside the theory.

They eventually turned to me to referee, and I honestly couldn't — both seemed to me to have a real point at each stage. Please adjudicate their exchange: go through the four moves in order, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the ratings. Where a move is half-right, say exactly which half. And at the end, please say whether the whole dispute turns on an assumption that both of them share but neither states — because it struck me that they might be fighting over the referee while agreeing on something more basic about what self-control is.
