My foreign policy reading group keeps having the same fight and I want it audited properly. Whenever a crisis comes up and someone argues for restraint or negotiation, one guy says "this is Munich" -- meaning: the adversary is an expansionist who reads concession as weakness, the people urging diplomacy are Chamberlains, and if we don't act now we'll just pay more later. Someone else always fires back with "this is Vietnam" -- meaning: the case for action rests on domino-theory reasoning, we'll get sucked into a quagmire, and the people crying Munich are manufacturing resolve. Neither side ever produces anything but the analogy. Both are treated as if they settle things.

Here's what I want. Take the Munich one first and actually map it out: what's supposed to play the role of Hitler, of Chamberlain, of the Sudetenland, of 1938 itself, of the "peace for our time" frame. Then show me where the mapping breaks. Some of the disanalogies I can guess (the adversary's actual ambitions and capacity, whether the concession on the table is comparable, whether there's a credible enforcement mechanism that 1938 lacked), but I want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the use it's being put to versus which are just noise. And crucially, what does the analogy smuggle in? It seems to me the whole thing runs on the premise that the adversary is Hitler-like, which is exactly what's in dispute, so using it may just be assuming the conclusion with extra steps. Or maybe not -- maybe there's a weaker structural version (concessions to an adversary with a record of broken agreements tend to be read as weakness) that survives even when the Hitler part is dropped.

Then do the same for Vietnam, which seems to have the mirror-image problem: it smuggles in that the adversary is NOT a real threat, also exactly what's in dispute. Does the quagmire logic depend on that premise or is it independent of it?

Two more things I keep chewing on and want your read on. First, there's an ugly historical irony here: the "lesson of Munich" was arguably applied in Vietnam -- don't appease aggression -- and produced the disaster the other analogy is named for. So it looks like the lessons themselves, learned from analogy, generate the next round of mistakes. Is that a reason to think historical analogies can't carry inferential weight at all, or just that these two are badly chosen? Second, there's a selection effect I can't get out of my head: "Munich" gets invoked after the fact whenever restraint was followed by disaster, but the cases where restraint was followed by peace don't get a name at all. If the analogy's track record is only counted one way, is it even evidence of anything?

If it helps, end with what a better analogy would be for the kind of situation we actually argue about, or an honest argument that no historical analogy can do this job and the debate has to be settled some other way.

One ask: tell me how confident you are in the various claims you make along the way, and flag the single point you're least sure about -- I suspect the smuggled-premise issue is where the real action is but I'd like to know if you think it's somewhere else instead.
