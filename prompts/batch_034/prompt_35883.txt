I've been reading about Epicurus's argument that death can't be bad for the one who dies, since there's no subject left over to be harmed by it, and I've mostly found it convincing. But the law seems to assume the opposite in some places and agree with it in others, and I can't square the two. On one side, wrongful death suits and life insurance never compensate the deceased for losing their life — the money always goes to surviving relatives, as if the harm of death itself were not owed to anyone. On the other side, desecrating a corpse or vandalizing a grave is a crime even when the family is gone or doesn't care, wills are enforced for decades after the testator dies, a dead person's recorded refusal to donate organs is treated as binding, and some countries let you be sued for defaming the dead. What theory of harm and of death do these practices jointly presuppose? Does the law hold an Epicurean view when compensating and an anti-Epicurean view when protecting dignity and wishes, or is there some single account — say, about enforcing promises, protecting the living's sensibilities, or maintaining social norms — that covers both halves? Where would such a unified reading break down, and what exactly would have to change in these practices if a legislature took Epicurus seriously? Please be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction you're least confident in, and what argument or consideration would push you toward the opposite conclusion about what the law presupposes.
