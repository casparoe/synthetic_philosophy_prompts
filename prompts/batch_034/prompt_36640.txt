So here's the thing I want to work through for my phil of science seminar this spring. Every year I teach the thought experiments unit as a catalog — Galileo's tower, Schrödinger's cat, Maxwell's demon — and it always feels like a tour of greatest hits rather than an actual analysis. This time I want to take one single episode and run it hard through one theory of how thought experiments yield knowledge, and I've settled on the following. The episode: Einstein's memory of being sixteen, a student at the Gymnasium in Aarau, imagining what he would see if he rode along a beam of light. As he told it decades later in his autobiographical notes, "If I pursue a beam of light with the velocity c (velocity of light in a vacuum), I should observe such a beam of light as an electromagnetic field at rest though spatially oscillating. There seems to be no such thing, however, neither on the basis of experience nor according to Maxwell's equations." The theory: what's usually called the argument view of thought experiments, roughly John Norton's position — the claim, as he puts it, that a good thought experiment is a good argument, a bad one a bad argument. On this view a thought experiment is a disguised argument; whatever knowledge it delivers comes entirely from premises already available beforehand, and the vivid staging (the demon, the cat, the teenager on the light beam) contributes persuasive and psychological force but never any extra warrant.

What I want from you is a disciplined mapping, and then an honest verdict on both directions. First the mapping: reconstruct the thought experiment as an explicit argument — identify the premises (the principle of relativity, the claim that Maxwell's equations admit no stationary light wave, whatever else), say which of those premises were actually available to a sixteen-year-old in the 1890s, and be careful here, because the historiography is tricky in a way that cuts in several directions. It's doubtful he had properly learned Maxwell's theory before university, a couple of years later; the recollection itself is famously colored by a half-century of hindsight; and an aether theorist of the day could apparently have accepted the frozen beam without batting an eye — so the young Einstein's reaction looks less like a derivation from known premises and more like an intuitive conviction that the laws of optics had to obey the relativity principle. So does the argument view, which after all insists the knowledge comes only from what was already known, end up saying he learned nothing? And then the counterfactual twist: it took him roughly ten more years to get to special relativity, and the 1905 theory is a new theory, not something squeezed out of the 1895 reverie by rearrangement. Second, the verdict: does this episode come out as a friendly case for the argument view, an anomaly, or the kind of case the view never anticipated? Where exactly is the view silent, and don't let it speak where it can't. And finally, if the argument view strains, tell me whether the main rival — the Platonic line, roughly James Robert Brown's, on which some thought experiments are a kind of perception of abstract laws — does any better on this material, or whether both views are misdescribing what was going on: not an argument and not a mystical insight into the platonic realm, but something else that neither camp has a category for. Be as concrete as you can, and if you think I've mis-stated the physics or the history anywhere, say so rather than building on it.
