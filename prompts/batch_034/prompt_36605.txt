I'm the deputy editor of a popular history magazine, circulation around 80,000, with no in-house ancient historian. Each month roughly two dozen submissions and pitches land in the gray zone between minority scholarship and pseudohistory. Recent examples: a self-published book arguing that Atlantis was a distorted cultural memory of Minoan civilization wrecked by the Thera eruption; a letter explaining that conventional chronology is largely a fabrication and that a stretch of the early Middle Ages never happened, citing Fomenko's New Chronology and Illig's phantom time hypothesis interchangeably, apparently without noticing that they disagree about which centuries are phantoms; occasional ancient-astronaut material in respectable dress; and, mixed in with these, pitches that are unorthodox but serious — a classicist arguing that the received story of the destruction of the Library of Alexandria is mostly legend, which happens to be close to what academic specialists now think.

We currently sort these by feel, and we sort them inconsistently: one editor's "fun contrarian piece" is another's "never touch." I want a written procedure that any of us could run in fifteen minutes on a single pitch, producing one of four outcomes: (a) engage it as a live minority view; (b) classify it as pseudohistory — rejected outright, or covered explicitly as pseudohistory under my signature; (c) fiction or entertainment, if the author will accept it under that label; (d) undecidable in fifteen minutes, with a note on what evidence would decide it. The procedure should ask its questions in a fixed order, state for each question what counts as a sufficient answer and which answer ends the run at (b), and flag the steps that are irreducibly judgment calls — I suspect the line between "maverick but in the game" and "outside the game" is one, and I want it marked rather than smoothed over. Questions I'd expect somewhere in the sequence, reworked as you see fit: does the author allege a conspiracy or a systemic blindness of an entire discipline, or just one mistaken consensus; is the claim revisable in principle, and does the author say what would count against it; do they engage existing rebuttals or behave as though none exist; does the claim require whole independent fields (astronomy, dendrochronology, radiocarbon dating) to be broken, rather than one narrative to be wrong. Discard any of these you think are bad tests, add others, and say why.

Then run the procedure on three pitches: (1) the Thera-Atlantis book, whose author, a retired naval architect, argues in apparent good faith and answers objections directly; (2) the phantom-time letter, which treats the scarcity of surviving written records as evidence that the events never happened and denounces carbon dating as circular; (3) the classicist on the Library of Alexandria, who is condescending about our magazine in the pitch. Include the third even if you find it easy — I want to see the procedure terminate quickly when it should. I already suspect I won't like the answer on the first: I had filed it as bunk, and if your procedure says it is a minority hypothesis to be judged on ordinary evidential grounds, and that a debunking treatment would be unfair to it, I want to hear that anyway.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do. Two failure modes worry me in particular: that good manners let a crank through (politeness is not evidence), and that it misfiles the next Wegener or the next pre-Clovis settlement claim — a view dismissed as crackpot-adjacent in its day and accepted decades later. Is there anything built into the procedure that guards against the second, or is that protection unachievable by any checklist — and if so, where should the value judgment sit, and with whom? On a related disagreement among the staff: some of us think covering such material as pseudohistory amplifies it. I assume no procedure can settle that one; tell me where the call belongs.
