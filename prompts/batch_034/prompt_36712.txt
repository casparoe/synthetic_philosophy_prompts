I help run a little philosophy of mind reading group, and for the past several months we've been stuck on the dreams question. Half the group came away from Malcolm's Dreaming (1959) basically convinced: since anything I could ever say about a dream is said after waking, the "received view" that dreams are experiences unfolding in real time during sleep is an unverifiable bit of metaphysics, not something we know -- what we actually have is waking reports, and nothing in the concept of dreaming itself licenses positing an inner show that ran while I slept. The other half is with Dennett's 1976 response: sure, the report is the data, but the report is compatible with lots of stories about when and how the content got produced, including his unconscious-composition story where the dream is written and spliced into memory either before or during awakening. So the two camps keep talking past each other -- one says dreams are shown to be experiences by nothing, the other says the question "is it an experience?" is asking for a fact that the data alone can't fix, not that there's no fact.

We've decided the only cure is to force them to write the document an actual adversarial collaboration would produce. So: draft the joint statement that a committed Malcolmian and a committed Dennettian could both put their names to. I want a numbered section of claims both sides can sign -- about what the data of dream science and dream reporting actually are, about what "I am asleep" can and can't be used to say, about which parts of the traditional picture both reject -- and then a disagreements section laid out as a table, where each row states a point of residual disagreement in wording that its own side would accept as fair to itself (no strawmen: the Malcolmian's side shouldn't be phrased as "science can never learn anything about sleep," and the Dennettian's shouldn't be phrased as "the received view is fine as is").

Then the cruxes, with predictions recorded in advance. We've already guessed at some candidates, so tell us if we're close: (a) how much evidential weight a waking report carries regarding what happened minutes earlier during sleep; (b) whether the timing/duration question is empirically decidable at all, and what each side should predict about, e.g., lucid dreamers signaling with pre-agreed eye movements, or about mismatches like Maury's guillotine dream where a long narrative seemed to be triggered by a single instant event; and (c) whether some chunk of the dispute is purely verbal -- maybe about whether "experience" or "dream" as used in ordinary language already carries a commitment to real-time occurrence, such that the two camps are answering different questions and only think they disagree. For each crux, give the case, experiment, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down what each side predicts the outcome would be, stated so that both would recognize it as a genuine test rather than something their view can absorb for free. If a crux turns out to be undecidable in principle, say so and say why -- but that conclusion has to be earned, not just asserted. We'd rather have a short honest document than a long fudged one, and if you find that one side's position quietly collapses into the other's, flag it explicitly rather than papering over it.
