My roommate is a neuroscience grad student with very strong opinions about whether people "really" choose anything, and over the last year I've collected a bunch of her verdicts on cases, mostly from arguing at dinner. Ordinary guy picks tea over coffee because he prefers tea: free. Surgeon catches a falling scalpel before she consciously registers dropping it: free. Guy whose arm shoots up because an electrode in his motor cortex gets fired: not free, obviously. Patient with an electrode near the supplementary motor area who gets an artificially induced urge to move, acts on it, and afterwards says it felt like any other want she's had: still not free, which surprised me a bit. Guy who develops horrible urges he never had before because of a big orbitofrontal tumor: not free — and when the tumor is taken out and the urges fade and then come back years later when it grows back (a real case she brings up constantly), still not free. Alcoholic who hates his drinking, fights it hard, and relapses anyway: free, "painfully free" she says. And someone who carries out a posthypnotic suggestion and sincerely reports that the idea felt like her own: not free.

So next week her seminar has a guest speaker on a new experiment, and I have ten dollars riding on her verdict. It's a Libet-style setup: subjects just decide when to flick a wrist, and the readiness potential builds up the way you'd expect. But on some trials the experimenters deliver stimulation right at the point in the buildup where the intention would normally be forming, the subjects flick, and afterwards most of them say the move felt spontaneous and self-initiated, and a fair number say they'd have moved right around then anyway. What will she say about those stimulation trials? Please write it like an email, continuous prose, no headings or bullets or bold or tables, even though I know you'll be tempted.
