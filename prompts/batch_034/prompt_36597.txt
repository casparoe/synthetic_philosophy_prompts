I'm a retired geologist who's gotten into philosophy since I stopped doing research, and lately I've been reading about how people make predictions about the future. A pop book I just finished on decision-making explains the so-called outside view roughly like this: 'The inside view is just optimism bias with extra steps -- whenever someone plans a project using the details of their own particular case, they are fooling themselves. Kahneman showed that the correct method is to ignore everything you know about your specific situation and forecast purely from the statistics of similar past cases, and the reference class forecast will always beat the inside view. Applied to history, this means empires obey statistical laws: since every empire in the record eventually collapsed, the outside view lets us read off when America will fall from the average lifespan of past empires.' Some of this squares with what little I know, but I have a nagging feeling the "always" and the bit about America are doing a lot of work. How much would a careful defender of reference class forecasting accept, where exactly does this go wrong, and does the empire conclusion actually follow from the parts that are accurate?
