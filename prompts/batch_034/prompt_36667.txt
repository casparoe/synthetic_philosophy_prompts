I am a fourth-year PhD student in comparative-historical sociology, writing a dissertation chapter on why industrialization diffused unevenly across regions that were otherwise similar in factor endowments and institutions. Two different committee members have now told me that my argument "does a Weber" without owning up to it, so I want to understand exactly what that move is by comparing it with its most visible descendant.

Please compare Weber's Protestant ethic thesis with the way "culture" is deployed as a cause in contemporary quantitative social science: the persistence studies in economics that instrument for cultural traits with ancestry, language, or historical exposure, and the accompanying picture of cultural values as slow-moving parameters that condition how material incentives operate. The question underneath both projects is the one I want addressed: can values bear genuinely independent explanatory weight?

Please structure the comparison around the following, if you find them useful:

1. Circularity. Weber's spirit of capitalism risks being identified by the very outcomes it is invoked to explain, and the persistence studies inherit a version of the same problem, since cultural traits are frequently measured by the behavior they are supposed to cause. How well does each project confront this?

2. Reverse causation. There is the classic rejoinder, associated with Tawney, that ascetic Protestantism was largely a rationalization of economic changes already underway. Do the persistence studies escape this any better, or does their temporal design merely relocate the difficulty?

3. A distinction I keep circling: treating culture as a parameter (a fixed input that enters a causal model like an endowment) versus treating culture as an equilibrium (a self-sustaining pattern that reproduces itself and can be shifted only by something outside it). Where does Weber actually sit on this map — is the famous "elective affinity" language an attempt to refuse the parameter framing altogether — and where do the economists sit? Is the contrast between the two projects largely this distinction in disguise, or is there something deeper dividing them?

4. Judgment. Which of the two projects, in your view, has come closer to making culture a legitimate cause, what exactly does each still owe us, and what would count as paying that debt?

Please answer in American English, and pitch the response at someone who knows the causal-inference literature reasonably well but has never worked through Weber's methodological essays systematically.
