I run a lunchtime "philosophy for engineers" thing at my company — about a dozen regulars, mostly software people who are comfortable with probability but get suspicous of anything that smells like a word game. Next session is on the preface paradox and the lottery paradox together, and what I want from you is a plan for the hour, not the content itself. My central problem is the ordering: I keep going back and fourth on whether to lead with the lottery paradox as a warm-up or hold it back as the contrast case. My worry with leading with it is that half the room will conclude it's just an artifact of pretending belief is all-or-nothing, that credences dissolve it, and then when I get to the preface they'll treat it as the same thing in a different costume and I'll have lost the room. So tell me which order to do it in and why, and what has to be established *before* either paradox can do any work — I'm guessing something like the idea that "believing" a claim and the claim's probability coming out below 1 aren't obviously the same relation, but you'd know better than me whether that's the real prerequisite or whether something else has to be in place first.

Then I want the one example that carries the whole session. I assume it's the author's preface itself — the writer who has checked each individual claim in her book and stands behind every one of them, yet is entirely confident the book contains at least one error she can't identify. If so, spell out what the example has to include to actually generate the tension (e.g. does it matter that her belief in the hidden error comes from base rates about books like hers rather than from finding a specific mistake?) and where the standard telling of it goes flat. Also the misconception map: I can already predict the engineers will try to cure it with "everything's just probabilities, so no contradiction," and someone will say the disclaimer is merely humility or publisher's convention rather than a belief the author holds. For each predictable reaction, tell me the point in the sequence where I should preempt it rather than argue with it after it's formed — my experience is that once an engineer has filed something under "sloppy wording," it stays filed. Relatedly, I want the session to end with them being able to say what makes the lottery case and the preface case pull in different directions (interchangeable statistically-grounded beliefs vs. individually supported ones? something about where the risk is located?), so give me the closing question that would test whether they can actually draw that line on a fresh case, not just nod along.

Last thing: be explicit about what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing it — I'm assuming the formal literature on closure principles and rational non-closure is out, but if there's some other trap (people trying to relitigate whether the paradox is "real," or dragging in Dutch books) tell me how to fence it off in one sentence and move on.
