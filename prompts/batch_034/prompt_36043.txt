In history we just got to the Treaty of Versailles, and something keeps bugging me about the famous war guilt clause. The treaty declared Germany responsible for the whole war and used that to justify making it pay reperations for decades afterward. So an entire country — including people who voted against the government, and even people who weren't born yet — gets billed for a decision that a handful of leaders made, or maybe that nobody really made at all, if you think the war just slid into existence through alliances and mobilization schedules. What view of responsibility does that practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if you treat a nation as a single agent that can want things and be blamed like a person can, or could you rebuild it as blaming just the decision-makers, with ordinary citizens as collateral that happened to guarantee the debt? And here's what confuses me: normal legal systems refuse to punish people for things they had no control over, and after WWII the same countries that wrote Versailles made a big point of trying individual leaders instead of blaming whole peoples. So did the Versailles approach presuppose some theory of collective responsibility that we later quietly dropped, or was it never a real theory in the first place, just an excuse to collect money? Also, if it turned out Germany's leaders did plan the war on purpose (I've heard some historians argue this), would that even change the treaty's logic, since the treaty blamed the nation and not just the planners?
