I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and for the past four years I've run a monthly discussion evening — call it a salon — for about fifteen people: roughly half are forum old-timers who can recite the standard philosophical snippets the way we all once recited the sequences, and the rest are newer members with no formal philosophy but plenty of opinions. Next month's topic is the Euthyphro dilemma, and my problem is precisely that most of the room thinks it is a solved gotcha. In forum culture the dilemma functions as a one-move refutation of divine command theory: either the gods love the pious because it is pious, in which case piety is independent of them and they are redundant for ethics, or it is pious because they love it, in which case morality is arbitrary and the gods could have made cruelty holy. The old-timers have deployed this a hundred times and consider it closed. I don't want to reopen it by cheerleading for theism — I'm an atheist myself — but I've come to suspect the crowd that treats the third reply (God's nature simply is the good, so the horns never fork) as a transparent dodge has never actually worked out why the people who offer it think it works, and that something structurally identical to the dilemma resurfaces the moment you swap in the authorities our crowd does accept: evolved preferences, reflective equilibrium, coherent extrapolated volition, the idealised adviser. If the good is whatever my idealised self endorses, one wants to ask whether it is good because that self endorses it, or endorsed because it is good — and I've watched good discussers go quiet at that point. That, not the theology, is what I want the evening to deliver people to.

So: don't explain the dilemma to me; plan how I should explain it. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no slides. I want your recommended order of components and your reasons for the dependency structure — what has to be established before the generalisation to secular authorities can land without seeming like a cheap trick. I want a single carrying example to hold the whole evening, and a definite verdict from you on whether it should be the original piety material from Plato's dialogue or a modern substitute you devise — decide, and defend the choice against the obvious rival; note in particular what the carrying example must have and what it must lack (my instinct is it must not be God, at least not at first, or half the room checks out and the other half starts reciting). I want a misconception map: the errors this specific audience will predictably bring or form, in the order they will surface — I'd expect at least the misconception that the dilemma presupposes God exists, the one where the third horn is dismissed as wordplay before it is understood, and the confident slide from "the dilemma refutes divine command theory" to "metaethics is settled" — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be pre-empted rather than left to fester until the closing discussion. I also want you to tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level and to say so to the room: I'm inclined to skip the scholarly dating of the dialogue and most of divine simplicity, but overrule me if you think one of those is load-bearing.

Two further requirements. First, take positions: commit to a definite verdict on whether the third horn succeeds on its own terms, and on whether generalising the dilemma to idealised-adviser theories is a fair application or a pun, and let the plan reflect those verdicts rather than presenting options for me to choose between. I would rather get a plan I disagree with than a menu. Second, since we are leaning on Plato, mark clearly any place where the plan rests on a contested reading of the dialogue or on a tendentious reconstruction of the third horn, so I know where I'm on firm ground and where I'm carrying an interpretation. And a check for understanding: give me one question I can put to the room at the end whose answer would show they have grasped the structure rather than merely enjoyed the debate.

Please write the whole answer in British English.
