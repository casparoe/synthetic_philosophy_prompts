You are a senior scholar working at the intersection of philosophy of history and science and technology studies, with long experience designing seminar simulations that put abstract debates into participants' hands. I am a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation on historical explanation, and I've been asked to run one session of an undergraduate course called 'Contingency and Convergence in History' — twelve students, ninety minutes, no prior exposure to the formal literature. The session's target is the debate over path dependence: whether outcomes in history get 'locked in' through early, often accidental, choices and self-reinforcing dynamics, or whether roughly the same outcomes would eventually have arrived anyway, so that the early choices only shuffled the order of arrival. I want them to hit this question experientially, not lecture-first.

Design a runnable in-class game in which lock-in happens to the participants rather than being described to them. Sketch something like the following shape, but feel free to improve on it: students are sorted into small groups that must successively adopt one of two or three competing 'standards' — you could use something as low-tech as different paper-folding conventions, coordinate systems for drawing a shared map, or protocols for exchanging hand-written messages — where each round's payoff to a group depends partly on its own choice and partly on how many other groups have already committed to each option, so that early coordination snowballs. Somewhere in the middle of the game, quietly introduce a rule change or new information that reveals the standard the class has converged on is worse on some independent measure than a rival that never caught on. The debrief should then make them reconstruct the game's history and argue about the counterfactual: had the class run the same game again, or had one early group chosen differently, would they still have ended up where they did? I want the debrief to force a real clash between the two live positions — the 'locked-in' reading on which the early accidents were decisive, and the convergence reading on which the class would have arrived at a workable standard regardless — and to map specific moments of the game onto the theoretical distinction, including whatever your design does to make switching costs or network effects salient.

Please begin with a few minutes of brainstorming before settling on your design, and think carefully about the mechanics rather than reaching for the first analogy. Give me the full package: materials, group structure, round-by-round rules, the payoff scheme (no real money), the planted 'revealed inferiority' moment and its timing, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief script. Also tell me honestly and concretely what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g., how to keep students from coordinating instantly out of social politeness, or what to do if one group sandbaggingly exploits the payoff structure — and be frank about which features of the actual historical debate the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply verbally, such as the fact that real historical actors lack the clean common knowledge of payoffs that a classroom game hands participants. Do not assume the students know any of the vocabulary in advance; no lecture up front, and the technical terms should only enter at the debrief.
