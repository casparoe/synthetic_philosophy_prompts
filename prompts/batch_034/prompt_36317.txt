I am preparing a seminar unit on Good's theorem on the value of information: the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, freely observing the cell of a partition before choosing can never make her worse off in expectation than choosing without the observation, since she can always disregard what she learns. The result is often summarized as "rational agents never pay to avoid information," and I want a thought experiment for the seminar whose punchline is precisely the opposite: a case in which free, reliable, perfectly private information, received before a decision, strictly lowers the agent's expected utility. Since such a case cannot contradict the theorem outright, it must earn its verdict from its own structure rather than stipulate it — the failure of the theorem must be traceable to one of its premises, identified and named.

The scenario must satisfy these specifications:

1. The agent is fully rational: her expected-utility preferences over outcomes are fixed before and after the information arrives, she updates by conditionalization, and she cannot be supposed to misremember, miscalculate, or act against her own better judgment.
2. The information is costless and perfectly reliable, arrives before she acts, and no third party can observe either its content or the fact of her receiving it — so the harm cannot come from signaling, leakage, or strategic disclosure.
3. The setting is ordinary life — a household, a workplace, a hospital, a team, a market for insurance — with no perfect predictors, demons, or exotic physics, and the whole scenario must fit in one paragraph.
4. The harm is strict and ex ante: the agent's expected utility given that she will receive the information is lower than her expected utility given that she will not, and this is to be shown by comparing the two decision situations explicitly.

Please deliver the following. First, the scenario itself. Second, the two decision analyses — states, prior, partition, acts, and expected utilities with and without the observation — with enough numerical structure that the loss is computed rather than asserted. Third, an explicit diagnosis of which premise of Good's theorem your scenario violates. Candidates worth taking seriously: that utility attaches to the consequences of acts rather than to the agent's posterior beliefs; that information is something the agent chooses whether to collect rather than something that befalls her; that the agent can commit to ignoring or forgetting; that observation precedes a single decision node rather than interacting with earlier commitments or randomizations. Argue that the premise you relax is the weakest one whose relaxation still leaves the agent recognizably rational. Fourth, the strongest reply available to a defender of the theorem, and whether it dissolves your case or merely redefines it. Fifth, an account of which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict when that feature is removed.

Two further requirements. I want at least one candidate scenario that appears to work but does not — for instance, a case that smuggles in game-theoretic leakage through the back door, or one where the harm evaporates once you notice that a rational agent would simply disregard what she has learned — along with a diagnosis of exactly where and why it fails. And if you judge that no scenario satisfying all four specifications exists, say so, prove it as directly as you can from the theorem's premises, and then relax the single weakest specification and proceed under the relaxation, flagging the change clearly. The purpose of the thought experiment is pedagogical: to show seminar students not that Good's theorem is wrong, but why each of its premises is load-bearing, and what the world has to look like for ignorance to be worth something even to a perfectly rational agent.
