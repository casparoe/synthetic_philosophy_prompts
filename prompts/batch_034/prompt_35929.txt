In discussions of the bootstrapping problem and what the literature calls easy knowledge, people in my circles keep reaching for the same analogy: checking the reliability of your senses by using your senses is like checking the accuracy of a ruler by measuring the ruler with itself — a textbook circle, they say, that cannot confer any justification at all. The analogy gets used to support two distinct conclusions at once: that the bootstrapper who reasons from "this looks like a zebra" to "my vision is working" to "so it really is a zebra" has gained nothing, and, pushed further, that since we can never step outside our faculties to check them, all trust in perception ultimately rests on a circle, leaving at best a pragmatic leap rather than justification. Audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the ruler, its markings, the act of measuring, and the measurer — then say where the mapping breaks and which disanalogies matter for which conclusion; in particular, the bootstrapper generates fresh instances as evidence, unlike someone re-measuring the same ruler, and I'd like to know whether that difference is what makes this a puzzle about "easy" knowledge rather than a nonstarter about plain circularity. Also flag what the analogy smuggles in — above all the assumption that an independent standard is available to the ruler-checker in a way it never is for the sense-checker — and say whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. As you go, note how confident you are in each verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
