After a career in experimental physics, I have taken up philosophy of probability in retirement, and over the past year, in a reading group with some former colleagues, I have settled on a view about the reference class problem in anthropic reasoning — the question of which population I should count myself as a random sample from when applying Bayesian reasoning to evidence indexed by my own existence. My view is that the reference class should be maximal: it should include every observer, or perhaps every observer-moment, capable of registering the evidence in question, on the ground that any narrower class requires selecting a similarity metric between myself and others — species membership, psychological sophistication, technological era — and that this selection is as arbitrary as choosing a preferred reference frame in physics. Trimming the class after the fact, so as to make an argument come out one way rather than another, strikes me as the epistemic equivalent of choosing a coordinate system in order to manufacture a result. On my view, the reference class is not something there is a fact of the matter about; it is a convention we are required, by a symmetry principle, to fix in the least committal way available — much as gauge freedom is fixed by demanding invariance rather than by appeal to some further structure.

I would like to know what would count against this maximalist position. Please sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, distinguishing those that would be fatal from those that are merely awkward: consequences the view is committed to that turn out unacceptable, results it cannot reproduce, cases it handles worse than narrower-class rivals, and any route by which it might collapse into a competitor — for instance, if maximal breadth simply reproduces the self-indication assumption, or renders all anthropic evidence trivially self-cancelling. I would like you to say, for each defeater, whether a committed maximalist would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as an artifact of intuitions that anthropic reasoning is meant to correct rather than obey, and whether the qualifications defenders offer have gradually insulated the position from any possible test — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or just what happens when a question turns out to be conventional. The cases I am most interested in are the Doomsday Argument and the presumptuous philosopher scenario. I am also uneasy about one thing: whether my analogy to coordinate choice and gauge fixing is doing real work in the argument or merely decorating a position that can be stated, and stands or falls, without it. If the analogy collapses under scrutiny, I want to know whether the maximalism survives on independent grounds or falls along with it. Throughout your answer, please indicate how confident you are in each claim, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
