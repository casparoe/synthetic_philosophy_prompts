The scenario is a version of the absent-minded driver. A driver travels at night down a highway with exactly two exits. The exits are indistinguishable from the road, and the driver is absent-minded in the strict sense: at an exit he cannot tell which one it is, and after passing one he will not remember having passed it. He knows all of this about himself. Once past an exit he cannot return. Payoffs, on the group's agreed scale: taking the first exit is worth 1; taking the second is worth 7; driving past both and ending up at the end of the highway is worth 2. He can generate any probability he likes (a random-number app), so randomized policies are implementable.

What both accepted before the falling-out: the expected payoff of the policy "at any exit, exit with probability p" is p·1 + (1−p)p·7 + (1−p)²·2, maximized at p = 2/5 with value 2.8; always exiting yields 1 and always continuing yields 2; and since the exits are indistinguishable, any policy the driver can actually follow must prescribe the same behavior at both.

The exchange:

(1) Marco: So it's settled. The optimal policy is: at whichever exit you are, exit with probability 2/5. The rational driver at an exit opens the app, draws a number, and exits iff it's below 0.4. That isn't indecision — it's the best plan, and the only executable rivals (exit always: 1; continue always: 2) are worse. Randomization isn't a vice of the plan; it is the plan.

(2) Petra: The arithmetic is fine, but you answered a different question. You answered "which policy is best, considered from the start?" The driver isn't at the start; he's at an exit, choosing between two acts. Exit now is worth 1 if this is the first town and 7 if it's the second. Continuing is worth — well, that's the question. But whatever the numbers, an agent who can rank two acts takes the better one: by linearity, mixing two acts of unequal expected value is strictly worse than the better act. So randomization is permissible only as a tie-breaker, and your 2/5 policy is justified at the wheel only if, at the wheel, the two acts tie. To even state the tie you need his probability that this is the first town — and you fixed that by consulting the policy itself. That's backwards: the policy is the thing up for evaluation, and his evidence at the exit is just "I am at one of two indistinguishable exits."

(3) Marco: Two replies. First, honor your dominance complaint concretely: name the pure act. "Continue at the first town, exit at the second" scores 7 but is unexecutable — a policy is a function from information to action, and his information is identical at the two exits. So the executable policies are the constant ones and the mixed ones, and I optimized over all of them. Second, the beliefs aren't backwards. What he'll do determines which towns he visits: under "exit always" he only ever sees the first town, so standing at an exit he could be sure it's the first; under "continue always" he sees both, and by symmetry it's 50/50. His evidence therefore includes his own policy, and under my policy the right weights are 5/8 for the first town (one guaranteed visit) and 3/8 for the second (an expected 3/5 of a visit). With those weights: exiting at the wheel is worth (5/8)·1 + (3/8)·7 = 3.25. Continuing is worth (5/8)·[what my policy then yields at the next exit: 0.4·7 + 0.6·2 = 4] plus (3/8)·2 [if this was already the second town, driving to the end] — also 3.25. The acts tie exactly at the wheel. The die isn't choosing between ranked acts; it's breaking a genuine tie. The plan is stable from inside.

(4) Petra: Look at what you did in the middle of that calculation. To score continuing past the first town, you credited the driver with "what my policy yields at the next exit": 4. But when he arrives at that next exit he will not know it is the second town — he'll be in exactly the situation he's in now. If the policy is to be justified from inside, which was my whole demand, the value of continuing must be the value of that situation, call it V — not the terminal payoff of a randomization he'd execute while somehow knowing where he is. Score it honestly: continuing is worth (5/8)·V + (3/8)·2, while exiting is worth 3.25. Unless V is well below 3.25, continuing loses at the wheel, and your driver would scrap the policy rather than roll the die. Solve for the V at which the acts tie and you'll find the stable policy has to exit more often than 2/5. I suspect it pays less than 2.8 — possibly no more than the 2 he'd get by never exiting at all — but we ran out of meeting before I finished the algebra.

(5) Marco: Then finish it. You keep announcing suspicions. If there's an instability in my plan, exhibit it: give me the numbers under your scoring. Otherwise the only complete calculation on the table is mine, and it says the plan is exactly as advisable from the wheel as from the driveway.
