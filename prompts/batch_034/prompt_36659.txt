I run a philosophy discussion night at a local bookstore (mixed crowd: a couple of grad students, some curious retirees, one guy who only comes for the snacks) and next month's topic is which animals are conscious — not the hard problem itself, but the distribution question: does it stop at mammals? Birds? Octopuses? Crabs? Insects? Roundworms? To keep it from turning into everyone just going "well, dogs obviously," I want to hand out a decision tree that starts from a commitment people already hold and walks them outward to a definite position on the distribution.

Please act as a philosopher of mind who also designs surveys, and think carefully before writing — brainstorm the diagnostic questions first, then build the tree from those, not the other way around. Every branch node should be a question someone can answer by thinking about concrete cases rather than by knowing the jargon: stuff like whether an alien or a sufficiently sophisticated robot that behaved conscious would thereby raise the question of its consciousness, whether an animal that can learn flexibly but is plausibly "just" running a gating mechanism (as in the Birch-style picture where sentience is what makes attention and learning ungated) thereby counts, whether self-recognition in a mirror or pain-behavior after analgesics tells us anything, whether a carageen-style view that consciousness requires higher-order representations can survive the possibility that infants feel pain, and so on. No node should just ask "do you believe functionalism is true?" — the point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

The leaves should include at least: liberal distribution views that extend consciousness to insects (the Barron-and-Klein sort of move), the graded gating/census view that places the edge of sentience somewhere around crustaceans and cephalopods, panpsychism, higher-order-thought skepticism that restricts consciousness to creatures with metacognition, and precautionary-principle agnosticism that says we should act as if animals are conscious while refusing a verdict. Also mark any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate (e.g., some of the liberal and panpsychist branches), and flag any combination of answers that lands nowhere — say whether nobody holds it because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because it's unexplored. Aim for around eight to ten branch nodes, and keep the language of the handout accessible enough that the snack guy can follow it. One more thing: end the tree with a note on where the tree itself sneaks in assumptions, so the group can turn on the instrument and not just each other.
