I spent thirty-odd years doing population genetics before retiring, and lately I've been trying to catch up on the philosophy literature around the levels of selection question, partly because I lived through the fights in the 70s and 80s and thought they were over. Back then the Dawkins view won the day among the people I worked with: selection "sees" only heritable variance in fitness, genes are the only things with the right kind of fidelity across generations, so talk of organism-level or group-level selection is just a bookkeeping convention, a redescription of what's ultimately gene-frequency change. Then I read that Sober and Wilson revived group selection in the late 90s, that multilevel selection models are now fairly mainstream, and that people claim kin selection and group selection formalisms can be shown to be mathematically equivalent -- different partitions of the same selection covariance, same predictions. And yet the argument is STILL going, fifty years on, with the same tone of exasperation on all sides. Which puzzles me, because in my day if two formalisms made identical predictions we'd shrug and pick whichever was computationally convenient.

So here's what I want diagnosed, not adjudicated. Given that the formal machinery (Price equation, contextual analysis, whatever you like) seems to let everyone translate everyone else's results into their own preferred currency, what exactly are the remaining parties disagreeing about? My working hypothesis, as a newcomer to the philosophy of it, is that it's not about the biology at all but about what an explanation of adaptation is supposed to do -- one side wants the account of where the cumulative benefit lands (the gene's "interests"), the other wants an account of the causal structure of selection episodes, and those are just different questions wearing one label. But a colleague of mine insists the residue is real and metaphysical: whether genes are literally interactors or just bookkeepers is supposed to be a fact about the world, not about our prose. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "unit," a substantive dispute about causation that the equivalence results don't touch, or maybe a pedagogical/political fight about what we should teach students that has outlived its scientific content? And is there anything -- a class of cases the formalisms don't handle symmetrically, a distinction somebody hasn't drawn yet, or just a change of vocabulary -- that would actually settle it, or should I expect my grandchildren's textbooks to still be arguing about this?
