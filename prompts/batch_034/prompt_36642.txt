I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar — 14 students, one 80-minute session — on the special composition question: under what conditions do some things compose one further thing? Design an in-class game in which students staff the customs checkpoint of a small fictional republic where import duty is levied per object: whatever crosses the border as a single thing pays one fee, and officers are personally fined whenever an appeals court overturns one of their rulings.

Smugglers (role-played by a rotating subset of the students, with their own payoff scheme) arrive with trays of carefully engineered arrangements: two bricks resting in contact, three planks nailed at a single corner, a mobile held together by one drop of glue, seven pebbles arranged in a perfect circle, a live fish swimming in water scooped from the far bank. The officers must rule on each shipment, but the game only ends when they publish a customs code: a general, non-circular criterion that decides every past and future case. The smugglers' firm prospers precisely by probing whatever vagueness or circularity the code contains — so the students should feel the pressure toward either "everything composed of nothing in contact counts" or "nothing counts" as the only codes that survive litigation.

Please provide the full rules, the materials list, a payoff scheme requiring no real money, a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the main candidate answers — universalism, nihilism, and the life-based restrictionism — with students constructing the positions themselves rather than hearing them named first. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about the one feature of the debate the game cannot reproduce: that the universalist insists there was never a real decision to make at the border, since composition is not a matter of policy at all. Suggest how I should supply that point, and to whom it will feel like cheating.
