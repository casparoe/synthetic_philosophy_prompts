I run a philosophy-of-science discussion group that publishes a monthly newsletter, and for the next issue I want an interactive feature: a self-administered quiz that sorts readers by their actual inductive practice rather than by the school of thought they would claim allegiance to. The topic is the problem of induction, with Goodman's new riddle as the live wire, but the quiz must be answerable by someone who has read Hume and never heard of Goodman: every question presents a concrete case, and the answer options must be phrased as plain first-person reactions (I'd expect this, I wouldn't take either side of that bet), never using terms like projectible, entrenchment, prior, natural kind, or corroborated. No question may simply ask whether the reader believes in induction.

Please produce the following:

1. Eight to ten questions. Each presents a scenario and offers two or three options. At least one question must include, among its options, the option that the evidence underdetermines the answer — I want to catch readers whose real view is that observation never settles extrapolation — but that option has to appear where a case makes it tempting, not as a standing escape hatch attached to every question.

2. The following cases must appear somewhere among the questions, though you may recast them:
   - The grue case, stated with a concrete future cutoff and with the predicate defined inside the question itself, so that a reader who has never encountered Goodman faces it cold.
   - A wager case that separates readers who will act on their extrapolations from readers who will not act at all, and thereby prices the skeptic's position — for instance, whether to accept a bet whose payout turns on the next observation.
   - A case built around a predicate defined by an arbitrary external event — say, a machine counts as "dependable" only if it worked on every day before the day some unrelated lottery is drawn — where the reader must say whether its record of past successes supports anything. This should probe whether their projectibility judgments track how entrenched a word is in their community or how unified the underlying property is, without letting them see which label each answer earns.
   - A case involving something with no linguistic history at all — the first samples of a synthetic compound, or an instrument recovered from a wreck with no surviving manual — so that entrenchment has nothing to grip. Readers must say whether uniformity expectations apply to a predicate nobody has ever used.
   - A retroactive case: a generalization that held for centuries and then broke, something like jade turning out to be two distinct minerals that happen to share a name. Ask the reader what they now think of the people who extrapolated, and whether their mistake lay in their method or merely in their luck.

3. A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves must include, at minimum: the Humean skeptic; the Popperian, who holds that no observation ever confirms anything; the subjective Bayesian, for whom any coherent assignment of expectations is as rational as any other; the Goodmanian conventionalist, for whom what projects is fixed by entrenchment in linguistic practice; a natural-necessity or natural-kind view, on which induction succeeds because many predicates track real joints in nature; and the Reichenbachian pragmatist, who licenses induction not by showing it reliable but by showing that if any method would work, this one would. Name each leaf in plain terms in the key — a reader should learn their label only at the end — and attach to each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. A note flagging any pair of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate. If nothing in the cases distinguishes the subjective Bayesian from the Goodmanian conventionalist, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a question whose options covertly name the schools. Where you do think a question splits a pair, identify for at least two questions which leaf pair it was built to divide and why the more obvious candidate questions fail to divide them.

5. Guidance for readers whose answers fit no leaf. The key should say what to make of the common residual pattern — answers that presuppose uniformity in the wager cases while denying it in the grue case — rather than forcing every reader into a box.

The quiz should take an attentive reader about ten minutes. Keep the cases vivid and specific, with dates, quantities, and stakes, since the whole point is that readers are sorted by what they do with cases, not by which position they find flattering.
