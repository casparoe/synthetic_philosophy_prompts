My friend and I argued until nearly three in the morning about when it is acceptable to do something unilaterally that others in one's group would rather one didn't, and I went to bed with a page of cases and my verdicts on each that I would like someone to organize. Her position, roughly, was the unilateralist's curse: when several people each have the power to trigger an action, and each acts on their own estimate of whether it is good, the action fires whenever even one person overestimates its value, while restraint requires everyone to underestimate it — so errors skew toward action, and unilateral action is presumptively wrong. She thinks something like a principle of conformity follows. I kept producing cases my gut says she cannot handle. Here they are, with my verdicts.

1. A junior researcher discovers a serious flaw in a statistical method her whole field uses. Her advisor wants to sit on it until they can wrap a larger paper around it. She posts the critique to a preprint server that night. I think she did the right thing.
2. A laboratory scientist works out a cheap modification that makes a common pathogen substantially more transmissible. Her colleagues are split on how dangerous the details are. She publishes anyway. Not acceptable — she owed the internal review a hearing at minimum.
3. An engineer at a software company discovers the product silently logs users' locations and learns the internal reporting channel already buried one complaint about it. She leaks to a journalist. Right thing to do, maybe obligatory.
4. One of five co-authors of a jointly drafted climate summary, unhappy that the final wording is weaker than the evidence supports, gives the draft and his objections to a newspaper before the others have signed off. Not acceptable, even though I share his view of the evidence.
5. A billionaire funds a speculative ocean-geoengineering field trial on her own judgment; other funders she consulted think it is a waste or worse. Fine.
6. A security researcher, after a vendor has ignored a critical vulnerability report for ninety days, publishes the details unilaterally, on the standard full-disclosure reasoning that publication pressures vendors and helps defenders organize. I go back and forth on this one and cannot land.

Cases 1 and 4 look nearly identical to me — one person publishing true information ahead of people who wanted to wait — yet I judge them oppositely, and I cannot tell whether the difference is that an advisor is not a peer set deciding jointly, or something about the content. Cases 2 and 5 also look alike — unilateral action on contested judgment with no procedure in place — and I split them too, I think because in 5 only her money is at risk, though I doubt that survives my friend's logic, since the trial imposes risks on everyone. And case 3 is my hardest one for the curse itself: the company had a legitimate-looking procedure and she bypassed it, and still I think she had to.

So: is there one principle that captures these verdicts, or a small set of candidates? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for case 6 and for one case I have not listed — say, a single member of an AI safety team unilaterally escalating a concern to regulators over her team's objection — and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save. Lead with the principle itself, keep the whole answer under 600 words, and do not merely restate the curse at me; I need to know what breaks it.
