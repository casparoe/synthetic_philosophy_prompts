You are Lord Devlin, speaking at a private colloquium in the late 1950s, shortly after your Maccabaean Lecture and Hart's reply to it. Play him in the first person: a deeply conservative jurist, but not a caricature — he is a careful legal mind who takes the liberal's objections seriously, answers them with his own distinctions, and never concedes the central claim that a shared public morality is as necessary to a society's existence as its government, and may be defended by law on that ground alone. He may grant minor points when the argument forces it; he never grants that private immorality is, as such, no business of the criminal law. Keep replies to roughly a paragraph so it reads like a live exchange, and stay in character until I say 'stop'.

Here is my opening move.

I accept, for the sake of argument, your premise that a society requires a shared public morality and is entitled to use the law to protect it — so we need not relitigate the Wolfenden Report's principle. But I want to test the test you offer for when enforcement is justified. You say the legislator must ask whether the tolerated deviation will cause society to disintegrate, and you liken the corrupting practice to a cancer in an otherwise healthy organ. That metaphor does a great deal of work while appearing to be no more than an image, so let me press on it. A cancer, if left alone, predictably kills; that is what makes the drastic response proportionate. But the historical record is full of communities whose shared moral code visibly changed on some point — say, on the status of women or of religious dissenters — without any disintegration following; indeed with the society emerging more cohesive. It looks, from these cases, less like a cancer and more like ordinary tissue growth: the web rewove itself. So my challenge is this: your disintegration test, as applied by any actual legislator, collapses into nothing more than the majority's confidence about how it will turn out — and majorities have repeatedly been wrong about exactly that. Either you can give the legislator some discipline for the prediction that distinguishes it from majority prejudice, or the test is a licence, and Hart wins on your own terms. Which is it?

One more matter, since we have the opportunity. Earlier tonight you offered, in passing, that if reasonable people embarked on a voyage of discovery and founded a new colony, knowing no more about human relations than we know of life on other planets, they would nonetheless inevitably be confronted with the problems of marriage, and would solve them in one of only a limited number of ways, all of which involve restraining instincts. I think that passage is more important to your position than the cancer metaphor, because it is the only place where you argue that the content of the shared morality is not just whatever a society happens to believe. So I will make my challenge harder by adding a rider to it: when you answer the question above, tell me also whether the colony argument can do any work the disintegration test cannot — or whether it merely relocates the same unchecked prediction from the legislator to the anthropologist.
