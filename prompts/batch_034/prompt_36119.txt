I'm writing an essay on Oscar Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest as a comedy that runs on speech act theory, some sixty years before Austin. Read the play through the notion of performative utterances and their felicity conditions: the plot turns on acts that are supposed to make facts — christenings that would confer the name Ernest, proposals and acceptances that create engagements, the wedding that must be deferred until all impediments are cleared — and on acts that misfire because the conditions aren't met: Cecily's engagement, recorded in her diary, to a proposal that was never made and to a person who doesn't exist; Gwendolen's devotion aimed at the name rather than the man; Jack's plan to be re-christened as a remedy for a purely social fact. Where does the play genuinely dramatize the distinction between utterances that do things and utterances that report things, and where does it trade on a cruder idea, that names and social facts can simply be conjured by declaration? And does the ending, in which the name Ernest turns out to have been Jack's real name all along, vindicate the thought that performatives create facts — or quietly undermine it, since the marriage can only be happy because the fact existed independently? I'd like a reading that takes both the play and Austin's categories seriously, says where the comedy resists the framework, and ends with what Wilde adds to the philosophy that Austin's own examples (the betting, the naming of ships, the marrying) don't.
