So, context: I'm a novelist, five books in, and my latest got picked up by two separate book clubs and a grad seminar, and each one found a completely different "real meaning" in it, none of which is what I meant, and one of which is honestly better. I've been lying awake about it. What I want from you is the thing I can't write myself: the appendix I wish I could staple into the paperback.

Write me an "Author's Note on Interpretation" — about 600–750 words, deadpan, in the voice of a novelist who has decided to settle the whole question once and for all by fixing her book's meaning by fiat, the way you'd fix a typo. Think of it as an interpretive terms-of-service, but warm, the kind of note that starts "Dear Reader" and ends with something like gratitude. She is a diehard intentionalist — meaning comes from what the author meant, full stop — and she has to stay one to the very last line. No winking at the reader, no moment where she concedes anything to the critics. The irony has to come from the actual philosophical trouble she walks into, not from her being dumb.

Specifically, I want her to hit at least these, each without noticing it's a problem:

- The question of WHICH intention. She drafted the book in one marriage, revised it during a divorce, and did the publicity in a third voice that contradicts both. At some point she has to pick which of her selves is "the author" for interpretation purposes, and the way she does it should quietly show she's constructing that author rather than finding one — she just shouldn't clock that that's what she's doing.
- A theme she denies that the text plainly supports. Her remedy should be an "errata" policy: passages where the book means something she didn't put there get flagged as mistakes of the medium, with maybe a refund/exchange scheme for readers who were moved by something she disavows.
- The problem that language doesn't obey her. She should try to rule that the rain in chapter nine is grief, and then explain, patiently, that obviously she can also rule that "grief" means something slightly different in this book than in ordinary English — and she should trip right over the fact that at some point words just mean what the language means, without ever seeing the trip.
- A named critic character who argues the question isn't what she actually intended but what an ideally informed version of her would have intended. She should be personally offended at being replaced by a better drafts of herself, and her rebuttal should be a bit moving and completely beside the point.

Also — and this matters to me — no name-dropping. I don't want the famous "death of the author" essay anywhere near it, that's the most regurgitated bit going and everyone reaches for it the second this topic comes up. Same for the big names in this debate. She can gesture at having heard of the argument secondhand, maybe from her cousin who did a semester of critical theory, but no lecture. I want someone who knows the actual literature on interpretation (the arguments about whether authorial intention fixes meaning, whether readers can be wrong, what happens when the author is wrong about her own book) to laugh because the piece is fair to every side — the anti-intentionalists should feel their conclusion being built out of her own materials, and intentionalists should feel the temptation of her position even as it collapses. Someone with no philosophy background should just find it a funny, slightly sad author's note.

End it with her oblivious and doubling down, ideally with a line that accidentally concedes the whole game without her hearing it.

Keep it straight-faced and readable. I'm trusting you not to make her a strawman — she's me, basicaly, if I were slightly more litigious.
