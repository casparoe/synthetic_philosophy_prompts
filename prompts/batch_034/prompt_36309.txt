I'm taking a philosophy of history course, and our seminar has split over the old Great Man question in a way I want to settle with something more precise than the usual essay. The provocation we were given: would there have been something recognizably like Nazi Germany without Hitler? Half the room says the structural conditions — Versailles, the Depression, Weimar's legitimacy deficit, the depth of völkisch antisemitism, the elites who wanted an authoritarian solution anyway — made some version of it nearly inevitable, so removing Hitler just swaps in a different demagogue and the broad arc survives. The other half says the specific combination of genocidal antisemitism, a war of continental conquest, and the machinery of industrial killing was a radically contingent assemblage held together by one man's peculiar coalition-building, and that without him Germany gets, at most, a nastier version of a Horthy or a Pinochet.

Here's what I want from you, and please be disciplined about the format: state your overall credence in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my setup.

But I don't want a bare number, because I've come to suspect the bare number is meaningless until the proposition is carved up. So decompose it into at least these terms, assigning each a probability and a one-line justification, and tell me how you're combining them:

1. P(some authoritarian, antisemitic, revanchist nationalist regime rules Germany by the mid-1930s, conditional on Hitler dying, say, in the trenches in 1918).
2. P(that regime, or its successor, initiates a general European war by the early 1940s).
3. P(that regime carries out a deliberate program of extermination that kills Jews (and other targeted groups) on the order of millions, as opposed to persecution, forced emigration, or pogrom-scale violence).
4. P(that the question, as I've framed it, is even well-posed — that is, that "the same event" across counterfactual worlds picks out something determinate, given that the closest worlds where Hitler dies will presumably differ from ours in hundreds of downstream respects).

On that last term, I've been reading about closest-possible-worlds treatments of causal claims, and it seems to me that the seminar's two halves may not actually disagree about the history at all — they may just be using different similarity metrics, one that holds the structural background fixed and varies the leadership, and one that lets everything downstream of the individual vary. If you agree that's a live possibility, say so and fold it in; if you think it's a way of dodging, say that too.

Then tell me: which term is the overall credence most sensitive to, and what kind of evidence or argument would move it most — base rates from other countries that had the structural conditions without the man, something about the internal politics of the Nazi coalition, something about the decision points in 1933, or something I haven't listed? Finally, if my professor is at 0.8 that the broad arc survives Hitler's removal and my study partner is at 0.3, tell me where you'd bet the actual disagreement lives — in term 1, 2, 3, 4, or in the similarity metric itself — and what question I could put to each of them that would reveal it.
