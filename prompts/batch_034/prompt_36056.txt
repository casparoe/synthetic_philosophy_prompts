My flatmate, who's writing his dissertation on dispositions, has an argument he thinks is a knockout, but to me it seems to prove way too much. (1) On the standard conditional analysis, to say a glass is fragile is just to say that if it were struck, it would break. (2) But wrap the glass in packing foam and strike it: it doesn't break, and it's still fragile — the foam doesn't cure the glass of it's fragility, it just stops the fragility from getting out. So a genuinely fragile thing can fail the conditional. (3) And if foam can do that, so can anything else that stands between the trigger and the breaking: there's no conditional of the form 'if struck, breaks' that fragility by itself ever guarantees. (4) Now generalize: for any disposition you can always imagine some obstacle of this kind, so no disposition ever guarantees any 'if S then M' conditional. (5) So a fragile glass can be fragile while there's literally no possible situation in which its being fragile makes any difference to whether it breaks — at which point fragility has nothing to do with breaking, and dispositions are inert labels we stick on things. He says the moral is that dispositions are categorical intrinsic properties and the whole if-then story was a mistake from the start. I feel like the cheat is somewhere between (3) and (5) but I can't name it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Also, he kept contrasting his foam case with the 'electro-fink' setup, where the live wire is rigged to a device that cuts the power the moment you go to test it, so the wire is live but wouldn't conduct if you tested it — is the foam case really the same kind of thing as that one, or does the difference matter for his argument? For what it's worth, I'd much rather keep (1) than accept his conclusion.
