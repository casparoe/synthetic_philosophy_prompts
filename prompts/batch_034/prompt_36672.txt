I'm doing a PhD in economics, mostly social choice and decision theory, and out of curiosity I've been sitting in on a value theory seminar where we just spent six weeks on whether "better than" is transitive. In my world this isn't even a question — half the machinery we use (money pump arguments, representation theorems, basically all of welfare economics) quietly assumes that if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. But the philosophers in the seminar kept trotting out these spectrum arguments: line up outcomes so that each one is only *barely* different from its neighbor, where you genuinely can't see how anyone could prefer the neighbor, and then you zoom out and the two ends of the spectrum are things like a hundred years of the most horrible suffering versus one afternoon of a mild headache. If each local judgment is fine and the endpoints are obvious, something has to give, and one camp (the Temkin / Rachels style people) concludes that betterness just isn't transitive, full stop. The other camp, the Broome-ish side, thinks that's a contradiction in terms — that "better than" is a comparative like "longer than" and an intransitive betterness relation is as incoherent as an intransitive length relation — so the spectrum arguments must be smuggling in some mistaken principle about how to aggregate or compare lives.

Here's why I find the whole thing frustrating in a specific way. Every response to the spectrum cases seems to come from a fixed menu: deny that the "no noticeable difference" steps support strict preference; insist the intuitions about endpoints are the confused ones; or retreat to "betterness is transitive but what we're tracking in these cases is something else, like equivalence-or-betterness, or a non-comparative excellence." And this same menu has been getting ordered from since the nineties. Nobody in the seminar ever moved an inch, and from what I can tell reading around, nobody in the literature has either. So I'm not asking you to tell me who's right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — are the two sides secretly using "better than" for two different relations, one that has to be transitive because it's defined for the purposes of choice, and one that's some free-floating evaluative relation that can do whatever it wants? Or is it substantive, with the anti-transitivists making a real claim that our axiology has a certain shape and the other side making a real claim that it can't? Or is the real fight upstream — about whether an axiology even has to be something an agent could choose by?

My working hypothesis, for what it's worth, is that the economists are answering a question about rational choice and the Temkin people are answering a question about the world's evaluative structure, and "better than" is just one phrase being stretched over both jobs, which would explain why the spectrum arguments feel devastating to one side and like a cheap trick to the other. But I'm not confident, because the Broome people explicitly deny they're only talking about choice — they think the analogy with "longer than" is doing real metaphysical work. So: give me your diagnosis with actual reasons drawn from what each side says, tell me what kind of thing would even count as progress here (a formal result? a cleaner separation of relations? a decision about which question we should be asking?), and be honest if you think the impasse is just what you get when a question is genuinely hard rather than confused.
