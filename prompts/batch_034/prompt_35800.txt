I'm a high school student writing an essay on the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and I think I've found a fatal flaw in it. The interpretation says that when a quantum measurement has several possible outcomes, the universe branches and every outcome actually occurs somewhere. Since all branches are equally real, it seems to follow that all outcomes are equally probable: if a spin measurement can come out up or down, then up happens in one branch and down in another, so the odds should be 50/50 no matter what the wavefunction says. Yet experiments confirm the Born rule, which assigns probabilities from the squared amplitudes and can yield any values at all. The only escape I can see is to add a postulate saying that some branches count more than others — but that appears to give up the interpretation's chief selling point, namely that it needs no extra assumptions beyond the Schrödinger equation. Is weighting the branches by squared amplitude the standard fix physicists have adopted, and how do they justify that weighting rather than some other one? Please answer in American English.
