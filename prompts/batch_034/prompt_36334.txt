Two colleagues of mine at a policy institute have spent a year arguing past each other about democratic backsliding, and they've finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. One of them, a comparativist by temperament, holds that backsliding is a distinctive and analysable phenomenon: the incremental, largely legal-in-form degradation of democratic institutions by elected incumbents — court-packing, capture of electoral commissions and media regulators, harassment of civil society — usefully distinguished both from old-fashioned military coups and from mere bad governance, with Hungary after 2010 as her paradigm case. The other, a sceptic, holds that backsliding is less an analytic category than a post-Cold-War framing: it presupposes a liberal-democratic baseline that is itself historically recent and contested (few called American democracy consolidated in the 1950s, when a large share of Southern adults effectively could not vote), its case selection flags governments that Western capitals dislike, and the scholarly obsession with gradual incumbent-led decay has generated alarm more readily than insight. They have asked me to draft the joint statement for the collaboration, but I am out of my depth. Please draft it for me: (1) a numbered section of claims both could sign — covering what has in fact happened in Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States since 2010; what democracy indexes do and do not measure; and the distinction between a democracy becoming less liberal and one ceasing to be a democracy at all; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's residual position is stated in wording that side would itself accept — I suspect one genuine dispute is whether backsliding names a mechanism or merely a direction of travel, and another is whether the concept's partisan uptake is a defect of the concept itself or merely of its users; and (3) for each crux, a concrete observable case or future scenario both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — for instance, what each would expect to see if Poland's post-2023 governments fully reverse, or conspicuously fail to reverse, the changes made to the constitutional court and public media between 2015 and 2023. Please answer in British English.
