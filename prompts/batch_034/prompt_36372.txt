I sit on the conservation advisory committee for a mid-sized town, and I've been tasked with writing our recommendation on a proposal to drain and pave over an old retention pond behind a shopping plaza. The committee has split, and the argument has taken a turn I wasn't prepared for.

Our chair, a retired wetlands biologist, wants the pond preserved, and her case is what you'd expect: it's used by a resident heron, it supports breeding amphibians and dragonflies, and it's the only wet habitat for a half-mile in any direction. But another member — someone who reads a lot of animal-welfare material — made an argument I can't dismiss and also can't shake. He points out that the pond's dominant residents are frogs, and frogs are spectacular breeders: a single female lays thousands of eggs, and virtually all of the resulting tadpoles are eaten, starve, or get stranded as the pond dries down in late summer. Only a handful per generation make it to adulthood. So, he says, the pond is not a little community of animals living their frog lives; it's a machine for producing enormous numbers of short deaths, decorated with one photogenic heron. On his view, preserving the pond isn't protecting animals — it's protecting a system whose residents are, on balance, probably better off not existing at all, and the fact that we find it peaceful just means we only look at the survivors. The chair replied that this assumes you can do arithmetic over experiences — that you can sum up a tadpole's bad week and a heron's good season into some total, and she doubts anyone knows how to do that, or whether a tadpole's dying badly counts the way a dog's would. Meanwhile, the developer's engineer keeps pointing out the pond was dug by humans in the 1980s to catch parking-lot runoff, so "nature" isn't obviously on either side.

I have to write the recommendation, and I've realized I don't know how to think about the underlying question, which seems to be: is this small stretch of habitat, considered as a whole, a good thing for the creatures living in it, or a bad thing? Is there any principled way to approach that question rather than just picking whichever framing — the heron or the tadpoles — I find more vivid? Specifically: what would someone have to believe about animal minds, and about how to weigh a large number of short, probably bad lives against a small number of longer, probably good ones, for the drain-it case to actually win? And if the honest answer is that we can't do that arithmetic, what should a committee like ours do when the arithmetic is the whole disagreement?
