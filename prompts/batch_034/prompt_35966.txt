Write a joke about the aggregation problem in T.M. Scanlon's contractualism, for an audience that actually knows the debate. To be concrete about the target: Scanlon holds that an act is wrong when it is disallowed by principles of right conduct that no one could reasonably reject, and he reads that test as imposing what commentators call the individualist restriction — the strength of one person's complaint against a principle cannot be inflated by the fact that other people have the same complaint. This generates the notorious headache case: if a policy lets one person die in order to spare fifty million people each a mild headache, contractualism seems unable to say the policy is impermissible, because no single headache-sufferer has a complaint strong enough to outweigh the one death, and the complaints cannot simply be summed. The punchline should turn on this actual structural feature of the view — the refusal to add up burdens across lives, or the awkwardness of what that refusal then permits — rather than on generic philosopher stereotypes. A listener who knows the difference between Scanlonian contractualism and utilitarianism should find it genuinely funny, and someone who has never heard of the individualist restriction should still be able to follow what happened. Give me one joke, not a list of candidates: either a short dialogue or a setup and punchline, whichever serves the material better, plus at most two sentences of framing before it if the framing earns its place. Please use continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
