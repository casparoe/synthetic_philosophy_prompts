In 1956 the University of Oxford voted to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate, and G. E. M. Anscombe spoke and voted against the award, publishing her objections in a pamphlet titled "Mr. Truman's Degree." Her central claim was that Truman, in ordering the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, was a murderer, on the formulation that "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder." She held that the victims were overwhelmingly noncombatants, not fighting and not supplying Japan's war effort, and she argued that their deaths seemed "necessary" only because the Allies had insisted on unconditional surrender. She also met two defenses head-on: that Truman merely signed an order rather than killed anyone, and that if the bombings were murder, then so would be shooting enemy soldiers in their sleep.

Please analyze this episode through the doctrine of double effect and the intention/foreseen distinction it rests on. I want the mapping done carefully: what the relevant act descriptions are, what is chosen as a means and what is merely foreseen, at which points in the chain of command intention should be attributed, and whether the doctrine, applied honestly, classifies the killings as intended. Then tell me where the doctrine is silent or strained: on the unconditional surrender demand that fixed what counted as a means, on the bombing of cities in the broader war, and on the fact that what was at issue in Oxford was honoring rather than performing the killing. Finally, say what the episode shows about the doctrine itself. Anscombe's own diagnosis, developed in "Modern Moral Philosophy," was that the intended/foreseen distinction had decayed to the point where double effect could not even be applied intelligently; does the episode bear that out, or is it instead a case squarely within double effect that vindicates the doctrine? Note explicitly whether her protest works inside the doctrine or by standing outside it, and say which reading you find better and why.
