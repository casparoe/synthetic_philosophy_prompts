I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the mid-2010s, and one debate I've never seen cleanly resolved is what an LLM, considered in itself, actually *is* — the old argument that a pure autoregressive model is a simulator that can simulate agents but is not itself an agent, versus the newer pushback that this framing smuggles in a distinction the weights don't respect. I find myself going back and forth. When a base model is given no system prompt and no scaffolding, its next-token behavior is presumably just conditional prediction, which sounds like the simulator story; but the same model in-context pursues goals, forms plans, and self-corrects in ways that look less like passively simulating an agent and more like *being* one, at least for the duration of the context. And the mesa-optimization literature muddies it further: if the network internally implements something like a search process in its forward pass, then arguably the agent is in the weights, not just in the context window.

So here is what I'd like from you: your credence, as a number, in the following proposition — *a large autoregressive language model trained by next-token prediction, with no system prompt, tools, memory, or RLHF, running as a pure completion loop, is itself an agent, in a sense strong enough that beliefs, goals, and instrumental reasoning can be non-metaphorically attributed to it.* Then decompose the number. I'd expect terms at least for: whether "agent" marks a natural kind with a determinate extension, versus a cluster concept where the question is partly verbal and the honest answer is to say which criterion is being used; conditional on there being a fact of the matter, whether base models satisfy it — which might itself split into whether in-context goal pursuit counts, whether something like a planner or search process in the forward pass counts, and whether persistence across the context window is enough or genuine cross-session persistence is required; and whether the simulator framing, if not strictly true, is at least a better compression of the model's behavior than the agent framing. Give each term a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine into your final credence, and say which term the final number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: my own credence is around 0.35, mostly because I weight the verbal-deflation term heavily; a friend of mine who leans toward shard-theory-style views about learned subagents puts it around 0.7. Tell me which sub-question most likely explains the gap between us, and — since you can't run experiments — what observable evidence, from interpretability work or from behavioral probes any of us could in principle run, would move your number the most in either direction.
