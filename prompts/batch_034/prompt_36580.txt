I'm teaching a grad seminar on formal epistemology this term, and we just finished objective chance (Lewis's Principal Principle) and are about to do Reflection (van Fraassen). After last week's session a student hung around and asked me something I've been chewing on ever since, and now I want to see how someone else would organize the whole thing. The two principles look structurally parallel: the Principal Principle tells you to set your credences to match what you take the objective chances to be, conditional on that chance assignment, while Reflection tells you to set your current credences to match what you expect your future credences to be, conditional on having them. Chance on one side, your own future self on the other — both are deference principles, just with a different expert.

But the failure modes are interestingly different, and this is where I want the comparison to bite. The Principal Principle looks broken when the "expert" isn't independent of your evidence — the old undermining problem, where information about how things will actually go bears on what the chances themselves are, so you can't conditionalize on the chance hypothesis and defer to it in one motion. Reflection looks broken in cases where you can foresee your own irrationality: you know that after the third glass of wine you'll be confident you're fine to drive, but you shouldn't defer to that future confidence now. So one natural diagnosis is that both principles are instances of a single deeper rule — something like "defer to X only if you treat X as an expert" — and neither chances nor future selves get that status for free. My student thinks this makes Reflection the more fundamental one, since self-trust is in some sense built into epistemology from the ground up; I keep wanting to say chance is the real expert and my future self is at best a stand-in for it when I expect to be epistemically lucky.

So here's what I want from you: compare the two principles seriously, not just note the parallel and move on. Is Reflection really just the Principal Principle in a mirror, or is the disanalogy deeper than the shared structure suggests? Which principle is better defended on its home turf, and can either be repaired using the resources of the other — e.g., can the style of fix people proposed for the undermining problem, where you tinker with what exactly you're conditionalizing on, be ported over to handle Reflection's foreseen-irrationality cases? Take a position and defend it; my student and I have gone back and forth for a week and neither of us will budge, so I'm curious what a tiebreaker looks like. Please don't just survey the literature — I want an actual verdict on which deference principle, if either, deserves the status of the more basic one.
