I am a high school senior working on an extended essay, and I am trying to work out, from my own judgments about particular cases, what I actually believe about whether higher-level things can cause lower-level things — about whether, as one writer puts the question, symbols can push the substrate around. Here are my verdicts. When my friend decides to open a door and her arm moves, I am comfortable saying both that her decision caused her arm to move and that her motor neurons' firing caused it; I do not feel forced to choose. When a thermostat switches on the furnace, I am happy to say the room's being too cold caused the furnace to fire, even though the bending of the bimetallic strip is what physically did it. But when a glider sails diagonally across the grid in the Game of Life, I do not want to say the glider causes anything — the cell-update rule does everything, and the glider is just a name we give a pattern. With a chess program that moves a rook to protect its queen, I will say the program's plan caused those particular voltage changes, yet I balk at saying that the queen's being threatened, a fact purely of chess, caused voltages to change, even though the plan existed only because of the threat. Finally, there is a thought experiment I came across in Douglas Hofstadter's writing, which I will describe as I remember it in case you have not seen it: an idealized two-dimensional billiard hall, the Careenium, in which perfectly elastic balls, called simmballs, careen about under Newton's laws; the simmballs sometimes settle into stable, long-lived clusters, and because those clusters are stable, an incoming ball tends to bounce off in such a way that the cluster reforms and endures, so the persistence of the pattern reliably shapes the trajectories of its own parts. My verdict there is that it seems right to say the cluster's staying together caused certain simmballs to carom off at just those angles, even though every collision is fixed by Newton. Is there a single principle that captures all five verdicts — especially the difference between the glider, which I refuse any causal power, and the Careenium cluster, which I seem to grant it? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, apply it to a few cases I have not listed (for instance, whether a gene's 'interest' in being copied causes anything, or whether a stock-market panic causes the sell orders that make it up), and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot save — and whether the simpler course would be to overturn that verdict instead.
