Please argue that the Principal Principle (roughly: set your credence equal to what you take the objective chance to be, unless your evidence is "inadmissible") and van Fraassen's Reflection principle (defer to your expected future credences, assuming your future self will be rational) are not just analogous but literally the same norm operating at two different levels — and that this cuts both ways. The standard counterexamples to Reflection, where you know you're about to be drugged or brainwashed into crazy opinions, should be understood as revealing a defeater structure that's also latent in the Principal Principle: the "inadmissible evidence" clause is really an excusing condition for anticipated epistemic malfunction, not a deep disanalogy between chance and opinion. If that's right, then any argument that deference to chance is constitutive of the very meaning of "chance" proves too much, since the parallel argument would make deference to your rational future self constitutive of "credence," which seems absurd.
