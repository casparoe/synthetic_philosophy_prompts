I've recently been asked to run the professional-development seminars at my firm, and a senior partner wants me to teach ethics the biographical way — Plutarch's way, he says: put admirable lives in front of the young associates and let imitation do the rest. Preparing for it, I've run into a problem I can't name. Every biography worth assigning was chosen because things ended well, and the virtues it credits look reverse-engineered from the ending: in a founder whose gamble paid off, stubbornness reads as conviction; in one whose identical gamble ruined him, the same trait reads as vanity. When I deliberately pair each success with a matched failure, the failure teaches the opposite lesson with what seems like the same textual support. I get the same impression from saints' lives, where childhood anecdotes count as signs of holiness only because the ending was already known. And when a life ends ambiguously — a brilliant career wrecked by one late mistake — colleagues extract opposite lessons from the very same life depending on which ending they weight. So the moral lesson of a life seems to be manufactured by selection and hindsight rather than found in the life itself. Is there an established concept for exactly this? I can see it's somewhere near moral luck, survivorship bias, and hindsight bias, but none of those, as far as I can tell, is about whether a life can function as a moral lesson at all, which is the question I actually face. Please answer in four sections, in this order: "Nearest concepts and where they miss" (about 200 words), "A name and definition, or why none fits" (about 150), "Boundary cases" (about 150), and "Verdict on the biographical method" (about 200). In the last section, don't just lay out the options — commit to a definite position on whether teaching ethics through lives survives this problem, and tell me on what terms.
