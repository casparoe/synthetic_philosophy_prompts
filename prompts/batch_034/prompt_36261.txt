taking a moral psych elective right now and we just hit the unit on self-conscious emotions, so I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about guilt vs shame instead of just reciting the textbook's "guilt = I did something bad, shame = I am bad" line. here are my gut verdicts on cases, tell me if there's a principle behind them:

- I cheat on an exam and feel awful about it: guilt, and guilt seems like the fitting response -- it's about the act, and it's the kind of feeling that makes you want to confess and not do it again.
- I get way too drunk at the department party and do a catastophic karaoke performance in front of my advisor: shame, and shame seems right, even though I didn't wrong anybody. I just want to disappear.
- A racist thought pops into my head unbidden about a classmate, and I'm horrified by it: this feels like shame is fitting, MORE fitting than guilt, even though I didn't do anything. But my friend in the seminar says guilt is the fitting one precisely because there was no action, so there's nothing for shame to latch onto. I keep going back and forth.
- I'm embarrassed/ashamed of my accent when I speak in French class: shame here seems flat-out unfitting. It's just something to practice and get over, there's no moral failing anywhere in the vicinity.
- I borrowed money from a friend and can't pay it back: guilt, obviously. But my roommate is from a family where this would be shame, and shame for his whole family, not just him -- and honestly when he describes it I can't say his reaction is irrational.
- I spent a semester being quietly ashamed that I'm the only person at my uni who works a dish shift in the dining hall: shame seems unfitting here too, kind of toxic actually, even though it's the same "I am bad / they'll see me as less" feeling as the karaoke one.
- Cheating on a partner and feeling so much total shame that you spiral, hide, and never actually apologize vs. feeling guilt that lets you go say sorry: the guilt version seems clearly the better response.

so is there a principle that captures all of these? my first pass was something like "shame is fitting when the thing reveals something about your character that you're responsible for being, and unfitting when it's just a circumstance or a trait you couldn't help" -- but that saves the racist-thought case and the poverty case at the cost of making the karaoke one unfitting, which I don't buy, and I'm not even sure intrusive thoughts count as character. second pass was "shame is fitting when the exposure of it to others is the harm," which handles karaoke but makes the private racist thought weird since nobody saw it.

give me your best version of the principle, precisely stated, with which case forces each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it just can't save (I suspect the poverty one and the karaoke one pull in opposite directions). also test it on a case I didn't list -- say, shame about your country's history, which I genuinely can't get a read on. and last thing: our prof floated the question of whether a culture can choose to be more guilt-centered vs shame-centered, so tell me if your principle implies anything about that -- like whether "fitting" here even leaves room for a culture to shift what its members feel.
