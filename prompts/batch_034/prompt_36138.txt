My younger brother and I keep a running list of traits and argue over which ones deserve to be called adaptations. His verdicts so far: the vertebrate eye, adaptation (clearly built by selection for seeing); our ability to learn to read, not an adaptation (writing is far too recent, so reading must ride on brain capacities selected for other jobs); the red color of blood, not one (a byproduct of a molecule selected for carrying oxygen); the belly button, not one (a byproduct of the umbilical cord); fever, adaptation (the raised body temperature was selected because it helps fight infection); and the four-limbed body plan we share with other vertebrates, not one (an inherited blueprint that every descendant builds on, not something selected for having four limbs). Last week he announced that the bridge of the human nose is an adaptation because it holds up eyeglasses, and I realized I cannot tell whether he is being consistent with his own track record. It seems to me his earlier verdicts could come from more than one underlying rule — one asking what selection originally built a trait for, another asking whether the trait's present form serves some function, and perhaps a third that refuses to call anything an adaptation if it can be explained as a byproduct or an inherited constraint. I also half-remember a popular article accusing biologists of finding purpose in everything, with an analogy to the curved spaces between the arches of some Italian church, though I don't want to build anything on my memory of it. Could you set out the candidate rules that fit his six earlier verdicts, say which rules his nose-bridge claim commits him to and whether it clashes with anything he has already ruled on, and tell me what new case would most cleanly separate the rules? We suspect goosebumps would do it, since they raise fur usefully in hairy mammals but seem to accomplish nothing in us — is that right, or does the settling case need to be something else? Throughout, please keep separate what is settled biology from what biologists and philosophers of biology still genuinely dispute, including whether there is even a single official definition of adaptation, and don't pass off one camp's terminology as the standard one.
