I'm a high school junior, and last semester a friend showed me an essay about what it called multipolar traps — the idea that when many actors compete, each doing what is individually rational, they can end up in an equilibrium that is worse for every one of them than an outcome they all prefer, but that no single actor can bring about by acting alone. Our economics teacher then used overfishing as an example, and it's been sitting with me since. For my school's philosophy club I've drafted a one-page position statement on competition and collective action, and before I read it to the group I'd like an outside audit of whether the views in it actually hang together. Here is the set as it stands: (1) I'm a consequentialist about these matters — what ultimately matters is how things go for everyone affected, and rules and institutions are justified by their results. (2) In a genuine race to the bottom (fleets overfishing, firms cutting safety or quality to undercut rivals, companies releasing products faster than they'd like because a competitor will release anyway), unilateral restraint by a single actor typically accomplishes nothing: the restrained actor is simply outcompeted and replaced by someone without the scruples, and the bad outcome occurs regardless. (3) Even so, I hold the individual decision-makers who cut these corners morally blameworthy — I don't accept "the system left them no choice" as an excuse, and I think public criticism of them is deserved. (4) I believe the only real cure is coordination that binds everyone at once — regulation, treaties, enforceable standards — since that is the only thing that changes what each participant's best move is. (5) Yet I'm also, by temperament, a decentralist: I distrust concentrated coercive power, I think regulators tend to be captured by the very industries they police, and my preference is to address these problems through voluntary cooperation, reputation, and bottom-up norms rather than top-down control. (6) Finally, I believe the small choices of ordinary people — where to work, what to buy, whom to praise and whom to shame — are morally significant and can, in aggregate, turn races to the bottom into races to the top, even though each single choice is a drop in the ocean.

I'd like you to go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which merely look that way on first inspection — please don't flag everything at the same level of alarm, since I'm told a good audit discriminates. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. I honestly cannot locate the problem myself, so don't soften the verdict: if something in my list has to go, tell me which and why, and if there's more than one way to repair the set, compare the repairs and say which you'd recommend.
