I have been reading my way into decision theory since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, and I have hit a passage in a popular book on the Ellsberg paradox that summarizes the result as follows:

"Ellsberg offered subjects a choice between betting on drawing a red ball from an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls, and betting on red from a second urn containing one hundred balls in some unknown mix of the two colors. Nearly everyone preferred the first urn, even though the expected proportion of red in the unknown urn is also one half. Ellsberg dubbed this preference for known odds ambiguity aversion — essentially a cousin of ordinary risk aversion. But since a rational agent's expected utility is identical across the two urns, the preference is simply irrational, and it directly violates the sure-thing principle, the rule that if you prefer A to B in one state of the world and also prefer A to B in its negation, you must prefer A to B outright. The upshot is that Ellsberg disproved expected utility theory as an account of rational choice, and with it the Bayesian idea that all uncertainty can be represented by a single probability measure."

The experiment itself seems unimpeachable to me — I would have chosen the first urn without hesitation, and I suspect my colleagues would have too — but the author's inferences strike me as the sort of thing I was trained, in the lab, not to accept on faith. Would you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a working decision theorist would accept, where distinctions are being run together, and, most importantly, whether the sweeping final conclusion survives once the middle claims are corrected? I am especially unsure what to make of the charge that a single preference between two urns, taken by itself, could violate the sure-thing principle as the author defines it.
