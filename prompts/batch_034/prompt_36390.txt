I'm a senior analyst at a national body that decides which health services get publicly funded. We have informally adopted a policy of giving priority to the worse off — benefits to more disadvantaged patients count for more than equal benefits to better-off ones — but we have no worked-out method, and committee members keep disputing particular cases on instinct. Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run on a funding proposal.

Context: we make roughly ten of these decisions a year, each involving thousands of patients and significant budgets. An analyst has about two weeks per case, with QALY-style effectiveness estimates and subgroup data, but no way of making fine-grained interpersonal utility comparisons. Final sign-off is by a mixed committee of clinicians, economists, and lay members, so every step must be explainable to a non-theorist in a paragraph.

The procedure should cover, in order: how to characterize the relevant sense of "worse off," including when current condition, lifetime health prospects, and socioeconomic position point in different directions and which controls; how to set the priority weights, and how to report the recommendations that flip as the weights vary; an explicit leveling-down test — what the procedure should say about a policy that benefits no one but narrows the gap between groups, and whether prioritarianism has any principled basis for rejecting it; and the points at which the questions stop being technical and must go to the committee as questions of values.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a drug extending life by a few months for patients with a severe progressive condition, competing against a mental-health program with a larger total benefit spread across many mildly affected people; (2) a case where following the procedure's own weights yields a recommendation I will dislike — a small benefit to the very worst off purchased at a very large cost in aggregate health — and the procedure must show how it handles that rather than hiding it; and (3) a rationing rule that withholds a service from the better-off group without extending it to anyone, which is pure leveling-down.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in distributive justice, with experience translating theory for health priority-setting bodies. Before writing anything, brainstorm which distinctions from the prioritarianism literature must survive the simplification — e.g., prioritarianism's reason for rejecting leveling-down versus the objection's usual use against egalitarianism — and which can safely be dropped. Mark honestly wherever the procedure cannot settle the matter. Answer in American English.
