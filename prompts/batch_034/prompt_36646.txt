I'm taking an economic history course and we just did the unit on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. The professor framed it as this thesis that keeps dying and coming back: originally it said certain Protestant ideas (the calling, anxiety over salvation, worldly asceticism) helped kick off modern capitalism, then everyone decided it was wrong for decades, then people started finding correlations again (like Protestants vs Catholics in 19th century Germany doing better in education etc), and now nobody's sure whether the whole idea of culture causing economic outcomes is even coherent.

After class I got into it with two friends and we ended up camped on a metaphor one of them kept using and I can't shake it. She says think of it as software vs hardware: institutions, geography, property rights, trade routes are the hardware, and culture/religion/values are the software running on top. On this picture Weber is basically claiming that installing a different operating system changed what the machine did, and the materialists in the debate are like people insisting it must have been a hardware upgrade all along. My other friend thinks the whole framing is rigged: he says values on his view are more like the documentation, the part of the system that describes and justifies what the machine is already doing for other reasons, and that swapping it changes nothing. So capitalism generated a theology that made it look chosen, rather than the theology generating capitalism.

What I'd like is a real audit of the software analogy since it's doing a lot of work in our argument. What's supposed to correspond to what exactly — is a religious doctrine really something that gets "installed," and by whom, and can you even have the hardware running with no values at all (what would a culture-less population be, in this picture)? Where does the mapping break — like the fact that the "software" seems to rewrite itself based on what the hardware is doing, or that two machines with identical hardware and different software sometimes converge in behavior anyway over a few generations? And then the actual payoff question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the analogy still support the conclusion my friend wants from it — that values can be an independent causal input into economic outcomes — or does it smuggle that in (software is written by someone with intentions, it runs deterministically, it's cleanly separable from the machine...). If the software picture fails, is there a better comparison that keeps what's right about Weber without the baggage, or does culture-as-cause just need to be stated without any metaphor at all? Ideally also say something about whether the documentation guy is actually a third option or just the software analogy with the polarity reversed.
