I've been going around with my sister about fairness stuff and it hit me that everyone I talk to just answers the question "should things be equal?" but nobody stops to ask equal WHAT. Like, if we all have the same money but I'm prone to terrible migraines and you're not, are we equal? If we both have the same resources but I was raised to want nothing so I'm happy with a bowl of rice and you weren't, are we equal? She says happiness is what matters, I keep feeling like that lets you get away with counting someone's contentment in a bad situation as a win. But I don't actually want to argue for my side right now, I want to figure out what an answer to this has to get right before I even pick one.

So here's what I'm asking for: write me the list of requirements that any measure of "what people have for purposes of equality" has to meet -- the cases it has to get right, with the reasoning behind each. The ones I can already half-see: it has to deal with people who cultivate expensive tastes (if equality is about getting people satisfied, does someone who trains themselves to only enjoy champagne deserve more champagne? surely not), it has to deal with people who've lowered their sights because life beat them down (you can't just ask them "are you happy?" and take the yes at face value), it has to deal with disabilities and bad luck at birth (equal money doesn't look equal when one person needs a wheelchair and hearing aids to do what the other does barefoot), and it has to draw some line between things you caused yourself and things that just happened to you -- though honestly I don't know if that line is a real requirement or just something people like me want to be there, tell me if you think it's a prejudice and not a genuine demand.

Also two things I specifically want you to address. First: do any of the requirements contradict each other? Like I have this feeling that "deal with lowered sights" and "respect what people actually want" are going to fight each other and you can't have both, and if so that's actually an interesting result about the list itself, not a failure of it -- I'd want that flagged, not papered over. Second, once the list is done, run the obvious candidates against it quickly -- happiness/satisfaction, money and stuff, and something like "what a person is actually able to do and be" (I'm vaguely aware people have pushed that last one but I don't remember the details, don't worry about names or citations, just the ideas). Which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, that's the part I care about most. Keep it plain-spoken please, I never went to college and I'll bounce off anything too jargon-y. A table is fine if you think it helps.
