My notes (still rough):

- Franklin's 13-virtue scheme in the Autobiography is usually read either as earnest self-improvement or as a semi-comic confession of vanity. I want to argue it's neither: it's best understood as an early experiment in what we'd now call habit retraining / behavioral self-engineering. The whole apparatus — one virtue per week, the little book with a column for each day of the week, marking a black spot for every fault, then cycling through the entire list again — is a self-monitoring and graded-attention system, not a program of character formation in the Aristotelian sense. There's no role for practical wisdom, no doctrine of the mean, no teleology of the soul. A vice is just a habit loop to be extinguished by sequential, focused attention, and the sequential part seems essential to it: you don't attack all thirteen at once because that would overload the attentional budget.

- Supporting evidence: how he handles humility. A Quaker friend tells him he's generally thought proud, so he adds humility to the list — but he admits he never really acquired the virtue, only the appearance of it, and that the appearance got him all the practical benefits, since people are more willing to be persuaded by someone who doesn't seem convinced he's right. On a virtue-ethics reading this is failure, or straight-up hypocrisy. On a habit-training reading it's a success, because what the system was optimizing all along was behavioral output (don't talk over people, don't alienate interlocutors), not inner state.

- The big datum: he claims the project worked for twelve of the thirteen virtues, failing only on Order — keeping things in their places, doing everything in its time. He eventually gives up on perfecting it, settling for a tolerable approximation, and tells the story of the man who wanted his axe polished bright but got tired of the work and decided a speckled axe was good enough. I want to use this asymmetry as evidence for my reading. The conversational and self-command virtues yielded to attention plus self-monitoring, because their failure mode is a discrete act you can catch yourself doing. Order is different: its failure mode is diffuse and environmental — no amount of noticing that you're messy produces organization — so the method has a principled limit, and I think Franklin half-saw this himself.
