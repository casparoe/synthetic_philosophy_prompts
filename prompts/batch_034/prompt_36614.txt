A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about where pseudohistory ends and merely wrong, unfashionable, or prematurely dismissed history begins — Fomenko came up, as did Atlantis, ancient aliens, and a long detour through nineteenth-century archaeology — and I came away with something embarrassing: a page of my own verdicts on particular cases that I cannot make cohere under any principle I can state. I would like you to help me find the principle, or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Here are my verdicts, with the reasoning I found myself giving as I gave them. Fomenko's New Chronology is pseudohistory, full stop: the statistical and astronomical methods are window dressing, the thesis is structured so that any evidence against it is reinterpreted as evidence of a later forgery, and the whole thing happens to flatter a nationalist narrative. Ancient aliens is pseudohistory too, and something additionally ugly — it works by default attributing non-European achievements to outside intervention. Atlantis is stranger, because I hold two verdicts under one name: the occult, Atlantean-super-civilization tradition is pseudohistory, but the sober question of whether Plato's story has a Bronze Age kernel, perhaps something behind the Minoan world and the Santorini eruption, strikes me as legitimate if unresolvable speculation. So the demarcation seems to track what is being done with the claim rather than the claim's content.

Then come the hard ones. Norse presence in North America was a minority position defended from saga texts until the Newfoundland excavation in the 1960s, and I do not want to call the pre-vindication defenders pseudohistorians. But I do call the defenders of the Kensington runestone pseudohistorians, even though they too rely on an artifact plus documentary enthusiasm; the honest difference is that the sagas are medieval manuscripts and the runestone's language is a modern pastiche, yet that is an evidential difference, and I don't want demarcation to collapse into evidence quality, because bad evidence is not automatically pseudohistory. Similarly: Schliemann took a poetic text as a travel guide, was obsessive, and destroyed the stratigraphy of the very site he was looking for — and I file him under bad archaeology, not pseudohistory — while Menzies's 1421, structurally the same story of an amateur with a text and a hunch, gets the pseudohistory label from me without hesitation. Black Athena seems to me wrong and overreaching, but it is contested history, not pseudohistory. The Solutrean hypothesis was published by credentialed archaeologists in mainstream venues, and yet I want to call it fringe — though I am not sure whether "fringe" and "pseudohistory" are the same category or merely overlap. And the pre-Clovis settlement advocates were ridiculed for decades before the archaeological community accepted their case, which means vindication came to people who had been dismissed, without my wanting to retroactively classify them either way.

My tentative principle, which my friend promptly dismantled: pseudohistory is a property of argumentative practice rather than of claims — immunization against possible disconfirmation, an implied conspiracy of suppression, methods that could not have failed — not of a claim's truth, its proponents' credentials, or its eventual fate. Her objection is that stated this way the principle cannot do the work: the L'Anse aux Meadows defenders and the Fomenkoites both relied on texts against a skeptical consensus, Schliemann's circular readings of Homer look structurally like the immunizing moves I condemn elsewhere, and "immunization" risks being a label I attach post hoc to whatever I already decided. She also insists the demarcation is partly sociological — what matters is whose criticism gets engaged with, and by whom — while I want it to be purely about how the claim is held.

So here is what I am asking for. State, precisely, one principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures these verdicts, with the specific case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated and would have to go, and why those rather than others. Tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed — I would start with Graham Hancock's lost ice-age civilization, which keeps me ambivalent because he does respond to counter-evidence, though selectively and with much goalpost-shifting, and with the Oxfordian attribution of Shakespeare's plays, which I genuinely cannot place. And tell me honestly whether the vindication cases force the principle to be retrospect-dependent after all, which I very much do not want it to be. I want an analysis of my judgments and the principle behind them, not a survey of the literature.
