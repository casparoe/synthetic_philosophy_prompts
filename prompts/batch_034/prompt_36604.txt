I'm a third-year PhD student in moral philosophy, and at a departmental dinner two fellow graduate students — one writing a dissertation in metaphysics, the other in applied ethics — spent the better part of an hour arguing about a case from a massively multiplayer online game. A player had spent two years earning a server-unique item, genuinely one of a kind, having reportedly turned down a real-money offer of several thousand dollars for it; a botched server rollback then erased it, and the company offered only an apology and some generic in-game currency as compensation.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) The metaphysician argued that nothing was really destroyed, because there was never an object there to begin with: a virtual item is a pattern of mutable server states, not a concrete particular like a trading card that burns in a fire, so calling its deletion a loss is like calling a character's in-game death a death. (2) The ethicist replied that the metaphysics is doing less work than claimed — the value resides in scarcity, standing, and two years of effort, which are exactly the sorts of things whose loss we count as harms in ordinary life, and no one thinks reputation or standing is a physical object either. (3) The metaphysician answered that unlike a painting or a reputation, the item's existence and value are wholly revocable by corporate fiat — the company could conjure a thousand duplicates tomorrow — so the player's loss is a loss within a shared pretense, and mourning it is closer to an actor grieving a character. (4) The ethicist retorted that a bank balance is also revocable ledger entries whose value depends on institutional backing, and that by this standard the metaphysician would have to tell a fraud victim whose savings were a database entry that nothing real was taken from her. They eventually gave up and asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, and structure your response in three sections, in this order: "The four moves, assessed in turn" (roughly 100–150 words per move), "What both are missing" (about 150 words), and "Overall verdict" (about 100 words). In the verdict, say not just who comes out ahead but precisely which of their claims you would endorse, which you would reject, and which depend on distinctions neither of them articulated clearly.
