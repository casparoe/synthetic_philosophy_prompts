I teach an intro-level philosophy of language unit, and this term I'm pairing Putnam's Twin Earth with Peter Weir's The Truman Show, mostly because the film hands you a ready-made natural kind experiment that Putnam never thought to run. Seahaven sits inside a giant dome: Truman's "sky" is a painted ceiling, his "moon" is a stage light, and Christof can literally make his "sun" rise on cue. But other things around him seem ordinary — real water in the harbor, real rain on him when he sails. So Truman looks like the inhabitant of a partial Twin Earth, living among a mix of genuine natural kinds and artifacts disguised as natural kinds. When Truman says he wants to sail away and see the world, what is he even talking about? Do his "sky" and "moon" fail to refer, refer to dome interior surfaces and lighting rigs, or refer to the real sky and moon via whatever causal chain connects Seahaven to the outside? And what does Burge's division of linguistic labor do here — the producers and cast know exactly what Truman's words are true of, while he doesn't, so is Truman in the position of a Twin Earth Oscar with "elm" and "beech," deferring to a community that is deceiving him?

I want a reading that takes both the film and the externalism literature seriously and doesn't just turn the movie into an illustration. In particular, tell me where the film resists the analogy. One thing I keep tripping over: externalism is usually framed in terms of causal environment, but Truman's predicament is driven by intentional deception — the extensions of his terms are engineered by someone, which suggests a normative and political dimension to semantic externalism that Putnam's papers don't have anything to say about. Christof doesn't just control what Truman's words mean; he controls what would count as Truman being right. Does that matter for the semantics, or only for the ethics? A second wrinkle: Truman grows suspicious and starts testing his world long before he has any external vantage point. What does that do to the standard thought experiment, where the inhabitant is oblivious and the difference between Earth and Twin Earth is invisible "from the inside"? And once Truman walks through the door in the studio wall and faces Christof, do the two of them mean different things by "the world" when they each use that phrase?

If it helps you decide where the analogy holds, the film's final act is fair game — the storm Christof manufactures to turn him back, the boat's prow piercing the painted sky, the staircase and the exit door. I'd also like a closing section on what the film adds to the Twin Earth debate that the thought experiment alone doesn't: something about what it feels like, from the inside, to have your meanings depend on an environment you can't see. Please write the answer in American English.
