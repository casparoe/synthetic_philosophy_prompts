My department runs a grad reading group that has spent the better part of two semesters stuck on luminosity, and I'm starting to wonder if we're even disagreeing about a single question. The setup, in case it's helpful: Williamson argued in Knowledge and Its Limits that no mental state is "luminous" in the sense that whenever you're in it, you're in a position to know you're in it -- his famous example is feeling cold at dawn, where tiny imperceptible changes in temperature mean any reliable belief you form about being cold needs a margin for error, so there'll be a last cold moment you can't know you're cold at. Since knowledge (arguably) entails knowing that you know, this kills the KK principle too. The anti-luminosity argument is elegant and the people in my group are sharp, and yet the same three moves just get recycled: the Williamsonians say the argument is airtight and the KK defender is just changing the subject to "appropriate confidence" or "justified belief"; the KK defenders say the argument smuggles in an externalist, safety-flavored conception of knowledge that no internalist ever accepted, and that if you think of knowledge as requiring internal accessibility the margin-for-error reasoning never gets a grip; and a third faction shrugs and says degrees of belief were always the right tool and binary knowledge-state talk is doing the argument's work only by being ambiguous.

So here's what I want, and I want it to be a diagnosis rather than a verdict on whether Williamson is right. Is this disagreement substantive or is it, as one person in the group put it, two debates wearing one coat -- one about the metaphysics of knowledge and whether it has margins, one about the epistemology of self-knowledge and how confident you can be about your own current states? It seems telling to me that both sides happily concede that as you warm up there's a moment where you're cold but can't *tell* whether you're cold -- nobody disputes the phenomenology, only whether "know" applies at that moment. Can the whole fight really be about a word? On the other hand, the safety-based conception of knowledge isn't just a verbal preference -- the anti-luminist claims it explains things (like why knowledge is better for action than justified belief), so maybe the debate is really about which total package of epistemology to adopt, which would make it substantive but holistic, and thus hard to move.

Also, maybe fold in this wrinkle we keep hitting: some of the group think the dispute bottoms out in whether the KK principle is supposed to be a conceptual truth about knowledge, an idealization for finite agents like us, or something true only of a godlike reasoner who never lapses -- and if it's the idealization reading, then Williamson's finite-agent examples might be beside the point, or they might be exactly the point, depending on who you ask. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually, what would even count as progress on it (a new distinction, a formal model, a reinterpretation of the margin-for-error principle, just biting a bullet and moving on), or is there a decent case that it's irresolvable in principle? My own tentative hunch is that it's mostly a verbal dispute over "know" that becomes substantive only once you fix what explanatory work you want the concept to do -- but I'd genuinely like to be told why that hunch is wrong, with reference to what the two sides actually say rather than a generic "verbal vs substantive" taxonomy. Sorry for the length, this has been bugging me for months.
