A friend and I spent most of last evening on Berkeley, specifically on the argument that I cannot so much as conceive of an unperceived tree, since in attempting to conceive it I am perceiving it — the one critics have labeled the master argument. He dismissed it in about ten seconds as trading on an ambiguity between an act and an object of thought, the same objection Lichtenberg raised against Descartes: there is thinking going on, but nothing entitles us to the I that thinks it. I am prepared to concede that the crude version is invalid: from whatever I conceive is conceived, nothing follows about whatever I conceive being a mental item.

What I want to hold onto, and where we actually got stuck, is a repaired version: that conceivability gives us no evidence whatsoever about whether objects exist unperceived, because every object of thought is, in being an object of thought, the wrong sort of thing to serve as a sample of mind-independent being. Whatever I put before my mind as a candidate for independent existence is something as-thought-by-me, and that aspect is ineliminable, so thought never reaches past itself to check. On this reading the argument is not a proof of idealism but the demolition of the natural common-sense rejoinder — surely I can just imagine a tree standing alone in a forest with no one around — and the burden of proof shifts to whoever claims that conceivability favors realism about unperceived objects.

Tell me what would count against this repaired view. I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and specifically: whether the objector's distinction between conceiving a tree that happens to be unconceived and merely conceiving a tree-as-conceived genuinely breaks the lock or just relabels it; whether the point that a thought of a tree need not be a thought of a mental item — the tree being what the thought is about rather than what it consists of — shows that my central premise is false rather than merely obscure; and whether the retreat from the strong claim that unconceived objects are inconceivable to the weaker claim that conceiving them is no evidence for their independence has insulated my position into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a defect in a view whose job is to shift a burden of proof rather than to make predictions. I would also like an honest account of what, if anything, could show me that the unperceivedness of ordinary objects is straightforwardly conceivable — as opposed to people simply insisting that they are conceiving it — or whether that insistence is all the other side ever has. Finally, distinguish what a defender of the repaired argument would concede genuinely counts against it from what critics of Berkeley standardly allege but a defender could shrug off, and say plainly where you think my position is most exposed.
