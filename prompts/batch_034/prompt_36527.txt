Philosopher here, teaching a cross-listed seminar on trust, and the bio grad students keep invoking the handicap principle like it's a magic word. Last week one of them dismissed a public corporate apology as "just cheap talk — no handicap, so it can't be an honest signal," and the room sort of nodded along. I suspect the slogan they're actually leaning on, roughly 'honest signals must be costly', is doing a lot of unexamined work, and I want to push on it next session without just asserting my own reading.

So: what are the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing? I can think of at least a few that need separating -- that any honest signal must be costly at all, that costs only matter where sender and receiver interests conflict, that what's crucial is differential cost (prohibitively costly for deceivers, cheap for genuinely honest types), and that the cost has to be strategically connected to the thing being signaled rather than just a toll paid at the door. There may be others. For each reading, say whether it's true, whether it's substantive or a near-platitude, and -- crucially -- whether it actually delivers the verdict my student wanted, that a costless apology can't be honest.

Then tell me which reading the bio students probably have in mind, and whether it commits them to anything odd about apologies in particular, e.g. that an apology only counts as honest if it demonstrably burns reputation or future options. Lay it out as a list I can bring into class, and be blunt about which readings are doing real explanatory work versus which are just signaling sophistication about signaling.
