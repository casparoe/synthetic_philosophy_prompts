My reading group spent last month on the self-reference chapters of Gödel, Escher, Bach, and two members — I'll call them Dara and Marcus — have been continuing the argument in our group chat ever since. I've watched the whole thing unfold and, to my embarrassment, can't referee it. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, in order.

(1) Dara opened with the strong claim: Gödel showed that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains truths it cannot prove, so an idealized mathematician cannot be a formal system — whatever the system spits out, we can step outside it and see that its Gödel sentence is true. Minds outrun machines in principle, and that's that.

(2) Marcus answered that this trades on three ambiguities. First, the theorem applies only if the system is consistent, and we can't establish our own consistency any better than a machine can; second, the system could simply adopt its Gödel sentence as a new axiom, at which point the human faces a fresh one, so the supposed advantage regresses forever; third, the theorem concerns effectively axiomatized systems specifically, and nothing says the brain is one in the relevant sense — or that it's consistent, since humans contradict themselves and remain minded. He added, more pointedly, that if the human "sees" the truth of the Gödel sentence, that seeing is only as good as the assumption of consistency doing the work underneath it.

(3) Dara retreated, but claimed the retreat strengthens her position. Set aside out-proving machines, she said; the real lesson of the book is constructive. The Gödel construction demonstrates how a formal system, by coding statements about its own formulas into its own formulas, can achieve genuine self-reference — and that capacity, generalized into quining and self-reproducing structures, is the seed of self-awareness. The "I" is a strange loop, and Gödel's proof is at least a working model of how such a loop can be built out of syntax that is nowhere about anything.

(4) Marcus wasn't persuaded that the analogy survives the retreat. The Gödel sentence, he said, refers to itself only through an arithmetization mapping imposed from outside; under a different coding, the very same formula says nothing about itself. So the self-reference is projected onto the formula by us, not achieved by the system — the system just grinds out arithmetic, and the loop is in the eye of the interpreter. Conscious self-reference, whatever else it is, is not like that: when I think about my own thinking, the aboutness is mine, not a spectator's.

(5) Dara replied that all self-reference is mediated like that — the brain models itself via internal symbols rather than by some unmediated inner sight, so indirectness is not a disqualification but the only form self-reference ever takes. If human self-reference is also mediated, then Gödel's construction is not a cheap illustration; it's a proof of concept that a chain of symbol manipulation can close the loop at all.

(6) Marcus closed with the loop's need for a floor. Even Hofstadter, he said, insists that Escher's Drawing Hands works only because of the inviolate level — the untouched paper that neither hand draws. For a mind, the neural substrate is doing what the paper does: it is what makes the loop possible without being part of the loop, and nothing in the Gödel construction explains why the loop, once running, is accompanied by experience rather than running in the dark.

They've now asked me, as the bystander who has read the book most carefully, to settle it, and I would genuinely appreciate an adjudication. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the six numbered moves, say whether it is basically right, basically wrong, or right only in a qualified way, and justify each verdict. Then say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall — noting explicitly if the two of them are sometimes talking past each other — and identify the point, if any, that both keep missing.
