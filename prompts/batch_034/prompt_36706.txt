so, something from work has been bugging me for a week and I want a second opinion on it.

I work on fraud detection at a payments company. Last quarter, to make the system extra safe, we trained two completely seperate instances of the same model: different random seeds, different initializations, different shuffling of the training data, different engineers babysitting each run. The plan was to run them side by side and flag a transaction if either one fires — the thinking being that even if one model has some weird blind spot, the odds of both having the same blind spot are tiny, so the overlap covers for each one's individual mistakes.

Then last week a fraud pattern slipped past both of them. When we dug in, it wasn't that the attack was especially hard. It's that both models had quietly learned the exact same lesson from the same historical data — something like "small grocery-store transactions are basically never fraud" — and the attackers were doing a hundred tiny fake grocery charges that exploited precisely that.

My teammate's reaction: "no big deal, this is just one bug, train a third model and majority-vote the three of them, the bug gets outvoted." My reaction: something feels off about describing these runs as "independent" in the first place. They weren't byte-identical, sure, but they saw the same world — same data distribution, same absence of examples of this attack, same everything that matters. So of course they converged on the same beliefs. To me, adding a third model feels less like adding a genuinely fresh opinion and more like polling a third witness who read the same newspaper as the first two. It'll agree with them not because the evidence is independent, but because its coming from the same source.

So my questions. Is my teammate right that agreement between the runs is fine and a third one fixes it, or am I right that the "independence" here is mostly an illusion we're paying for? And more generally — is there any principled way to decide when we're allowed to treat two things as independent, when we can't actually verify it? Like, is there a real difference between "different random seeds" independence and the kind of independence that actually protects you, and if so where exactly does the line fall? I keep going back and forth so I'd love a straight answer, not just "it depends."
