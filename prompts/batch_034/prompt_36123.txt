EXAM QUESTION (25 points):

In discussions of the Great Divergence, economic historians ask why sustained industrial growth began in northwestern Europe—paradigmatically in Britain—rather than in China's Yangzi Delta, arguably one of the most commercially advanced regions in the world as late as the mid-eighteenth century. Joseph Needham, the great historian of Chinese science and technology, posed a related but distinct question: why did modern science—mathematical, experimental, and cumulative—develop in Europe and not in China, given China's earlier record of technological achievement? Some writers treat the Needham question and the Great Divergence question as two labels for a single puzzle.

(a) Explain why the two questions are not the same, and why conflating them can distort explanations of each. (b) What is required for a contrastive explanation of the form "Why Britain and not China?" to be well posed? Discuss how the choice of contrast class (Britain versus China; Britain versus the Netherlands; the Yangzi Delta versus England versus Bengal) affects what counts as a good answer. (c) Illustrate with at least one substantive explanation from the literature (for example, coal and New World resources, the high-level equilibrium trap, or institutional accounts) and assess whether it presupposes a particular contrast.

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

The Needham question and the Great Divergence question are really two ways of asking the same thing: why did the modern world emerge in Europe and not in China? Needham himself coined the term Great Divergence, and his own answer to the puzzle—the high-level equilibrium trap—shows why the two questions cannot be separated. On this view, late imperial China's economy was so efficient and its labor so cheap that there was no profitable incentive to mechanize. But the same logic explains the failure of modern science in China as well: with no economic pressure toward innovation, there was likewise no pressure for a scientific revolution, so the economic and scientific questions collapse into one.

Timing still matters. The California school, above all Kenneth Pomeranz, argues that the divergence came late: the Yangzi Delta was as developed as the most advanced parts of Europe well into the eighteenth century, so the real question is why the nineteenth century went so differently. Pomeranz's explanation is essentially resource-based. Britain had abundant, accessible coal, and China simply lacked coal deposits, so Britain could substitute mineral for organic energy and China could not. The New World mattered too, since American land relieved ecological constraints that would otherwise have bound Britain.

On whether the question is well posed: a contrastive question is well posed only if the two candidates being compared were otherwise identical in every causally relevant respect at some point before the divergence. Britain and China were not—they differed in geography, institutions, culture, and political scale—so no single contrastive explanation is possible. What is needed instead is a complete narrative history of each region. One good result of taking contrasts seriously is that it changes with the comparison: asking why Britain and not the Netherlands is a different question with different answers, since the Dutch had high wages and commercial sophistication too. This shows that any explanation offered for Britain versus China must really be a bundle of different explanations bundled together.

In conclusion, the Needham question and the Great Divergence are one puzzle with one solution, though the timing of that solution is contested, and the contrastive form of the question is ultimately ill posed because the comparanda were never otherwise identical.
