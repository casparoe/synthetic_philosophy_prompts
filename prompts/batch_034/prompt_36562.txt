I am a senior risk analyst at an engineering consultancy. A recurring part of my job is Monte Carlo simulation for failure and reliability analysis, and several times a month a model comes up in which someone has to generate random straight lines or chords through a circular cross-section: fatigue-crack paths across a pipe weld, a scratch left by a dropped tool across a circular viewport, debris modeled as a straight-line cut through a containment boundary, an arc-fault path crossing a circular electrode. Last quarter an internal review caught two of our analysts modeling what was nominally the same scenario — a random crack traversing a circular weld region — with two different chord samplers: one drew the two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference, the other drew the crack's direction uniformly and its perpendicular distance from the center uniformly. For the probability of the crack severing an inscribed safety zone they got what look like the classic one-third and one-half answers, which was enough for the review to question every geometric-probability figure we have ever issued.

As I understand it, the underlying problem is Bertrand's paradox: a random chord of a circle is not a well-defined object until you specify the mechanism that generates it, and uniform sampling over different natural parameters (endpoints, midpoint, direction and offset) induces genuinely different probability measures. The principle of indifference — treat all possibilities as equally likely when you have no reason to prefer one — cannot arbitrate, because it is silent about which description of the possibilities to be indifferent over. When two of my engineers code what the client calls the same event and get different numbers, the disagreement is not an error either of them made; it is a choice about what random means that nobody made consciously.

I want you to write a decision procedure that my group can actually run at the start of a simulation build, in roughly fifteen minutes, without any background in philosophy. The procedure should take as input a physical scenario involving lines or chords and a circular region, and output either a specific sampling scheme with a defensible justification, or an explicit finding that no principled choice exists, in which case it should say what to do instead — presumably bracketing the answer across the plausible measures and flagging what physical data would collapse the bracket. In detail, I am asking for:

1. The questions, in order, that the analyst should ask about the scenario, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each. I expect the core of the procedure to be an interrogation of the physical generating process behind the randomness — what mechanism actually produces the line, and hence which parameter that mechanism plausibly treats as uniform — with symmetry and invariance considerations doing the work when the mechanism is only partly known. The ordering matters: some questions should be able to settle the choice early, and some should be able to kill the choice early.

2. Points where the procedure stops with a definite verdict of no principled answer, and what the deliverable to the client looks like in that case — including how to present a probability that is only defined to within a band without it sounding like we do not know how to do our jobs.

3. Worked applications to three cases: (a) a fatigue crack that nucleates at a random point on the weld circumference and propagates inward; (b) a scratch from a dropped tool that is better modeled as a line segment whose position and orientation over the whole region are, as far as we know, uniform; and (c) a case where the generating mechanism is genuinely unknown — say, a crack in a composite whose initiation physics our client does not understand and has no data on. Case (c) should be the one where the procedure returns the answer I will not like.

4. An honest annotation of the procedure: which steps are real tests with determinate outputs, and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, so that my analysts know where they are still making the philosophical choice themselves and should say so in the model documentation.

5. A statement of what the procedure cannot do. In particular, I want you to address whether the invariance-style arguments that sometimes pick out a unique measure in these problems actually resolve the choice or merely relocate it, and what the procedure does when the symmetries of the physical setup underdetermine or, worse, conflict.

The vocabulary should be operational throughout — my analysts are engineers, not philosophers, and the procedure should translate whatever the principle of indifference and its critics have to say into questions they can ask about nucleation sites, tooling, and drop heights, with the philosophical content surfacing only where it genuinely changes what they do. The final test the procedure must pass: two analysts given the same physical scenario, different codebases, and no communication should reach the same number or the same documented refusal. I would also like a short paragraph on how to document each run so that a reviewer can audit the measure choice later without reconstructing the whole reasoning — a record of the mechanism assumption, the parameter it was made over, and what would have to be true physically for the assumption to fail. Please do not give me a reading list; give me the thing my team can run on Monday.
