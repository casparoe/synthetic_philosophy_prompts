I'm taking a graduate seminar in moral philosophy and writing a term paper on whether utilitarianism's critics have anything better to offer when they complain that it fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. My position going in is sympathetic to the critics, but when I sat down and catalogued my own verdicts on cases, they came out embarrassingly friendly to aggregation, and I can no longer tell whether a principled middle position exists or whether I'm just stitching together intuitions case by case. So I'd like you to do reflective equilibrium for me, honestly, and tell me what I actually think.

Here are my verdicts. A surgeon who could secretly harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five dying ones: flatly wrong, and wrong even in a possible world where it is never discovered and no trust in doctors erodes. A bystander who diverts a runaway trolley from five onto one: permissible — numbers matter there, and I have no impulse to deny it. A health ministry that spends a fixed budget on cheap treatments saving five rather than one expensive treatment saving one: fine. A city that imposes a mild one-day headache on a thousand residents to prevent a year of chronic pain for ten: fine. A sheriff who frames an innocent drifter to stop a riot that would kill twenty: wrong, and making it two hundred or twenty thousand doesn't budge me. And then the two cases that keep me up at night. First, the transplant scenario again, but instead of the surgeon choosing, all six are placed in a fair lottery in which each has an equal chance of being the one who dies so the other five live, everyone's consent obtained: I think this is actually permissible, not merely less bad than the surgeon's choosing. Second, a mandatory vaccination program that predictably kills one in a million recipients — a real, though unidentified, person — in order to prevent an outbreak that would kill thousands: fine. My tentative principle, which I don't trust, runs roughly like this: numbers count across persons when the burden on each is small or merely statistical, but a specific identified person's life may not be directly taken, and a fair ex ante lottery converts the latter into the former.

Test that principle against each of my verdicts, repair it if it can be repaired, and be blunt about which of my verdicts has to go if it can't. Then tell me what the repaired principle says about a case I haven't decided: a mining company can configure a new operation either so that one specific worker, known to management by name, faces near-certain death, or so that each of a thousand workers faces an independent one-in-a-thousand risk — identical expected deaths, radically different distribution. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle as you would finally state it, or your verdict that no single principle captures my verdicts — and give the reasoning afterward, with no preamble and without restating my question.
