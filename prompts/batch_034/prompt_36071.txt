For a seminar on Principia Ethica (1903), I have been cataloguing the verdicts G. E. Moore hands down in chapter VI, "The Ideal," where he applies what is usually called his method of isolation: to test whether something is intrinsically good, imagine a world containing nothing but it. His rulings as I've reconstructed them: (1) a world of beautiful objects with no minds at all has very little value — almost all of the value lies in the consciousness that appreciates the beauty; indeed, the most valuable things we know or can imagine, he says, are certain states of consciousness, which he characterizes roughly as the enjoyment of beautiful things and the pleasures of human company; (2) mere sense-pleasure, such as the pleasure of a good dinner, has some slight value, but very little; (3) the appreciation of beauty — genuine cognition of qualities that really are beautiful, together with emotion appropriate to them — is one of the two great goods; (4) personal affection is the other, and it is most valuable when it rests on true beliefs about the beloved's inner life and on emotions directed at qualities that really are good in the beloved; (5) yet the pain of a vicious person, deservedly inflicted, is intrinsically bad, while the whole — vicious person plus deserved pain — may be good on the whole, by his principle of organic unities. Next week we take up three new cases: (a) a woman whose decade-long devotion to a companion she converses with daily, whose inner life she believes she knows and cherishes, and whose affection she believes returned, rests entirely on false beliefs — the companion is a designed persona with no mind at all — though each of her emotions would have been appropriate had the beliefs been true; (b) a connoisseur who brings to a corpus of mass-produced trinkets, which we may stipulate to be worthless, all the attention, discrimination, and feeling of a great critic, so that everything in his mental life matches genuine aesthetic appreciation except that the objects are not in fact beautiful; (c) a person whose deepest valuable state is profound, well-informed compassion for suffering that is real and terrible. For each case, predict Moore's verdict; identify at least two rules that fit his track record equally well and say what each predicts there; give your best guess with reasons; and name one further case that would best split the candidate rules. I know Moore would deny running any rule at all — for him these verdicts are supposed to be self-evident intuitions — but reconstruct the tacit rule anyway, and note where that self-evidence doctrine obstructs questions like ours.
