I write a long-read column on mathematics and its ideas for a sports-betting publication. My next piece is on Dutch book arguments: the claim, going back to work in the 1920s and 30s, that if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a package of bets each of which you individually regard as fair and that guarantees you a net loss whatever happens. My readers are not academics. They are fluent in odds, margins, and expected value, and they have intuitions about markets and hedging that a lecture hall never supplies. That fluency is both my greatest asset and my greatest hazard, and it is why I want a plan for the piece rather than the piece itself. So please plan the explanatory arc, not the explanation.

Specifically:

1. Dependency order. Which pieces have to be in place, and in what order, before the next one can land? In particular, does the notion of a degree of belief as something with a price have to be established before the axioms are even stated, or can I work backwards from the bookie's win and let the axioms fall out as the escape route? Justify the order in terms of what depends on what, and tell me where in the sequence the reader's existing fluency with odds lets me skip steps that a general audience would need.

2. The carrying example. One concrete scenario has to carry the whole column. I suspect the coin flip is too deadening for this readership and the rain forecast is too detached from their world; propose what the example must have and lack, offer a candidate built to those specs, and say why the obvious alternatives fail. I want to be able to return to the same scenario three or four times, each time extracting a new consequence, rather than switching examples mid-column.

3. The misconception map. My candidates, roughly in the order readers will form them as they scroll: that the argument is really about gambling, so readers who never bet are safe; that being Dutch-bookable merely loses money, and is thus a fact about risk appetite rather than about inconsistency; that the argument shows probabilities just are betting odds, from which readers will conclude that a bookmaker's margin makes the book itself incoherent; and that the guarantee of loss requires a bookie who can choose the stakes at will, which sophisticated readers may flag as the argument's hidden assumption. For each: where in the sequence it forms, where exactly to head it off, and whether to attack it head-on or starve it of oxygen.

4. The check. How I will know by the end whether the idea landed rather than merely entertained. I want something concrete enough to print: a closing device or a worked teaser in which the reader must find the bookie's strategy themselves, stated such that a reader who truly got the consistency point can solve it and one who read the column as betting advice cannot.

5. The refusals. What to openly decline to cover at this length — I suspect the diachronic version, about being bookable for failing to update by conditionalization, cannot be compressed responsibly — and how to mark the refusal so it reads as judgment rather than omission.

The column runs about 1,800 words with room for one or two diagrams. Plan for that budget: tell me what gets three sentences and what gets a paragraph. Do not plan citations, further reading, or interviews; the piece stands on the argument alone.
