Next month I'm giving a talk at a workshop on judging the past, jointly run by my university's history and philosophy departments, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, roughly: the "man of his time" defence gets treated in public debate as one single move, but it's really three different moves that come apart -- (1) excusing the person on the grounds that they couldn't have known better, (2) explaining their conduct without evaluating it, and (3) claiming the standards by which we *commemorate* people should be indexed to the person's own era. I want to argue that (1) fails more often than people think, because "his time" almost always contained its dissenters: Quakers were petitioning against slavery throughout Jefferson's adult life while he owned hundreds of people, Columbus got shipped back to Spain in chains after his own colonists' complaints and the Dominicans were denouncing the cruelty from the pulpit within a decade, and Churchill's views on race and empire were attacked by his contemporaries -- so when the defence does work, I think it works by silently taking the opinion of the dominant group as "the time," which erases the moral testimony of everyone else alive then. That said, I want to concede something to the historians too: presentist blame as a historical exercise is often just bad history, and I suspect the really live question is about our practices of commemoration, not about blaming the dead, since it's unclear the dead can be wronged at all (that bit is genuinely underdeveloped in my thinking).

The audience: roughly half historians of the early-modern Atlantic world and of modern Britain -- people who work on slavery, empire, and the archive every day -- plus a few philosophers who work on moral responsibility, and a couple of grad students who'll argue with anything. The historians as a group are allergic to the whole "was he a good person by our standards" framing; several of them think moral evaluation of historical figures is a category mistake that isn't their job, and one has said in print that the entire debate is the public's problem, not ours. The philosophers know the Strawson reactive-attitudes material cold and will press me on whether my "could have known better" test is meant to be epistemic or moral, and on moral luck.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a historian of slavery or a responsibility theorist would actually put it (not the way a newspaper columnist would). For each one, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the questions I'm hoping nobody asks -- I can already think of one about whether the dead can be wronged, where my view is half-baked, and I suspect there's another about whether commemoration standards can really be seperated from blame. Also tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and what I should concede up front to buy goodwill. Don't bother giving me citations for the replies, I know the literature well enough to fill that part in myself.
