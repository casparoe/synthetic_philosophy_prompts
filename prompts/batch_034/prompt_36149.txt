I write novels, which means my entire craft rests on attributing beliefs, desires, and intentions to imagined people, so I have more than a spectator's stake in the debate over eliminative materialism. My sense is that the argument has been frozen for decades. The eliminativist holds that belief-desire psychology is a folk theory, comparable to phlogiston or demonic possession, that mature neuroscience will displace rather than reduce; the critic answers that the view is self-refuting, since asserting it requires believing it; the eliminativist rejoins that a theorist who denied the existence of witches could still entertain witch-beliefs, so the objection misfires. Each move is well known, and nobody moves. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not empirical but semantic at bottom. It may really turn on what counts as elimination versus mere revision of a concept — whether finding that nothing in the brain does exactly what "belief" predicts is like discarding phlogiston, or like the fate of "mass" after relativity, where we said the term picked out something real but not quite what we thought. If the disagreement is about the analogy rather than the science, more neuroscience cannot settle it, and the debate could be unwinding indefinitely. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, drawing on what each side in fact says; tell me whether my semantic reading is right or whether a genuine substantive question survives it; and be candid about what, if anything, would count as progress. I would also like to know whether the answer should change how I write interiority, or whether the novelist's practical question floats free of the metaphysics.
