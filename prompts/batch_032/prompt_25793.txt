I serve on my school's Honor Council, and we are currently deadlocked on a case that has split us four to four. I have been asked to draft the deciding opinion, and I would appreciate your help. The facts are these: a junior, M, made an explicit, serious promise to her close friend N that she would never disclose that N had plagiarized a major history paper. The two swore this to each other in private; by all accounts, M understood the promise as binding and N relied on it. Several weeks later, M, increasingly troubled by what she described to us as the weight of complicity, went to the history teacher and reported N's plagiarism. N is now facing academic sanctions, and N has filed a complaint alleging that M violated the Honor Code by breaking a solemn promise. Our Honor Code prohibits "dishonest or dishonorable conduct" but is silent on promises specifically. The Council is split: four members argue that a promise freely made creates an obligation that cannot be set aside merely because one later feels uncomfortable with it, and that M's breach was therefore dishonorable. The other four argue that a promise to conceal wrongdoing is void from the start — that no one can be bound to aid dishonesty — and that M in fact acted honorably by ultimately telling the truth. I need to circulate a short written opinion (maximum 400 words) before our meeting on Friday. It must recommend a clear finding — either that M's conduct was or was not an honor violation — and it must give a fair, serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The opinion should not merely invoke rules or precedent (we have little of either) but should engage the deeper question: under what conditions does a promise genuinely bind, and does a promise to conceal another's wrongdoing meet those conditions?
