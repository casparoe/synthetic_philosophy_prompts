I'm a retired neuroscientist and I've been getting into philosophy of mind lately — reading Dennett, Frankish, Chalmers, the whole lot. My old lab partner (she does comparative cognition) has been humouring me with her verdicts on which creatures are phenomenally conscious. Here's what she's said so far:

Conscious: humans, chimpanzees, dogs, octopuses, crows.
Not conscious: salmon, honeybees, current large language models, a human in deep dreamless sleep, a human under propofol anesthesia.

She's pretty consistant about it and she's clearly thought this through, but she's never spelled out the rule she's running. I can see at least two or three principles that fit her verdicts equally well. The new case I want to pin her down with: an artificial system that runs a global workspace architecture, maintains a self-model it uses for planning, and passes a battery of behavioral tests for flexible, domain-general learning — but it's running on silicon, no biology at all. What would she say, and why?

I'd like your response in these sections:

1. Candidate Rules (about 300 words): Lay out the two or three most plausible principles that fit her verdicts. Give each a name and state it clearly.

2. What Each Rule Predicts (about 200 words): Apply each rule to the silicon global-workspace system. Say where they agree and where they diverge.

3. Best Guess (about 200 words): Which rule do you think she's actually running, and why? What in her pattern of verdicts tips you off?

4. The Decisive Case (about 150 words): Name one further case — real or invented — that would cleanly separate the leading candidate rules, and say what her answer would mean.

Think carefully and reason step by step. Respond in the style of an expert philosopher of mind who's comfortable with the empirical details.
