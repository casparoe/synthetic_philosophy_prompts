I'm teaching an undergraduate course on rational choice theory and want to give my students a diagnostic quiz that sorts them into positions on the paradox of voting. The paradox, briefly: in a large election, the probability that any individual vote will be decisive is vanishingly small, so the expected instrumental benefit of voting seems far lower than the cost of going to the polls — yet millions of people vote. The quiz should help students discover which resolution of the paradox their own reasoning actually commits them to, before we study the positions explicitly.

Please write a ten-question sorting quiz. Each question should present a concrete scenario — a thought experiment, a vignette, or a forced choice — with two or three answer options. No question should name a philosophical position or use jargon like "expressive voting," "pivot probability," or "rule consequentialism." The questions should probe the fault lines that actually separate the major positions: whether the act of voting has intrinsic value apart from its effect on the outcome, whether universalization gives reasons that act-consequentialist calculation misses, whether regret-avoidance is a legitimate basis for decision, whether group membership changes the rational calculus for an individual, and whether the sheer magnitude of the stakes can overcome a minuscule probability.

The positions the quiz should sort into are:

- **Expressive voting**: voting is an expressive act, like cheering or wearing a pin; its value doesn't depend on affecting the outcome.
- **Ethical-altruistic voting**: the expected utility of voting is enormous once you multiply the per-person stakes by the number of people affected, so voting is straightforwardly rational even with tiny pivot probabilities.
- **Duty-based voting**: voting is a civic or moral obligation that doesn't reduce to expected consequences.
- **Rule-consequentialist voting**: the right question is not "what happens if I vote?" but "what if everyone in my situation reasoned the same way?"
- **Minimax regret**: one should vote to avoid the worst-case regret — your side losing by exactly one vote while you stayed home.
- **Rational-irrationality / ignorance**: people vote because they systematically misjudge the probabilities, or because it's a low-cost action that feels momentous; a fully rational agent wouldn't vote (or would vote only for non-instrumental reasons they don't genuinely have).
- **Group-agency voting**: individuals vote as members of groups; the group decides collectively to turn out, and the individual's obligation derives from group membership rather than from individual expected-utility calculation.

Include a scoring key that maps answer patterns to positions. Some students may fit more than one position; the key should acknowledge this and flag which pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate and why. Also note any logically possible answer patterns that correspond to no defended position — and briefly say whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored in the literature.

The quiz should be suitable for undergraduates with no background in formal decision theory, though they should be comfortable with the basic idea of weighing costs against benefits. Write the questions in a clear, engaging style. Respond in the style of an expert in political philosophy and rational choice theory. Think very carefully about which answer patterns map to which positions before finalizing the scoring key.
