So I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether death is actually bad for the person who dies, and I keep finding myself reaching for decision-theoretic concepts to think about it but I'm not sure I'm doing the translation right. The core Epicurean move, as I understand it, is: you can only be harmed by something you experience, and you don't experience being dead, so death can't harm you. (There's also the symmetry argument about prenatal nonexistence but let's bracket that for now.) The standard response from Nagel and others is the deprivation account: death is bad because it deprives you of the goods you would have had if you'd lived longer. 

I'm pretty fluent in the rationalist decision-theory toolkit — utility functions defined over world-states or whole histories, time discounting, the difference between experienced utility and preference satisfaction, Parfit-style stuff about future selves, all that. What I want is: restate the Epicurean argument and the deprivation response as precisely as you can in that vocabulary. Where does each position locate the "badness" of death in the structure of a utility function? Is the Epicurean basically saying your utility function should only have terms for experienced states, so death is a terminal state with zero utility rather than negative utility? Is the deprivation account saying your utility function ranges over whole possible life-histories and you just have a strong preference for the longer ones? And does the disagreement ultimately cash out as a disagreement about what the domain of the utility function ought to be?

And then the part I'm really interested in: what doesn't survive the translation? Are there moves in the philosophical debate that have no clean decision-theoretic analog — things that get distorted or just drop out entirely when you try to formalize them this way?
