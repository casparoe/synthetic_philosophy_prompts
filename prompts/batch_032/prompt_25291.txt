I'm in an epistemology seminar and we just did Fitch's paradox of knowability and I cannot stop thinking about it. The argument is so short — like barely a paragraph — but it seems to prove something completely absurd, and every way out I try just makes things worse. I'd love someone to walk me through this.

Here's the setup. A lot of anti-realists about truth (Dummett, etc.) hold what's called the knowability principle: for any true proposition, it's possible in principle for someone to know it. Not that anyone does know it, just that there's no truth that's essentially unknowable. Formally: if p is true, then possibly someone knows p. Seems modest enough.

Now here's Fitch's argument, step by step:

(1) Assume the knowability principle: if p is true, then it's possible to know p. (p → ◇Kp)
(2) Now consider any truth that happens to be unknown. Let q be the proposition "p is true and nobody knows p" — i.e., q = (p ∧ ¬Kp).
(3) Suppose q is true. (Surely some truths are unknown — I don't know the exact number of stars, but there's a fact of the matter.)
(4) By the knowability principle applied to q, it's possible to know q: ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp).
(5) Knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct. So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) entails Kp and K(¬Kp).
(6) Knowledge is factive: K(¬Kp) entails ¬Kp.
(7) So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) entails Kp ∧ ¬Kp, which is a contradiction.
(8) Since a contradiction is impossible, K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible. So ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp).
(9) But by (4) we had ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Contradiction.
(10) Therefore our assumption at (3) — that q is true — must be false. So ¬(p ∧ ¬Kp).
(11) ¬(p ∧ ¬Kp) is classically equivalent to p → Kp.
(12) So the knowability principle entails that all truths are actually known.

I mean, what? The conclusion is that if you think all truths are knowable, you're commited to saying we already know everything. That's wild. Our professor mentioned a few responses — restricting the principle to "non-epistemic" propositions, going intuitionistic, denying that (3) is ever true — but each one felt like it was giving up something huge. Like, if you restrict the principle to non-epistemic propositions, aren't you just admitting the principle was false as stated? And if you go intuitionistic, you dodge the derivation but you still owe an explanation of what's wrong with the classical steps — they feel valid.

I think I want to keep the knowability principle in some form, and I definitely want to keep the idea that there are unknown truths. So I guess I have to find a flaw in the derivation. But where? Is there a step here that's less innocent than it looks? And what's the least painful fix — the one that preserves the most of the original anti-realist motivation?
