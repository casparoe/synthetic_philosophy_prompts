Here is a pattern in ordinary ethical thought about food that I find puzzling, and I want to know what theory of moral responsibility — or of value, or of both — it presupposes, if any coherent theory does.

Consider the following. When someone adopts a vegetarian or vegan diet for ethical reasons, they typically eliminate beef, pork, chicken, eggs, and dairy. They do not, however, typically extend the same concern to the animals killed in producing the plant foods they eat instead: the mice and voles crushed by combine harvesters, the insects killed by pesticides, the ground-nesting birds destroyed by mowing, the amphibians drained out of rice paddies. These deaths are not hidden or unknown — anyone who thinks about agriculture for a few minutes knows they occur — yet they are treated as morally different in kind from the deaths involved in raising livestock. The same person who will scrutinize a restaurant menu for hidden dairy will eat a loaf of bread without asking how many field animals died to produce the wheat.

Similarly, animal welfare campaigns, certification schemes, and legislation focus overwhelmingly on the conditions of farmed animals: cage-free eggs, gestation-crate bans, humane slaughter requirements. There is no "harvest-mouse-friendly" label for wheat, no campaign to reduce insect deaths in soybean farming, no regulatory standard for minimizing combine-harvester kills. The suffering of wild animals in crop fields appears to have no moral claim on us comparable to the suffering of a pig or a laying hen, even when the number of individual animals affected by crop production is vast.

Now consider a different asymmetry within the same domain. If you ask an ethically motivated consumer whether it is worse to eat beef or chicken, a common answer is that chicken is less bad — the animal is smaller, its carbon footprint is lower, perhaps less total suffering is involved per meal. But if you count the number of individual animals killed, the opposite is true: a single steer yields hundreds of meals, while a single chicken yields only a few. On any view that treats each animal's death as a distinct harm to be counted, eating chicken is far worse than eating beef. Yet the intuition that switching from beef to chicken is an ethical improvement is widespread even among reflective people who are aware of the numbers.

I want to know: what theory of moral responsibility, or of value, or of both, does this whole pattern of judgment presuppose? Is there a single coherent view that makes sense of treating livestock deaths as morally weighty, field deaths as negligible, and beef as worse than chicken — all at once? Or does the pattern turn out to presuppose inconsistent commitments, such that at least one of these judgments has to be revised?

Please take a definite position. Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where more than one theory could license the same pattern. If the pattern is incoherent, say which judgment should be abandoned and what the practice would look like if we replaced the implicit theory with a better one.
