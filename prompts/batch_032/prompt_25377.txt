I'm in a graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and we've spent the past three weeks on Dutch book arguments. I've reconstructed what I take to be the core argument for probabilism, and I've found that pushing it just a little further lands me somewhere I cannot accept. I'm hoping someone can walk me through exactly where the trouble enters and what the major options are for getting out.

Here is the argument as I understand it:

(1) If an agent's credences violate the probability axioms, then there exists a Dutch book against that agent: a finite set of bets, each of which the agent considers fair or favorable at their stated credences, whose net payoff is strictly negative in every possible world.
(2) Susceptibility to a Dutch book is sufficient for irrationality — a rational agent cannot be made to accept a set of bets that guarantees a loss.
(3) Therefore, rationality requires that credences satisfy the probability axioms.

So far this is the standard story. But now:

(4) Among the probability axioms is the requirement that every logical truth receive credence 1.
(5) No cognitively bounded agent — no human being, and plausibly no finite computational system of any kind — can assign credence 1 to all logical truths, because there are logical truths far too complex for any such agent to represent, entertain, or recognize as logically true.
(6) Therefore, the Dutch book argument entails that every actual agent is irrational.
(7) A normative theory that condemns every actual agent as irrational is self-defeating: it cannot guide the belief-formation or decision-making of the agents it is supposed to govern.
(8) So the Dutch book argument proves too much, and either the argument itself or its standard interpretation must be rejected.

I find (1) through (4) individually plausible, and (5) seems like a straightforward fact about our cognitive and computational limits. (6) appears to follow, (7) seems like a reasonable constraint on normative theories, and (8) follows from the rest. So the argument looks valid to me. But I do not want to give up on Dutch book arguments entirely — they seem to capture something deep about coherence, and I suspect the problem lies in how we interpret them rather than in their mathematical core. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially interested in whether the real pressure falls on (2), on (4), or on (5), and what the main positions in the literature would say about each option. If I have to choose, I would most like to keep (1) and (3) intact, but I want to know what I am paying for that.
