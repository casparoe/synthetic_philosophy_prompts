I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and I keep encountering some version of the claim that "rational agents cannot agree to disagree." The slogan gets deployed in remarkably different contexts: in AI alignment discussions, it is offered as a reason to expect convergent beliefs among sufficiently advanced systems that share the same evidence; in epistemic-modesty arguments, it is wielded to suggest that persistent disagreement with an epistemic peer is evidence of one's own irrationality; in forecasting communities, it is invoked to justify the claim that aggregation mechanisms should, in the limit, eliminate all disagreement. I have started to suspect that the same sentence is doing very different work in these different arguments, and that the apparent force of the slogan comes from sliding between distinct claims without noticing the joints.

I would like you to disambiguate "rational agents cannot agree to disagree." Lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing. In particular, distinguish among: (a) claims that are mathematical theorems about ideal Bayesian reasoners who share a common prior; (b) claims that extend the result to computationally bounded agents, or to agents who do not literally share a prior but whose priors are in some sense "close enough"; (c) normative claims about what a rational person ought to do upon discovering that an epistemic peer disagrees with them; and (d) claims about what persistent disagreement in a community tells us about the epistemic health of that community. For each reading, say whether it is a proven theorem, a substantive philosophical thesis, an empirical conjecture, or something else, and indicate whether it is defensible or whether it collapses under scrutiny. Finally, tell me which reading someone is most likely invoking — whether they know it or not — when they use the slogan to argue that persistent, widespread disagreement on a topic is evidence that the discourse around that topic is epistemically broken.
