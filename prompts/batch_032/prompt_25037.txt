I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy trying to settle on a dissertation topic within value theory, and I've become increasingly convinced that the heterogeneity problem for hedonism is both underappreciated and potentially decisive — not just for classical hedonism but for any view that treats pleasure or well-being as a unified kind. I'm having trouble, however, figuring out where exactly to direct my efforts over the next three years, and I'd like your help constructing a defended research agenda.

Here's how I understand the landscape. The heterogeneity problem, at its core, is the challenge of saying what all pleasures have in common such that they deserve a single name and a single normative role. The pleasure of finishing a proof, the pleasure of a backrub, the pleasure of revenge, the pleasure of a drug high, the pleasure of watching a child take her first steps — these seem phenomenologically, functionally, and neurologically disparate. If there is no common thread, then hedonism either collapses into an unstructured list of disparate goods (at which point it is not really hedonism anymore) or it is making an unmotivated claim that these things all happen to be good in the same way. And the problem radiates outward: if pleasure lacks a unified nature, then any welfare theory that gives pleasure a central role — even a non-hedonistic one — owes an account of what it is talking about.

The main families of response I am aware of are: (1) phenomenal accounts that try to identify a common felt quality or a shared hedonic tone across all pleasures; (2) attitudinal accounts that locate the commonality not in how pleasures feel but in the subject's pro-attitude toward the experience — roughly, pleasure is an experience you want to be having while you are having it; (3) functional or representational accounts that identify pleasure with a kind of signal about what is good for the organism; (4) pluralist or disjunctive accounts that accept heterogeneity and deny that pleasure needs a unified essence, treating the category as a family-resemblance concept or a merely nominal kind; and (5) revisionary approaches that try to dissolve the problem by rethinking what hedonism actually requires — for instance, by arguing that hedonism only needs a common value-making property, not a common phenomenology.

I have about three years and a background stronger in philosophy of mind and philosophical psychology than in neurobiology or empirical affective science. I want a defended ranking of which family of response — or which specific proposal within a family — is most worth spending a dissertation on. For each candidate, I would like to know: how tractable is it for someone with my background, and what methods would it actually draw on? How much would a successful account change downstream debates in value theory, or would it mostly remain of interest to card-carrying hedonists? What would count as genuine progress over three years, and what would be an early sign that a line of inquiry has stalled or is unlikely to repay the investment? Are there approaches I am missing that cut across these categories or that have emerged in the last decade? And finally, which approach would you explicitly deprioritize even if it is philosophically interesting, because the ratio of insight gained to effort required simply does not justify it?
