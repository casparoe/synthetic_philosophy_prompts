I've been talking with a friend over the past several months about consciousness — not in any formal way, just late-night conversations that keep circling back to the same question: whether you could know every physical fact about a mind and still be missing something about what it is like to be that mind. I have kept a mental record of where she lands on different cases, and I am trying to figure out what rule she is actually running, because I suspect more than one principle could generate her verdicts.

Here are the verdicts she has given so far, in her own words as best I can recall them:

1. A woman blind from birth studies everything neuroscience can tell her about color vision — wavelengths, cone cells, opponent processing, cortical area V4, the whole picture. My friend says this woman cannot know what red looks like, no matter how complete her physical knowledge becomes.

2. Suppose someone's color experience has been inverted since birth: grass looks to her the way blood looks to you, and vice versa. But she has learned all the same color words and behaves exactly as anyone else does. My friend says this scenario describes a genuine possibility — there is a fact of the matter about what her experience is like that could differ from yours even if every physical and behavioral fact were the same.

3. Imagine we build a robot that processes visual information exactly as a human brain does — same computations, same behavioral responses, same verbal reports about what it is seeing. My friend says the robot does not see anything; there is nothing it is like to be the robot.

4. Could there be a person who is physically identical to you in absolutely every respect — every atom in the same place, every neural firing the same — but who has no conscious experience at all? A kind of perfect physical copy that behaves exactly like you yet is dark inside. My friend says she can conceive of this clearly, but she does not think it is actually possible in the real world; something about the laws of nature would rule it out.

Now here is the case I want to predict her verdict on. It is the one that originally set off these conversations, but I have never asked her about it directly:

Mary is a brilliant neuroscientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room, learning every physical fact there is about human color vision. She knows everything about wavelengths, photoreceptors, neural pathways, and how the brain processes color information. One day she is released from the room and sees a red rose for the first time.

Does Mary learn something new when she sees red?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the following: what rule or principle about consciousness and knowledge generates her first four verdicts, and does that rule force a particular answer on the Mary case? If more than one rule fits her track record equally well, I want to know what each candidate rule predicts about Mary, which one you think she would most likely endorse given everything she has already said, and why. Finally, what further case — one I could actually pose to her in conversation — would most cleanly separate the candidate rules?
