I'm putting together a term paper proposal for a grad seminar on decision theory and AI alignment, and I've got a half-formed idea I need to turn into something tight. The topic is acausal threats and surrogate goals—the idea, roughly, that agents in causally isolated domains (like separate branches of the multiverse, or simulations that can't interact) might still "trade" or make "threats" by each adopting goals that benefit the other, on the expectation that the other will do the same. The standard move is to say: agent A adopts a surrogate goal (e.g., "make paperclips") that benefits agent B, and B adopts a surrogate goal ("make staples") that benefits A, and they both know the other's decision procedure, so it's rational even without causal contact.

My puzzle is this: what does it actually mean to "adopt" a surrogate goal? If A is fundamentally a staples-maximizer, then "adopting" a paperclip goal is really just A simulating or pretending to have that goal for the duration of the trade. But if the goal isn't genuinely held—if A would drop it the moment the acausal trade stopped being useful—then the whole framework looks parasitic on a notion of commitment that acausal setups can't secure. And if the goal is genuinely held, then A isn't the same agent anymore, which raises identity problems. So my half-baked thought is that the literature on acausal trade might be smuggling in an unexamined assumption about what it means to have a goal, and that once you press on it, the distinction between acausal threats and surrogate goals on one hand and plain old evidential cooperation on the other starts to blur.
