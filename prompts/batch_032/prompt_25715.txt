I've been thinking about a pattern in moral judgment that I can't find a name for, and I'm wondering whether philosophers have already mapped this out.

Most actions seem to sort into three buckets: the required (don't lie, keep your promises), the forbidden (don't steal, don't hurt people for fun), and the neutral (what to eat for breakfast, which route to walk home). But I keep noticing actions that don't fit. A stranger needs a bone marrow donation and you happen to be a match. If you go through with it, people call it heroic. If you don't, nobody says you did anything wrong — it wasn't your duty. The same goes for jumping into a freezing river to save someone else's dog, or spending your Saturdays volunteering at a hospice when you could be resting. These aren't neutral in the way picking a cereal is — they feel morally weighty — but they also aren't demanded the way ordinary obligations are.

What puzzles me is the asymmetry: you can't be blamed for skipping them, but you can be praised for doing them. And the praise has a strange fragility to it. If you keep bringing up that bone marrow donation, the bragging seems to spoil the praiseworthiness in a way that doesn't happen when someone merely mentions having done their duty. That suggests this isn't just a fourth point on a single scale — the category might have its own internal logic.

Is there an established term for this class of actions? I'm not just after a label. I want to know how the boundary is drawn. Does an action have to involve significant sacrifice to count, or could small kindnesses qualify? Where does this category shade into ordinary politeness on one side and into obligation on the other? And if someone performs the action for selfish reasons — say, donating bone marrow purely for the attention — does it still belong to the category, or does the motive determine the classification?
