"The inefficacy objection says that an individual's action — like not eating a burger or skipping one flight — makes no perceptible difference to the overall harm. One less burger doesn't reduce the number of cows slaughtered, because the meat industry operates at such a massive scale that individual demand doesn't shift production. Similarly, one person's emissions don't measurably affect climate outcomes. So if your action doesn't make any difference, you can't have an obligation to do it.

The most powerful response is the expected utility argument, developed by Derek Parfit. The idea is that even if the chance your individual action makes a difference is tiny, the harm at stake is enormous. For example, if there's a one-in-a-million chance that your decision to go vegetarian is the one that pushes the industry past a threshold where they reduce production, the expected harm you prevent is still huge when you multiply that tiny probability by the massive suffering at stake. This is basically the same point Parfit makes with his harmless torturers thought experiment — each torturer's contribution is imperceptible, but together they cause real harm, so each individual action does cause a share of the harm. Therefore the inefficacy objection fails and we do have obligations to avoid participating in these practices."
