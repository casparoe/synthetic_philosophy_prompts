I'm teaching an undergraduate epistemology module next term, and I want to use Kurosawa's *Rashomon* (1950) to stage the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony. The film presents four conflicting accounts of a violent encounter in a forest — the bandit's, the wife's, the dead samurai's (spoken through a medium), and the woodcutter's — and famously never resolves which, if any, is true. My thought is that the viewer's predicament mirrors the epistemological question directly: when all you have is testimony, and the testifiers contradict each other, are you forced back onto perceptual and inferential grounds to decide whom to believe (reductionism), or does the very fact that you lack independent access to the events show that testimony must function as a basic, non-reducible source of justification, however unsettling that is when the sources clash (anti-reductionism)?

I'd like a reading of the film that takes both the epistemology and the film seriously — not just using the film as an illustration of positions the students already know, but asking whether the film dramatizes something the philosophical debate misses. In particular: does *Rashomon* suggest that the reductionist/anti-reductionist dichotomy is itself inadequate to cases of radically conflicting testimony, where the usual background conditions that make testimony reliable — sincerity, competence, shared norms — have collapsed? And what should my students take away from the fact that the film ends not with an epistemic resolution but with the woodcutter's decision to adopt an abandoned baby, an act that seems to bypass the question of who is telling the truth in favor of a moral response to the despair the question produces?

Please keep the response to around 800 words; I need something I can adapt into a lecture plan and a handout.
