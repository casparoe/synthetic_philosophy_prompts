I've been turning over a question in my mind for a while now, and I'd like to see it argued out properly. The question is whether death is actually bad for the person who dies. I don't mean whether dying is painful or whether death is bad for the people left behind — I mean whether being dead, the state of not existing anymore, is a harm to the one who's dead.

The line of thought I keep coming back to goes like this: you can only be harmed by something if you exist to experience the harm. But when you're dead, you don't exist. So there's no "you" there to be harmed. And while you're alive, death isn't present — it hasn't happened yet. So it seems like death and the person never overlap in the right way for a harm to occur. I've also wondered about the symmetry argument: the time before we were born doesn't bother any of us, and that was also a period of nonexistence. Why should the time after we die be any different?

I know this is an old line of reasoning — I've since learned it goes back to Epicurus — but I've never studied philosophy formally, and I'd like to see someone really build out the case. Please argue for the position that death is not bad for the person who dies. I want the strongest version of the argument you can give: address the obvious objections (like the deprivation account, which says death is bad because it deprives you of goods you would have enjoyed), handle the asymmetry between prenatal and postmortem nonexistence if you think it matters, and don't shy away from the practical implications if the argument succeeds. I'm not looking for a balanced survey — I want to see the position defended as rigorously as possible.
