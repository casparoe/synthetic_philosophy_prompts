I teach a graduate seminar on vagueness and the sorites paradox, and this term a colleague from the linguistics department has been sitting in. She has not declared allegiance to any particular theory—epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism—but she has been remarkably consistent in the verdicts she offers when I press her on individual cases. I have kept a running list:

(1) Clear cases: "A man with zero hairs is bald"—true; "A man with a hundred thousand hairs is bald"—false.
(2) The inductive premise: "For any n, if a man with n hairs is bald, then a man with n+1 hairs is bald"—false.
(3) Sharp boundaries: "There is some number n such that a man with n hairs is bald and a man with n+1 hairs is not bald"—true.
(4) Knowability: "We cannot know, of any particular n, that it marks the boundary between the bald and the non-bald"—true.
(5) Excluded middle: "Either Jones is bald or Jones is not bald," where Jones is a borderline case—true.
(6) Borderline cases: "The predicate 'bald' has borderline cases"—true.
(7) Higher-order vagueness: "The predicate 'borderline bald' itself has borderline cases"—true.

Next week I plan to ask her about bivalence directly. The case will be: "Consider Smith, who is a borderline case of baldness—not clearly bald, not clearly not bald. Is the sentence 'Smith is bald' either true or false? That is, does it possess a classical truth-value, setting aside whether we can know which one?"

I have my own prediction, but I would like an independent assessment before I put her on the spot. Please predict her answer to the Smith case. Identify the two or three leading theories of vagueness that are jointly consistent with her seven verdicts above, explain which of them diverge on the bivalence question and why, and—if you think the Smith case does not fully settle the matter—name the further case or question that would most cleanly discriminate among the remaining candidates. Keep your response to no more than 400 words.
