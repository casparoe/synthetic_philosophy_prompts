In a seminar on time and physics, we worked through an argument that seems to derive a radical metaphysical conclusion from special relativity alone. I've reconstructed it as follows:

(1) According to special relativity, simultaneity is relative to an inertial frame: two events simultaneous in one frame need not be simultaneous in another.
(2) For any two spacelike-separated events, there exists some inertial frame in which they are simultaneous.
(3) Take an event in my future — say, my deciding tomorrow morning whether to accept a job offer. There exists a distant observer moving at an appropriate velocity such that, in her frame, my decision is simultaneous with her lighting a candle right now.
(4) If an event is simultaneous with something happening now — something whose reality we do not doubt — then that event must itself be real, not merely potential or not-yet-existent.
(5) So my future decision is already real.
(6) The same reasoning applies to any future event whatsoever.
(7) Therefore, all events — past, present, and future — are equally real. The universe is a block; temporal passage is an illusion (eternalism).
(8) But if the future is already fixed and passage is an illusion, then deliberation — the experience of weighing options as though more than one could come about — is systematically misleading. And if deliberation is systematically misleading, it is unclear how we can be responsible for choices that were always already there in the block.

I find (7) and (8) hard to accept, but (1)–(6) each seem individually plausible. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I want to preserve the reality of temporal passage and the coherence of deliberation, what is my best move — and what else must I give up to make it?
