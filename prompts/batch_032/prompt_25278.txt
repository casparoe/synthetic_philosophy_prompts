Explain the Ellsberg paradox. What does it reveal about ambiguity aversion, and does it show that ambiguity aversion is irrational?

[Student answer]
The Ellsberg paradox is a thought experiment where you have an urn with 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are either black or yellow in unknown proportion. You're offered two pairs of bets. In the first pair, you choose between betting on red (win $100 if red is drawn) or betting on black (win $100 if black is drawn). Most people prefer red, because they know the exact probability (1/3) whereas the probability of black is unknown (somewhere between 0 and 2/3). In the second pair, you choose between betting on red-or-yellow or black-or-yellow. Now most people prefer black-or-yellow, because that gives a known 2/3 probability while red-or-yellow gives an unknown probability. This pattern of choices violates the Sure-Thing Principle, which says that if you prefer A to B when some event E occurs and also when E doesn't occur, you should prefer A to B overall. The paradox shows that people are ambiguity-averse: they prefer known probabilities over unknown ones. And because this violates the Sure-Thing Principle, which is a basic axiom of rational choice, the Ellsberg paradox demonstrates that ambiguity aversion is irrational. Rational agents should form subjective probabilities for unknown events and then maximize expected utility.
