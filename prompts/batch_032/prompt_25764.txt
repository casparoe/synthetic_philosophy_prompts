I'm a novelist. I've been asked to give a ninety-minute session at a writers' retreat next month. The audience is about twenty-five working writers — novelists, poets, essayists — with no background in philosophy or computer science, but they're unsettled by what large language models mean for their craft. The organizer asked me to address the nature of creativity itself, not just the practical threats.

I don't want to give a survey of opinions. I want to build an explanation that takes them somewhere they haven't been. Plan the explanatory arc. What idea has to be in place first, before the question of machine creativity even makes proper sense? What single example or case should carry the whole thing — and what must it have and what must it lack? Which misconceptions will this audience predictably bring (I'm guessing something about "real art requires suffering" or "it's all just recombination") and at what point in the sequence should I head each one off? What should I openly refuse to settle at this level? And give me one question I can ask near the end to see whether they've actually grasped the distinction I'm after, rather than just feeling defensive or reassured.
