My behavorial econ professor summarized the Allais paradox in lecture like this: "Allais showed that the independence axiom of expected utility theory is descriptively false. He asked people to choose between a sure $1 million and a gamble with an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing — most took the sure thing. Then he asked them to choose between an 11% chance of $1 million and a 10% chance of $5 million — most took the $5 million gamble. But if you apply the independence axiom, the two choice pairs are identical except that an 89% chance of $1 million is added to both options in the first pair. So people's preferences flip based on an irrelevant common factor, which means the axiom fails. And since the independence axiom is the core of rational choice under uncertainty, this shows that expected utility theory isn't just descriptively inadequate — it's normatively broken, and we should replace it with something like prospect theory." I get that the descriptive point is solid, but the leap to "normatively broken" feels like it needs more argument. Can you go through this and tell me what a decision theorist would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the normative conclusion follows?
