I've spent the better part of this semester in a political philosophy seminar watching the same exchange play out in different costumes. Someone presents a version of utilitarianism that seems to handle a counterexample — perhaps by moving from act to rule, or by incorporating a sophisticated account of well-being, or by arguing that the alleged counterexample rests on a misdescription of the consequences. Someone else objects that the fix still treats the boundaries between persons as morally insignificant in a way that no amount of clever aggregation can fix: the theory remains willing to impose a serious loss on one person for the sake of a slightly larger benefit spread across many others, and that willingness, the objector says, is the problem, not a downstream feature of any particular utilitarian formula. The utilitarian replies that the objector is smuggling in a deontological intuition and dressing it up as a structural feature of practical reason — that any moral theory will have to weigh competing claims, and the separateness of persons does not by itself tell us why aggregation across lives is impermissible when aggregation across moments within a life is not. We have read Parfit, we have read Rawls, we have read Nozick, we have read the contemporary defenders on both sides, and I cannot tell whether anyone has moved an inch since the 1970s.

My question is not who is right. My question is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive moral disagreement that turns on a hidden empirical or metaphysical claim — for instance, about whether personal identity has the kind of deep unity that would make the person-versus-aggregation distinction metaphysically robust rather than merely intuitive? Is it a disagreement about what counts as a moral reason in the first place, such that each side's starting criteria rule out the other's conclusion before the argument begins? Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about fairness and impartiality that neither side can argue for without begging the question? Or is it, as I sometimes suspect late at night, a disagreement about which analogies are apt — about whether trade-offs across lives are relevantly like trade-offs within a life — where neither side has a non-question-begging way to establish which analogy controls?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, grounded in what the two sides actually say when they press their strongest objections, and an honest answer about what, if anything, could count as progress. If the answer is that we should stop expecting a resolution and instead treat the separateness of persons as a fixed point that distinguishes moral theories rather than a consideration that could decide between them, I want to hear that argued for rather than merely sighed.
