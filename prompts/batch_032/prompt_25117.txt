I serve on my university's Research Integrity Committee. Over the past three years we've investigated five labs in psychology and neuroscience for questionable research practices — running underpowered studies, dropping conditions that fail to reach significance, and filing null results away as "pilot data" that never see the light of day. In every case the PIs offered essentially the same defense. They didn't claim their practices were optimal. They claimed that labs adhering scrupulously to best practices publish slower, place their graduate students worse, and lose grants to the labs that don't. The individual incentive, they argued, points one way; the collective outcome points another. No single lab can unilaterally adopt stricter standards without burning its own people.

The committee is now weighing a concrete proposal: a department-wide mandate that all confirmatory studies be preregistered with a publicly timestamped analysis plan before data collection begins, with any deviation from that plan flagged and explained in the resulting publication. The alternative is to retain our current voluntary preregistration badge system — which, by the committee's own assessment, has done nothing to alter the underlying dynamics.

I need to draft the committee's recommendation memo for the Dean, no more than 500 words. It should recommend either the mandate, the voluntary status quo, or some third option, with a clear rationale. The strongest objection to the mandate is that it amounts to the university dictating scientific method to its faculty, and that preregistration suits hypothesis-testing work but actively stifles exploratory, computational, and qualitative research. The strongest argument for the mandate is that the status quo is a textbook case of individually rational choices producing a collectively self-defeating equilibrium — and that only a binding coordination mechanism, not a voluntary nudge, can break a trap of that structure. Engage both arguments seriously, and make sure the recommendation is something the Dean could actually act on.
