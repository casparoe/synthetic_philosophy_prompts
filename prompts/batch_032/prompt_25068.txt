I serve on the board of a small philanthropic foundation that allocates grants across cause areas, with a focus on interventions aimed at reducing existential risk. At our last meeting, a fellow board member — a statistician by training — argued that we should stop using expected-value models to compare grants in this area. His argument, as I understood it, ran as follows.

The Pasadena game is a decision-theoretic puzzle in which a gamble lacks a well-defined expected value: the infinite sum that would represent its expectation is conditionally convergent, meaning it can be rearranged to converge to any real number, or to diverge, depending on the order in which the terms are summed. Long-term futures, he argued, present payoff distributions that are at least as pathologically unordered as the Pasadena game — there is no privileged way to sequence the possible states of the far future — and so expected-value calculations across those futures are similarly indeterminate. He concluded that quantitative expected-value comparisons in existential-risk grantmaking are not merely difficult but meaningless, and that we should rely on structured qualitative judgment instead.

I find the analogy intuitively forceful, but I lack the decision-theoretic background to assess whether it actually carries the weight he puts on it. I would like you to audit the analogy in detail: what maps onto what between the Pasadena game and longtermist cause prioritization, where the mapping breaks, and which features of the Pasadena game the analogy quietly imports that the target domain lacks. Most importantly, tell me whether his conclusion — that we should abandon expected-value reasoning for existential-risk grantmaking — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether a more modest lesson is warranted.

Please also indicate how confident you are in the key claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
