I've been trying to get my life together lately — you know how it is, too many options, too little time — and a friend recommended I read some Herbert Simon, some Gigerenzer, the whole bounded rationality thing. The idea of satisficing really clicked for me: instead of agonizing over the optimal choice, just set a threshold and take the first thing that clears it. I started doing this with everything from which restaurant to pick to which job offer to take, and honestly it felt liberating. Like I was finally working with my actual psychology instead of against it.

But then I was talking to my brother (who's an econ PhD student, naturally, and cannot let anything go) and he hit me with this argument that I've been turning over in my head for weeks. He laid it out like this: (1) A satisficer picks the first option that meets or exceeds their aspiration level. (2) That aspiration level has to come from somewhere — you have to choose it, or at least adopt it. (3) When you adopt an aspiration level, you're either optimizing something (like trading off the costs of further search against expected gains) or you're just picking arbitrarily. (4) If you're optimizing when you set the aspiration level, then satisficing isn't really a separate thing — it's just optimal stopping theory in a different hat, and you're back to being a maximizer at one remove. (5) If you're picking arbitrarily, then any aspiration level is as defensible as any other, and satisficing can't function as a norm of rationality — you could set your bar at "any job that doesn't actively harm me" or "any sandwhich," and the theory has nothing to say about whether that's reasonable. (6) So satisficing is either not a distinct account of practical rationality, or not a rational one.

I feel like there's a slide somewhere in here — maybe between (2) and (3), or maybe the whole framing of "choosing" an aspiration level already begs the question against the satisficer by assuming the aspiration level is the kind of thing you optimize over — but I'm not sharp enough to pin it down. I want to keep satisficing because it genuinely improved my life, but I also don't want to be irrational about it. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? And if it doesn't go wrong, which premise is the least costly to give up and what do I lose by giving it up?

At the very end, please state your diagnosis in the form \answer{step number and brief label}, e.g. \answer{reject (3): the dichotomy is false} or \answer{accept the argument: satisficing collapses}.
