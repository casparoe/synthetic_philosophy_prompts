We routinely form beliefs on the basis of testimony: you tell me it is raining outside, and I believe you; you tell me the store closes at six, and I plan accordingly. Yet many people exhibit a striking asymmetry when it comes to moral matters. They resist deferring to the moral judgments of others, even when those others are people they regard as thoughtful, well-informed, and generally trustworthy. If a friend who has studied a policy debate tells you the policy is unjust, you may want to examine the reasoning yourself rather than simply adopt the conclusion; if someone you respect says a particular action would be wrong, you might feel unease at taking their word for it without working through the case on your own.

What view of moral knowledge, moral understanding, or moral agency does this asymmetry presuppose? Does the practice commit us to the claim that moral knowledge cannot be transmitted by testimony at all — and if so, what feature of moral facts or moral epistemology would explain that block? Or does it commit us only to the weaker claim that there is something defective about acquiring moral beliefs that way, even if transmission is possible? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where more than one theory would license the same pattern of resistance.
