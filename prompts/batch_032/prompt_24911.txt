I've been following the intellectualism vs. anti-intellectualism debate about knowledge-how for years now — ever since Stanley and Williamson's 2001 paper revived the question, through the back-and-forth in epistemology and philosophy of mind, and into the occasional LessWrong thread about tacit knowledge and whether LLMs "know how" to do anything at all. I want to see what a genuine adversarial collaboration between a committed intellectualist and a committed anti-intellectualist would actually produce — not another paper arguing past the other side, but a document both could sign.

The intellectualist position (defended most prominently by Stanley and Williamson) holds that knowing-how is a species of knowing-that: to know how to φ is to know, for some way w, that w is a way to φ, under a practical mode of presentation. The anti-intellectualist position (descending from Ryle) holds that knowing-how is a distinct kind of knowledge — a capacity or disposition that cannot be reduced to propositional knowledge without a regress, and that attributing propositional knowledge in these cases over-intellectualizes skilled performance.

Please draft the joint statement such a collaboration would produce. It should include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about the data, about what needs explaining, about what various canonical arguments (Ryle's regress, the linguistic arguments from embedded questions, the cases involving amnesic performers or unreflective experts) do and do not show. These should be substantive, not just truisms.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state a precise point of disagreement, with the intellectualist's position phrased so an intellectualist would accept the wording and the anti-intellectualist's position phrased so an anti-intellectualist would accept the wording. No strawmen.

3. A crux list: the sub-questions such that if they were settled, at least one side would change its view. For each crux, describe a concrete case, experimental paradigm, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about what that case or argument would show.

4. A brief assessment of whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — and if so, which part and why. (For example: are the two sides using "knowledge" to pick out different things such that both could be right about their respective targets, or is there a genuine disagreement about a single phenomenon?)

Respond in the style of an expert philosopher of mind and epistemology. Think very carefully before answering; the point is to produce formulations that are precise enough that neither side would disown them, and that make the residual disagreement exact rather than gestural.
