Suppose you are advising a philosopher of language who has just completed a dissertation defending a broadly two-dimensionalist approach to meaning and modality. They now have three years of postdoctoral funding and want to spend that time on whichever open problems in or around two-dimensional semantics would be most productive to tackle. They are committed to the core idea that expressions have both a primary intension (a function from centered worlds to extensions, meant to capture what is a priori knowable) and a secondary intension (a function from worlds to extensions, meant to capture what is metaphysically necessary), and they believe this apparatus does genuine explanatory work. But they are open-minded about which version of the framework is best and which objections are most urgent.

They have asked you: which two or three open problems should they prioritize, and in what order? I want a defended prioritization, not a survey of the field. For each problem you recommend, please address the following:

(a) Importance. What else in philosophy of language, mind, or metaphysics depends on how this problem is resolved? If it were settled one way, what downstream work would become possible or unnecessary? If settled the other way, what would need to be rethought?

(b) Tractability. Can a single philosopher with three years and a philosophy-of-language background make real progress, or does the problem require formal tools, empirical methods, or interdisciplinary collaboration that would take years to acquire? What would count as genuine progress within three years — a full solution, a clarified dialectic, a new formal result?

(c) What a resolution would unlock. Be concrete: not just "we would know whether the nesting argument succeeds," but what new questions or applications would open up once that piece is in place.

Also tell me which open problems or live objections you would deliberately deprioritize, and why. I am especially interested in cases where a problem has received a great deal of attention but its resolution would not shift much else, or where the debate has become so entrenched that a postdoc's three years are unlikely to move it.

To make the terrain concrete, here are some of the issues they are considering — but do not feel constrained to this list, and do not try to address all of them:

- Whether the nesting argument and related objections show that no single intension can simultaneously play the apriority role and the necessity role that two-dimensionalists require.
- How to handle indexicals and demonstratives within the framework — whether the centered-worlds apparatus is adequate or generates counterintuitive predictions about what sentences involving "I," "here," and "now" express.
- Whether there are strong necessities — necessities that are only knowable a posteriori and whose primary intensions are not themselves a priori — and what admitting them would do to the broader two-dimensionalist project of connecting modality to apriority.
- The relationship between epistemic two-dimensionalism and contextual or metasemantic two-dimensionalism: whether these are genuine rivals or whether one can be reduced to the other.
- Whether two-dimensional semantics can be extended to handle attitude ascriptions in a way that improves on standard possible-worlds accounts.
- How the framework applies outside core philosophy of language — in particular, whether it genuinely illuminates the metaphysics of consciousness, or whether those applications depend on controversial moves in the semantic theory that remain unsettled.
