At a department colloquium last week, two of my colleagues got into an argument about a third colleague's conduct, and I have been turning it over ever since. Here is what happened.

A candidate for a postdoc asked our department chair after the talk, "Is the department hiring in the next two years?" The chair answered, "The dean has approved no new lines for next year." That was true — no new lines for next year — but the dean had approved two new lines for the year after, and the chair knew the candidate was really asking about the whole two-year window. The candidate took the answer as a "no" and accepted a position elsewhere.

When this came up at dinner, Mara said the chair had lied. Daniel said that was unfair: the chair asserted a true proposition, so whatever was wrong with the answer, it was not lying. What followed was a back-and-forth that I will reconstruct as best I can.

(1) Daniel: Lying requires asserting something you believe to be false. The chair believed — correctly — that the dean had approved no lines for next year. So he did not lie. He may have been unhelpful or even deceptive, but calling it lying stretches the concept past its breaking point.

(2) Mara: That is a technicality that ignores the whole point of the moral prohibition on lying. We prohibit lying because deliberately causing someone to form a false belief by exploiting their trust in your communicative acts is wrong. The chair did exactly that. If your account says that was not lying, then your account has lost touch with why we care about lying in the first place.

(3) Daniel: But the mechanism matters. When you lie, you break the rule that governs assertion — you say something you believe false. When you mislead, you follow the rules of assertion but manipulate the inferences the hearer will draw. The hearer bears some responsibility for those inferences in a way they do not for the literal content of an assertion. That difference is not nothing.

(4) Mara: The hearer bears no responsibility when the speaker has deliberately engineered the inference. The chair knew exactly what conclusion the candidate would draw and intended it. The fact that the deception operated through implicature rather than literal content does not make the chair any less responsible for the false belief. If anything, it makes the deception more insidious, because the chair gets to maintain deniability.

(5) Daniel: I am not saying the chair's conduct was fine. I am saying it is a different kind of wrong. Lying is wrong in the way breaking a promise is wrong — it violates a specific practice. Misleading is wrong in the way exploiting a loophole is wrong — it is bad faith, but it does not violate the same norm. And this distinction can matter practically: if you are being coerced or questioned under duress, misleading might be permissible when lying is not, precisely because you have not asserted anything false.

(6) Mara: That is exactly backwards. If misleading is permissible when lying is not, that is because misleading is the more manipulative act — it lets you achieve the same deceptive effect while preserving deniability. That is not a moral point in favor of misleading; it is an indictment of it. The chair's conduct was not a lesser wrong than lying; it was lying's sneakier cousin.

They went back and forth a while longer but never resolved it. I would like you to adjudicate this dispute.
