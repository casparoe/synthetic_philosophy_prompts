Most legal systems prohibit torture absolutely: no ticking-bomb exception, no necessity defense. An official who tortures a suspect to prevent a terrorist attack has still committed a crime, regardless of how many lives the torture might have saved. This looks like the legal system endorsing a deontological constraint — the prohibition is agent-relative, binding each agent regardless of the consequences of compliance. But the same legal systems also permit harm-weighing elsewhere: police may use lethal force to stop a mass shooter, and states may wage war in self-defense knowing it will kill civilians. What theory of moral wrongness does the absolute prohibition on torture presuppose? Is it consistent with the harm-weighing the law permits in other contexts? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in legal doctrine if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly act-consequentialist one.
