I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student, and I've been taking this seminar on the extended mind thesis where the professor refuses to just tell us her view — she's been feeding us cases all semester and making us triangulate. I've kept a running list of her verdicts, and now we're supposed to predict what she'll say about a new case next week. I thought I had her rule pinned down but the more I stare at it the more I think there are at least two or three principles that all fit the data.

Here are the cases she's ruled on so far:

- Otto's notebook (the classic): YES, part of his cognitive system.
- A mathematician working through a proof on a chalkboard, where the erasing and rewriting is inseperable from the thinking: YES.
- A library card catalog that a historian uses to locate sources: NO.
- A cochlear implant that transduces sound into neural signals: YES.
- A smartphone calendar that someone checks for appointments and would be nonfunctional without: YES.
- A GPS navigation system that a driver follows turn-by-turn, without thinking about the route at all: NO.
- A spouse who reliably remembers all the couple's social obligations and reminds the other partner: YES.
- A book sitting on someone's shelf that they read once and could consult if they wanted to: NO.

The new case she's giving us: a PhD student (not me, but basically me) who uses a large language model as an ongoing reasoning partner for their work. They externalize arguments to it, have it find flaws, and over time they've come to incorporate its outputs about as automatically as they'd consult their own memory. It's always available on their laptop, they've calibrated their trust — they know which kinds of reasoning it's reliable on and which it isn't — and on the reliable kinds they endorse its outputs without double-checking. The question is whether the LLM counts as part of the student's cognitive system.

I can see at least three candidate rules that fit her verdicts. One is something like the classic parity principle plus reliable access and automatic endorsement. Another is about tight mutual interactivity — whether there's a real reciprocal causal loop between agent and resource, not just one-way consultation. A third is about functional transparency — whether the resource has become so incorporated that the agent experiences it as part of their own cognitive equipment, the way a blind person experiences their cane. They all handle the eight cases but they come apart on the LLM, and I keep going back and forth on which one she's actually running. I'd love to hear someone work through the mapping, say what each rule predicts for the new case, and — maybe most usefully — identify what further case would cleanly discriminate between them. Also, if you spot an inconsistency in her pattern that I'm missing, I want to know about it.
