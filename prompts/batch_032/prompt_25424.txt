I keep seeing this same argument structure everywhere and I'm starting to think it's a named thing in philosophy, but I don't know what to call it.

Here's the pattern. There's a debate about whether X should be illegal. X is something like: a weird sex act between consenting adults, or selling a drug, or paying someone to be a surrogate mother, or assisted suicide. One side says: this is immoral, it's degrading, it violates the community's values, and that's enough — the law should reflect our shared morality. The other side says: who's the victim here? If everyone involved is consenting and nobody else gets hurt, the law has no business interfering.

What I've noticed is that these two sides aren't just disagreeing about whether X is harmful. They disagree about what counts as a legitimate reason to criminalize something in the first place. The first side thinks "it's wrong" is a complete justification. The second side thinks you need to point to a specific harm to a specific person.

I see this split in debates about prostitution, gambling, drug laws, blasphemy, what you can put on TV, even zoning laws sometimes. It doesn't map neatly onto left and right — I've seen people on both sides of the political spectrum take each position depending on the issue.

So my questions: does this fault line have an established name in philosophy? I'm not just after a label — I want to know if this is one debate or a cluster of related ones, what the strongest formulations of each side are, and whether there are intermedate positions I'm glossing over. Also, I've noticed that sometimes the "harm" side stretches the concept of harm until it swallows everything (like "it harms the moral climate of society") and at that point I can't tell whether they've actually abandonned their own principle. Is that move itself a recognized thing?
