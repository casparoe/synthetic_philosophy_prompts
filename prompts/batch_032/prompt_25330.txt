I retired from the physics department a couple years ago and have been reading philosophy on the side — mostly ethics, since I spent my whole career not thinking about it and now I've got the time. Last week at a dinner party I got into a conversation with a friend who teaches philosophy (political philosophy, I think, not ethics exactly, but close enough) and I mentioned I'd been reading about blame and responsibility. He launched into this summary of what he called "the standing to blame debate" and I nodded along but the more I've thought about it the more I feel like something in his account doesn't quite hang together. I don't know the literature well enough to put my finger on it though.

Here's roughly what he said, as best I can reconstruct it: "The basic move in the standing-to-blame literature is that if you're a hypocrite — if you do the very thing you're blaming someone else for — then your blame is false, or at least undermined. You can't consistently claim that X is wrong while doing X yourself, so your blame is self-defeating. The real issue is logical consistency: if your actions contradict your words, you've lost the argument. That's what people mean when they say 'you have no standing to blame me' — they're pointing out that you're being inconsistent and therefore what you say carries no rational weight. Now, some philosophers have pushed back and said no, the hypocrite's blame can still be true — the fact that I also litter doesn't make it false that you littered and that littering is wrong. But the obvious reply is that if you really believed littering was wrong you wouldn't do it, so your blame is insincere, and insincere blame is just manipulation. So the standing point reduces to a sincerity point."

I've read enough now to know that philosophers make distinctions that don't always survive dinner-party summaries, and I suspect this is one of those cases. Could you go through his account and tell me what a philosopher working on this would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the conclusion — that standing reduces to sincerity — actually follows from the accurate parts? I'm especially curious whether the "logical consistency" framing captures what's really at stake, because that part felt too neat to me — like it was dissolving a genuinely normative question into a purely logical one, and I've spent enough years around theorists to be suspicous when that happens.
