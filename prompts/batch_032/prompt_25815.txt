Read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973) through the lens of David Lewis's account of convention in *Convention: A Philosophical Study* (1969). Lewis analyzes conventions as arbitrary but stable solutions to recurring coordination problems, maintained by common knowledge and mutual expectations: a regularity is a convention when nearly everyone conforms, expects others to conform, and prefers to conform conditional on others doing so — and when an alternative regularity could have served the same coordinating function.

The citizens of Omelas maintain a practice: they know about the suffering child in the cellar, they accept the arrangement, and they go on with their flourishing lives. A few walk away. I want a reading that is faithful to both the story and Lewis's framework. Is the citizens' treatment of the child a Lewis-style convention, and if so, what coordination problem does it solve? The story makes the child's existence common knowledge among adults — does this map cleanly onto Lewis's common-knowledge condition, or does the moral content of that knowledge introduce something his formalism cannot capture? Lewis insists conventions are arbitrary because an alternative could have done the same job; is the Omelas arrangement arbitrary in this sense, or does the story suggest the flourishing really does depend on the suffering in a way that is not merely conventional? And what are the ones who walk away in Lewis's terms — defectors from a convention, or something that exposes a limit in analyzing moral practices as conventions at all? Conclude by saying what the story adds to the philosophical understanding of convention that argument alone does not.
