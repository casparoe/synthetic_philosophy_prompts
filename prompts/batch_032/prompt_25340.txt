So I had a kid about six months ago, and it's honestly scrambled my sense of time. I keep catching myself thinking about 2080 or 2100 like it's actually relevant to my decisions now — which is completely new for me. Before this I was basicly living in a 5-year horizon.

Anyway, at a barbecue last weekend two friends got into it about discounting the future, and I've been replaying it in my head all week. I was just listening while holding the baby, but now I genuinly don't know who's right. Here's roughly how it went:

Maya started it. She's an economist, works on climate damage models. She said she's been getting increasingly frustrated with how discount rates get used in policy, and that pure time preference — discounting future wellbeing just because it's in the future — is basically a cognitive error dressed up as methodology. She said if you really think about it, the mere fact that someone exists in 2150 rather than 2025 doesn't make their suffering less real, and discounting it is just a failure to fully imagine that those people are actual people with actual lives.

Dan pushed back. He's a philosopher, but not in a pretentious way. He said he basically agreed with her on the moral intuition, but that she was running together a bunch of different things. He said there are perfectly good reasons to care more about nearer-term effects: future people will probably be richer (so an extra dollar means less to them), there's some probability humanity goes extinct each year (so expected value declines), and our ability to predict consequences degrades with time. None of that, he said, is "pure" time preference — it's just being reasonable about uncertainty and diminishing returns.

Maya said fine, but even after you factor all that out, the standard models still build in an extra discount for pure time preference, usually around 1-3% per year. She said over a century that basically makes future people invisible — a life in 2125 is worth like 5% of a life today — and it's not justified by anything except impatience.

Dan said okay, but if you set pure time preference to exactly zero, you get some pretty strange results. Like, a life saved in 10,000 years counts exactly as much as a life saved today, even though we have basically no idea what the world will look like in 10,000 years — whether there will even be humans in any recognizable sense, whether our actions today will have the effects we intend, whether the concept of "saving a life" will mean anything similar. He said at some point the uncertainty is so deep that it becomes indistinguishable from discounting, and trying to separate them is kind of a fake precision.

Maya said that's still not an argument for pure time preference — it's an argument about uncertainty, which we already agreed to handle separately. Pure time preference says: even if I'm totally certain that a future person will exist and suffer just as much as a present person, I still care less because they're far away in time. And that, she said, is just temporal parochialism. No different in principle from caring less about people far away in space.

Dan conceded that but then came at it from a different angle. He said there's a pragmatic problem: if you take zero pure time preference seriously, the sheer number of potential future people swamps everything. Any small action that has even a tiny chance of affecting the long-run trajectory of humanity ends up dominating all your near-term concerns. He said that can't be the right way to live — it's not just psychologically impossible, it's arguably the wrong moral theory if it tells you to ignore the suffering of actual people in front of you in favor of speculative effects on hypothetical people in the year 3000.

Maya said that sounds like an objection to consequentialism in general, not to zero discounting specifically. And anyway, maybe we should be taking the far future a lot more seriously than we do — maybe the fact that it feels absurd is a problem with our intuitions, not with the theory.

At that point someone spilled a drink and the conversation moved on. But I've been stuck on it. I have this new kid, and I'm trying to figure out how much to weigh her future — and her kids' futures — against stuff that matters right now. These aren't abstract questions anymore.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what? I want a move-by-move breakdown: which points land, which don't, and whether there's a way to resolve this that neither of them articulated.
