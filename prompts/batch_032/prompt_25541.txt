Argue that teleosemantic theories of mental content face a serious and underappreciated difficulty: the problem of functional shift. Many cognitive mechanisms have evolutionary histories in which they were selected for one function during one period and a different function during another, with no single function dominating the cumulative selection regime. When a teleosemanticist attempts to fix the content of the representations such a mechanism produces by appealing to its proper function, the notion of a proper function becomes indeterminate — there is no fact of the matter about which of the several historical functions is the proper one. Argue that this problem cannot be resolved by privileging the most recent selection episode (since recency has no principled connection to content determination), by individuating the mechanism more finely (since this merely relocates the problem without resolving the underlying indeterminacy), or by appealing to the mechanism's current contribution to fitness (since this abandons the historical character of teleosemantics and collapses it into a form of causal or informational semantics). Conclude by assessing whether the problem of functional shift reveals a deeper tension in the teleosemantic project: the attempt to extract normative facts about representational content from purely historical facts about selection.
