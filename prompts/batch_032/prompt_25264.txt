I'm presenting a paper next month at a joint philosophy and political-science workshop on Kant's *Perpetual Peace*, and I need to walk in ready for a combative Q&A. The paper defends a specific interpretive thesis: that Kant's three Definitive Articles — republican constitution, pacific federation, and cosmopolitan right — are not a menu from which we can cherry-pick the most empirically tractable item, but a tightly integrated normative system. Each article supplies a necessary condition for the others to function, and the whole architecture is driven by Kant's deeper commitment to the idea that peace must arise from a rightful juridical condition, not merely from prudence, commerce, or the balance of power. I argue that the empirical democratic-peace literature, for all its suggestiveness, effectively isolates the first article and in doing so inverts Kant's own priorities. For Kant, republican constitutions do not cause peace directly; they create the domestic conditions under which a lawful international order becomes rationally mandatory, and that international order in turn stabilizes republicanism internally. The practical upshot, I claim, is that Kant gets conscripted — against the grain of his own framework — into defenses of democratic interventionism, the forcible export of republican government, which his federation model and his cosmopolitan right of hospitality jointly rule out.

The audience falls into three camps. First, four or five Kant scholars who know the text of *Perpetual Peace*, the *Metaphysics of Morals*, and the relevant *Reflexionen* in detail and will care above all about whether my reading is exegetically defensible. Second, a cluster of empirical political scientists who work on the democratic peace and will treat me as trying to talk them out of their most distinguished philosophical ally. Third, two just-war theorists and an international-law scholar who will press me on institutional implications: What does my reading actually say about the UN Security Council, the ICC, the Responsibility to Protect, or humanitarian intervention?
