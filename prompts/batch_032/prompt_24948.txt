I'm writing an essay that reads Christopher Nolan's *Memento* (2000) through Daniel Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity. I'd like a careful philosophical reading of the film that does justice to both the theory and the artwork — one that uses the theory to illuminate the film but also lets the film push back where it resists.

To set things up: Dennett argues that a self is not a substantial entity, not a thing in the brain or an immaterial soul, but an abstraction — something like a center of gravity. A center of gravity is not a real object you can point to, yet it is perfectly real as a predictive and explanatory posit within the framework of physics. Likewise, Dennett suggests, the self is the posit of a certain kind of narrative: we tell stories about a person (including ourselves), and the self is whatever those stories are about. The self is not the author of the narrative but its product — a character that emerges from the telling.

*Memento* seems to dramatize something very close to this. Leonard Shelby has anterograde amnesia and cannot form new memories. He constructs his identity and his project of vengeance entirely through external artifacts — Polaroid photographs, tattoos on his body, a system of handwritten notes. The film's reverse-chronological structure (color sequences moving backward in time, intercut with a forward-moving black-and-white strand) forces the viewer to piece together Leonard's story in something like the way Leonard himself must: fragment by fragment, without the usual narrative scaffolding. And the climactic revelation — that Leonard has, at some level, chosen to construct and sustain a false narrative because it gives him a purpose — raises the stakes considerably.

Here is what I would like the reading to address:

1. **Where the film and the theory align.** Which scenes, structural choices, or character details support the idea that the self is a narrative construct rather than a fixed essence? Does the film make this point in a way that philosophical prose cannot — that is, does the cinematic form itself do argumentative work that a paper could not?

2. **Where the film complicates the theory.** Leonard's self-narrative is not just any narrative — it is systematically distorted, and the distortion seems to require an agent who is doing the distorting. If Leonard is deliberately suppressing inconvenient truths (about his wife, about the attacker he already killed, about his own role in events), then there appears to be a self who knows the truth and chooses to bury it. Does this pose a genuine problem for Dennett's view, or can a center-of-narrative-gravity account accommodate self-deception without positing a hidden substantial self?

3. **The charge of eliminativism.** Dennett's view is often criticized for eliminating the self — if the self is "just" an abstraction, then in what sense am I real, and in what sense does anything matter for me? Does *Memento* offer resources for answering this charge, or does it leave us with the uncomfortable conclusion that Leonard (and perhaps we) are nothing more than the stories we happen to tell? If the film's emotional force depends on our sense that something has been lost — that there is a real Leonard who has been hollowed out — does that very response count against Dennett?

The essay should take the film seriously as a work of art, not merely as an illustration of a pre-existing thesis. It should be willing to note places where the film resists the reading or suggests something the theory misses. And it should reach a conclusion — not necessarily a tidy one — about what the encounter between Dennett and *Memento* yields that neither, taken alone, can supply.
