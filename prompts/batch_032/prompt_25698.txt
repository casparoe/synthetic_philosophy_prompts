I'm in a political philosophy seminar and we've been working through Rawls's idea of public reason. I tried to reconstruct the argument for why religious reasons are supposed to be inadmissible in public justification, and I ended up with something that seems to prove way too much. I can't figure out where the slip is, and my professor just told me to "sit with the tension," which is professor-speak for "I'm not going to answer your question."

Here's the argument as I've got it:

(1) Coercive laws are legitimate only if they can be justified to all reasonable citizens in terms they can accept.
(2) A justification in terms someone can accept must rely only on premises that they can reasonably endorse.
(3) Religious premises — claims about God's commands, scriptural authority, revelation — cannot be reasonably endorsed by citizens who don't share the relevant faith.
(4) So religious premises are inadmissible in public justification. (This is the standard conclusion, and I'm fine with it for now.)

(5) But secular comprehensive doctrines — utilitarianism, Kantian ethics, Aristotelian virtue theory — also cannot be reasonably endorsed by all reasonable citizens. A reasonable Kantian and a reasonable utilitarian disagree about fundamental normative truths, and neither is being irrational.
(6) By the same reasoning as (2)–(4), secular comprehensive doctrines are also inadmissible in public justification.
(7) Yet the "political conceptions of justice" that Rawls wants to admit — justice as fairness, the original position, the difference principle — are themselves built on controversial normative premises that many reasonable citizens reject. (Plenty of reasonable people think the difference principle is too egalitarian, or that the original position stacks the deck.)
(8) So public reason either rules out basically everything substantive, or the line between "political" and "comprehensive" is just gerrymandered to let Rawls's own commitments in while keeping religion out.

I'm pretty confident (8) can't be right, or at least it's too strong, but I can't tell whether the problem is in (5) — maybe I'm using "reasonable" in a looser sense than Rawls does — or in (7) — maybe political conceptions really are freestanding in a way I'm not appreciating — or somewhere in the move from (2) to (6), where the standard for "reasonably endorsable" might shift between the two applications. I'd like to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. In particular, if the fix involves tightening up what "reasonable" means so that (5) comes out false, does that end up just defining religious citizens as unreasonable by fiat? Because that seems like a different kind of problem.
