I host a philosophy of probability podcast, and next week's guest is a prominent defender of imprecise probabilism—the view that rational credences are represented by a set of probability functions rather than a single one. I don't want the conversation to be an advertisement for the view; I want it to be a genuine stress test. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. Cover at least these four pressure points:

(i) Dilation. Learning some fact can expand your credal set, making you seem less opinionated after gaining information. The standard reply is that this is a feature, not a bug—the new information reveals ambiguity the agent hadn't noticed. Press past that.

(ii) Sequential choice. There are cases where applying a permissible imprecise decision rule (e.g., Γ-maxmin) at each step of a sequential decision leads to a sequence of acts that is dominated by an available alternative. The standard reply appeals to resolute choice—binding yourself to a plan in advance. Press on what resolute choice costs, and whether it sits comfortably with the permissivist motivation behind the view.

(iii) The proliferation problem. If the argument for making credences imprecise generalizes, it seems utilities, higher-order credences, and even the boundaries of the credal set itself should also be imprecise. Ask whether this triggers a regress, and what principled reason there is for it to stop at the first order.

(iv) Phenomenology. We often feel we have precise credences—I am exactly 50% confident a fair coin lands heads. The imprecise probabilist can say that's a limiting case where the set is a singleton, but the worry runs deeper: when I say I'm roughly 70% confident it will rain, the precise Bayesian can model this as uncertainty over a precise credence (a higher-order distribution). What does the set-of-functions model buy us that the higher-order precise model doesn't? And if it buys us something, why doesn't ordinary introspection reveal sets rather than distributions?

For each question, anticipate the most natural defensive move and build the follow-up to block it, so the guest can't simply recycle a well-rehearsed reply.
