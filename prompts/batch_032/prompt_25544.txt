I serve on a university sexual misconduct hearing panel. We hear about two dozen cases a year, and the single question we spend the most time on is whether the complainant's consent was valid — or, in cases where the respondent claims they reasonably believed consent was present, whether that belief was reasonable given what they knew about deception, pressure, or the complainant's capacity at the time. The panel includes faculty from across the university (history, chemistry, engineering) and two students; nobody has formal training in the philosophy of consent, and our training materials mostly recite policy language without helping us operationalize it. We are not lawyers, we do not apply rules of evidence, and we deliberate in a conference room with a whiteboard, not a courtroom.

I want a decision procedure we can use during deliberations, after the factual disputes have been resolved as best we can, to assess whether consent was validly given. The procedure should walk us through the three main threats to validity — deception, coercion, and incompetence — in a structured order, with concrete questions for each, thresholds for what counts as sufficient to undermine consent, and explicit flags for where the procedure cannot decide and the panel must exercise judgment. The procedure should be usable by non-philosophers in under thirty minutes, and it should be honest about which steps are genuinely rule-like and which are judgment calls dressed up as criteria. Where the philosophical literature contains a genuine dispute that bears on a step — for instance, about whether consent is fundamentally a mental state or a communicative act, or about whether the standard for competence should be fixed or vary with the stakes of the decision — please flag that dispute and tell us how to proceed in spite of it, rather than pretending it does not exist.

Please then apply the procedure to three cases. First: a complainant alleges they consented to sex only because the respondent told them they were in a committed relationship and had ended things with their other partner; the respondent admits they were still seeing the other partner and had no intention of ending that relationship, but argues that people misrepresent their romantic intentions all the time and this does not rise to the level of invalidating consent. Second: a graduate student alleges they consented to a sexual relationship with their thesis advisor only because they feared that refusing would damage their career; the advisor never made any explicit threat but acknowledges they were aware of the power differential. The university's policy is silent on whether advisor-student relationships are per se prohibited. Third: both parties agree the complainant had been drinking heavily — somewhere between six and nine drinks over four hours — and initiated the sexual encounter, but the complainant says they were too intoxicated to meaningfully consent and the respondent says the complainant was lucid, conversational, and appeared to be making decisions normally.

For each case, walk through the procedure step by step and tell me what it yields. I especially want to see a case where the procedure returns an answer the panel will find uncomfortable — perhaps because it says consent was valid when our instincts say otherwise, or invalid when we would prefer to find it valid. Finally, tell me what this procedure systematically cannot handle: the kinds of cases or considerations it will miss, and where the underlying philosophical theory itself is unsettled enough that no procedure could settle it.
