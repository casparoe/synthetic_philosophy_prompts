I've been trying to teach myself about voting theory, and I keep coming back to something that doesn't sit right. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, as I understand it, says that any voting method for three or more candidates that isn't a dictatorship will sometimes give voters an incentive to misrepresent their preferences — to vote strategically rather than honestly. If that's what the theorem shows, then it seems to apply to every real-world voting system: plurality, ranked-choice, approval voting, all of them. They all fail the same test.

But if every system fails in the same way, why is there so much debate about which one to adopt? People argue passionately that ranked-choice voting is fairer than plurality, or that approval voting avoids the spoiler effect, or that Condorcet methods are the only ones that respect majority rule. Yet if Gibbard–Satterthwaite is correct, aren't all these systems equally susceptible to manipulation, just in different costumes? Doesn't the theorem imply that the entire project of comparing voting systems on grounds of fairness or resistance to strategy is misguided, since they all share the same fundamental flaw? If the theorem doesn't imply that, then what exactly does it rule out, and what room does it leave for one system to be genuinely better than another?
