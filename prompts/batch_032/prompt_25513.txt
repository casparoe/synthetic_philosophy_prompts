I spent forty years in experimental physics, where the moral valence of a decision rarely enters the analysis — you check your assumptions, you control for confounds, you report what the data say. Since retiring, I have been reading political philosophy, and I find myself repeatedly stuck on what Michael Walzer calls the problem of dirty hands: the idea that a political leader may be morally required to do something that is, by ordinary moral standards, wrong, because the consequences of not doing it would be catastrophic, and that the leader ought to feel guilt for having done it rather than simply treating it as a hard-nosed calculation.

I would like to work through this idea by applying it to a concrete case: Truman's decision to use atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. I know the broad outlines of the episode — the estimated invasion casualties, the Potsdam Declaration, the two bombings, the Japanese surrender — and I am not asking for new historical facts. What I want is a disciplined mapping of the dirty hands framework onto this decision.

Specifically: Does the framework require us to say that Truman did something wrong, or merely something that would ordinarily be wrong but was justified in the circumstances? If the former, what exactly was the wrong — the targeting of civilians, the scale of destruction, the use of a novel weapon without warning, something else? What does the framework say about the fact that there were two bombs, not one, and that the second followed the first by only three days? And then the harder question: if Truman had not felt guilt afterward — if he had treated the decision as a straightforward optimization problem — would the framework say that something important was missing from his moral psychology, or would that simply be a different, equally defensible way of bearing the burden? Finally, I want to know whether the episode, analyzed this way, reveals a weakness in the dirty hands concept itself — for instance, whether the framework's insistence on retained guilt is psychologically unrealistic, or whether it inadvertently lets leaders off the hook by treating moral anguish as a substitute for moral accountability.
