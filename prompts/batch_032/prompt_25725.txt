**The Skeptic and the Rule**

Imagine I ask you to continue the sequence "2, 4, 6, 8..." You say "10, 12, 14," and you feel certain you have understood the rule: add two each time. But now imagine a philosopher stops you and asks: how do you know the rule is not something else? Suppose the rule is actually "add two until you reach 1000, and thereafter add four"—call this function "quadd." All the examples you have encountered so far are below 1000, so they are equally consistent with "add two" and with "quadd." On what basis, the philosopher asks, do you claim you were following "add two" all along rather than "quadd"?

The natural first response is that you meant "add two." You had a certain intention, a certain rule in mind. But the skeptic presses: what did that intention consist in? If you try to articulate it—"I meant: at each step, take the sum of the previous number and two"—you have just used the concept of addition again, which is exactly what is in question. If you appeal to a mental image or a feeling of what comes next, the skeptic can point out that the same image or feeling is compatible with quadd, since you have never applied it past 1000. If you appeal to a disposition—you are disposed to say "1002" after "1000"—the skeptic notes that dispositions are finite (you will eventually make mistakes, or die), while the rule extends infinitely. Moreover, dispositions can be gerrymandered: you have a disposition to say "1004" after "1000" under the "quadd" interpretation too, since that is what quadd would yield if you misapplied it in exactly the way you are disposed to misapply addition at large numbers.

The skeptical conclusion Kripke draws from Wittgenstein is radical: there is no fact about you—no mental state, no disposition, no past intention—that makes it true that you meant "add two" rather than "quadd." Meaning is not a matter of fact. The paradox is not that we cannot know what we mean; it is that there is nothing to know.

**The Skeptical Solution**

Kripke's Wittgenstein does not leave us in the abyss. He offers what he calls a "skeptical solution": we grant the skeptic's negative conclusion—there are no meaning-constituting facts about the individual considered in isolation—but we show that our ordinary practice of attributing meaning is nevertheless legitimate, because it rests on a different kind of justification.

The move is to shift from truth conditions to assertability conditions. We do not need a fact that makes "Jones means addition" true; we need to understand the circumstances under which we are warranted in asserting it. And those circumstances are social. We assert that Jones means addition when Jones's responses agree sufficiently with those of the community. If Jones says "1002" after "1000," and the rest of us do too, we count Jones as having meant addition all along. There is no deeper fact.

This means that meaning is irreducibly social. A person considered in isolation—a "Crusoe" figure—cannot mean anything determinately, because there is no community to supply the standard of correctness. What makes a response correct or incorrect is not its correspondence to some private mental fact but its agreement or disagreement with the responses of the linguistic community.

**The Upshot**

If this is right, then the authority of a rule—whether it is a mathematical rule, a legal rule, or the rule for using a word—is not grounded in the intentions of the rule-giver or in the Platonic nature of the rule itself. It is grounded in the ongoing practice of a community that holds one another to account. This has unsettling implications. When a judge interprets a statute, or when an engineer specifies the behavior of an algorithm, we want to say there is a fact of the matter about what the rule requires. Kripkenstein suggests there is not—or rather, that what counts as "what the rule requires" is just what the relevant community converges on treating as required.
