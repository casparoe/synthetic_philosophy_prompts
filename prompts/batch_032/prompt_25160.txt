I'm in a cognitive science reading group that's been working through the predictive processing literature — Friston, Hohwy, Clark, Seth — and one of our members, a neuroscientist who's been in the field since the early 2000s, keeps reaching for the same analogy to explain the free energy principle to the rest of us: "The brain is basically a scientist, constantly generating hypotheses about the causes of its sensory inputs and testing them against incoming data." He uses this to motivate the claim that perception, action, and learning are all just varieties of Bayesian inference, and that the free energy principle unifies them under a single imperative — minimize prediction error. He'll often note that the analogy goes back to Helmholtz's notion of unconscious inference, as if that pedigree settles the matter. I'm sympathetic to the framework, but I'm worried the analogy is doing too much work. A scientist designs experiments, chooses what to measure, formulates explicit theories, revises beliefs in light of anomalous results, and cares about truth in a way that seems very different from, say, my visual system compensating for shadows, my hand automatically adjusting grip force on a cup, or a bacterium swimming up a chemical gradient. I'd like you to audit the analogy properly: lay out what maps onto what — where in the brain's operation is the hypothesis, the experiment, the data, the prior, the peer review? — then identify where the mapping breaks, and tell me which of those breaks matter for the claim that perception really is inference and which are just harmless mismatches. Does the conclusion that action is a form of inference survive the disanalogies, or does the analogy quietly import features of scientific practice — deliberate experimental design, representational content, normative commitments to truth — that the brain simply doesn't have?
