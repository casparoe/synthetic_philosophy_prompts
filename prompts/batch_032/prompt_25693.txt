I've been tracking a colleague's judgments on a particular kind of question: when someone's detailed, first-personal "inside view" of a situation conflicts with a statistical or consensus-based "outside view," should they trust their own reasoning or defer? Here are six cases with her verdicts:

1. A physician sees a patient with symptoms consistent with a disease whose population prevalence is 0.01%. A test with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity returns positive. The physician computes the posterior probability at roughly 0.02% and concludes the patient almost certainly does not have the disease, the positive test notwithstanding. My colleague says the physician reasoned correctly by deferring to the base rate.

2. A film director has made three previous films, all critical and commercial successes. Her vision for her fourth film is stylistically unusual and departs from current market trends. Studio executives note that roughly 80% of films that depart substantially from market trends underperform. She trusts her vision and insists on her approach. My colleague says she is right to trust her own judgment.

3. A retail investor with no formal finance training trades based on patterns she perceives in price charts. The base rate for day traders is that roughly 95% lose money over any five-year window. She believes she is in the successful 5%. My colleague says she should defer to the base rate.

4. A firefighter with twenty years of experience arrives at a burning building and has a sudden, strong intuition that the floor is about to give way, though the visible structural indicators do not suggest imminent collapse. He orders his team out. My colleague says he was right to trust his intuition given his experience, regardless of how things turned out.

5. A political commentator has a detailed theory predicting the incumbent will lose, contradicting the polling average, prediction markets, and the near-unanimous consensus of other analysts. The commentator has made similarly contrarian election predictions before and has been wrong more often than right. My colleague says he should defer to the consensus.

6. A master violin maker selects tonewood by tapping it and feeling the vibrations, ignoring the measured density and grain specifications in the guild manual. She has been making acclaimed instruments for over thirty years. My colleague says she is right to trust her tactile judgment.

Now a new case: A software engineer with fifteen years of experience has spent six months designing a new architecture for her company's core system. She has led three previous major rewrites — two succeeded, one failed. The industry base rate for large-scale system rewrites is that roughly 70% either fail outright or are abandoned before completion. She believes her design avoids the specific pitfalls that cause most rewrites to fail, and she can articulate in detail what those pitfalls are and how her design addresses each one. She advocates for proceeding.

What would my colleague say? More importantly, what rule or rules is she implicitly applying? I can see at least two or three candidate principles that fit her first six verdicts equally well but diverge on the software engineer. Please identify those candidate rules, say what each predicts for the new case, give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and why, and propose a further case — not among the seven described — that would cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidates.
