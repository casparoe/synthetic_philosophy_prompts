I'm working on a paper about Bradley's regress and I keep finding that people talk past each other because they don't agree on what would actually count as solving the problem. Before I commit to any particular view, I want the full list of desiderata: what does a theory of the unity of the proposition have to get right?

I mean: what cases or phenomena must it handle (e.g., false propositions, propositions about non-existents, logically complex propositions), what distinctions can't it collapse (like the difference between a proposition and a mere list of its constituents), and what constraints from elsewhere in philosophy does it have to respect (compositionality, the fact that we grasp propositions, whatever)? For each one, give me the consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than just someone's pet preference.

And here's what I most want to know: are any pairs of these desiderata in tension — maybe even jointly unsatisfiable? If so, which ones, and what's the nature of the tension? Also, which single requirement does the most to narrow the field — i.e., which one knocks out the most otherwise-plausible solutions?

Don't bother scoring the main views (Fregean, Russellian, primitivist, etc.) against the list — I'll do that myself once I have the criteria straight.
