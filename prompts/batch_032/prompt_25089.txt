I'm teaching an upper-division seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and we have just spent two weeks on the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether punishment can be justified in terms of desert. Dennett defends a compatibilist position on which moral responsibility and just deserts survive even in a deterministic world, provided agents are reasons-responsive and embedded in the right kind of social practices. Caruso argues that free will skepticism undermines retributive justice entirely and that we should replace punishment-as-desert with a public-health or quarantine model aimed at rehabilitation and incapacitation rather than blame and suffering imposed for its own sake.

Next session I want to use Steven Spielberg's *Minority Report* (2002) as a case study. The film's Precrime division arrests people for murders they have not yet committed but are predicted—by the precognitive "precogs"—to commit. The plot turns on whether the predictions are genuinely inevitable or can be thwarted by an agent's awareness of them, and the protagonist, John Anderton, is himself accused of a future murder he is certain he will not commit. The obvious lesson is that pre-punishment is unjust. But I want to push the discussion past that surface and into the deeper question the Dennett–Caruso debate raises: even in our own world, where we punish people after the fact, are we doing something importantly different from Precrime if determinism is true? Does the film inadvertently make Caruso's case—that the difference between pre-punishment and post-punishment is less principled than we think, because both rest on a picture of agency that cannot sustain desert—or does it dramatize something Dennett would point to about the role of reasons-responsiveness, self-control, and the social practice of holding one another accountable in grounding genuine desert? And what should we make of the fact that the precogs' visions are not of a single fixed future but of possible futures, and that Anderton's knowledge of the prediction itself alters his course—does this complication help Dennett more than Caruso, or the reverse?

Please give me a close reading of *Minority Report* through the lens of this debate. I want you to identify specific scenes, characters, and plot turns that bear on the disagreement, and to say where the film supports each side and where it complicates both. Be honest about where the film resists the reading: if a scene cuts against the philosophical point you want to make, say so, and do not force the film into an allegory it cannot sustain. I am less interested in whether the film "takes a position" than in what it shows or dramatizes that the philosophical arguments alone do not capture—and in what the arguments capture that the film, for all its virtues, may miss or distort. Respond in the style of an expert, someone equally comfortable with the film and with the philosophical literature on free will and moral responsibility. Think very carefully before answering, and do not hesitate to identify ambiguities or tensions rather than forcing a clean resolution.
