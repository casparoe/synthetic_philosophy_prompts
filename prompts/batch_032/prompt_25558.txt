I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've become convinced that the correct response to counterfactual mugging scenarios is to pay up — that is, one should act on the basis of what one would have done in a counterfactual branch, even when the causal consequences of paying are strictly negative in the actual branch. The standard case: a highly reliable predictor tells you it flipped a fair coin. Had the coin come up heads, the predictor would have given you a large reward if and only if you would pay it a smaller sum had the coin come up tails. The coin came up tails. The predictor asks for the smaller sum. On my view, you should pay, and the same reasoning extends to XOR blackmail and related cases.

I want to know what would count against this position. Not just the standard causal decision theorist's objection — I know that one — but a systematic map of the position's exposure. What empirical findings, formal results, or further thought experiments would undermine it? For instance: at what level of predictor reliability does the obligation to pay disappear, and does the view give a principled answer or does it turn on vague intuitions about stakes and probabilities? Does the view collapse into requiring one to pay in Pascal's mugging, or can it draw a stable line? Are there results in logic or computability — say, about the impossibility of a predictor that reasons about your decision procedure while running it — that would make the whole setup incoherent? What would a defender of the position concede as a genuine cost, and what would they dismiss as question-begging? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that's a problem for a normative view of this kind.
