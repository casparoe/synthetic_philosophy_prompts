I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and I'm supposed to moderate the Q&A at our department colloquium next month. The speaker is a philosopher who defends a teleosemantic theory of mental content — roughly, the view that what a mental state represents is fixed by the biological function of the mechanisms that produce it, where functions are understood in terms of natural selection (Millikan-style, though shes got her own version of the view). Ive read some of the core papers but I'm not a specialist, and I dont want to waste everyone's time with questions she's answered a hundred times.

Could you help me draft maybe seven or eight questions that get into the weeds? And for each one, give me the reply she's most likely to give and a decent follow-up, so I don't get steamrolled.

Here's what I've got so far — feel free to reorder, cut, or add:

1. The indeterminacy problem: the frog's fly-detector fires at flies, sure, but it also fires at BBs and shadows. Why is the content "fly" and not "small dark moving thing" or "fly-or-BB"? I know the standard move is to appeal to the consumer system — the content is whatever the downstream mechanisms *use* the representation as — but doesn't that just relocate the problem? What fixes the content of the consumer's states?

2. Misrepresentation: if the function of the detector is fixed by its selection history, then when it fires at a BB, is that a misrepresentation (it "said" fly when it wasn't a fly) or is it just doing exactly what it was selected to do (fire at small-dark-moving-things, which BBs are)? I want to know whether teleosemantics can actually get a determinate notion of error off the ground, or whether every token state ends up representing whatever it was selected to be triggered by, which makes error impossible.

3. Swampman: if a physical duplicate of me forms by chance in a swamp, it has no evolutionary history. So on her view, swamp-me has no contentful mental states — it's not thinking about anything when it looks at a tree. I know some teleosemanticists bite this bullet, but if she does, doesn't that mean the theory is a theory of content-for-evolved-organisms rather than content *simpliciter*? And if the naturalizing project was supposed to tell us what content *is*, not just what it is for creatures with the right kind of history, hasn't something been given up?

4. Novel concepts: how do we get concepts like "quark" or "hedge fund" that played no role in our evolutionary past? The standard answer is compositionality plus learning, but I want to press on whether the learning story itself requires a non-teleosemantic notion of content at the ground floor — like, when I learn "quark," am I just recombining innate representational capacities, or is there something new being built that selection history doesn't cover?

5. This one's a bit more meta: a lot of the examples in the literature are very simple — frogs, bacteria, magnetosomes. Is there a point where the teleosemantic story just runs out of steam as we scale up to propositional attitudes and conceptual content? Or does she think the same basic machinery carries all the way up?

6. How does her view handle the content of logical and mathematical concepts? Selection history seems to give us "predator," "food," "mate" — but "modus ponens"? "The number three"? Is the idea that these are somehow downstream of more basic adaptive representations, or does she think there's a separate story to tell?

7. Finally, I want to ask her what she thinks is the single hardest problem for the view right now — not the one that comes up in every Q&A, but the one she actually worries about when she's working on this stuff.

Does this hang together as a Q&A session? Am I missing any major objection she'll be expecting me to raise? And for each question, what's the reply I should be ready for?
