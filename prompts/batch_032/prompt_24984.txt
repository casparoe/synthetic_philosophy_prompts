I am a novelist. My current project is a cycle of political novels, each set in a different fictional polity that uses sortition — the random selection of ordinary citizens for political office — as a central mechanism of governance. I find that I keep reinventing the institutional details from scratch, and I want a reusable design procedure I can run for each book. The procedure should take a brief specification of the society (population, scale, technological level, the political problem sortition is meant to solve) and walk me through the institutional design choices: the selection pool, term length, scope of authority, relationship to non-sortition bodies, treatment of unwilling selectees, incorporation of specialized knowledge, and accountability mechanisms. For each choice point, distinguish between constraints that follow from the core normative case for sortition — and thus cannot be fudged without abandoning the premise — and options that are genuinely open. Flag the points where the design is most vulnerable to capture, drift, or de facto re-oligarchization; these are where my plots will live. Then run the procedure on two settings: (a) a small lunar colony of a few thousand where sortition selects the executive council, and (b) a densely populated post-national city of several million where a sortition chamber shares legislative power with an elected technocratic body. For each, identify the design choices the procedure forces that I would likely have overlooked, and the tensions it cannot resolve — which, for a novelist, are features, not bugs.
