I hold the view that grounding is a strict partial order — irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive — and that grounding chains terminate in a level of fundamental facts that do not themselves hold in virtue of anything further. This picture is widely, though not universally, endorsed in the grounding literature. I want to know what would count against it. What sorts of cases, formal results, or theoretical pressures should move me away from one or more of these commitments — irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, or well-foundedness? Which putative counterexamples (e.g., cases that seem to exhibit symmetric metaphysical dependence, or chains that appear not to bottom out, or holistic dependence structures) are treated as serious challenges by defenders of the view, and which are dismissed as resting on a misunderstanding of what grounding is supposed to be? I would like the defeaters sorted from those that would require abandoning the whole picture to those that would only force a local revision — weakening transitivity, say, or allowing that some facts are ungrounded without being fundamental. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the view, as standardly defended, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a metaphysical thesis of this kind, or whether it is simply what one should expect from a claim about the structure of reality that is not itself an empirical hypothesis.
