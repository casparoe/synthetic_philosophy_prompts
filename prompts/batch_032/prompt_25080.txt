So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend about uploading and personal identity and now I'm not sure I even know what I think anymore. He kept catching me in contradictions and I want to figure out if there's actually a coherent view underneath my gut reactions or if I need to give something up.

Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, as honestly as I can report them:

(1) The gradual neuron swap. Over the course of a year, surgeons replace each of my neurons one at a time with a silicon chip that does exactly the same input-output function. I'm awake for the whole thing, having conversations, noticing no change. At the end there's no biological brain left. I say: that's still me, I survive the whole way through.

(2) Same as (1) but I'm under anesthesia the whole year. I wake up with a fully silicon brain. I still want to say it's me, but I'm slightly less confident. Something about the continuity of consciousness being absent bugs me, though I can't articulate why it should matter.

(3) The destructive upload. A scanner reads my brain at t1, destroys it in the process, and constructs a functionally identical brain from the scan data at t2. The new brain has all my memories and personality. I say: I die at t1. The thing at t2 is a copy, not me.

(4) The non-destructive upload. Same scan but my original brain is left intact. Now there's me and an upload. I say: I'm the original, the upload is a distinct person who happens to be psychologically continuous with me. We diverge from the moment of scanning.

(5) The teletransporter. Like (3) but my body is disassembled here and reassembled on Mars from the same matter. I say: I die. The Mars person is a copy. My friend thinks this is inconsistent with (1) but I don't see it — the matter is the same in (5) but the causal process is totally different, it's not gradual.

(6) The gap case. Like the destructive upload in (3), but the reconstruction happens ten years after the scan-and-destroy. I say: definitely not me. The gap makes it worse than (3), but (3) was already death, so maybe the gap just makes it more obvious.

(7) The branching case. Like (4), but immediately after the non-destructive scan, the original brain is destroyed. So there's a brief moment where both exist, then the original dies. I want to say: I survive as the upload, because at the moment of branching we were both me, and the destruction of one branch doesn't kill me as long as the other continues. But this feels suspiciously close to (3) and I might be cheating.

(8) The fading case. Like (1), but each replaced neuron's function is gradually transferred to the chip over the course of an hour, with a period where both the biological neuron and the chip are active and contributing to my cognition. I say: still me, even more obviously than (1) because there's overlap.

Is there a single principle — about what personal identity consists in, or what matters for survival — that gets all or most of these verdicts right? I feel like I'm tracking something about gradual causal continuity plus psychological continuity, but then (3) and (5) should have psychological continuity and I'm denying survival there, so that can't be the whole story. And (2) versus (1) suggests I secretly care about consciousness being uninterrupted, but then (8) should bother me if the overlap means my consciousness is somehow split or blurred during the transition, and it doesn't.

Tell me what principle or small set of candidate principles best fits my verdicts, which verdicts I probably have to abandon, and what the resulting view says about a case I haven't considered: suppose the gradual replacement in (1) is done in such tiny steps that each chip is only 1% as functional as the neuron it replaces, but over thousands of steps the full function is restored — does that change anything? Also, please say how confident you are in the principle you're proposing and which part of the analysis you're least sure about.
