I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on causation and moral responsibility, and I keep coming back to Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" as a test case I can't quite settle. The story, as you'll recall, describes a city of almost unimaginable happiness and beauty — festivals, music, tender relationships, genuine flourishing — but the entire edifice rests on a single condition: one child is kept in filth, darkness, and utter misery in a locked cellar. Every citizen learns of the child when they come of age. Most, after an initial shock, come to accept the bargain: the child's suffering is the price of everyone else's joy, and freeing the child would destroy Omelas. They stay. A few, however, walk away — alone, into the darkness beyond the city, and the story tells us they do not come back.

What I'm wrestling with is this. The citizens who stay don't actively harm the child; they merely fail to intervene. They omit to open the door. Yet the story plainly treats their inaction as morally damning — and, I think, as causally implicated in the child's ongoing suffering. This seems to map directly onto a live debate in the metaphysics of causation: can omissions be genuine causes, or are they merely absences that we single out for normative reasons after the fact? A counterfactual approach seems to handle the Omelas case neatly: if the citizens freed the child, the child would stop suffering; so their failure to act is a cause of the suffering. But counterfactual theories notoriously overgenerate — by the same logic, every person in every possible world who fails to travel to Omelas and free the child also counts as a cause, which looks like causal overdetermination gone mad. Normative or "duty-based" views, on the other hand, say we pick out an omission as a cause precisely when the agent had a duty or a reasonable expectation to act. That would explain why we focus on the citizens of Omelas rather than on distant strangers, but it risks collapsing causation into moral judgment — and some metaphysicians find that objectionable on grounds that causation should be a worldly relation, not a projection of our normative attitudes.

I'd like you to read Omelas through the lens of philosophical theories of causation by omission. Where does the story vindicate a particular theoretical approach — for instance, does the fact that the citizens could easily free the child but choose not to track something like the "causal relevance" view, where omissions count as causes when the omitted action was a live possibility? Where does the story complicate or resist philosophical analysis — for instance, does the collective nature of the omission (it is not one person's failure but an entire society's) expose a problem that the literature on individual omissions misses? And what about the ones who walk away: are they causally different from the ones who stay, or merely morally different, and does any theory of causation by omission have the resources to mark that distinction? I want a reading that takes both the story and the metaphysics seriously, that does not reduce the story to a mere illustration of a theory, and that ends by saying what Le Guin's narrative adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture.
