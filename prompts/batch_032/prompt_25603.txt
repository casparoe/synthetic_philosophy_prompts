I'm a condensed matter physicist, and until last semester I would have called myself an unreflective scientific realist: I simply took it for granted that our best theories approximately describe real entities and structures in the world. Then a colleague in philosophy had me read through the pessimistic meta-induction, and I haven't been able to shake it. The argument, as I now understand it: the history of science is a graveyard of theories that were empirically successful by the standards of their day — Ptolemaic astronomy, phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous ether — yet turned out to be radically false, positing entities and structures that do not exist. By induction, our own best theories, successful though they are, are likely to meet the same fate. If the PMI is sound, scientific realism is in serious trouble. And this isn't abstract for me anymore: I now find myself wondering whether the quasiparticles and topological structures I work with daily are tracking anything real, or whether I'm just the latest in a long line of scientists confidently describing fictions.

I want to know what would count against the PMI. Not just the standard replies — I've now read the selective-realist responses about structural continuity and the divide-et-impera strategy — but a systematic map of what would have to be true for the PMI to fail as an argument against realism. Specifically:

What evidence, arguments, or developments would count against it, sorted from those that would be fatal to those that would merely be awkward? For each, what would the world have to look like for that defeater to obtain, and is it live or merely logically possible? Which defeaters would a committed defender of the PMI concede as genuine threats to their argument, and which would they dismiss as question-begging or beside the point? And finally: has the PMI itself been qualified or insulated in ways that make it unfalsifiable — and if so, is that a problem for an inductive argument of this kind, or is it simply what we should expect from a meta-level argument about the history of science?
