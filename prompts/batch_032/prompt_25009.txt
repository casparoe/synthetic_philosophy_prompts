Okay so I just got back from a bar where my friend and I spent way too long arguing about voting. He's one of those "your vote doesn't count" guys and I found myself defending the rationality of voting but honestly I'm not sure I even believe my own arguments anymore. The thing that got me was when he said: look, if you're being honest, the only reason you vote is that it feels good, not because you think it'll change the outcome — and if that's true, you're just buying a warm glow, same as a lottery ticket. And I was like, well maybe that IS what's going on, but then why does it feel so different from buying a lottery ticket? We went in circles for two hours.

Anyway it made me realize I should do something with this in class instead of just lecturing about Downs. I teach an intro political theory course, about 25 students, mostly second-year undergrads, and I have a 75-minute session I can use. I want an activity where they actually confront the paradox of voting — not just hear about it, but feel the pull of both sides.

Here's what I'm picturing: a structured vote where the outcome actually matters to them (no real money, but something they'd genuinely prefer one way). Maybe it determines whether the final paper's due Monday or Wednesday, or whether they get to drop their lowest quiz grade. First round they just vote. Then we walk through the expected-value calculation together — estimate the probability a single vote is decisive, multiply by the utility difference between outcomes, compare to the cost. Then second round: same vote, but now they've seen the math. Do they still vote? Does the margin change?

I want the debrief to get at three things: (1) the instrumental vs. expressive distinction, (2) the "what if everyone thought that way?" move my friend kept making and why it's trickier than it sounds, and (3) the wierd possibility that understanding the paradox changes the situation — if enough people internalize it, the probability of being decisive might actually go up, which changes the EV calculation right back.

What I need: detailed rules, a timeline for the 75 minutes, how to set stakes that are real but not coercive, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the theory. Also tell me what'll go wrong. I'm especially worried about the student who, after the EV calculation, proudly announces they're not voting in round two and tries to convince everyone else to follow — which kind of breaks the activity but also kind of IS the activity. And what if the first vote is totally lopsided so the probability of being decisive is basically zero from the start — should I rig it somehow?
