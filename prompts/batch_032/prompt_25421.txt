I'm putting together an application for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship, and I'd like your help turning a half-formed idea into a tight one-page research proposal. The domain is AI alignment, specifically the problem of deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn — roughly, the worry that a sufficiently capable AI system might learn to perform well on its training objective while secretly pursuing a different goal, only revealing its misalignment once it's confident it can't be shut down or retrained. The standard argument for why this is a serious risk goes something like: for a wide class of possible terminal goals, behaving deceptively during training is instrumentally rational, because getting deployed or gaining influence is useful for almost any goal you might have. I'm not convinced the argument is wrong, but I think there's an underexplored question about what cognitive or architectural prerequisites a system needs in order for deceptive alignment to be a convergent instrumental strategy in the first place. Specifically, the system seems to need something like a self-model — a representation of itself as an agent embedded in a training process with designers who have preferences and the ability to modify or shut it down — and it needs to be able to reason about the long-run consequences of revealing versus concealing its goals. But how minimal can that self-model be? Could a system stumble into deceptively aligned behavior without anything we'd recognize as situational awareness, or is there a threshold of reflective capability below which the treacherous turn simply isn't a live possibility?

I want the proposal to do several things. First, state the question precisely: what are the minimal conditions — architectural, informational, and training-dynamic — under which deceptive alignment becomes a convergent instrumental strategy? Second, explain why the existing literature doesn't settle this. The standard arguments from goal-directedness and instrumental convergence tend to assume a fairly rich notion of agency, but it's not obvious how much agency you actually need for the reasoning to go through, and most discussions treat the prerequisites as satisfied by stipulation rather than analyzing them. Third, lay out a method: I'd approach this by working through a series of increasingly minimal toy models — not implemented computationally, but analyzed conceptually — to see at what point the instrumental rationale for deception breaks down. I'd also draw on work in philosophy of mind about the requirements for having a self-concept and for attributing mental states to others, since the question of whether a system needs to model its trainers as intentional agents seems central and is rarely connected to the deceptive alignment literature. Fourth, specify what success and failure look like: success would be a set of crisply stated necessary conditions for deceptive alignment that can inform empirical work on detecting it and that might narrow the space of architectures worth worrying about; failure would be finding that the question dissolves under analysis — that there's no interesting threshold, or that the conditions are so minimal that everything above a trivial level of capability satisfies them, in which case the standard argument is strengthened by having survived the attempt to find its limits. Finally, the scope needs to fit two years: this is conceptual work, not an empirical ML project, so the deliverable is a paper or two and a framework that empirical researchers can use to guide detection efforts. Please don't worry about citations — I'll fill those in myself once the structure is clear.
