Two friends of mine got into it over Kavka's toxin puzzle at a party — you know, the one where a billionaire offers you a million dollars if you *intend* tonight to drink a toxin tomorrow that'll make you sick for a day, and the money hits your account the moment you've formed the intention, before you actually have to drink anything.

(1) Dan said the puzzle is overblown: of course you can intend to drink it. You just decide to be the kind of person who follows through, and then you follow through. The million dollars is worth a day of misery, so just drink the damn thing and the problem evaporates.

(2) Mira pushed back: that's cheating. The whole point is you get the money *before* you drink, and you know you'll have no reason to drink once you've got it. If you're rational, you won't drink — and since you know that now, you can't genuinely intend to drink. You can't sincerely tell yourself "I will do X" while simultaneously knowing you won't.

(3) Dan said that's too restrictive a theory of intention. People intend to quit smoking all the time, knowing full well they'll probably fail. The intention is genuine even if the follow-through is shaky. The toxin case just makes the shakiness 100% instead of 70%.

(4) Mira said no, the smoking case is different: the smoker still *wants* to quit, still thinks quitting would be good, and the failure is a failure of willpower against craving. In the toxin case, there's no craving to resist — there's literally no reason to drink, and you know it. It's not weakness of will, it's that drinking would be pointlessly self-destructive once the money's secured.

(5) Dan came back with: fine, then what about a parent who tells their kid "I'll pick you up at 5," fully intending to do so, even though they know a work emergency might come up? They're forming an intention while knowing there's a chance they'll have overriding reasons later. The toxin case is just the limiting case where the chance is 100%.

(6) Mira said that's still not it, because the parent has a *reason* to pick up the kid — they made a promise. The toxin-drinker has no reason at all, not even a promissory one, because the deal explicitly says you get the money regardless of whether you drink.

(7) Dan then said: but that's exactly what makes the puzzle interesting — can you give yourself a reason by forming the intention? If I intend to drink, I've now made myself a promise to myself, and breaking promises to yourself corrodes your self-trust. So there *is* a reason to drink: preserving your ability to form credible intentions in the future.

(8) Mira replied that this just pushes the question back: can you form a genuine intention to drink *in order to* create that very reason? It's circular. You'd need to already have the intention for the reason to exist, but you need the reason to exist before you can form the intention.

They went in circles for another twenty minutes and eventually asked me to settle it, and I had no idea what to say. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what at each step?
