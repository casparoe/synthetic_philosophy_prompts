I've been tracking a friend's views on animal signals for years — she's a behavioral ecologist who works on honest signaling theory — and I'm trying to figure out what rule she's actually running, and what she'd say about a case I haven't brought up yet. I suspect there's some unacknowledged tension in her verdicts, and I'd like to get it clear before I see her next.

Here are the cases I've collected from our conversations, with her classification of whether each trait counts as a "Zahavian handicap" — an honest signal of individual quality whose reliability is guaranteed by its cost:

1. **Peacock's train**: YES. The tail is metabolically expensive, impairs locomotion, and attracts predators. Only high-quality males can produce an elaborate one, so the signal is honest. Textbook case.

2. **Gazelle stotting**: YES. The stiff-legged vertical leap that gazelles perform when they spot a predator burns energy and slows escape. She says only a gazelle fast enough to spare the cost can afford to advertise, so the signal honestly tells the predator "don't bother chasing me."

3. **Poison dart frog bright coloration**: NO. She says this is aposematism, not a handicap. The signal advertises toxicity, not individual quality, and the cost of conspicuousness is offset by the protection the toxicity provides. Different mechanism entirely.

4. **Bowerbird bower**: YES. Males build and decorate elaborate structures that take weeks, require fine motor control and cognitive skill, and offer no direct survival benefit. She calls it a classic extended-phenotype handicap.

5. **Deer antlers**: YES. I pushed her on this one because antlers obviously function as weapons, not just signals. She acknowledged the dual function but said they're still a handicap: they're regrown annually at enormous metabolic cost, their size is strongly condition-dependent, and they honestly signal fighting ability even before any combat occurs. The fact that they're also useful in fights doesn't disqualify them.

6. **Firefly flashes**: YES. Bioluminescent courtship signals are energetically expensive, and flash rate and intensity correlate with male nutritional condition. Straightforward handicap, she says.

7. **Human male beard**: NO. She's skeptical. She says there's no good evidence that beard growth is differentially costly for low-quality males in a way that would enforce honesty, and she suspects it's either a Fisherian arbitrary trait or a byproduct of hormonal physiology. The handicap framework doesn't apply.

8. **Cichlid egg spots** (the colorful spots on the anal fins of male haplochromine cichlids that resemble eggs): YES. She says these are costly — they increase predation risk and use carotenoid pigments that could go to immune function — and they honestly signal male quality to females. The fact that they also exploit a female sensory bias for egg-like shapes doesn't make them any less of a handicap.

Now here's the new case I want to predict her verdict on: **human height as a signal in mating contexts.** Taller men have higher reproductive success across many cultures, and height is condition-dependent — it's sensitive to childhood nutrition and disease load, so it honestly signals developmental health. But height is also obviously functional for reasons having nothing to do with signaling: it helps with reach, strength, and historically with predator detection and combat. It's not a dedicated signal; it's a whole-body trait that gets used as a cue.

I can see at least two rules that fit all eight of her verdicts:

- **Rule A (dedicated signal):** A trait is a Zahavian handicap only if it evolved primarily for signaling and its cost is a wasteful expenditure that serves no other fitness-relevant function. The antlers case strains this — she'd have to argue signaling is primary and combat is secondary — but she did say that explicitly.

- **Rule B (differential cost):** A trait is a Zahavian handicap if it's costly, condition-dependent, and the marginal cost of producing or maintaining the trait is higher for low-quality individuals, regardless of whether the trait serves other functions. On this rule, antlers are an easy yes, and height would also be a yes.

If she's running Rule A, she should say height is NO — it's not a dedicated signal, it's a functional trait that gets co-opted as a cue. If she's running Rule B, she should say height is YES — it's costly, condition-dependent, and the cost of achieving tall stature is higher for individuals with poor developmental health.

Which rule do you think she's actually running, based on her verdicts? What would she say about height? And is there a further case — ideally a trait in a non-human animal — that would cleanly discriminate between Rule A and Rule B, so I can bring it up next time and settle this?
