I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to think through utilitarianism on my own. The framework appeals to me because it's clean — you specify an objective function (total well-being) and you optimize. But I've constructed an argument that seems to show the framework has an unacceptable consequence, and I can't figure out where the argument goes wrong.

Here it is:

(1) According to utilitarianism, the right action is the one that maximizes total well-being, understood as the sum of well-being across all affected individuals.

(2) If the right action is the one that maximizes a sum across individuals, then the fact that a cost falls on one person and a benefit accrues to another is, in itself, morally irrelevant. All that matters is the net change in the total. (Compare: when I decide whether to undergo a painful surgery, I don't weigh Tuesday's pain against Wednesday's relief as if they belong to different people — I just compare magnitudes, because the sum is what counts.)

(3) If interpersonal distribution is morally irrelevant beyond its effect on the total sum, then it is permissible to impose a severe harm on one person whenever doing so produces a greater total benefit distributed across others — regardless of how that benefit is distributed, and regardless of whether the harmed person consents.

(4) If (3), then in a standard transplant case — one healthy person, five patients who will die without organ transplants — it is permissible to kill the one and harvest their organs, since five lives saved contributes more to the total sum than one life lost.

(5) It is not permissible to kill an innocent person to harvest their organs, even to save five.

(6) Therefore, by modus tollens from (4) and (5), (3) is false. And if (3) is false, then either (2) or (1) must be false.

I can see several places one might resist. The most interesting to me is premise (2). I've read enough to know that some philosophers object that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons — that it treats a group of people as if they were a single entity with a single utility function, when in fact each person is a distinct locus of value. But as a programmer, I find this puzzling. If I have an array of values and I'm minimizing a loss function, I sum across all elements; the fact that element [i] and element [j] are distinct indices doesn't block summation. What would it mean for persons to be separate in a way that makes aggregation illegitimate? And if I reject (2), what else do I lose? It seems like rejecting (2) might also undermine cost-benefit analysis in public policy, or the principle that we should allocate scarce resources to save the greatest number of lives — conclusions I'm reluctant to accept.

Another possibility: maybe the argument is valid and I should bite the bullet on (4). Some utilitarians do. But that strikes me as a reductio of the whole approach.

Or maybe there's a scope ambiguity I'm missing — perhaps between act- and rule-utilitarianism, or between permissible and obligatory. I'm not trained in this, so I could easily be making a mistake I can't see.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd particularly like to know whether the argument contains a subtle logical flaw (an equivocation, a quantifier shift, a modal slide) or whether it's valid and the real question is which plausible premise is least costly to abandon.
