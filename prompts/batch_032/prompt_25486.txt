I'm a PhD student in environmental policy and I've been stuck on something that came up in a seminar last week about congestion pricing. So the basic move is familiar: roads are a commons, drivers impose delay on each other but don't internalize that cost, so you slap a price on entry to the city center and call it a day—London, Singapore, Stockholm, Milan, now New York. Classic Pigou, neat and tidy. But here's what's been gnawing at me. We don't do this for a whole bunch of other congested shared resources. Crowded sidewalks, packed beaches on a hot day, the line for a public bathroom at a concert, popular hiking trails where you're basically in a conga-line up the mountain—we let all of those play out through first-come norms or just tolerating the mess. And even for roads, cities sometimes go the opposite direction: alternate-day driving bans (which kind of assume you can't trust people to calculate prices), or moral appeals during droughts ("take shorter showers, we're all in this together"), which treat the problem as one of collective responsibility rather than incentive alignment.

So what philosophical picture of a person—and of a collective action problem—does the choice to price rather than norm-manage a commons actually presuppose? I can think of a few candidats. Maybe it's assuming that the relevant agents are strangers interacting anonymously at scale, so reputational mechanisms have no grip (which would track why we price highways but not bathroom queues). Maybe it's assuming that the crowding is driven by genuinely inelastic needs that deliberation wouldn't shift, so you need the price signal to do the rationing. Or maybe—and this is the one that bothers me—it's assuming that people are fundamentally self-interested utility-maximizers and that appealing to norms is naive, which would make the policy not just a practical tool but a kind of implicit anthropology.

The Ostrom-shaped worry is that if you introduce pricing into a domain, you might crowd out whatever norms were there or prevent them from forming, and then you're locked into pricing forever. So does the congestion-pricing city have to be betting that there weren't any shared norms around road-use to begin with? Or can you coherently hold that pricing is just one governance tool among many and you switch between them as conditions change—which seems to be what Ostrom would want, but I'm not sure the institutions actually work that way in practice.

I guess what I'm after is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of collective action that congestion pricing commits a city to, separating what it strictly presupposes from what just happens to travel with it. And then: if a city actually took Ostrom's framework seriously—polycentric governance, communication, graduated sanctions, all of it—what would it do about downtown traffic instead, or would it end up adopting pricing anyway and just surround it with different institutional wrapping?
