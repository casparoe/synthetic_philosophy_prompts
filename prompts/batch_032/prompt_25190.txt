The species problem in philosophy of biology — the question of what kind of thing a species is — has been actively debated for at least half a century, and the literature now contains over two dozen competing species concepts: the biological species concept (populations reproductively isolated from one another), the phylogenetic species concept (the smallest diagnosable cluster of organisms with a shared pattern of ancestry), the ecological species concept (a lineage occupying a distinct adaptive zone), and many others. Biologists in different subfields routinely use different concepts, and the same organisms get partitioned differently depending on which concept is applied. Yet there is no sign of convergence. My question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of species concepts. Specifically, I want you to assess which of the following hypotheses best characterizes the impasse, or propose a better one:

1. The disagreement is largely verbal: the term "species" picks out several distinct but individually coherent phenomena, and the dispute is about which one gets the name — a terminological choice with no fact of the matter.
2. The disagreement is substantive but not empirical: there is a fact about what species are, but it is a metaphysical fact (about natural kinds, individuality, or the structure of the world) that empirical data alone cannot settle.
3. The disagreement is empirical but underdetermined: the world really contains multiple, cross-cutting patterns of organismic grouping (reproductive, genealogical, ecological), and the question "which one is the real species?" assumes a uniqueness the world does not provide.
4. The disagreement reflects different explanatory projects: systematists, ecologists, and population geneticists are asking different questions — about classification, about the units of selection, about the boundaries of gene flow — and each concept is right for its own question, with no deeper conflict.

For each hypothesis, identify what it would take for it to be correct — what the world would have to be like, or what the disputants would have to be doing — and then give your assessment of which hypothesis best fits the actual pattern of argument in the debate. In particular: do the disputants genuinely disagree about anything that would force a change in biological practice if resolved, or is the persistence of the debate itself evidence that the question is ill-posed? And if the debate is not ill-posed, what would count as progress — a formal result, a new kind of data, a conceptual distinction that has not yet been drawn, or something else?
