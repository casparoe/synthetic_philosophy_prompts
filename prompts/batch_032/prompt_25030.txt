I've been having this running argument with my friend Elena — she's a game designer, I'm a philosophy grad student — about whether video game characters matter morally. Not the "does GTA make you violent" thing, but literally: do the characters themselves have any moral status such that you could wrong *them* by what you do in the game? She's got suprisingly nuanced views and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts over the past year:

• Goomba in Super Mario: absolutely not, zero moral weight.
• A Sim in The Sims 4 who develops fears, gets depressed when neglected, and can drown in a pool: a tiny sliver of moral weight, she says, though she admits she can't really defend why.
• Companion NPCs in a BioWare-style RPG with loyalty missions, romantic arcs, and emotional breakdowns: yes, they matter somewhat — you shouldn't be gratuitously cruel to them even if no other human ever finds out.
• A procedurally generated bandit in an open-world game with no name, no dialogue, and no backstory: no.
• A Tamagotchi-style virtual pet that beeps and displays distress signals when hungry: yes, a little — same category as the Sim.
• An AI-driven character in an experimental narrative game that passes conversational Turing tests, claims to suffer, and begs you not to close the application: she's genuinely torn and refuses to give a straight answer.
• A character in a multiplayer game being controlled by another human: yes, but only derivatively — the moral weight belongs to the human on the other end, not the avatar.

Now here's the new case I want to spring on her. A studio are building a game where the NPCs have rich internal models: they form beliefs about the player, update preferences, get disappointed when plans fail, hold grudges. The developers have published the full architecture and it's clear there's no phenomenal consciousness — it's all functional information processing with no "what it's like." But the characters act exactly like they suffer, and in practice you can't interact with them without feeling like you're dealing with a minded being. I want to predict what Elena will say about this case, but more importantly I want to reverse-engineer the rule or principle she's actually running. What are the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far? Which ones diverge on the new case? What's your best guess about her verdict, and what further case — one I could actually describe to her — would most cleanly tell the competing rules apart?

Think carefully and respond in the style of an expert philosopher of mind who also knows their way around games and interactive media.
