I'm a grad student in cognitive science and I've been trying to get my head around the predictive processing / free energy principle literature for a few months now. One thing I keep tripping over is the so-called "dark room problem." As I understand it, the objection goes: if the brain's whole deal is minimizing prediction error (or free energy, or surprise), then the optimal policy would be to find a dark, quiet, unchanging room and just sit there forever — no new sensory input, no surprises, minimal prediction error. But organisms obviously don't do that, so something must be wrong with the framework.

Then I read the standard replies and they seem to come in layers. There's the reply that says you'd predict your own interoceptive signals going haywire (hunger, thirst, etc.) so a dark room wouldn't actually minimize prediction error once you account for those. There's the reply about prior preferences — the agent has high prior probability on occupying certain states (like being fed, being at the right temperature), and those priors generate prediction errors that drive action. And then there's the more sophisticated reply involving expected free energy and active inference, where the agent isn't just minimizing current prediction error but expected future prediction error, which actually pushes toward exploration and information-seeking rather than hiding in the dark.

What I can't figure out is whether these are three different replies to the same objection, or replies to progressively more sophisticated versions of the objection, or whether the later replies are supposed to make the earlier ones unnecessary. Like, if you buy the expected-free-energy story, do you even need the interoceptive one? And does the dark room problem actually land as a criticism of predictive processing *without* the full active inference apparatus, or is it only a problem for a stripped-down version that nobody really defends?

Basically I want someone to map out the dialectical terrain for me: what exactly is the dark room problem claiming, what are the main responses, which responses answer which version of the problem, and where the debate currently stands. I'm less interested in the technical details of the math and more in the conceptual architecture of the argument and counterarguments.
