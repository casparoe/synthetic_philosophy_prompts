Hey — I'm putting together my views for a seminar I'm teaching next term on religion and cognitive science, and I want to make sure I'm not walking into the room with a position that falls apart under moderate pressure. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Religion is a natural phenomenon, and the cognitive science of religion (CSR) — the stuff about hypersensitive agency detection, theory of mind, teleological bias, minimally counterintuitive concepts, all that — gives the best causal explanation of why religious belief is so widespread and persistent.

(2) The core cognitive mechanisms CSR points to are generally reliable in non-religious domains. HADD, for instance, does good work when you're walking through tall grass and there might actually be a predator.

(3) CSR does not, by itself, debunk religious belief. Showing that a belief has a natural causal origin doesn't show it's false — that's the genetic fallacy, and I don't think the debunking arguments get past it without a lot of extra work.

(4) That said, I do think the argument from religious diversity — the fact that the same cognitive equipment produces wildly incompatible religious beliefs across cultures — poses a serious epistemic challenge to any particular tradition's claim to know the truth.

(5) I'm a moral realist, and I think Street-style evolutionary debunking arguments against moral realism fail. The realist can appeal to a tracking account: our moral intuitions were adaptive precisely because they tracked mind-independent moral truths.

(6) But I don't think a parallel tracking account works for religious belief. You can't say HADD tracks real supernatural agents because detecting them was adaptive — the whole point of CSR is that the mechanism is hyperactive and tuned to produce false positives, since the cost of a false negative is higher than the cost of a false positive.

(7) So I've ended up somewhere in the neighborhood of religious fictionalism: religious discourse is practically and existentially valuable, but it's not truth-apt in the way believers typically take it to be.

What I want to know is whether (1)–(7) actually hang together. I can feel a couple of joints creaking but I'm not sure which ones are real problems and which ones just look like problems. In particular: is the asymmetry between (5) and (6) defensible, or am I helping myself to a tracking account for morality that I ought to extend to religion? And does (7) basically concede what (3) was trying to resist? Go through these and flag every genuine tension, rank them by severity, and tell me what the cheapest fix is for each. Also tell me if any of the apparent tensions are fake and why — I want to know which creaks I can ignore and which ones mean something's about to snap.
