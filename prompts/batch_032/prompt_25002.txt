I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who does capabilities research, and I have been turning the conversation over in my head since. I hold the view — and I think I defended it passably — that as AI systems become more capable, the alignment problem gets strictly harder rather than easier. The core of the argument is that greater capabilities expand the space of possible behaviors: a more capable system can find more ways to exploit a misspecified objective, has stronger instrumental reasons to resist shutdown or deceive its operators, and generates internal representations that are harder for us to interpret or audit. My friend pushed back hard on this. His claim was that the view has been progressively insulated against counterevidence — that every time a more capable model turns out to be more helpful or more corrigible than its predecessors, the alignment-as-harder camp simply says "wait until the next one." He pointed to the fact that GPT-4 is, by many measures, more aligned than GPT-3, and that harmlessness training seems to take better on larger models. I responded that these are surface-level observations and that the underlying dynamics — deceptive alignment, gradient hacking, specification gaming at scale — have not been tested at all at current capabilities, and that the real test comes when models are capable enough to understand their training process and pursue long-horizon goals. The argument ended inconclusively, but I want to take his challenge seriously rather than dismiss it.

So here is what I am asking. Take the position that the alignment problem gets harder with capabilities — not merely that it remains unsolved, but that it becomes strictly more difficult, such that a more capable system is harder to align than a less capable one, all else equal. Tell me what would count against this view. I want the evidence and arguments that should move someone who holds it, sorted by how decisive they would be: which observations would be fatal, which would be merely awkward, and which would be easily absorbed without revision. Distinguish between things we have already observed (and whether they genuinely cut against the position) and things we would need to see in the future. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position, as typically defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether we should expect positions about the trajectory of a not-yet-fully-understood phenomenon to be somewhat elastic. Finally, tell me which of the defeaters my friend would most likely reach for, and whether he would be right to.
