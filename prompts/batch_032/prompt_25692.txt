I'm a science journalist at a weekly, and I need a decision procedure for something I face every coupla days: when is a scientific finding solid enough that I should report it as true — or true enough — rather than burying it in hedges or killing the story entirely? I've read enough philosophy to know the pragmatists had two very different pictures of truth that might actually help here. Peirce said truth is what the community of inquirers would converge on in the long run, given enough investigation — so the question is whether the finding is on a trajectory toward consensus, whether the methods are sound, whether the people who should be skeptical are skeptical. James said truth is what works, what has cash value in experience — so the question is whether acting as if the finding is true would lead to better outcomes, whether the belief pays its way. These pull in different directions and I want a procedure that uses both.

I need a checklist I can run in a few hours max. It should walk me through: what to check first, what counts as good enough on each question, where I should spike the story, where I should run it with specific caveats, and — crucially — where the procedure is really just me making a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item. I don't need it to tell me what's true. I need it to tell me what's responsible to print.

Run it on three findings I've actually had to deal with: (1) a large observational study finding moderate coffee drinking associated with lower all-cause mortality — big sample, decent controls, but you can't randomize people to a lifetime of espresso; (2) a replication of a famous social priming study that found the effect in one of three conditions with a much smaller effect size; (3) a physics collaboration announcing a 3.5-sigma excess that could be a new particle, in a field where 5-sigma is the discovery threshold. For each, tell me what the procedure says, where it's silent, and whether I should lose sleep over the call.
