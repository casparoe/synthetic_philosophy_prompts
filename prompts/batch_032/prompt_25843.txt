I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social philosophy, and we're spending two weeks on David Lewis's account of convention from his book *Convention: A Philosophical Study* (1969). I'd like to pair the theoretical material with Shirley Jackson's short story "The Lottery" (1948), which I'm assigning as a companion text.

Lewis argues that conventions are arbitrary but stable regularities that solve recurrent coordination problems: they are self-enforcing because each person has reason to conform given that others do, they could have been otherwise, and they persist through mutual expectation and common knowledge without requiring explicit agreement. Jackson's story depicts a convention — the annual stoning — that seems, on its face, to be maintained through precisely these mechanisms: precedent, mutual expectation, and the sheer inertial weight of "that's how we've always done it." Yet the lottery is a convention that no one can articulate a genuine purpose for, that many participants seem privately uneasy about, and that everyone would presumably be better off abandoning. Old Man Warner's proverb — "Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon" — gestures at a long-forgotten rationale, but the ritual details have eroded over time while the essential violence remains intact.

I'd like a reading of "The Lottery" through Lewis's theory that takes both seriously and doesn't reduce either to a straw man. Specifically:

1. Where does the story illustrate Lewis's mechanisms — salience, precedent, common knowledge of mutual expectations — in ways that deepen our understanding of how conventions grip participants? Which scenes or details show Lewisian convention at work?

2. Where does the story pose genuine problems for Lewis's framework? Can his account explain a convention that persists when the original coordination problem has become opaque to participants, or when the convention is actively harmful and would be rejected if the population could coordinate on an alternative? Does the story suggest that Lewis's picture is too rationalistic — that it misses the way conventions can become ritualized, insulated from the purposes that originally sustained them, and propped up by superstition rather than by mutually recognized interests?

3. What does the story add to the philosophical debate that Lewis's formal account alone doesn't capture? I want students to see both that Lewis's framework genuinely illuminates the story's logic and that the story forces us to complicate the framework — perhaps by attending to the difference between a convention that is in fact a coordination equilibrium and one that is merely believed to be, or to the role of sanction and fear in maintaining regularities that have ceased to serve anyone's interests.

I'm not looking for a thematic essay that gestures vaguely at "tradition" or "conformity." I want a reading that engages the specific conceptual architecture of Lewis's account — arbitrariness, coordination equilibria, common knowledge, precedent, salience — and tests it against the specific narrative machinery of Jackson's story: the black box, the slips of paper, the children gathering stones before they fully grasp why, the neighboring village that is talking of giving up the lottery, Old Man Warner's insistence that abandoning it would return them to primitive chaos, and Tessie Hutchinson's protest that the process "wasn't fair" even as she participates in it until the moment it turns against her.
