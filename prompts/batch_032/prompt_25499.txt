In a faculty working group on technology policy, one of the senior engineers has a line he returns to whenever someone proposes regulating a technology itself rather than just its misuse: "A hammer can build a house or break a skull — the hammer doesn't have politics. Technology is just a tool." From this he concludes that ethical and legal scrutiny should target users and applications, never the design or deployment of the artifact, and that talk of "biased algorithms" or "coercive architecture" is a category mistake. I want to engage his analogy on its own terms rather than just citing the critical literature at him. Please audit the technology-as-tool analogy: lay out the mapping (what in the technological domain corresponds to the hammer, the handle, the act of swinging, the builder and the assailant, the house and the skull), identify where the mapping breaks, and flag which features of "tool" the analogy quietly imports that many real technologies lack — scale, embedded defaults, path-dependence, irreversibility of infrastructure, and so on. Then say whether his conclusion — that regulating the artifact itself is always a mistake — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better master analogy for what technology is, name it and say what it captures that "tool" misses. Keep the response to 600 words.
