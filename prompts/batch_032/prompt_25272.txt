I recently joined the board of a family foundation that makes grants across sharply different domains — medical research, arts funding, environmental conservation, direct poverty relief — and I have discovered that my own judgments about when tradeoffs are possible do not fit neatly into any single view I have encountered. I would like to know what theory of value and choice actually captures my verdicts, or comes closest.

Here are the cases, with my honest reactions:

1. We must choose between funding a malaria prevention program that will save roughly 200 lives and funding a clean-water project that will save roughly 150 lives and also substantially reduce waterborne illness. I find these straightforwardly comparable: you estimate the expected benefit and pick the larger one, with the comparison rough but unproblematic in principle.

2. We must choose between funding a contemporary art exhibition and funding a meals program for isolated elderly people. I do not think these are comparable on a single scale of goodness. But I also do not think they are equally good — the claim that they are equal strikes me as false. It is more that neither is better than the other, yet they are not equal either. This feels like a different kind of case from case 1, not merely a harder version of it.

3. We must choose between a program that prevents fifty cases of childhood blindness and a program that provides music education to five thousand children. I find myself thinking these are comparable — I can weigh the severity of blindness against the breadth of music education — but the comparison feels strained in a way that case 1 does not. Still, I come down on the side of comparability.

4. A donor offers us a very large sum on the condition that we exclude grant recipients of a particular ethnicity. Accepting would let us fund every one of our top-ranked programs. I think this is not a matter of weighing the good consequences against the bad: the condition makes acceptance impermissible regardless of the numbers. This is not incommensurability as I understand it — it is more like a constraint that is simply not up for trade.

5. We must choose between two medical-research programs, one on Alzheimer's and one on childhood leukemia. Both would save lives; the Alzheimer's program would save more. I think these are comparable and the numbers matter, but I also feel the pull of the thought that saving a child's life and saving an elderly person's life are not fully commensurable. I end up concluding they are comparable, but I am less confident than in case 1.

6. We must allocate our own small staff's time between evaluating arts grants and evaluating poverty grants. I think this is comparable in principle — we can estimate the marginal impact of staff time in each domain — but in practice the comparison requires judgments so coarse that I am unsure the exercise is meaningful. I am torn about whether to call this commensurable or incommensurable.

7. We have the option to fund a program that modestly improves conditions for thousands of animals in factory farms or a program that provides significant help to five human families in crisis. I find myself thinking the human claims have a kind of priority that is not just a matter of weighing — that even a large quantity of animal suffering does not straightforwardly outweigh a much smaller quantity of human suffering. But I am not comfortable with this verdict, and I am unsure it survives reflection.

Is there a coherent account of value comparability — of when and how values can be weighed against one another — that delivers these verdicts, or most of them? Which of my verdicts would such an account force me to revise? And what does the account say about a case I have not yet faced: choosing between preventing a harm (say, a disease outbreak that would disable a hundred people) and producing a positive good of a very different kind (say, creating a lasting cultural institution that would enrich a community for decades)?
