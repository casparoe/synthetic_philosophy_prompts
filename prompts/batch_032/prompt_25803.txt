At a department reception last week I overheard two colleagues arguing about origin essentialism — specifically, whether a given human being could have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from. I reconstructed their exchange afterward and would like you to adjudicate it, move by move. Here is what I recall.

(1) The first colleague — call her A — defended the Kripkean position: if you had come from a different sperm and a different egg, the resulting organism would have had a different genome, and that organism would simply not be you. She offered the standard thought experiment: consider any actual person. Could that very person have developed from gametes that produced someone with a different genetic makeup? Intuitively not; that would be a numerically distinct person. Origin is essential.

(2) The second colleague — call him B — replied that this intuition weakens once we consider early embryology. Monozygotic twins originate from the very same sperm and egg, yet they are distinct persons. So genetic identity at conception does not suffice for personal identity. If one zygote can split into two persons, he asked, why couldn't two different zygotes — from different sperm-egg pairs — converge onto the same person? He pressed A to say what blocks this possibility without begging the question.

(3) A answered that the twin case shows only that the relation "originates from gametes G" is one-to-many, not many-to-one. That the same gametes can produce two persons does not entail that different gametes could produce the same person. She added that B's convergence scenario would require a single person to be identical with two numerically distinct zygotes, which violates the transitivity of identity.

(4) B countered that A was assuming what needs to be shown — namely, that the person is identical with the zygote rather than with some later entity that could have been reached by more than one developmental route. He offered an analogy: a particular river could have originated from a different combination of tributaries, so long as the resulting watercourse is the same. If personhood attaches to the psychological organism that emerges later, what matters is whether that organism could have been reached from a different starting point, not whether the starting points themselves are identical.

(5) A responded that the river analogy fails because rivers are not substances in the relevant sense — they are aggregates whose material composition changes continuously. Persons, by contrast, are biological individuals whose identity conditions are tied to the organismic continuity that begins at conception. Different gametes, different organism. B replied that this merely restates the intuition rather than defending it, and that A has given no non-question-begging reason to treat conception as a metaphysical bright line.

At that point I was called away. Please adjudicate their exchange in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each point, identify who has the better of the argument at that stage and briefly justify your assessment.
