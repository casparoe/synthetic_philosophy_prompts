I keep noticing something in discussions about genetics and I'm wondering if there's a term for it. People talk about DNA as a "code" or as "carrying information," and they seem to mean at least two different things by it, but they slide between them without noticing — and the slide does real argumentative work.

Here's the kind of thing I mean. Someone starts by saying DNA contains the instructions for building proteins, like a blueprint or a recipe. When you push back and say "but blueprints are made by an agent with intentions, and recipes are for agents who can read and follow them — who's the reader of DNA?", they'll say oh no, they didn't mean it literally, it's just a metaphor for the causal role: the sequence determines the amino acid sequence, that's all. Fair enough. But then five minutes later they're arguing that because DNA "encodes" information, there's a sense in which the organism was "specified" in the genome, or that we can talk about "misinformation" when a mutation occurs, or that genes "carry meaning." And those claims only make sense if you've drifted back to the semantic, intentional sense of information — the one they just disavowed.

Another instance: in debates about whether genes alone determine traits, you'll see someone defend a strong genetic determinism by saying "the information is all in the DNA," and when challenged they'll retreat to a purely statistical or Shannon-style definition of information (correlation, reduction of uncertainty). But then they'll turn around and use the same phrase — "the information is in the DNA" — to imply that the environment is merely a background condition, which doesn't follow at all from the Shannon definition.

It's not exactly equivocation in the standard sense, because the two meanings are related — they're not completely different words that happen to sound the same. And it's not quite motte-and-bailey, because the person isn't always aware they're switching. It's more like there's a fuzzy concept that gets to borrow prestige from the semantic sense while only paying the argumentative costs of the causal sense.

Does this pattern have an established name in philosophy of biology or philosophy of language? If not, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from ordinary equivocation, from motte-and-bailey, and from the kind of metaphor that just gets taken litterally over time? I'm not just after a label — I want to know what exactly the pattern is, what its edges are, and whether it shows up in other domains too or is specific to the way people talk about genes.
