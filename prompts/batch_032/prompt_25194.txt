I'm a junior in high school and I run my school's philosophy club. In two weeks I'm leading a session on the question "Why is there something rather than nothing?" The club has about fifteen members, mostly juniors and seniors. None of them have studied philosophy formally — they've read some Camus and Sartre for English class and a few have watched online lectures, but that's the ceiling. We have forty minutes.

I don't need you to explain the question to me; I've read enough to know the basic landscape. What I need is a plan for how to lead them into it so that by the end they feel the weight of the question rather than just having heard about it. Specifically:

- What idea has to be established first, before the question even registers as a philosophical problem rather than a scientific one? I'm worried they'll immediately go to the Big Bang and think cosmology has already answered it.

- What single example, analogy, or thought-experiment should carry the whole session — something concrete enough to return to whenever the discussion drifts — and why that one rather than the obvious alternatives?

- What misconceptions will they form, in what order, and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? I'm anticipating at least three: that the question is literally meaningless (the verificationist objection), that "God did it" is an answer that stops the regress rather than relocating it, and that quantum fluctuations or the multiverse explain why there is something rather than nothing. There may be others I haven't thought of.

- What should I deliberately leave out or openly refuse to explain at this level? I want them to leave with a clear sense of the question, not a survey of twenty positions they'll half-remember.

- What is one question I can pose in the last three minutes that will tell me whether they've understood the question itself — its distinctive difficulty — rather than just enjoyed the conversation?
