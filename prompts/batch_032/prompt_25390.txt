I recently read the following summary of Aumann's agreement theorem in a colleague's introductory book on game theory and social epistemology:

"Aumann proved in his famous agreement theorem that two rational agents with the same information cannot agree to disagree. If you and I both observe the same evidence and update on it using Bayesian reasoning, our posterior probabilities for any given proposition must converge — and if we announce our posteriors to each other and each knows the other is rational, they must become identical. This is a powerful result because it shows that persistent disagreement between reasonable people is, in a deep sense, impossible: whenever you encounter enduring disagreement in the real world, at least one party must be processing information irrationally or concealing private evidence. The theorem thus gives us reason to treat intractable political and religious disputes not as clashes of legitimate perspectives but as symptoms of cognitive failure somewhere in the chain."

Something about this summary feels like it overclaims, but I am a historian of philosophy and lack the technical background to pinpoint where. Please assess the summary carefully: distinguish what Aumann actually proved from what the summary asserts, identify any conditions of the theorem that the summary omits or glosses, and tell me whether the practical conclusions drawn in the final two sentences are licensed by the theorem as stated — or whether they depend on additional premises that Aumann himself would not have endorsed. I am particularly interested in whether the phrase "same information" adequately captures the theorem's actual epistemic requirements.
