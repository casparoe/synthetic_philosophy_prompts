I'm a high school student, and I've been reading about Bertrand's paradox in my spare time. I think I grasp the surface of it: given a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle, the probability that a randomly drawn chord is longer than a side of the triangle comes out to 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether you randomize by picking two endpoints on the circumference, by picking a radius and a point along it, or by picking a random midpoint inside the circle. Each method seems like a reasonable way to interpret "a random chord," yet they give different answers.

What I want to understand better is what this paradox actually does to the principle of indifference. I've seen people draw very different morals from it. Some say it's a straightforward refutation: the principle of indifference tells you to assign equal probability to possibilities when you have no reason to favor one over another, but Bertrand's paradox shows that this instruction is empty because what counts as a "possibility" depends on how you parameterize the problem, and different parameterizations yield incompatible probability assignments. On this view, the principle is simply unusable.

Others seem to think the paradox only shows that the principle of indifference needs to be applied at the right level of description — that you need to identify the correct symmetry group or the correct set of "indifferent" alternatives before applying it, and that the paradox dissolves once you do that. But then I wonder: how do you identify the correct level of description without already knowing the answer you want? Doesn't that just push the arbitrariness back a step?

Could you walk me through the conceptual stakes here? I'm less interested in the technical details of the three probability calculations (I've worked through those) and more interested in what the paradox reveals about the principle of indifference as a piece of epistemology. Is the principle salvageable, and if so, at what cost?
