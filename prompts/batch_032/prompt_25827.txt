I'm an undergraduate taking a course called "Rationality and Strategic Choice" this semester, and a puzzle came up during our unit on the Prisoner's Dilemma that I cannot seem to let go of.

The standard analysis we were taught is that defection strictly dominates cooperation: no matter what the other player does, you get a higher payoff by defecting, so defecting is the uniquely rational choice. Full stop. But here is the scenario that bothers me. Suppose I am playing a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against another player, and I know the following: the other player is as rational as I am, has exactly the same information about the game that I have, faces the same payoff matrix, and — crucially — is going to reason about what to do in precisely the same way I reason about it. We are, in every relevant respect, identical decision-makers confronting identical circumstances with identical tools. Now, if I go through my deliberation and conclude that defecting is rational, I should expect them to go through the same deliberation and reach the same conclusion: defect. So I should expect mutual defection. If instead I conclude that cooperating is rational, I should expect them to cooperate too. Mutual cooperation gives me a higher payoff than mutual defection, so doesn't that mean I should cooperate? The reasoning seems to be that my choice effectively "chooses for" the pair of us, because whatever decision procedure I end up endorsing will be the one they endorse too—we are, in effect, a single mind instantated twice.

When I raised something like this in class, my professor said I was just rediscovering evidential decision theory and confusing causal and evidential reasoning. But that doesn't seem right to me. As I understand it, the evidential move in the Prisoner's Dilemma is: "my choosing to cooperate is evidence that the other player cooperated, perhaps because we share some psychological disposition or because the universe contains correlations between similar agents, so conditional on my cooperation the expected payoff is higher." But I am not making an evidential claim about what my action reveals about a pre-existing state of the world. I am making a claim about what follows from the fact that two identical decision procedures are being run in parallel on the same inputs and must therefore produce the same outputs. This feels like a different idea entirely—one about the logical or structural relationship between the two deliberation processes, not about the evidence my act provides.

Does this pattern of reasoning have an established name in decision theory or philosophy? I am less interested in the label itself than in whether anyone has taken this seriously as a distinct position, how precisely it relates to and differs from evidential decision theory, what the standard objections to it are, and whether it can be formulated rigorously enough to stand on its own. I would also appreciate a note on how confident you are in your characterisation and what you think the weakest or most contested point in your answer is.
