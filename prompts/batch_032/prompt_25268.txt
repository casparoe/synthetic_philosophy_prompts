I spent forty years in experimental physics before retiring, and I've recently turned my attention to epistemology. In a reading group on higher-order evidence, someone cut off a tangled discussion about epistemic akrasia by declaring, "You should always follow your evidence." The room nodded and we moved on. But the more I think about it, the less clear I am about what precise claim was being invoked — and whether it actually settles anything about the puzzles we were discussing.

I'd like you to disambiguate the slogan "You should always follow your evidence" into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. In particular, I want to know: (a) what counts as "your evidence" — does it include higher-order evidence about your own cognitive reliability, or only first-order evidence bearing directly on the proposition? (b) what does "follow" amount to — believing in proportion to, adopting the attitude supported by, or something else? (c) for each reading, does it permit or forbid epistemic akrasia (roughly: believing P while also believing that your total evidence does not support P)? And (d) which reading, if any, would actually do the work my discussion-group colleague needed it to do — namely, dissolving the apparent conflict between first-order and higher-order evidence in a single stroke?

Please lay out the readings as a clear menu rather than arguing for one, and for each, give your verdict on whether it's plausible, whether it resolves the akrasia puzzle, and whether it's probably what the person who says the slogan has in mind. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert epistemologist.
