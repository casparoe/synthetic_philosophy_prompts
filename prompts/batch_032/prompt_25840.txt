A colleague and I have been going back and forth about Lewis on convention, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to show his whole framework either proves too much or proves nothing. I can't tell whether I've found a genuine problem or just talked myself into a corner. Here's the argument:

(1) For Lewis, a regularity R is a convention in a population only if it is common knowledge that everyone (or almost everyone) conforms to R and prefers to conform given that others do.

(2) Common knowledge, as Lewis uses it, requires an infinite hierarchy of epistemic states: I know that you know that I know that you know…, with no top floor.

(3) Finite creatures in ordinary social settings never actually satisfy this requirement. At best we have mutual belief up to some modest level — perhaps two or three iterations — supported by rough-and-ready evidence about what others expect.

(4) So, by Lewis's own definition, there are no conventions in any actual human population. Not language, not driving practices, not dress codes — none of them clear the bar.

(5) That conclusion is absurd. We plainly do have conventions.

(6) The natural fix is to relax the requirement: replace strict common knowledge with something weaker, like "sufficiently high-order mutual belief" or "mutual belief simpliciter."

(7) But once we relax the requirement, we face a sorites problem. If three levels of mutual belief aren't enough to make a regularity a convention, neither are four; and if four aren't enough, neither are five; and so on. No finite number of iterations seems to mark the threshold between mere regularities and genuine conventions.

(8) So either we draw a sharp line at some arbitrary number of iterations (which looks ad hoc and psychologically unreal), or we accept that "convention" has no sharp boundaries at all.

(9) If "convention" has no sharp boundaries, then Lewis's claim that conventions are regularities sustained by common knowledge is false — they are sustained by something inherently vague, and the common-knowledge condition was doing the wrong kind of work all along.

(10) Therefore, Lewis's analysis fails: it either entails that there are no conventions (by insisting on full common knowledge), or it collapses into vagueness the moment we try to make it empirically applicable (by relaxing the requirement).

I feel the pull of each step, but I can't accept the conclusion. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid and the real work is choosing which premise to give up, I'd especially like to know which premise is least damaging to the broader Lewisian picture.
