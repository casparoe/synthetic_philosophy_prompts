I'm writing an essay on the problem of dirty hands in politics and I want to use Macbeth as a central case study. The problem, as developed in the philosophical literature, is that political life sometimes requires doing what would be morally wrong for a private person — lying, breaking promises, ordering violence — for the sake of the common good, and that the politician who does so rightly feels guilt or remorse even while being justified. My question is whether Macbeth actually fits this framework or whether it complicates it in ways the philosophical literature hasn't fully absorbed.

On one reading, Macbeth is simply not a case of dirty hands at all: he kills Duncan out of ambition, not for the common good, and the play traces his descent from usurper to tyrant. On another reading, the play dramatizes something the philosophical literature underplays — the psychological dynamics of dirtying one's hands, the way one violation makes the next easier, the way guilt can paralyze or unhinge a person, and the difference between feeling guilt and being morally clean. Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking scene and Macbeth's speech about being so far in blood that turning back is as hard as going forward seem especially relevant here.

I'd like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophical debate seriously. Where does Macbeth illuminate the dirty hands problem, and where does it resist being read that way? Does the play suggest that the very idea of a necessary dirty-handed act is a rationalization — that once you start, you can't stop, and the distinction between the responsible politician and the tyrant collapses? Or does it leave room for the view that some dirty-handed acts really are necessary and that the guilt is the mark of moral seriousness rather than of having gone too far? I'm particularly interested in whether the play's treatment of guilt and the impossibility of washing one's hands clean is meant to apply to all dirty-handed politics or only to unjustified violence. And what should we make of the fact that the play's most famous image of dirty hands belongs to Lady Macbeth, who was never a political office-holder, rather than to Macbeth himself?
