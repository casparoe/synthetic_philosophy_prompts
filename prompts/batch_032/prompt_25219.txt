I've been teaching a graduate seminar on social choice theory, and we spent two full sessions on Sen's liberal paradox. For those unfamiliar: Sen showed that if a social decision function satisfies unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and a condition he called minimal liberalism — where at least two individuals each have a recognized personal sphere over which their preference between a pair of alternatives is socially decisive — the result can be a cyclic social preference, and thus no best element. The canonical illustration is the prude and the lewd with *Lady Chatterley's Lover*: each wants to determine whether they themselves read the book, but each also has preferences about the other's reading, and the combination of individual decisiveness over the personal choices plus the Pareto principle yields a cycle.

I've been struck by how differently my students and colleagues interpret what this result actually shows. Some treat it as a deep discovery: a genuine, inescapable tension between two values — individual liberty and respect for unanimous preference — that any democratic theory must confront. Others dismiss it as an artifact of the social choice framework: it exploits the fact that individuals hold nosy preferences about others' private spheres, and once you restrict the domain to exclude those, or reformulate rights in a game-form rather than a social-choice framework, the paradox dissolves.

I'd like you to give me your actual credence — a number between 0 and 1 — that Sen's liberal paradox reveals a genuine normative conflict between individual liberty and the Pareto principle, one that survives reasonable refinements of the framework and is not merely an artifact of how rights are formalized. But I don't just want the number. I want you to decompose the question into the sub-questions your credence depends on, assign a rough probability to each, and show how they combine. In particular, I'd like to see terms for at least the following: (a) whether the paradox depends essentially on the presence of nosy preferences that a well-designed rights framework should screen out, and whether screening them out is independently motivated or ad hoc; (b) whether game-form formulations of rights genuinely dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it into questions about how the game form itself is justified; (c) whether the tension reappears in richer frameworks — e.g., with multiple issues, sequential choice, or incomplete information — even after domain restrictions are imposed; and (d) whether the normative force of the Pareto principle is itself diminished once we recognize that unanimous preferences can be based on objectionable reasons (e.g., the prude's desire to censor the lewd), and if so, whether that weakening matters for the paradox's significance. You may add further terms if you think the decomposition demands them. Then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to — where a small shift in your probability on that sub-question would produce the largest shift in your overall number — and what evidence or argument would most likely shift that term.
