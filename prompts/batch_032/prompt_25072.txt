I've been reading about the Challenger disaster for a course on risk and decision-making, and I keep coming back to the moment the night before the launch when the Thiokol engineers were trying to convince NASA not to launch in the cold. The standard story is that the engineers knew the O-rings were dangerous at low temperatures but couldn't "prove" it quantitatively, and NASA managers, operating with a kind of burden-of-proof standard that required showing it was unsafe rather than showing it was safe, pushed through the launch.

I'm taking a philosophy of probability seminar at the same time, and we just covered imprecise probabilities — the idea that in many cases your credal state isn't best represented by a single probability distribution but by a set of them, and that this isn't just a failure to be properly Bayesian but can be the rational response to sparse or ambiguous evidence. And it struck me that the Challenger decision is almost a perfect case study for this. The engineers had data on O-ring erosion vs. temperature, but only a handfull of cold-weather launches, so the data underdetermined any single probability of failure. They seemed to be operating with something like an imprecise credence — they knew the risk was elevated but couldn't pin down by how much.

I want you to analyze the Challenger launch decision through the lens of imprecise probabilities. Specifically: (1) Map the decision as it actually happened onto an imprecise-probability framework — who had what credal set, what the decision rule was, and where the breakdown happened. (2) Would a formal imprecise-probability decision rule (say, maximality or E-admissibility or Γ-maximin) have recommended a different decision than what NASA made? (3) What does the episode reveal about the practical viability of imprecise probabilities — does it show they're essential for capturing real-world uncertainty, or does it expose a problem with them (like, that they make it too easy to avoid committing to a course of action, or that they don't give determinate enough recommendations when you need to actually decide)? I'm less interested in the engineering details and more in whether the conceptual machinery of imprecise probabilities actually helps here or just redescribes the problem in fancier language.
