I host a philosophy podcast, and I've just scheduled an episode with a philosopher who defends the view that Moore's paradox is best explained by the knowledge norm of assertion — the principle that one should assert that p only if one knows that p. I spent most of last night arguing about this with a friend, and the conversation exposed several pressure points I want to press the guest on rather than letting the interview become a restatement of the view.

My friend's central challenge was that the knowledge norm is overkill for explaining Moore's paradox. The omissive case — "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" — is already handled by a simple sincerity norm (assert only what you believe): asserting the first conjunct requires believing it's raining, while the second conjunct denies that belief, yielding a pragmatic contradiction with no need to invoke knowledge. The guest's reply, I expect, will be that the sincerity norm cannot handle the commissive version — "It's not raining, but I believe it is" — without extra machinery, whereas the knowledge norm handles both uniformly: if you know it's not raining, you don't believe it is, so the second conjunct is false; if you don't know it's not raining, the first conjunct is improperly asserted. But my friend pushed back that the knowledge norm then overgenerates: it predicts that asserting "my lottery ticket will lose" is improper, since you don't know it will lose, and while some philosophers accept that result, ordinary speakers plainly do not. I want the interview to force the guest to confront this and related objections head-on.

Beyond that, I want questions that probe whether the knowledge norm can explain why Moore-paradoxical thoughts — not just assertions — are absurd, whether the explanation extends naturally to the third-person case ("It's raining, but she doesn't believe it"), which is not paradoxical, and what the guest would count as evidence against their own view.
