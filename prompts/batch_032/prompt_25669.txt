I'm writing a paper on theories of vagueness for a graduate seminar in philosophy of language, and I've been going back and forth between epistemicism and supervaluationism for weeks. Every time I think I've settled on one, a new objection pulls me back. I've read Williamson's *Vagueness* and Fine's "Vagueness, Truth and Logic," and I can articulate both views and their standard objections, but I'm stuck on the question of which one is actually more likely to be right — not just which one I find more elegant or which one has the more devastating objections to its rival, but which one I should assign higher credence to given everything I know.

I'd like you to give me your actual credence that epistemicism is the correct theory of vagueness — where "epistemicism" means the view that vagueness is a matter of irremediable ignorance about precise boundaries, that classical logic and bivalence hold without qualification, and that there is a single correct extension for every vague predicate, just one we cannot know. By contrast, the supervaluationist alternative holds that vagueness is semantic indecision: there are multiple admissible ways of making a vague predicate precise, and a sentence is true (false) if it comes out true (false) on all admissible precisifications, and neither otherwise. I'm not asking you to compare epistemicism to every theory on the market — just to supervaluationism and its close relatives, treating them as the main rival.

Decompose the question into the sub-questions your credence most depends on. For each sub-question, give a rough probability and a one- or two-sentence justification. Then show how they combine to yield your overall credence. Finally, tell me which sub-question your overall number is most sensitive to — the one where, if you learned you were wrong, your credence would shift the most — and what kind of evidence or argument would move it.

To get you started, here are some candidate sub-questions, though you should modify, drop, or add to these as your own reasoning requires: (1) Is bivalence — the principle that every declarative sentence is either true or false — correct for natural language, or does vagueness provide a genuine counterexample? (2) If there are multiple admissible precisifications, can the metasemantic facts really favor "many" over "exactly one that we cannot locate," or does the epistemicist have the simpler story about what determines meaning? (3) Does the epistemicist's appeal to unknowably sharp cutoffs impose an unacceptable cost on our picture of linguistic competence — that is, does it make speakers systematically ignorant of the extensions of their own words in a way that undermines the claim that they mean anything determinate by them? (4) Can supervaluationism give an adequate account of higher-order vagueness without collapsing into epistemicism or requiring an implausible hierarchy of meta-languages? (5) Setting aside internal theoretical virtues, which view better handles the sorites paradox — not just blocks it, but explains why it is compelling in the first place?
