I serve on my university's university-wide promotions committee, the body that reviews tenure and promotion cases after they have cleared departmental and college-level review. We are currently deadlocked on a case from the business school, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo we will vote on when we reconvene next Tuesday. The candidate's quantitative record is, on its face, outstanding by every metric our guidelines enumerate: citation counts well above the field median, an h-index in the top decile, a long list of publications in journals with high impact factors, and grant funding comfortably exceeding the threshold our handbook labels "research excellence." The difficulty is that the college-level committee appended a sharply worded dissenting minority report arguing that these numbers are artifacts of systematic gaming rather than evidence of genuine scholarly contribution. The report documents a pattern of salami-slicing findings across minimally differentiated papers, self-citation rates above forty percent, a practice of placing articles in special issues guest-edited by close collaborators where peer review was, in the dissenters' description, perfunctory at best, and a grant record built on repackaging the same project for multiple funding bodies with only cosmetic alterations.

The candidate's defenders on our committee argue that the metrics are the metrics, that the university cannot set aside its own published standards in favor of ad hoc qualitative judgments without inviting charges of arbitrariness, favoritism, and bias, and that the dissenting report is best understood as sour grapes from departmental rivals who simply have weaker numbers and resent being outperformed. They further note that none of the practices identified in the dissenting report violate any explicit rule: self-citation is not capped, salami-slicing is not defined in our guidelines, and serving on editorial boards with one's collaborators is common across disciplines. The candidate's critics argue that ratifying a promotion when there is strong evidence the metrics have been hollowed out would make a mockery of the entire process and signal to every junior faculty member that the rational strategy is to abandon substantive research in favor of metric optimization, thereby degrading the university's intellectual culture over time. They contend that a committee charged with exercising judgment cannot outsource that judgment to numbers while knowing the numbers have been manipulated, even if the manipulation stays within the letter of the rules.

I need a memo that recommends a clear decision — approve or deny the promotion — and that engages seriously with the strongest arguments on the losing side, so that even those who vote the other way can recognize that their position was fairly considered and understood. Please write the memo in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a formal memorandum.
