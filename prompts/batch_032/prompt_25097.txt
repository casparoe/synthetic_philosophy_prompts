At a discussion group I've been attending since retiring from the lab, two regulars got into a sharp exchange about the Condorcet jury theorem and I've been turning it over ever since. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) David opened by saying the theorem shows that democratic decision-making has a solid epistemic foundation: if each voter has a better-than-even chance of being right on a binary question and they vote independently, then the probability that a majority gets it right converges to certainty as the group grows. He called this a genuine reason to trust democratic outcomes over, say, a single expert.

(2) Miriam replied that the independence condition makes the whole thing irrelevant to actual politics. Voters read the same outlets, watch the same debates, and talk to each other. Their errors aren't independent — they're correlated. And with correlated errors, she said, adding more voters doesn't add independent signals; it just amplifies whatever shared biases the electorate already has.

(3) David answered that this is too quick. The theorem only requires conditional independence given the evidence. If voters share information because they're all looking at the same facts, that correlation is fine — it's driven by a common signal, not by copying each other's mistakes. He said the real question is whether, conditional on the evidence available, each voter's judgment is an independent draw from a distribution that's better than chance.

(4) Miriam came back with two points. First, voters share cognitive architecture — confirmation bias, framing effects, and so on — that isn't driven by evidence at all, so errors remain correlated even conditional on the facts. Second, on many real political questions, voters aren't better than chance in the first place; they're systematically misinformed, and the theorem's p > 0.5 premise fails outright. She said David was defending a theorem that describes a voting body that has never existed.

I'd like you to go through this exchange step by step and say who has the better of each move, and then give an overall assessment of who is more right. Please take a definite position rather than splitting the difference — I want a committed verdict, with your reasoning at each stage.
