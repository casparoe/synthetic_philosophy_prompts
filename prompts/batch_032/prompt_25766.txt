At a recent faculty meeting, two of my colleagues got into a protracted argument about a hiring decision that had just been made, and I have been turning it over ever since. The situation was this. Our department's hiring committee has three members, and they were evaluating a candidate for a tenure-track position. The department had agreed in advance on two criteria: the candidate should be hired if and only if (a) their research program is excellent, and (b) they can cover our undergraduate teaching needs. The three committee members voted as follows:

- Alvarez: research excellent (yes), teaching fit good (yes) → would vote to hire.
- Berg: research excellent (yes), teaching fit poor (no) → would vote not to hire.
- Chen: research mediocre (no), teaching fit good (yes) → would vote not to hire.

The committee then faced a procedural question: should they vote on each criterion separately and let the conjunction determine the outcome, or should they vote directly on the overall hiring decision? If they vote on criteria separately, both criteria pass 2–1, the conjunction is satisfied, and the candidate is hired. If they vote on the overall decision directly, the vote is 2–1 against hiring.

My colleague David argued that voting on the criteria separately is the only coherent approach. The department set out two necessary and jointly sufficient conditions; the committee's job is to determine whether those conditions are met. If a majority finds each condition satisfied, then by the department's own standard, the candidate should be hired. To do otherwise — to vote down a candidate who satisfies both criteria according to majority judgment — is to contradict the very standard the department adopted.

My colleague Esther argued that this outcome is perverse. A majority of the committee does not want to hire this candidate. Two out of three members would vote no on the final decision. If a procedure generates an outcome that a majority opposes, you have undermined the point of having a vote at all. The criteria are tools for reaching a judgment, not axioms from which a hiring decision must be mechanically deduced against the will of the majority.

David replied that Esther's position amounts to letting the majority be inconsistent: the majority says the research is excellent and the teaching fit is good, yet somehow the candidate should not be hired. If the department's own criteria are correct, that combination makes no sense. Esther countered that there is no inconsistency because "the majority" is not a single person — it is different majorities on different questions, and the only thing that should ultimately matter is what a majority of the committee wants to do.

David came back with this: suppose the committee had instead voted on each criterion and then, seeing that both passed, someone proposed ignoring that result and voting on the conclusion directly. Wouldn't that look like an attempt to overturn a decision one dislikes by switching the procedure after the fact? Esther answered that this gets the order backwards — the question is which procedure is correct in the first place, not whether you can change it midstream, and her point is precisely that conclusion-based voting is the correct procedure from the start.

They went back and forth for some time without resolution. I have been asked to weigh in, but I find both arguments compelling in different ways. Please adjudicate this dispute: who is right about which specific point, and is there a principled resolution, or does this reveal a genuine tension in how groups should make decisions?
