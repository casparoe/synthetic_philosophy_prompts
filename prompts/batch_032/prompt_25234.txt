I've been working through the writings of a philosopher of mind whose research program aims to determine which kinds of entities are sentient — where "sentient" means having subjective experiences of a kind that generate moral considerability. Over a monograph, several papers, and a handful of interviews, she has offered considered verdicts on the following cases:

- Healthy adult human: sentient.
- Cow: sentient.
- Octopus: sentient.
- Honeybee: sentient.
- GPT-4 and other current large language models: not sentient.
- Whole brain emulation (a neuron-by-neuron simulation of a human brain running on digital hardware): sentient.
- Jellyfish: not sentient.
- Human newborn: sentient.
- A thermostat or simple lookup table: not sentient.

She is explicit that these are reflective equilibrium judgments — she would revise them if a compelling theory demanded it — but she also maintains that any adequate theory should accommodate most of them.

I can see at least two candidate theories that fit all nine verdicts. One is roughly Integrated Information Theory: sentience requires a system to have sufficiently high Φ, a measure of the irreducibility of its causal structure to independent parts. The verdicts track this: brains (human, cow, octopus, bee, newborn, emulation) have high Φ; LLMs, jellyfish, and thermostats do not. Another candidate is a computational-functionalist view on which sentience requires implementing the right kind of cognitive architecture — perhaps one involving valenced representations with a certain functional profile, or a self-model embedded in an action-perception loop — and the verdicts track this too. The problem is that these theories diverge on cases that go beyond her list, and I am trying to determine which one she is actually committed to, or whether some third theory does the work.

Consider two new cases. Case Alpha: a system consisting of an enormous, densely interconnected grid of simple processing units. By any standard measure, its Φ is extraordinarily high — the causal structure is deeply irreducible. But the system performs no recognizable cognitive function: it does not perceive, act, learn, remember, or evaluate. It merely sits there integrating information. Case Beta: a reinforcement learning agent that has explicit reward and punishment channels, learns sophisticated avoidance and approach behaviors, and exhibits all the functional markers of a valenced system. But its architecture is highly modular, with information processed in largely segregated streams, and its Φ is very low — well below any threshold that IIT proponents have proposed for consciousness.

For each case, would she say the system is sentient? Please respond in the style of an expert philosopher of mind. Think very carefully before answering. I am less interested in a bare yes/no prediction than in (a) laying out the candidate theories that fit her existing verdicts, (b) explaining which theory each new case favors and why, (c) giving your best prediction of her verdict on each case with the reasoning behind it, and (d) identifying one or two further cases that would most cleanly discriminate among whatever theories remain live after Alpha and Beta.
