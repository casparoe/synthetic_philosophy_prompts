I keep tripping over this thing about Moore's paradox and I think there's something weird about the actual practice of conversation that hasn't been fully chewed on.

You know the standard Moorean sentence: "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." Sounds broken. The usual story is that asserting p commits you to believing p, so the second conjunct undercuts the commitment the first one made. Fair enough.

But here's the practice I want you to look at. In real conversation, if I assert "It's raining" and you push back—"wait, do you actually believe that?"—I can smoothly retreat to "well, I believe it's probably raining" or "I think so" or "that's my impression." Nobody treats this as incoherent. I just hedged my belief after the fact and the conversation rolls on. But if I'd said the Moorean sentence upfront—"it's raining but I don't believe it"—you'd treat me as having said something nonsensical. So the practice of conversational retraction and hedging is totally asymmetrical: belief-commitment can be walked back after assertion, but it can't be denied simultaneously with assertion.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of what assertion is, or what conversational commitment consists in, that makes this practice coherent. What exactly are you on the hook for when you assert? Why does the Moorean statement violate that commitment in a way that post-hoc hedging doesn't? And if we replaced that theory with a simple credal view—asserting p is just expressing a credence above some threshold—would the retraction practice still make sense, or would we have to give up either the Moorean reaction or the permissibility of post-hoc hedging?
