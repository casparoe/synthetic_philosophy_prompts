I'm trying to get my head around the relationship between Leibniz's mill argument and Searle's Chinese Room. Here's the Leibniz passage (Monadology §17, 1714):

"One is obliged to admit that perception and what depends upon it is inexplicable on mechanical principles, that is, by figures and motions. In imagining that there is a machine whose construction would enable it to think, to sense, and to have perception, one could conceive it enlarged while retaining the same proportions, so that one could enter into it, just like into a windmill. Supposing this, one should, when visiting within it, find only parts pushing one another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine, that one must look for perception."

People often say this is basically the Chinese Room avant la lettre, but when I read them side by side they seem to be doing different things. Like, Searle's point is about syntax vs. semantics and the systems reply, while Leibniz seems to be making a point about unity — that you need a simple substance to have perception, not just an aggregate of parts. Are these actually the same argument in different clothes, or is the family resemblance misleading? And if they are different, does the Chinese Room actually inherit any problems that the mill argument already had? I'd especially like to know whether the standard replies to one map onto the other in any clean way.
