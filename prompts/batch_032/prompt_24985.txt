Question:

Explain Bradley's regress as it applies to the unity of the proposition, and critically evaluate whether Frege's distinction between saturated objects and unsaturated concepts provides a satisfactory response.

Student Answer:

Bradley's regress, in its most general form, is an argument against the reality of relations. Bradley argued that if we try to understand how a relation R relates two terms a and b, we find ourselves in difficulty: R must stand in some relation to a, and in some relation to b. But then those new relations must themselves stand in relations to their terms, and so on indefinitely. The regress is meant to show that relations cannot be independent entities that tie terms together from the outside.

When applied to the unity of the proposition, the regress takes a specific form. Consider the proposition that Socrates is wise. If we analyze this proposition as consisting of three constituents — the object Socrates, the property wisdom, and the relation of instantiation — we face the question: what makes these three items cohere into a single proposition rather than remaining a mere list? If we answer that instantiation relates Socrates to wisdom, then we must ask what relates instantiation to Socrates, and what relates instantiation to wisdom. Positing further relations only pushes the problem back, generating a vicious infinite regress. The conclusion seems to be that no analysis of the proposition as a complex of independently specifiable constituents can account for its unity.

Frege's response turns on his fundamental distinction between saturated and unsaturated entities. Objects, for Frege, are saturated: they are complete in themselves. Concepts, by contrast, are unsaturated: they contain argument places that must be filled by objects to yield a complete thought. The concept expressed by "is wise" is unsaturated; it takes an object as argument and, when saturated, yields the proposition that that object is wise. On this picture, no additional relation of instantiation is required to unify the constituents, because the concept's unsaturatedness is precisely its capacity to combine with objects. The regress never gets started because we are not treating the predicative component as another object-like constituent that needs to be tied to the subject. Frege's solution shows that Bradley's regress rests on a category mistake: treating the copula as an entity of the same logical type as the terms it connects. Once we recognize that the predicative element is fundamentally different in kind — it is a function, not an object — the demand for a connecting relation dissolves.

This solution was adopted and extended by Russell in his landmark paper "On Denoting" (1905), where Russell endorses the Fregean analysis of propositional unity as part of his broader account of logical form. The only remaining difficulty for the Fregean picture is the problem of false propositions — how a proposition can be unified and meaningful when what it represents does not obtain — but this is a separate issue from Bradley's regress and does not affect Frege's resolution of it.
