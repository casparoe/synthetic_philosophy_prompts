In the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics, Aristotle draws a sharp distinction between activities that are ends in themselves—above all, theoretical contemplation (theōria)—and activities pursued merely as means, among which he counts most forms of manual labor and commerce. For Aristotle, a life organized around such instrumental activities is not a fully flourishing human life; the highest human good, eudaimonia, requires leisure (scholē) devoted to intrinsically worthwhile activity, and he famously holds that citizens in the best polis should be freed from the necessity of working for a living.

Aristotle could not, of course, have anticipated a world in which advanced artificial intelligence and automation render the vast majority of human labor economically unnecessary. Suppose we imagine such a world: one in which machines and AI systems produce all essential goods and services, and human beings are no longer required to work in order to secure their material subsistence. The question is how Aristotle would evaluate this state of affairs.

Please offer a detailed and textually grounded reconstruction of what Aristotle's position would most likely be. In particular, address the following tensions:

1. On the one hand, Aristotle valorizes leisure and disparages lives consumed by necessary labor. Universal automation might seem to deliver precisely the condition—universal scholē—that he treats as a precondition for the good life. On the other hand, Aristotle also holds that virtue is developed through habitual action and that a life of mere passive consumption or amusement is not eudaimonic but slavish. Would Aristotle regard a post-work world as an opportunity for universal human flourishing, or would he worry that most people, lacking the character formation that disciplined activity provides, would squander their leisure on base pleasures?

2. Aristotle's political philosophy ties the exercise of practical wisdom (phronēsis) and civic virtue to active participation in the life of the polis, including deliberation about means and ends. If automation eliminates not only manual labor but also many forms of managerial and administrative work, what becomes of the domain in which practical wisdom is exercised? Would Aristotle see this as an expansion of the space for contemplation, or as a dangerous narrowing of the sphere in which distinctively human rational capacities are developed and expressed?

3. Aristotle's conception of human nature is teleological: the ergon (function) of a human being is rational activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. If work—broadly construed as purposeful, effortful activity directed at some end—has historically been a central arena for the exercise of practical reason, what does Aristotle's framework imply about a future in which such activity is no longer necessary? Is there a risk that human beings would lose an essential occasion for realizing their nature, or does Aristotle's framework contain resources for reconceiving human purpose beyond the category of work?

Where possible, ground your analysis in specific passages or doctrines from the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics, and be explicit about which aspects of Aristotle's thought support each side of these tensions.
