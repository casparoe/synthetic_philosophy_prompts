Write a sonnet about the ethical implications of panpsychism, specifically the worry that if consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous, then suffering may be woven into the fabric of physics itself. The octave should present the panpsychist picture with some sympathy: the intuition that matter is not dead, that experience does not arise mysteriously from non-experiencing parts, that the fundamental constituents of reality may already possess some form of inner life — however dim, however unlike our own. The volta should turn sharply from metaphysical appeal to ethical vertigo. If every physical interaction involves some measure of sentience, then the universe is not merely indifferent but saturated with suffering — in the collisions of particles, in the dissolution of structures, in the relentless march of entropy. The sestet should hold this tension open rather than resolve it, acknowledging both the theoretical elegance of taking consciousness as fundamental and the moral horror of a cosmos in which suffering is not an accident of biology but a feature of the basic laws. The closing couplet should leave the reader with the uneasy thought that the price of solving the hard problem of consciousness might be discovering that the universe is not a dead machine but a live wound. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with panpsychism and its ethical ramifications would recognize the debate, but it should work as a poem first.
