I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics — you know, the one where the wavefunction never collapses and the universe just keeps branching into parallel realities every time a measurement happens. I think I mostly get the basic picture, but there's this one thing that keeps tripping me up and I'm not sure if it's a known problem with a name or if I'm just missing something obvious.

Here's what's bugging me. In many-worlds, every possible outcome of a quantum event actually happens in some branch. So if you do a spin measurement and the wavefunction says the amplitude for "up" is √0.7 and for "down" is √0.3, well, both outcomes happen — there's a branch where the experimenter sees up and a branch where she sees down. So far so good. But then what do the numbers 0.7 and 0.3 even mean? In ordinary quantum mechanics they're probabilities — you've got a 70% chance of seeing up. But in many-worlds there's no "chance" because both things definitely happen. You can't say "70% of the time you'll see up" because there's no "time" across branches — every version of you sees exactly one outcome and thinks it was the only one.

I've seen people try to get around this in a few ways. One move is to say that there are more branches with the up outcome than the down outcome, so if you count branches you get the right ratio. But that doesn't seem to work because amplitudes aren't whole numbers — you'd need some rule for how many branches each amplitude corresponds to, and that rule would just be the probability rule you're trying to derive. Another move is to say that you should bet as if the probabilities are given by the squared amplitudes, because that's what a rational agent would do when facing branching. But that feels circular to me — how do you define "rational" here without already knowing what the probabilities are supposed to be? And then there's a third approach I've seen hints of, something about self-locating uncertainty: before the measurement you don't know which branch you'll end up in, so you can assign probabilities to being in each branch based on... something. But again, on what grounds do you assign one probability rather than another?

I guess what I'm asking is: does this specific problem have a name in the philosophy of physics literature? And are the different attempted solutions I'm gesturing at — branch counting, decision-theoretic, self-locating uncertainty — recognized as distinct families of responses with their own labels? I'm less interested in whether anyone thinks the problem is "solved" and more in understanding what exactly the problem is, what would count as a solution, and where the line is between deriving probability and quietly assuming it.
