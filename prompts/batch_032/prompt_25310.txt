I've been reading this pop-science book on famous paradoxes — the kind you pick up at an airport — and the chapter on confirmation theory has been bugging me ever since. The author summarizes Hempel's raven paradox like this:

"Hempel pointed out that the hypothesis 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, 'All non-black things are non-ravens.' Now, if you think a generalization gets confirmed by observing positive instances of it, then seeing a black raven confirms the raven hypothesis. But by the exact same principle, seeing a non-black thing that isn't a raven — a green apple, say — confirms the contrapositive. And since the two statements are logically equivalent, anything that confirms one confirms the other. So every time you glance at a yellow pencil or a white sneaker, you're accumulating evidence that all ravens are black. The author calls this result 'plainly absurd' and says it shows that confirmation cannot be a purely formal, logical relation: you have to bring in background knowledge about the relative sizes of categories, and once you do, the paradox evaporates because a green apple doesn't actually budge the probability that all ravens are black given how many non-black things there are in the universe."
