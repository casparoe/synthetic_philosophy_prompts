For the past several months I have been working through Hilary Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" and the subsequent literature on semantic externalism — the Twin Earth thought experiment, the thesis that meanings "just ain't in the head," the claim that reference is partly fixed by the causal history of a term and the actual nature of the stuff in one's environment. My background is in geology, not philosophy, and I retired three years ago, so I am still getting my bearings in these debates. But something has been nagging at me, and I would like a careful reading that brings a work of fiction to bear on the question.

It seems to me that Jorge Luis Borges anticipated something very like the externalist thesis in his 1939 story "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote." As you will recall, the story takes the form of a mock-scholarly essay about a fictional twentieth-century French symbolist who sets out to compose — not transcribe, not copy, but genuinely compose — several chapters of Cervantes' *Don Quixote*. Menard succeeds in producing a text verbally identical to Cervantes', and Borges then compares the two, arguing that the identical sentences carry entirely different meanings because they issue from different authors embedded in different historical circumstances. A sentence about the relation between history and truth reads one way when written by a seventeenth-century Spaniard who took such commonplaces at face value, and quite another when written by a contemporary of William James who has deliberately adopted an archaic style. The words are the same; what they say is not.

I would like you to read "Pierre Menard" through the lens of Putnam's semantic externalism and the Twin Earth argument. On its face, the story seems to dramatize the claim that meaning is not exhausted by what is in the speaker's head — that identical verbal productions can differ in meaning because of differences in the external context of production. But I suspect the fit is not perfect, and I want to know where it strains. For instance: Putnam's externalism is largely a thesis about natural-kind terms and indexicals, whereas Borges is concerned with whole literary texts and the kinds of meaning that depend on authorial intention and historical position. Does Borges' argument rest on a different notion of "meaning" than Putnam's? Does the story, if taken seriously as a thought experiment, push externalism into territory it was not designed to cover — and if so, does that reveal a limitation of the philosophical view or an unnoticed extension of it? And finally: if we treat Menard's Quixote and Cervantes' Quixote as a kind of Twin Earth case, what does the story show that the philosophical thought experiment misses? I am less interested in whether Borges "got there first" than in whether the literary version of the idea captures something the philosophical version loses, and whether an honest critic has to admit that the two projects are ultimately doing quite different things.
