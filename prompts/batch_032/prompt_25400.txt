I've become convinced that program equilibrium offers the most promising resolution to the apparent conflict between individual rationality and mutual benefit in one-shot social dilemmas. The core idea, as I understand it, is this: in the standard one-shot prisoner's dilemma, mutual defection is the only Nash equilibrium because each player treats the other's action as causally fixed when deciding what to do. But if we allow players to choose decision procedures — programs — that can make their actions depend on what the other player's program would output under various conditions, then mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium. A program that says, roughly, "I cooperate if and only if you would cooperate conditional on my cooperating" can meet a relevantly similar program and both cooperate. Neither has an incentive to deviate unilaterally, because any deviation to a program that defects unconditionally would be detected and met with defection by the partner program. On this view, the problem with the one-shot prisoner's dilemma was never fundamentally about rationality or self-interest; it was about an impoverished model of what agents are permitted to condition their choices on. Once agents can condition on each other's decision procedures rather than merely on actions, cooperation is restored without appealing to evidential decision theory, indefinite repetition, reputation, or external enforcement.

I want to know what would count against this view, treated as a normative theory of rational choice in idealized settings. I already grant the practical objections about source-code access and transparency; I am interested in whether the view works on its own terms. Specifically:

- Does the view have a well-defined answer to the grain problem — the question of what counts as "the same program" or "a relevantly similar program" — or does it achieve cooperation only by equivocating between different levels of description of what an agent decides?
- Does the view implicitly rely on the very evidential decision theory it claims to avoid, once we ask what it means for one program to "condition on" what another program would output, and what the relevant counterfactuals are?
- Are there formal results showing that program equilibrium collapses into standard causal decision theory under plausible constraints, or that it generates multiple incompatible equilibria with no principled selection criterion?
- How does the view handle self-reference and the threat of paradox when programs condition on their own outputs or on programs that condition on them in turn? Is there a general account of which fixed points are admissible and why?
- Does the view make predictions that distinguish it from rival approaches — for instance, about which games admit cooperative equilibria and which do not — or does it retrospectively rationalize cooperation wherever we find it intuitive?

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what a committed defender of program equilibrium would concede as a genuine problem from what they would dismiss as a misunderstanding or as a problem for everyone. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been (or can be) qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — and if so, whether that is a problem for a normative theory of rationality, or whether we should expect such theories to be insulated from empirical defeat in ways that empirical theories are not.
