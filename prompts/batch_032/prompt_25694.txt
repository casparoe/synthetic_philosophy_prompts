Okay so this has become weirdly personal for me recently. My partner and I had this huge fight a couple months ago, and in the middle of it I realized I was absolutely certain I was right about what happened, but I also knew — at the very same time — that I was furious, sleep-deprived, and that my memory for arguments is notoriously terrible. I had this clear higher-order belief that my belief-forming processes were compromised, and yet my first-order confidence didn't budge. I was a walking case of epistemic akrasia. And it felt, honestly, irrational. Like I was doing something wrong epistemically, not just interpersonally.

So I went down a rabbit hole on this and I've come to hold the view that epistemic akrasia is always irrational. Specifically: it's irrational to have a credence of, say, 0.9 in P while simultaneously believing that, given your evidence and your current cognitive state, a credence of 0.9 in P isn't the one your evidence supports. Your first-order attitudes have to be sensitive to your higher-order evidence about your own reliability. The levels can't float free.

Not just the standard counterexamples that get trotted out in intro epistemology — I've already read about the drugged reasoner who still gets the math right. I want the full map of defeaters. What empirical results should worry me? What formal results? What edge cases that haven't been discussed much? Which objections do defenders of the no-akrasia view themselves concede have force, and which do they dismiss as question-begging? I want the defeaters ranked from "this might actually sink the view" down to "this is annoying but survivable."

And I want an honest answer to this: has the no-akrasia position been qualified and hedged to the point where nothing could count against it? And if it has — is that actually a problem for a normative epistemic claim, or is that just what normative claims are like?
