I'm teaching a grad seminar on epistemic game theory this semester, and I keep coming back to the Silicon Valley Bank run in March 2023 as a case study. I'd like you to analyze it through the lens of common knowledge of rationality — the idea that for a Nash equilibrium to hold, it's not enough that everyone is rational; everyone has to know that everyone is rational, and know that everyone knows, and so on.

Walk me through the episode as a breakdown in this iterated structure. At what point did depositors stop believing that other depositors were rational? Was there a moment where each depositor, individually, thought "I'm pulling my money because others are panicing, not because the bank is insolvent" — and if so, what does that do to the common knowledge of rationality? I want you to map the key moments — the CEO's letter on March 8th, the VC partners telling portfolio companies to pull funds, the cascading tweets — onto the formal structure of something like the electronic mail game or a global game. Be precise about who the players are, what the payoffs are, and what counts as the signal.

Then tell me where the model strains. The SVB run wasn't anonymous depositors at teller windows; it was a densely networked group of VCs and founders who all knew each other. Does that dense network make common knowledge easier to sustain or easier to shatter? And what does the episode tell us about the theory — is it vindicated by how cleanly the run unfolded once the signal went out, or does the role of social media and coordinated messaging break the standard framework in ways we should worry about?
