I've been working through Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* (1897) for a reading group, and I've been struck by how systematically he develops his criteria: genuine art must transmit a sincere feeling the artist has actually lived through, it must be intelligible to the broad mass of people without requiring special training or connoisseurship, and it must serve what he calls the "religious consciousness of the age" — by which he means, roughly, the moral ideal of universal brotherhood and the recognition that all human beings share a common life. On these grounds he dismisses most of the Western canon after the Renaissance as counterfeit, elite, or morally corrupt: Beethoven's Ninth Symphony fails because its complexity makes it inaccessible to peasants, Wagner's operas are decadent artifice, and even his own novels he judges to be bad art because they were written for the cultivated classes and don't transmit a morally serious feeling that could unite people.

Suppose we could present Tolstoy with five artistic phenomena from the last thirty years and ask him to evaluate each using the criteria he laid out in *What Is Art?*. The five are: (1) a large-language-model image generator like DALL-E or Midjourney, where a user types a prompt and the system produces an image by statistically recombining patterns from millions of training images; (2) a massively popular TikTok dance trend, where millions of teenagers film themselves performing the same choreography to the same song and share it; (3) a contemporary art installation like Damien Hirst's shark in formaldehyde, which sold for millions and is discussed primarily in art-world publications; (4) a prestige television series like *The Wire*, which is narrative, morally serious, and widely watched but requires dozens of hours of sustained attention and is produced by a large corporate apparatus; and (5) a participatory video game like *Journey* or *Breath of the Wild*, where the experience is interactive, emotionally affecting, and accessible to a broad audience but is also a commercial product built by large teams.

For each of the five, give me your best reconstruction of how Tolstoy would judge it according to his own stated criteria — where he'd approve, where he'd condemn, and where his framework might produce an ambiguous or internally conflicted verdict. Then step back and tell me whether applying Tolstoy's framework to these cases reveals a deeper tension in his theory that wasn't visible when he was only attacking nineteenth-century opera and symbolist poetry. Specifically: does the framework handle these new cases coherently, or do the cases expose a fault line — for instance, between the sincerity criterion and the universal-accessibility criterion, or between the demand that art be morally serious and the demand that it transmit feeling the artist has actually lived? If there is a fault line, is it one Tolstoy could have repaired without abandoning the core of his view?
