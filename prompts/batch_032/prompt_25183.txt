So here's my beat: I cover philosophy and cognitive science for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the ethics of misleading-without-lying in the digital age. You know the classic distinction: lying requires an outright false statement, but misleading covers a lot of other moves — true but cherrypicked stats, strategic omissions, dog whistles, algorithmically surfaced content that creates false impressions without anyone at the company literally asserting anything false.

I keep coming back to Augustine, who was famously absolutist about lying — he basically thought no lie was ever justified, not even to save a life. But he wrote that in the 4th/5th century, way before the internet, targeted advertising, or any of the structures we're dealing with now.

So here's what I'd like: Predict, as best you can from his actual writings and theological commitments, what Augustine's position would be on three modern phenomena that mislead without technically lying. Specifically:

1. A company's PR department releasing a statement that's literally true but deliberatly omits the most damning context, knowing journalists will run with the incomplete version.
2. A political figure using terms that have an innocuous literal meaning but signal something quite different to a subset of their audience (i.e., a dog whistle).
3. A social media platform designing its recommendation algorithm to maximize engagement, knowing that emotionally charged falsehoods and half-truths tend to perform well, even though the platform itself never asserts anything.

Would Augustine collapse any of these into "lying"? Would his framework even have the resources to distinguish them from outright falsehood? And would he land in a different place on any of the three than, say, a contemporary Kantian?

Keep it to 600 words max. And don't give me a bibliography or reading list — I want your actual reasoning from what you know of his positions. If there's something his framework genuinly can't handle, flag it instead of papering over it.
