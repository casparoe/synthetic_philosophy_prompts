I'm writing an essay on the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, and I keep coming back to this one story — Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." You know it? Utopian city, everyone's happy, but it all depends on one kid locked in a basement in absolute misery. Everyone knows. Most people rationalize it and stay; a few just... walk away, into the mountains, into god-knows-what. Le Guin never says what they find.

Here's what I can't shake: the whole setup is basically an ambiguity problem in moral guise. Staying in Omelas is like picking the known urn — you know the distribution, the city thrives, the kid suffers, the terms are fixed. Walking away is the ambiguous urn — you have literally no information about what happens next, Le Guin withholds it on purpose. So are the walkers ambiguity-averse (they can't tolerate the known bad of the child's suffering) or ambiguity-seeking (they're choosing the unknown over the known)? And does the story suggest one of these is the right call, or is it doing something the Ellsberg literature can't capture — like saying that some moral choices aren't about maximizing anything at all?

I want a reading that takes both the story and the decision-theory seriously. And be honest about where the analogy strains — I don't want a just-so reading that pretends Le Guin was writing about Knightian uncertainty.
