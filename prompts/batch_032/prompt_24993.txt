I just spent an hour defending the quasi-emotion solution to the paradox of tragedy — the view that our sadness at fictional events is not genuine sadness but a phenomenologically similar state stripped of its ordinary motivational structure: we feel something sadness-like, but without the impulse to intervene, which is why we can sit through King Lear without rushing the stage and why we seek out experiences that, on their face, involve suffering. My friend pressed me on whether this merely relabels the problem rather than solving it, and whether the account can handle cases where audiences do seem motivated to act (shouting warnings, walking out, covering their eyes). The conversation left me wanting a clearer map of where the view is vulnerable. What would count against the quasi-emotion solution? I want the strongest objections sorted by how damaging they are, from considerations that would force a major revision to those that are merely awkward. In particular: are there empirical results from the psychology of fiction — on mirror neurons, emotional contagion, or the physiology of audience response — that the view gets wrong or cannot accommodate? Does the view misdescribe the phenomenology; is there something about the felt character of tragic sadness that resists being carved into a feeling-component and a motivation-component in the way the account requires? And if the view can be defended against all of this by drawing ever-finer distinctions between quasi-emotions and genuine ones, at what point does it become unfalsifiable, and would that unfalsifiability be a problem for a solution to a conceptual paradox of this kind?
