I've been running a small pottery studio for about a decade, and in my spare time I've been working through books on aesthetics — Hume, Kant, some contemporary stuff, whatever I can manage without a formal background. A phrase I keep running into, both in the reading and in conversations with other craftspeople, is "beauty is in the eye of the beholder." I've watched it get used to do very different kinds of work: to dismiss a negative review of an exhibition, to reassure a student who hates their own work, to argue that art prizes are meaningless, and to claim that there's no point discussing taste at all because it's all just personal.

What I can't sort out is what the slogan is actually saying. It seems to slide between claims that are obviously true — different people like different things — and claims that would, if true, make my entire practice of trying to make beautiful objects incoherent. When I got to Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste," he seemed to be arguing that there is a standard, but it depends on the joint verdict of qualified judges — which sounds like it's trying to split the difference.

Can you lay out, as clearly as possible, the distinct precise theses that "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" could be expressing? I want a menu: for each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or just false, and whether it actually licenses the move of treating all aesthetic disagreement as mere difference of preference. And I'd like to know which of these readings Hume was actually taking aim at, and whether his "standard of taste" refutes the slogan outright or just restricts its scope.
