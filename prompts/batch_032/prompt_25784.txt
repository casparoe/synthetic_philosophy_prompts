I'm a graduate student in modern European history, and at a reception after a department colloquium last week I ended up standing next to two senior faculty whose back-and-forth I can't stop thinking about. One — I'll call her Keller — is a historian of international relations who leans toward social-science methods and quantitative approaches. The other — I'll call him Morrow — is a diplomatic historian with more humanistic inclinations, the kind who works deep in archives and writes narrative-driven accounts. They were discussing what it means to explain the outbreak of the First World War, and specifically whether the July Crisis of 1914 is best understood through general regularities or through narrative. I didn't participate; I just listened, glass in hand, and I've been turning their exchange over in my head ever since. I'd like someone to go through their arguments move by move and adjudicate who is right about what.

Here's roughly how the exchange went, as I remember it:

(1) Keller said: If you claim to explain why war broke out in 1914, you're committed to general regularities whether you admit it or not. Saying that the Schlieffen Plan's rigid railway timetables made mobilization tantamount to war is only explanatory if there's a general connection between military planning of that kind and conflict escalation. If you deny that any regularity is at work, you haven't explained the outcome — you've chronicled it. Explanation means showing that the event was to be expected, given the conditions and the regularities that cover them.

(2) Morrow replied: But the regularities you'd need are either false or trivial. "Rigid mobilization plans lead to war" is false — plenty of rigid plans sat unused across Europe for decades. "Crises sometimes escalate" is true but vacuous, and it doesn't distinguish 1914 from 1913 or any other year. The actual explanation is in the concrete story: the timing of the Russian mobilization order, Bethmann-Hollweg's particular miscalculations about British neutrality, the delayed delivery of the German ultimatum, the way Grey's foreign office communicated ambiguity at crucial moments. You don't need laws; you need to get the narrative details right and lay them out in the right order.

(3) Keller answered: But why does getting the narrative details right explain anything? It's because they activate tacit generalizations we all accept — about how harried officials process partial information, how bureaucratic routines constrain options, how states respond to perceived threats under time pressure. If those generalizations didn't hold — if humans in crises acted randomly — the narrative would be unintelligible. So the explanatory force of narrative is borrowed from the regularities it presupposes, even if historians don't bother to state them explicitly.

(4) Morrow came back: I think you're running together "intelligibility" with "subsumption under laws." A good novel makes a character's actions perfectly intelligible without invoking psychological laws — it does so by rendering the particular person in the particular situation. Historical explanation is more like that than like physics. And here's the real problem for your view: if you insist that genuine explanation requires covering laws, you set a standard that no practicing historian has ever met, including the very best ones. That should make you suspect the standard is wrong, not that every historian is systematically failing at their own disciplinary task.

(5) Keller had one more: But if a novelist renders a character intelligible, it's still because the portrayal resonates with what we know — tacitly, generally — about how people are. The "particular situation" only makes sense against a background of shared generalities; otherwise it would be utterly opaque. So the dilemma remains: either you're relying on generalizations you having't stated, or you're not explaining at all — you're just telling a story and hoping we nod along. That's fine for literature, but history claims to do more.

At that point someone pulled them away to meet the visiting speaker, and the conversation ended unresolved. I've been trying to figure out who had the better arguments. Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point? For each move — (1) through (5) — say which claims are sound, which are vulnerable, and why. Then say whether either of them is missing a way of reframing the issue that would move the debate forward, and give an overall assessment of where the stronger case lies.
