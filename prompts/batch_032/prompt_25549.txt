I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science, and I've been working on an essay that I plan to submit to a journal. The working title is "Realism About Neural Network Representations: Why the Instrumentalist Challenge Falls Short." The core argument runs as follows. Interpretability researchers routinely talk about individual neurons or directions in activation space as representing features — curves, textures, truth values, syntactic categories, and so on. A growing sceptical literature, however, charges that this talk is merely instrumental: we project human concepts onto the network's internal structure because doing so helps us predict its behaviour, but there is no fact of the matter about whether neuron 47 *really* represents a car detector as opposed to something extensionally equivalent over the training distribution. I want to push back against this instrumentalism by drawing an analogy with the debate over scientific realism about unobservables. Just as the no-miracles argument suggests that the predictive success of our scientific theories would be a miracle if those theories weren't at least approximately true, I argue that the practical success of interpretability-based interventions — editing model behaviour via activation patching, using sparse autoencoders to find steerable features, and so on — would be a miracle if the posited representations were merely convenient fictions with no genuine referential connection to anything in the network. The essay then considers three disanalogies between the scientific-realism case and the interpretability case: first, that scientific theories make novel predictions while interpretability claims are typically evaluated only on the training distribution; second, that scientific unobservables are causally responsible for observations in a way that network representations arguably aren't for outputs; and third, that there is no agreed-upon theory of what it *means* for a component of a neural network to represent something, whereas scientific realism has reasonably well-developed causal and structural accounts of reference to draw on. I argue that each disanalogy can be weakened, though the third is the most serious and forces a concession: realism about network representations may need to be pluralist, with different representation relations holding for different kinds of features.

I'd be grateful for comments on the overall argumentative strategy. In particular: (1) Is the no-miracles move doing real work here, or am I just dressing up an intuition in borrowed clothes? (2) Are there further disanalogies I've missed that would make the analogy break down earlier than I think? (3) Does the pluralist concession at the end amount to giving up on realism in any interesting sense, or can a pluralist realism still earn its keep? I'm not looking for line-edits or stylistic suggestions — I want to know whether the architecture of the argument is sound before I sink more time into writing the full draft.
