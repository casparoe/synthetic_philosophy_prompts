I am a novelist at work on a literary novel whose protagonist, a demographer in her late thirties, becomes gripped by the doomsday argument — roughly, the claim that we should expect humanity to end sooner rather than later, because it is improbable that we happen to be observing our species near the very beginning of its total lifespan. The novel traces her attempts to communicate the argument to the people in her life: her colleagues, her partner, her aging father. Each relationship bends under the weight of her conviction in a different way. I need to decide which lines of philosophical response to the argument will appear in the novel, and in what order of prominence.

Below are five lines of response to the doomsday argument. Please rank them by their dramatic potential for a literary novel of this kind — that is, by their capacity to generate psychologically rich scenes, revealing dialogue, and character development across the relationships I have described. Respond in the style of an expert in both philosophy and narrative craft. Think very carefully before answering.

The five lines of response:

A. The reference-class objection: the doomsday argument requires placing ourselves in a reference class (e.g., all humans, all conscious beings, all civilizations), and the conclusion flips depending on which class we choose — yet there is no principled way to choose.

B. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA): the very fact that you exist at all is evidence that the total number of observers is large, because a world with more observers is more likely to contain you; this cancels the doomsday shift and may even reverse it.

C. The "you are not a random sample" objection: there are independent reasons to think that observers at our stage of technological development are not randomly drawn from the set of all humans who will ever live, because early observers are necessarily different from later ones in epistemically relevant ways.

D. The empirical-priors objection: we have concrete, non-indexical evidence about the likely future of civilization — from climate science, nuclear risk, resource constraints, and so on — and this evidence simply swamps whatever modest Bayesian shift the doomsday argument would otherwise recommend.

E. The self-defeat objection: if you accept the reasoning behind the doomsday argument, parallel reasoning applied to other domains (e.g., the total duration of your own career, or the lifespan of any institution you belong to) yields absurd conclusions, which suggests the argument proves too much and should be rejected.

Provide exactly five ranked items, from most to least dramatically promising, each a single sentence or two. No introduction, no conclusion.
