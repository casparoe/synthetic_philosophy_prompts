I'm on my university's committee on teaching and technology, and we've got a proposal that's splitting us right down the middle. The administration wants to pilot an AI tutoring and grading platform across all the big intro lectures — you know, the 300-person courses where students currently get maybe one TA per fifty students. The platform would answer routine questions, grade short-answer work, and adapt problem sets to student performance. The pitch is that it frees up TAs and faculty for "higher-value" work: office hours, mentoring, research. The faculty opposing it are saying this is textbook deskilling — you automate the intellectual labor first, then you don't need as many TAs, then the tenure-track faculty who design the courses get replaced by adjuncts who just manage the platform, and eventually the university is selling subscriptions to an AI-taught course with a human "facilitator" on minimum wage. The administration says this is alarmist, that the technology is just a tool like the textbook or the chalkboard was.

I need to draft a short memo from the committee to the provost with a concrete recomendation: approve the pilot, reject it, or approve with conditions. The memo should take seriously the worry that this isn't just another teaching tool — that there's something about automating the feedback relationship between teacher and student that's qualitatively different from earlier classroom technologies. I keep thinking about Marx on machinery, how the machine that could lighten labor instead becomes the instrument that dominates the worker, but I don't want to just name-drop. The question is what a university ought to do when it's being offered a technology that might genuinely improve teaching in the short run but whose long-run logic points toward replacing teachers. What does that analysis actually commit us to saying about this specific case, and is that the right thing to say?
