So I've been going down a rabbit hole about constitutional design lately — specifically this idea that constitutions are like Ulysses contracts, where a polity ties its own hands to stop itself from doing something stupid later. You know the story: Ulysses has his crew lash him to the mast so he can hear the sirens without steering the ship into the rocks. And the analogy gets used all the time to justify things like supermajority amendment rules, bills of rights that are hard to repeal, eternity clauses (like the one in Germany's Basic Law), and independent central banks.

But the more I think about it, the more the analogy seems to be doing a lot of sneaky work. In the original story, Ulysses is one guy — same body, same mind, same interests across time. He gives an order, and later he tries to countermand it, and the crew rightly ignores his later self because his earlier self knew better. Fine. But a constitution binds people who weren't even born when it was ratified. The "earlier self" is a bunch of long-dead politicians, and the "later self" is, well, us. So the whole thing seems to presuppose that a polity is the kind of thing that has a unified self persisting across generations — that "the people" in 1789 and "the people" in 2025 are, in some morally and politically relevant sense, the *same agent*.

What I'm trying to figure out is: what theory of collective identity or democratic legitimacy does constitutional precommitment actually presuppose? Does it need a full-on organic theory of the state where the nation is literally a person? Or can you make it work with something thinner, like an intergenerational contract or a kind of hypothetical consent? And if we decided that no, actually, the Ulysses analogy is busted and polities aren't agents in the relevant sense, what would have to give — would we need to make every constitutional provision amendable by a simple majority of the current electorate, or are there other justifications for entrenchment that don't lean on the precommitment idea at all?
