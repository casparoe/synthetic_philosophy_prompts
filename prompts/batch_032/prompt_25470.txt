In a graduate seminar on distributive justice, we spent a session on Nozick's entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain argument. At the end, a classmate summed up his sympathies by reciting the line "From each as they choose, to each as they are chosen" and claimed that this slogan captures the complete case against all patterned principles of justice—that is, against any principle that distributes goods according to a formula like equality, need, merit, or Rawls's difference principle. When I pressed him to say what the slogan actually asserts, he offered only that it means free people will disrupt any pattern through voluntary transactions, so maintaining a pattern requires continuous interference with liberty, and that's unjust.

I suspect the slogan packs together several distinct claims that need to be separated before one can assess whether it refutes patterned principles. Please lay out the different precise theses that "From each as they choose, to each as they are chosen" could be asserting, from relatively uncontroversial claims to radical ones. In particular, I'd like you to distinguish:

(a) Claims about the "from each" half—about what it means to say contributions must be chosen rather than coerced, and what kinds of extraction this rules out (taxation? conscription? emergency duties of rescue?).

(b) Claims about the "to each" half—about what it means to say receipts must be chosen by the giver, and whether this is a claim about sufficient conditions for just holdings (any distribution arising from voluntary transfers is just) or merely a necessary condition (a distribution cannot be just if it wasn't reached through voluntary transfers).

(c) Claims about the relationship between the two halves—does the second follow from the first, or do they rest on independent normative premises? If someone accepts the "from each" half, are they logically committed to the "to each" half?

(d) Claims about what the slogan is supposed to rule out—does it rule out all patterned principles, only end-state patterns (as opposed to patterned principles that incorporate historical constraints), or does it merely shift the argumentative burden without settling it?

For each thesis you identify, please assess its independent plausibility and indicate whether it actually supports the conclusion that all patterned principles of distributive justice are unjust. If the slogan only yields that conclusion when several controversial readings are combined in a specific way, I'd like to know exactly which combination is required and where the weakest link lies.
