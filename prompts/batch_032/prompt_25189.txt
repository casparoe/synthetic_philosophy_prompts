I've been reading Sartre on radical freedom and bad faith, and I keep running into what looks like a serious tension in the view. Sartre claims we are radically free: we always have choices, we cannot escape responsibility for what we make of ourselves, and even refusing to choose is itself a choice. But he also describes bad faith as a pervasive, almost default human condition — a systematic self-deception in which people treat themselves as determined objects rather than free subjects, as though their social role or their past or their "nature" fixed what they could do. If bad faith is as widespread as Sartre says it is — if most people, most of the time, are fleeing from the anxiety of their own freedom — doesn't this empirical fact undercut the claim that radical freedom is our basic condition? It seems like the near-universality of self-deception about freedom is evidence that freedom is not, in fact, the default; it's evidence that something like determinism or at least deep constraint is the more natural description of human life, and radical freedom is the exceptional state one has to work to achieve. How do Sartreans resolve this?
