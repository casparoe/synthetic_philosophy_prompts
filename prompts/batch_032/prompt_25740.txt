I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and last week I overheard two fellow students get into an argument that I've been turning over ever since. I'd like you to adjudicate it. The argument was about rule-following, but it started from a concrete example and escalated into the Kripkenstein problematic. Neither of them was obviously wrong at any point, and I genuinely cannot decide who has the better of it.

The exchange went roughly as follows (I'm reconstructing from notes, so the wording is approximate but the moves are accurate):

**A:** Suppose we teach a child addition. She masters it for all numbers under 100. Then one day we ask her "68 + 57." She answers "5." Now, you'll say she made a mistake. But consider: there is a function — call it *quaddition* — defined as x ⊕ y = x + y if both x and y are below 57, and 5 otherwise. Every past answer she gave is equally consistent with addition and with quaddition. So what fact about her makes it the case that she *meant* addition and therefore made a mistake, rather than that she meant quaddition and answered correctly?

**B:** That's a clever move, but it proves too much. The child would correct herself if we pointed out the error. She'd say "oh, right, 125." Someone following the quaddition rule wouldn't do that. So her disposition to self-correct settles which rule she was following.

**A:** The disposition to self-correct doesn't escape the problem — it just pushes it back. What does "correcting herself" consist in? She produces "125." But the skeptic can redescribe that too: she's now following a *different* rule, or she's applying a correction procedure whose interpretation is equally indeterminate. You're assuming that "correcting" has a determinate meaning, but that's exactly what's in question.

**B:** Fine, but your argument still overreaches. If no fact determines what anyone means by anything, then your own challenge is meaningless — the words in it don't have determinate content either. A skepticism that undercuts its own formulation isn't a position, it's a performative contradiction.

**A:** That's not a refutation, it's a complaint about the consequences. The skeptical conclusion might be uncomfortable, even paradoxical, but that doesn't make it false. The burden is on you to identify the fact that constitutes my meaning addition by "plus" — and no candidate has ever survived scrutiny. Dispositions are finite and include mistakes; platonic objects don't reach out and grip my mind; community standards just spread the problem across more people. If you can't produce the fact, you haven't answered the skeptic — you've just changed the subject.

**B:** Here's a fact you're overlooking: the child's past answers exhibit a *pattern*, and addition is the simplest rule that fits that pattern. Simplicity isn't just an aesthetic preference — it's a constitutive norm of interpretation. We're justified in attributing the simplest rule, and that justification is all the "fact" we need. Your demand for a stronger kind of fact is a demand for something metaphysics doesn't owe us.

**A:** Simplicity is relative to a descriptive language. Under one encoding, quaddition is simpler — it has a shorter description if you allow a certain primitive. You're smuggling in the very interpretative framework you're trying to justify. The skeptic's point is precisely that there's no framework-independent fact about which rule is "simpler."
