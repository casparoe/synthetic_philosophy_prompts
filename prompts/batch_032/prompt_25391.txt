I've been wrestling with an argument that seems to show the independence axiom condemns not just the Allais preferences but also precautionary reasoning about catastrophic risks — which surely proves too much. I need someone to locate the misstep.

Here's the argument:

(1) Independence: for any lotteries L1, L2, L3 and p ∈ (0,1], L1 ≻ L2 implies p·L1 + (1−p)·L3 ≻ p·L2 + (1−p)·L3.

(2) In the Allais problem, most people prefer A = ($1M, 1) to B = ($5M, 0.10; $1M, 0.89; $0, 0.01), and D = ($5M, 0.10; $0, 0.90) to C = ($1M, 0.11; $0, 0.89).

(3) These preferences jointly violate independence. (Mix A and B with a 0.89 chance of $0 at p=0.11; independence then requires C ≻ D, but we observe D ≻ C.)

(4) If independence is a requirement of rationality, the Allais preferences are irrational.

(5) The Allais preferences are driven by the certainty effect: people treat a shift from 1% to 0% probability of $0 as weightier than a shift from 90% to 89%, though both are a 1-point difference.

(6) If the certainty effect is irrational, then any preference that treats eliminating a small probability of a catastrophic outcome as especially weighty is also irrational. After all, eliminating a 1% existential risk is just achieving certainty that the catastrophe won't occur; independence says you must value that percentage-point reduction identically regardless of the baseline probability.

(7) But precautionary reasoning in public policy does exactly this: it devotes significant resources to reducing small catastrophic risks to zero, even when those resources would save more expected lives if directed elsewhere.

(8) If precautionary reasoning is irrational, much of climate policy, pandemic preparedness, and nuclear nonproliferation policy is irrational.

(9) That conclusion is absurd.

(10) So either independence is not a requirement of rationality, or the parallel between the Allais case and precautionary reasoning fails. But if it fails, we need a principle that condemns the certainty effect in Allais while permitting it for catastrophic risks — and it's not obvious such a principle exists without begging the question.

Where does this argument go wrong? If you reject a premise, explain what giving it up costs. If the reasoning contains a subtle flaw, name it and show why it looked valid. Respond in the style of an expert decision theorist. Think very carefully, and keep your answer to at most 400 words.
