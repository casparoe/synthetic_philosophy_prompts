I'm on the board of trustees for my town's public library and we've got a vote coming up on a proposal to replace our whole circulation desk with an RFID self-checkout system — you know, those kiosks where you wave your books and it prints a reciept, plus an automated sorter in the back that routes returns to the right shelf bin. The pitch is it'll save about $70k a year in staffing and basically wipe out checkout lines.

I've been losing sleep over this and reading whatever I can find online. Here's what's eating at me: I don't buy that this is just a neutral efficiency upgrade. Our circulation desk is where our librarians — some of them have been here 20, 30 years — actually see patrons. Mrs. Chen notices when the same kid checks out that book on divorce three times in a row and maybe says something, or at least knows to keep an eye out. The kiosk won't. And once you install it, that kind of interaction just isn't part of how the library works anymore. The technology doesn't just speed up the same thing — it changes what the thing is.

Some of what I've been reading argues that technologies have values or politics literally designed into them, that they're not just neutral tools sitting there waiting for us to decide how to use them — they push in a certain direction all by themselves. Other stuff I've read says that's overblown, it's all about how you implement it, and you could use the staff savings to fund more programs instead.

Half the board thinks I'm being sentimental and this is purely an operations question. The other half is uneasy but can't quite put their finger on why. The library director just wants a clear recommendation before the meeting.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate to the board before the vote? It should recommend something concrete — go ahead, don't go ahead, or go ahead with conditions — and lay out the reasoning so the board can actually discuss it. I want the memo to genuinely engage with the idea that the technology itself might not be neutral, not just wave it off as "we'll miss chatting with the librarians." And whatever side we come down on, the memo should honestly present the strongest argument on the other side, not a strawman.
