Two fellow graduate students in my department got into an argument at our colloquium reception last week, and I have been turning it over ever since. I would like you to walk through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point.

The argument started when Maria, who works on social ontology, said she was frustrated by how the phrase "socially constructed" gets used as if it meant the same thing across entirely different kinds of cases. She said: people say race is socially constructed and money is socially constructed in the same breath, but money is an institutional fact — it exists only because we collectively represent certain pieces of paper or ledger entries as currency. If collective representation ceased, money would cease. Race does not work that way. A person's racial classification structures their life chances whether or not anyone in the society explicitly represents them as belonging to that race. The mechanisms are fundamentally different, and the shared label obscures that.

David, who works on philosophy of race, pushed back. (1) He said Maria was conflating two questions: how a kind is constituted and how it is sustained. Even if money requires collective representation for its constitution, once the institution exists, individual tokens of money function as money whether or not the person handing them over explicitly represents them that way — just as racial classifications function whether or not anyone at a given moment is thinking about race. So the contrast is not as sharp as she claimed.

(2) Maria replied that David was missing her point. The difference, she said, is that with institutional facts like money, the collective representation is constitutive of the kind: the paper counts as money because we collectively take it to count as money. With race, the representation — where it exists — is a response to a social practice of classification that is itself sustained by material inequalities, inherited patterns of interaction, and differential treatment by institutions, none of which depend on anyone's representing those structures in racial terms. She said: money is a status function imposed on objects by collective acceptance; race is a social position imposed on people by a system of unequal relations. Those are different kinds of social construction, and using the same term for both encourages confusion about what would be required to change them.

(3) David answered that this distinction, while real, does not show what Maria thinks it shows. He argued that collective representation plays a larger role in sustaining racial categories than she was admitting. Racial categories persist in part because people — including the people classified — use racial concepts to navigate the social world, form expectations, and coordinate behavior. If racial concepts disappeared from collective thought entirely, the material inequalities might persist for a while, but the categories themselves — the social kinds — would eventually dissolve, or at least transform into something unrecognizable. So both money and race depend on collective representation; the difference is one of explicitness, time lag, and the density of the material scaffolding, not a difference in the kind of dependence.

(4) Maria came back with: but that is exactly the difference that matters. With money, if collective representation vanished overnight, money would vanish overnight — the paper would just be paper. With race, if racial concepts vanished overnight, the inequalities would persist, the segregated neighborhoods would persist, the differential treatment by institutions would persist — and those things would likely regenerate racial concepts. The direction of dependence runs the other way: the material structures sustain the concepts, not the other way around. That is not a difference of degree; it is a difference in what kind of social kind we are dealing with, and a good ontology of the social world ought to mark it.

At this point someone pulled them away to get more wine, and they never resumed. I am not sure who has the better case. Please go through the four numbered moves and assess each one: what is right about it, what is wrong or overstated, and whether it advances or weakens its speaker's overall position. Then give your own view on the underlying question: is there a principled distinction between the way money is socially constructed and the way race is socially constructed, or does "social construction" pick out a unified phenomenon that merely manifests differently across domains?
