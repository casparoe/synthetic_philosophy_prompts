I recently allocated a portion of my retirement savings to Bitcoin, a decision I made after reading several bullish analyses but without thinking much about the philosophical question of what money actually is. At a dinner party two weeks ago, my brother-in-law David, who teaches macroeconomics, and my cousin Elena, who works at a cryptocurrency exchange, got into a sustained argument about whether Bitcoin qualifies as money. I said very little at the time, but I have not been able to stop turning their exchange over in my mind, and I would like someone to adjudicate it move by move. Here is the argument as I remember it:

(1) David opened by saying Bitcoin is not money because money must serve three functions — medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account — and Bitcoin fails at all three: almost no one buys groceries with it, its price swings make it a poor store of value, and no merchant denominates prices in satoshis.

(2) Elena replied that this functional test proves too much. During hyperinflation, a national currency can fail catastrophically as a store of value without ceasing to be money. Gold is an excellent store of value but is not currently money. So the three-functions framework cannot be a necessary-and-sufficient-conditions test; it is at best a rough heuristic.

(3) David conceded the point about hyperinflation but said the deeper issue is institutional: money is whatever a sovereign authority declares is acceptable for settling debts and paying taxes. The dollar is money because the U.S. government accepts it for taxes and courts enforce dollar-denominated contracts. Bitcoin has no such backing, so it is a speculative asset, not money.

(4) Elena answered that this conflates fiat money with money as such. Historically, gold and silver coins circulated as money long before modern states existed. Cigarettes functioned as money in POW camps. Rai stones on Yap served as money without anything resembling a central bank. If a community converges on treating something as a medium of exchange and a store of value, it is money — state endorsement is sufficient but not necessary.

(5) David responded that this makes the definition of money purely a matter of social consensus, which is true in some ultimate sense but unhelpful: anything can be money if enough people treat it as money, but the interesting question is whether they actually do. And the evidence suggests Bitcoin is used overwhelmingly for speculation, not for buying goods and services. The fact that a handful of transactions occur does not make something a currency any more than the fact that people sometimes trade baseball cards makes baseball cards money.

(6) Elena said David was now shifting the goalposts from "what is money" to "how widely used must it be," and that this line-drawing problem afflicts his own view too: how many transactions must a state-backed currency lose before it stops being money? She added that in El Salvador, Bitcoin is legal tender, and in various grey-market contexts it functions as a medium of exchange right now. If the philosophical question is what kind of thing money is — a natural kind, an institutional fact, or a social convention — then Bitcoin's existence puts real pressure on purely institutional theories.

I would like you to adjudicate this disagreement. For each of the six moves above, say whether the speaker is clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who, if anyone, has the more defensible overall position, and identify any important considerations that both parties missed.
