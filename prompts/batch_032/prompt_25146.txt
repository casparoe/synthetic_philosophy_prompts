The debate between precise and imprecise probabilists has been running since at least the 1980s and, from where I sit, it has the feel of something that should have been resolvable by now. Both sides agree on the mathematics — sets of probability functions, dilation, dilation, the various decision rules for imprecise credences — and both agree that in cases of severe uncertainty (a coin of unknown bias, the classic "cube factory" example) ordinary reasoners do not behave as if they have a single precise credence. And yet the core question — whether rationality permits or even requires imprecise credences, or whether it demands a unique precise distribution even when evidence seems too thin to fix one — remains unsettled, with the same moves and countermoves reappearing in the literature year after year. My working hypothesis is that this is not a single disagreement but at least two layered on top of each other: one about the metaphysics of doxastic states (are credences psychological realities or normative posits?), and one about the function of formal epistemology (is it describing ideally rational agents or prescribing norms for bounded ones?). But I could be wrong about that, and I want a diagnosis that engages the actual arguments rather than my guess. So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive dispute about the norms of rationality that could in principle be settled by a decisive argument, a clash of bedrock intuitions about what counts as a rational requirement, a verbal dispute about the word "credence," or two research programs answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it — a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about human reasoning, or something else — or is the impasse a sign that the question was ill-posed from the start?
