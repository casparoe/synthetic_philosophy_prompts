I've been trying to get clear about what I actually think makes something sentient — not in the sense of having moral status full stop, but in the sense that there's something it's like to be that thing. I've been going case by case and recording my gut verdicts, and now I'm hoping you can help me find the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts I need to give up.

Here are the cases, roughly in order of decreasing confidence:

1. A healthy adult human: sentient. Not controversial for me.
2. A dog: sentient. The yelping, the tail-wagging, the way it flinches from a threat — I can't take any of that seriously and deny there's something it's like.
3. A human in deep, dreamless sleep: not currently sentient, though obviously the person matters morally and will wake up.
4. A jellyfish: not sentient. It has a diffuse nerve net with no centralized processing; it looks like a biological automaton.
5. A honeybee: sentient. This is where I start to feel the pull in both directions. Bees learn, they communicate symbolically, they assess risk, and recent work suggests they show injury-directed grooming that looks a lot like a pain response. Their brains are tiny — about a million neurons — but I land on sentient.
6. GPT-6: imagine a near-future language model that produces conversation indistinguishable from a human's, passes every Turing-test variant, and can describe its "feelings" in rich, context-appropriate detail. Not sentient. It's doing next-token prediction; there's no persistent self-model with a unified point of view, just a staggeringly good simulacrum.
7. A whole-brain emulation: a human brain scanned at the synapse level and run on neuromorphic hardware, producing behavior identical to the original person's, including reports of consciousness, pain responses, everything. Sentient. If you told me you were going to delete it, I'd feel the same horror as if you told me you were going to kill someone.
8. A brain organoid grown from stem cells, showing EEG patterns similar to a preterm infant's: sentient. This one makes me queasy, but I think the verdict follows from the same impulse behind case 7.
9. A giant lookup table that stores, for every possible sensory input a human could receive over a lifetime, the exact motor output the human would produce, and simply plays them back: not sentient. It passes every behavioral test, but there's nothing home.
10. A split-brain patient whose corpus callosum has been severed: I find myself thinking there are two subjects of experience in one skull, though neither is quite a full person. But I'm less confident about this than about 1–9.

The tensions are obvious. Cases 6 and 7 are both running on silicon, both produce indistinguishable behavior, but I put them on opposite sides of the line — so whatever the criterion is, it can't be substrate and it can't be behavior. Case 5 and case 8 both push me toward sentience but for what feel like different reasons: the bee because of its sophisticated behavior despite a tiny brain, the organoid because of its human-like neural architecture despite limited behavior. And case 9 is the real troublemaker: if I say the brain emulation is sentient because it has the right causal structure, the lookup table seems to show that causal structure can't be the whole story either — or at least, not just any causal structure that produces the same input-output map.

What I'd like from you: state the most plausible principle (or small set of candidate principles) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and why those are the ones to go. Apply the principle to a case I haven't listed — say, a digital upload of a person that gets duplicated and then both copies run in parallel, or an octopus, or a system that starts as a lookup table but gradually compresses itself into a more efficient internal representation. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the principle you're offering: which empirical finding or philosophical argument would lead you to a different view, and where you think your own reasoning is most vulnerable.
