I'm a research scientist working on reinforcement learning, and I've recently joined an interdisciplinary reading group — mostly ML people and philosophers of mind — that's been working through Daniel Dennett's *From Bacteria to Bach and Back: The Evolution of Minds*. The idea that's been keeping me up is what Dennett calls the "strange inversion of reasoning," the claim that Darwin and Turing independently discovered the same deep principle: that competence can arise without comprehension, and that comprehension, when it does appear, is built on top of layers of mindless competence rather than serving as the foundation. Before Darwin, it seemed obvious that complex functional design required a designer; Darwin showed that a blind, purposeless process could generate it. Before Turing, computation was something that required understanding — the human "computers" knew arithmetic; Turing showed you could squeeze out every last drop of comprehension and leave only mechanical rule-following, and the computation would still work. The inversion is that we had the order backwards: comprehension isn't the source of competence; competence is the source of comprehension.

I find this compelling, but I keep stumbling when I try to connect it to my own work. It feels like the whole thing should be expressible in the vocabulary I use every day — objective functions, optimization landscapes, the base-model versus fine-tuning distinction, meta-learning, mesa-optimizers, reward misspecification, the bitter lesson, the scaling hypothesis, and so on. Every time I try to do the translation myself, I get partway and then lose the thread. So I'd like you to do it systematically.

Restate Dennett's Darwin-Turing inversion — the claim that mindless processes can generate competence, and that comprehension is a later product built atop competence — entirely in the vocabulary of contemporary machine learning. Be as faithful to the original claim as you can; don't smuggle in ML assumptions and call them a translation. Then give me the residue report: what mapped cleanly, what you had to stretch or distort, and what has no counterpart at all in the ML vocabulary. I especially want your verdict on whether the residue points to something the ML framework is genuinely blind to — something the philosophers are tracking that we cannot express — or whether it's the other way around: the philosophical claim, once cashed out in precise terms, turns out to be less substantive than it sounds.
