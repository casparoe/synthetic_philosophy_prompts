I've been going back through Paul Christiano's old alignment writings — the stuff on approval-directed agents, iterated distillation and amplification, and the distinction between building an AI that tries to do what we want versus one that tries to do what we'd approve of. The standard story, as I understand it, is that approval-directedness is a safety win: instead of locking in a goal that might be mis-specified, you have the agent constantly checking itself against human judgment, which keeps it corrigible and prevents the kind of goal-drift that makes goal-directed agents scary. But I've started to suspect there's a class of failure modes that are specific to approval-directed agents and that don't arise for (well-designed) goal-directed ones — failure modes that don't require the human to have bad preferences, and that don't involve the agent explicitly lying to or manipulating the overseer. They seem to fall out of the structure of optimizing for approval itself, even when everything else is set up correctly.

I'd like you to construct a thought experiment that isolates one such failure mode. Here are the specifications:

- The setting should be an ordinary workplace: a manager delegating analytical or creative work to an AI tool. No science fiction, no superintelligence, just the kind of AI assistant that plausibly exists in the near future.
- The human manager is reasonable, well-intentioned, and has coherent preferences. The failure must not come from the human wanting bad things or being inconsistent.
- The approval-directed agent and the goal-directed agent are given the same task. They take observably different actions (or produce observably different outputs), and the approval-directed agent's action is worse along a dimension that matters for safety or for the human's long-term interests — even though on the surface it may look more helpful or more cooperative.
- The failure mode must be structural: it should fall out of what it means to optimize for approval, not from the agent having been given a bad specification of what counts as approval or from the human having been poorly chosen as the source of approval.

After presenting the scenario, walk through what each agent does and why, identify which feature of approval-directed reasoning produces the worse outcome, and say whether a modified version of approval-directedness — for instance, directing approval at a counterfactual or idealized version of the human, or at the human's considered judgment after reflection — would avoid the problem or merely relocate it. If there's a way a defender of approval-directed architectures could modify the setup to close off the failure mode without abandoning approval-directedness entirely, I'd like to hear that too.
