I have been working on a short essay concerning a difficulty for David Lewis's Principal Principle, and I would appreciate your comments. The essay is below. I am particularly interested in whether the objection I raise to Lewis's New Principle has genuine force, or whether it rests on a confusion. I would also welcome suggestions for how the argument might be strengthened or restructured.

---

The Principal Principle, as formulated by Lewis, states that a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of A is x (together with any admissible evidence), should equal x. More formally: Cr(A | Ch(A) = x ∧ E) = x, where E is any admissible evidence. The principle captures the intuitive thought that we ought to defer to objective chances: if a fair coin has chance 0.5 of landing heads, our credence that it will land heads should be 0.5.

Lewis himself identified a difficulty for the Principal Principle when combined with his Humean Best System Account (BSA) of chance. On the BSA, the chances are given by whatever system of probabilistic laws best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the total history of the world. This means that what the chances are depends on what actually happens across all of history, future included.

The problem of undermining futures runs as follows. Suppose the BSA assigns a non-zero chance to some future F that, if it occurred, would make a different system of laws the best system — and would therefore make a different chance function the correct one. Call F an undermining future. Conditional on F occurring, the chance of F according to the original chance function would not be what that chance function says it is, because F's occurrence would change which chance function is correct. This generates a conflict with the Principal Principle: the principle instructs us to set our credence in F equal to its chance, yet we also know that if F occurs, the chance of F was not what we took it to be.

Lewis's proposed remedy was the New Principle (NP). Rather than deferring to the chance function Ch simpliciter, we defer to Ch conditional on the proposition that Ch is the actual chance function. That is: Cr(A | Ch = f ∧ E) = f(A | Ch = f). Lewis argued that NP reduces to the original Principal Principle in most ordinary cases and avoids the undermining problem by explicitly conditioning on the chance theory being correct.

I wish to suggest that NP faces a residual difficulty. The New Principle requires that the chance function f assign a well-defined conditional probability to A given that f itself is the correct chance theory. But on the BSA, whether f is the correct chance theory depends on the entire pattern of events across all of history. The proposition "Ch = f" is a proposition about the total history of the world. For f to assign a probability to A conditional on this proposition, f must assign probabilities to propositions about its own correctness — propositions that, on the BSA, are global and partly about the future. This seems to require that f assign probabilities to futures that would make f incorrect, conditional on f being correct. If f is correct, then futures that would make f incorrect presumably have chance zero conditional on f being correct — but this looks as though it may beg the question, since whether those futures have chance zero is part of what is at issue.

I suspect this circularity is not vicious, but I am not confident. I would welcome comments on whether this line of objection has force, and whether there are alternative resolutions to the undermining problem that avoid the difficulty entirely.
