I'm a novelist, currently working on a book that involves a fair bit of natural history, and I keep tripping over something I don't have a word for. I'm hoping someone in philosophy of biology might of named it.

Here's the pattern. Two groups of organisms — say two populations of some bird on different islands — are studied by different researchers. One says they're subspecies of the same species; the other says they're two full species. The evidence about interbreeding, genetics, and morphology is the same; both researchers have seen it. But the conversation goes like this: the lumper says the splitter is inflating species counts to make their field site seem more important; the splitter says the lumper is erasing biodiversity that deserves protection. And then they argue about conservation policy or research funding, but they do it entirely in the language of taxonomy, as if the question "are these one species or two?" has a mind-independent answer that just happens to coincide with whichever policy they prefer.

I've seen this enough times now — in birding forums, in popular science books, in conversations with field biologists — that it feels like a named move. It's not just disagreement about species concepts (I know about the biological vs. phylogenetic vs. ecological species concepts, and that's part of it but not the whole thing). The specific thing is: treating a taxonomic question as purely empirical while your answer is doing obvious normative work, and your opponent's answer is doing obvious normative work, and neither of you acknowledges the normative part. It's like the fact-value distinction is being smuggled across a border inside a Latin binomial.

Does this have a name? If it does, what's the canonical statement of it, and how does it differ from the general species problem or from generic motivated reasoning? If it doesn't, what would you call it and how would you define its boundaries — does it require that both parties are doing it, or can one side be sincere? Does it count if the normative stakes are purely epistemic (e.g., career incentives) rather than ethical or political?

And if the whole way I've framed this is confused — if I'm seeing a pattern that isn't really there, or I'm conflating two things that should be kept apart — please say so. I'd rather be told I'm asking the wrong question than get a polite answer to a broken one.
