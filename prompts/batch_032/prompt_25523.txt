ok so I've been going around in circles on the Meno problem — the question why knowledge is supposed to be more valuable than mere true belief. Like, if I truly believe the road to Larissa goes east and you know it, we both get to Larissa. What's the extra value in your state? I've read a bunch of proposals (virtue epistemology, reliabilism, knowledge-first, the knowledge-is-the-norm-of-assertion line, and the debunkers who say there IS no extra value) but I keep feeling like everyone's just pushing their favorite theory without first agreeing on what the theory actually has to do.

So before I read another paper: write me the desiderata. What does an adequate theory of the value of knowledge (or an adequate debunking of that value) have to get right? I want:

- the cases it has to handle (Gettier cases, fake barns, the standard ones but also the weirder ones)
- the distinctions it can't collapse (like the difference between the value of knowledge and the value of true belief, or between instrumental and final value)
- results from other parts of epistemology it has to be consistent with
- the intuitions it has to respect or at least explain away rather than ignore

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it. Then flag which ones are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps. And tell me which single requirement you think knocks out the most existing theories — my money's on something related to the swamping problem but I want your view.
