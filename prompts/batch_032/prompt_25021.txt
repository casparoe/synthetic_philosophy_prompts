I have become convinced that the doomsday argument — the one that goes, roughly, by treating my birth rank among all humans who will ever live as a random draw and concluding that the total will not be enormous — is basically sound, and that this has real implications for how we should think about existential risk. I want to know what would count against it.

Please give me a map of the argument's exposure. I want the considerations, objections, and possible developments that should move a proponent of the argument, sorted by how decisive they would be if they held: which would be fatal, which would force a serious revision, which would merely be awkward. For each, tell me what the world would have to look like — or what we would have to discover — for it to actually land. I am especially interested in the ones that a committed defender of the argument would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say from the outside.

I also want an honest assessment of whether the argument has been insulated against refutation by its defenders — whether there are moves available that let it absorb almost any contrary consideration, and if so, whether that is a problem for an argument of this kind, or just what good philosophical arguments do. If there are escape routes that are logically possible but not live, say which ones and why they are not live. If there are live escape routes that have not been closed, flag them clearly.

For what it is worth, the considerations I personally find hardest to dismiss are the ones about the reference class problem and about whether the self-sampling assumption can bear the weight the argument puts on it. But I want to know whether those are actually the right places to push, or whether a critic should be aiming elsewhere.
