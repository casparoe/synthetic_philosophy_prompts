I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about Bernard Williams and his "one thought too many" objection to utilitarianism. The way I understand it: Williams says if you're in a burning building and you save your wife instead of two strangers, but you only do it after running the utilitarian math in your head—like you pause and think "well, my wife has more utility to me personally, so it's actually maximizing overall welfare to save her"—that pause itself is corrupting. The fact that you needed the calculation at all is "one thought too many." A person with integrity just acts from their ground-level commitments directly, without routing everything through an impartial justification engine.

I keep bumping into a work version of this. I'm on a team that builds content ranking systems, and we optimize for engagement metrics. Sometimes the data says we should push stuff that I find genuinely troubling—not illegal, just corrosive. My tech lead says "run the experiment, let the numbers decide." But I feel like there are some decisions where even running the A/B test is a mistake, because the willingness to test it already concedes something. Like, if you'd even consider A/B testing whether to serve pro-anorexia content to teenagers because the engagement numbers might go up, haven't you already lost the plot?

So here's what I actually want: can you operationalize Williams's integrity objection into a decision procedure I can use at work? I want something like a checklist or a set of questions I run through when I'm asked to optimize for a metric in a way that makes me queasy. The procedure should help me figure out: is this a legitimate "one thought too many" situation where I should refuse to even run the test, or am I just being squeamish about something that's actually fine to quantify? What are the criteria that distinguish "this metric shouldn't apply here" from "I don't like what the metric says"?

Some specific things I need it to handle:
- Cases where my personal "integrity" commitments conflict with what my teammates genuinely don't see a problem with. Like, am I just imposing my values, or is there something more objective going on?
- Cases where the metric actually does capture something real and important, and my discomfort is just me not liking the conclusion.
- Cases where I'm not the one making the final call—I'm three levels down from the VP who set the metric. Does Williams have anything to say about when you should push back vs. when you should just do your job?
- The possibility that I'm misusing the whole framework as a fancy way to rationalize doing what I already wanted to do.

Also, and this is important to me: please separate what's actually settled among people who study this stuff from what's still genuinely contested. I don't want to walk into a conversation with my manager and confidently announce that Williams proved utilitarianism is false, when actually most philosophers think his argument has a specific flaw that I dont know about. Like, tell me where the consensus is and where I'd be taking a side.
