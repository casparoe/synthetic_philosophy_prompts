I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Probability and Epistemology" — about fifteen students, mostly philosophy majors who've had at least one course in formal logic or probability, one 90-minute session per week. Next week we're tackling the Sleeping Beauty problem and self-locating belief, and I want them to feel the force of the puzzle before they see the formal arguments. I've found that when I just present the case and poll the room, the halfers and thirders talk past each other for an hour and nobody's mind moves. They need to experience the vertigo of not knowing which centered world they occupy, not just hear about it.

Design a classroom activity — a game, a structured betting setup, a role-play, whatever works — that makes the self-locating character of Beauty's uncertainty inescapable. The students should have to act on their credences under conditions that mirror the problem: they don't know which awakening they're in, they face real (small) stakes, and the payoff structure forces them to confront the difference between betting at the start of the experiment and betting upon awakening. Ideally, the activity should surface the halfer–thirder divide naturally — different students should find themselves pulled in different directions by the logic of their own bets — and the debrief should map what happened onto the formal arguments: the Elga–Lewis dispute, the Dutch book and betting arguments, the analogy to the many-awakenings variant, and the question of whether self-locating evidence works like ordinary evidence at all.

Be specific about rules, materials, timing, and what the facilitator says and doesn't say at each stage. Include a debrief plan that walks through the moments in the activity where the key philosophical moves show up, flags what will predictably go wrong when I run it (and how to handle it), and is honest about which feature of the Sleeping Beauty problem the activity can't fully reproduce — the memory wipe is notoriously hard to simulate in a classroom, and I want to know what you'd do about that rather than pretending it's not an issue. The activity should take a clear pedagogical line: don't just lay out a menu of options, commit to a specific design and defend why it's structured the way it is.
