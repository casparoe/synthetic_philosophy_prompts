I teach philosophy and I keep hearing colleauges — and that one grad student who's read exactly enough Kant to be dangerous — drop "let justice be done though the heavens fall" like it's a trump card. Most recently at a workshop on the paradox of deontology: someone was pressing the standard line that if you really care about rights, you should minimize rights violations, so you should violate one person's rights if that prevents five other violations. The speaker just said "fiat justitia ruat caelum" and the room nodded. I want to push back but I realize I'm not sure what precise claim the slogan is actually making. Is it a metaphysical claim about the separateness of persons and the non-additivity of deontic reasons? Is it an agent-relative claim about what *I* may do versus what I should prevent? Is it about the expressive function of action? Or is it just a poetic way of refusing to play the consequentialist game? Lay out the distinct theses this slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, and say which ones are defensible, which are false, and which reading would actually do the work my colleague needed it to do in that argument. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then reason it out — no preamble, no restating. And mark clearly where you're taking a side in a live dispute versus reporting what's broadly settled among deontologists.
