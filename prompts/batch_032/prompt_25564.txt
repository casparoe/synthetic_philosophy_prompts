I've been reading about the laws of armed conflict and there's something about them that strikes me as philosophically strange, and I'm trying to work out exactly what view of moral responsibility they presuppose.

Here's the practice. Under international humanitarian law, the rules governing conduct in war — the prohibition on targeting civilians, the proportionality requirement, the ban on certain weapons — apply symmetrically to all combatants regardless of which side they're on. A soldier fighting for a state that launched a war of aggression has exactly the same legal right to kill enemy soldiers as a soldier defending against that aggression, provided both follow the same in-war rules. This is sometimes called the "moral equality of combatants" doctrine. At the same time, international law does distinguish between just and unjust wars at another level: the crime of aggression exists, and political and military leaders can be prosecuted for waging an unjust war. So the system as a whole says: the war itself may be criminal, and the leaders who started it may be guilty of a grave offense, but the ordinary soldiers who fight in it are legally permitted to kill, and their killings are not treated as murder — even when those killings serve the unjust cause.

I want to understand what this practice presupposes. It seems to rule out a straightforward view on which individual moral responsibility simply inherits from the justice of the collective project one serves. But it also doesn't seem to adopt a pure "combatants are just instruments" view either, because soldiers are held responsible for how they fight, just not for which side they fight on. So: what theory of moral responsibility — or of the relationship between individual and collective action — best makes sense of this practice? Does the practice strictly presuppose that theory, or is it compatible with several? And what would have to change in the laws of war if we replaced the presupposed theory with, say, the view that individual soldiers are morally responsible for the justice of the war they fight in, in addition to their conduct within it? Finally, I'd like you to flag where your reasoning is most vulnerable: which assumption, if dropped, would most change your analysis, and what alternative conclusion might then follow.
