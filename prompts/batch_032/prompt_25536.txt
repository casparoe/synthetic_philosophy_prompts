"Reconstruct Hume's case against the three traditional prohibitions on suicide — that it violates one's duty to God, one's duty to one's neighbors, and one's duty to oneself. Then identify what you take to be the strongest objection to Hume's argument and explain whether Hume has the resources to answer it."

[Student Answer]

Hume's essay challenges three pillars of the traditional prohibition on suicide: that it offends God, harms society, and betrays the self.

On the first, Hume argues that suicide cannot violate a duty to God because the existence of such a duty presupposes that God has issued a clear command against it, and no such command appears in natural reason. Since we cannot derive moral rules from observations of nature alone, the alleged divine prohibition lacks rational foundation. Hume thus treats the religious objection as resting on revelation, which he sets aside as outside the scope of philosophical argument.

On the second, Hume argues that suicide does not genuinely harm one's neighbors. A person who takes her own life withdraws whatever contribution she was making to society, but she does not actively injure anyone. In fact, if she has become a burden — through illness, incapacity, or dependence — her death may be a net benefit to those around her. Hume concludes that suicide cannot possibly violate a duty to others, because duties require the possibility of wronging someone, and no one is wronged by the mere withdrawal of a benefit they had no right to demand in the first place.

On the third, Hume argues that the duty to oneself is the weakest of the three. When life has become miserable — through incurable illness, chronic pain, or irreversible decline — continuing to live serves no rational purpose. Suicide in such circumstances is not a failure of self-regard but an expression of it: the agent judges, correctly, that her future contains more suffering than good and acts accordingly. Hume treats this as a straightforward application of his broader principle that reason is and ought to be the slave of the passions, since the aversion to pain is among our strongest passions.

The strongest objection to Hume's argument concerns his treatment of the duty to others. Hume assumes that the only way suicide could wrong one's neighbors is by actively harming them. But this misses a class of obligations we clearly recognize elsewhere: obligations of role, commitment, or reliance. A parent, for example, has duties to her children that are not exhausted by the question of whether her death would "harm" them in a narrow sense. Her children rely on her in ways that generate obligations she cannot simply opt out of. Hume's framework seems to have no room for this kind of obligation, and his reply — that the suicide's contribution to society is small — does not address it, because the objection is not about the magnitude of the contribution but about the structure of the obligation itself. I do not think Hume can answer this objection without abandoning his reduction of social duty to the avoidance of active harm.
