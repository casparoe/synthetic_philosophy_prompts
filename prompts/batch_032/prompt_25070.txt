I'm giving a talk at a workshop next month where I defend the view that Lewis's triviality results, while mathematically correct, do not force us to abandon the Equation — the thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability. My positive proposal is that indicative conditionals are not propositions in the classical sense: they don't carve out regions of logical space with truth conditions, but are instead devices for expressing conditional credences, roughly in the way that the suppositional theory (Adams, Edgington) treats them. The triviality proofs go through only if you assume conditionals have truth-conditional content that composes with the probability calculus in the standard Boolean way. Drop that assumption, and the results are correct but beside the point. I'll also argue that this non-propositional account handles embedding under negation and in disjunctions better than its critics claim, and that the apparent propositional surface grammar of conditionals is a pragmatic illusion.

The audience is about thirty people, and I need to get my Q&A prep right because they're not a uniform crowd. Roughly a third are formal epistemologists and philosophers of language who work directly on conditionals. Among them, one is a committed Stalnakerian who thinks possible-worlds semantics already handles everything the Equation wants and more; another favors a three-valued truth-table semantics and thinks non-propositional views are a counsel of despair; a third is sympathetic to the restrictor view and will want to know why my account is preferable to just saying that if-clauses restrict modal operators. Another third are philosophers of probability and Bayesian statisticians who find the Equation deeply intuitive — they've internalized the idea that P(if A then C) just is P(C|A) — and they want to know whether triviality is a genuine obstacle or a technical curiosity they can safely ignore. The remaining third are a mix: a computer scientist who works on probabilistic programming languages and cares about whether conditionals can be treated as random variables, a couple of graduate students who've just read the triviality literature for the first time and are full of energetic skepticism, and one senior philosopher who thinks the whole debate is a category mistake and will say so.
