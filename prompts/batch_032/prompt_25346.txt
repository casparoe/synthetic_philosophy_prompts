I'm a novelist stuck on a manuscript and I think my problem is actually philosophical. The book is near-future sci-fi. The central technology is a teleporter that scans you, disassembles you, transmits the data, and rebuilds you at the destination. It's been around for about fifteen years in the world of the novel, widely used, statistically safer than driving.

My protagonist is a man whose wife uses it to commute. He's not a philosopher, he's a structural engineer. But he's started to feel this quiet dread every time she steps into the booth. He can't shake the thought that the woman who walks out the other side isn't his wife — she's someone else with his wife's memories, and his actual wife died the first time she used it. He's smart enough to know this sounds crazy. He's also smart enough to notice that "sounds crazy" isn't an argument.

Here's where I'm stuck as a writer: I need this man's position to have real weight. I don't want him to be obviously wrong and I don't want his wife to be obviously right. But every time I try to write their arguments — the late-night kitchen conversations where this comes out — I realize I don't actually know what the strongest version of his worry is. Like, if she says "I have all my memories, my personality, my relationships — what's missing?" what does he say back that isn't just a feeling? And if he says "but the original body was destroyed," she can say "so what, my cells replace themselves constantly anyway."

I guess what I'm asking is: can you help me map out the strongest case for each side of this, not as a philosophy paper but as something two married people would actually say to each other at midnight? I want to understand what each of them has to concede and what each of them can hold onto, so the disagreement feels real and not like I'm using one character as a mouthpiece for the Correct View. Bonus if you can point me to the move that would genuinly shake each of them — the thing they haven't thought of that makes their own position harder to maintain.
