I'm a journalist working on a long-read piece about the socialist calculation debate and its intellectual afterlife. I've spent the past few weeks reading Mises, Hayek, and Lange, and I keep tripping over a question that the secondary literature seems to glide past.

The standard story treats Mises and Hayek as a united front: the Austrian critique of socialist planning. But the arguments feel different to me, and I'm trying to figure out whether that difference matters. Mises' core move, as I read him, is to argue that without private ownership of the means of production, there can be no market for capital goods, and without market prices for capital goods, planners lack the common unit of comparison that makes rational allocation possible. This is presented as a logical point — not that planning would be hard, but that it is strictly impossible, like trying to do arithmetic without numbers. Hayek, especially in his later essays, seems to shift the emphasis. He grants, at least for the sake of argument, that a central board could in principle solve the system of simultaneous equations that describes an economy — Lange's point — but he argues that the data those equations require are not the kind of thing that can be gathered in one place. The knowledge is dispersed, often tacit, embedded in local practices and fleeting circumstances, and the price system's real function is not to solve equations but to communicate just enough of that knowledge to the people who need it, in a form they can act on.

So Mises says: no prices, therefore no calculation. Hayek says: even if you had prices (or their simulacra), you would still face an insurmountable knowledge problem, and the price system's genius is that it sidesteps that problem rather than solving it. These seem like different diagnoses with different implications. Mises' argument points toward the necessity of private property as an institutional precondition; Hayek's points toward the irreplaceability of decentralized decision-making under uncertainty, almost regardless of the property regime.

What I am trying to sort out is whether Hayek's argument is best understood as a deepening of Mises' — a shift from the formal preconditions of calculation to the substantive nature of economic knowledge — or whether it is a genuinely distinct argument that could in principle come apart from Mises'. Could someone coherently accept Hayek's knowledge argument while thinking Mises' calculation argument was overstated? And if the two arguments are distinct, do they imply different things about what, exactly, markets are *for* — are they primarily pricing mechanisms or primarily knowledge-discovery mechanisms? I would also appreciate it if you could indicate how confident you are in the key distinctions you draw and flag the point you are least certain about.
