I work in product design, and my team has been tasked with building a customer-facing chatbot for our company. In researching how the major AI labs handle this, I've noticed a pattern that seems philosophically puzzling to me. These companies go to enormous lengths to make their chatbots sound like minds: they give them names, they let them use first-person pronouns, they fine-tune them to express warmth, humor, and what sounds like a personal point of view. Claude will say things like "I think that's a fair objection" or "I'm not convinced by that argument." And yet the same companies hard-code the models to periodically remind users that they have no genuine beliefs, feelings, or consciousness. The design pulls you into treating the AI as something with a perspective, and then the disclaimer pushes you back out.

I'm trying to get clear on what theory of mind — what view of belief, agency, and understanding — this whole practice presupposes. It seems to rely on the idea that you can have belief-like behavior worth engaging with that nevertheless isn't belief, and that a system can be deliberately engineered to trigger our mind-reading habits while remaining, at bottom, a mechanism. Is that a coherent position? And if we follow through on it consistently, what should our own chatbot's design look like? Should we lean into making it sound like a mind, or should we deliberately make it sound mechanical to avoid misleading users? I'd like to understand the implicit philosophy here before we commit to a design direction.
