I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been reading through a lot of online ethics discussions over the past year or so — mostly forums and some blog posts — and there's a recurring argument structure I keep running into that I can't find a clean name for. I'm hoping someone who knows the landscape can tell me whether this has been carved out as a distinct problem.

The structure is this. Someone holds a moral prohibition of the form "never do X" — lying, torture, killing an innocent person, breaking a promise. Then a scenario comes up where doing X exactly once would prevent several other agents from doing X. If the person sticks to the rule and refuses to do X, the net result is that X occurs more times in the world than if they had broken the rule once. The person acknowledges this arithmetic but treats it as beside the point: what matters, they say, is that *they* didn't do X, and the other agents bear the responsibility for their own actions. The fact that their refusal predictably leads to more of the very thing the rule was meant to prevent doesn't seem to register as a cost of following the rule.

I've seen this play out in several concrete debates. One was about torture: someone argued torture is absolutely impermissible, full stop. Another person raised a ticking-bomb case where torturing one suspect would prevent a terrorist cell from torturing and executing dozens of hostages. The first person responded that they still wouldn't torture — torture is intrinsically wrong — and that the terrorists' actions are on the terrorists, not on the person who could have stopped them. A structurally identical move came up in a discussion of Kant's essay on lying: refusing to lie to a murderer at the door about whether your friend is hiding in your house, with the result that the friend is killed. And again in a debate about whether a doctor may kill one healthy patient to harvest organs that would save five others. In each case, the rule-follower seems to treat the rule as binding regardless of how many violations of the same underlying value it permits or causes.

What puzzles me is that the rule is presumably motivated by a concern about X happening — we don't want people tortured, or lied to, or killed. But when rule-following produces more of exactly that outcome, the rule-follower treats this as irrelevant to the rule's justification. It looks like the rule has decoupled from its original purpose and become self-justifying, and yet the people making these arguments don't seem to experience this as a tension. I've stumbled across terms like "agent-relative constraints" and "side-constraints" while searching, but those seem to label the kind of rule being used, not the specific paradoxical structure where adhering to the constraint leads to more violations of whatever the constraint was supposed to protect. I'm less interested in getting a bare label and more interested in whether philosophers have carved this at the joints: do they see it as a genuine paradox, how do they distinguish it from nearby ideas (threshold deontology? the paradox of duty? something else?), and is there any consensus on what exactly generates the problem and whether it can be resolved without abandoning the rule?
