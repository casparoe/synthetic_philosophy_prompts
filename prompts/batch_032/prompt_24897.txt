I'm teaching a unit on Molyneux's problem in a philosophy of perception course, and I'd like to use Raymond Carver's short story "Cathedral" as a literary companion text. Molyneux's problem asks whether a person born blind who can distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch would, upon gaining sight, recognize which is which by vision alone, before touching them. The problem has structured centuries of debate between empiricists and rationalists about whether spatial concepts are amodal or tied to specific sensory modalities, and about what kind of unity perception has across the senses.

Carver's story inverts the scenario. A sighted narrator, emotionally shut down and uneasy around his wife's blind friend Robert, spends an evening with the man. In the climactic scene, Robert has the narrator close his eyes and draw a cathedral on a paper bag, Robert's hand resting on the narrator's to follow the drawing. With his eyes closed, the narrator has something like a perceptual awakening — he feels he is inside the experience in a way he wasn't before, and when Robert tells him to open his eyes, he keeps them shut.

I want to give my students a reading that takes both the story and the philosophical problem seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Here is what I'm hoping you can help me think through.

First, does the story challenge an assumption that Molyneux's problem takes for granted — namely, that vision is the modality whose recovery matters, and that the interesting question is whether the blind can catch up to the sighted? Carver seems to suggest that the narrator learns something about perception precisely by giving up sight, guided by someone who has never had it. What would Locke, or a contemporary empiricist about Molyneux's problem, say in response? Does the story show something the philosophical debate misses about the relationship between sensory modalities, or does it trade on a confusion between perceptual recognition and something else — emotional openness, aesthetic attention, or social connection?

Second, the narrator's epiphany is not about recognizing shapes cross-modally; it is about discovering that tactile and kinesthetic experience can be richly meaningful on its own terms. Is this a point about perception that bears on Molyneux's problem, or is it a point about something else entirely — about attention, about intimacy, about what it takes to really see another person? I want my students to see where the philosophical lens illuminates the story and where the story resists the lens, and to come away with a sharper sense of what Molyneux's problem is actually about and what it leaves out.

Third, if we read the story as dramatizing a kind of answer to an inverted Molyneux's problem — can a sighted person, by closing his eyes and attending to touch, come to understand something about blind perceptual experience? — what does the story's answer imply for the original problem? Does it suggest that the empiricist answer to Molyneux's problem (no cross-modal transfer without learned association) is right but that the framing is wrong, because the real question is not about transfer but about what each modality makes possible? Or does the story's emotional resolution depend on the narrator already having a lifetime of visual experience to draw on, so that the inversion is not symmetrical and the story cannot speak directly to the original problem?

I am not looking for a thesis I can hand to students; I am looking for a nuanced critical reading that helps me teach the story and the problem together, that flags where the analogy holds and where it breaks down, and that says honestly what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not capture.
