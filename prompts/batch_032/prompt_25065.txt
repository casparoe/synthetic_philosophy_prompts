I'm trying to map out the theoretical landscape for metaphysical grounding before I take a position, and I want the full specification of what any adequate theory has to deliver. Write the desiderata for a theory of grounding — the phenomena it must capture, the cases it must get right, the formal constraints it must satisfy or explain away, and the distinctions it must not collapse. For each requirement, give the case or argument that motivates it.

Make sure to cover at least the following territory, though don't feel limited to it: the relationship between grounding and explanation (is grounding itself explanatory, or does it merely back explanation?); the formal properties (irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, well-foundedness) and which are up for grabs; hyperintensionality (why grounding can't just be necessary entailment); whether grounding is a single relation or a family of them; what grounds the grounding facts themselves, if anything; and the standard test cases — conjunctions grounded in their conjuncts, disjunctions grounded in their true disjuncts, determinates grounded in determinables (or the reverse), existentials grounded in their instances.

I especially want to know which desiderata are non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of only one camp. Flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied — I've heard there's trouble combining irreflexivity, transitivity, and the view that grounding facts are always themselves grounded, and if that's right I want the tension laid out clearly. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to narrow the field: which one, if you insist on it, rules out the largest number of otherwise viable theories, and why.
