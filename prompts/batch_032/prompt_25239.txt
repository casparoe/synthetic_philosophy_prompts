I'm on the board of the Longworth Foundation, a smallish philanthropy that funds research on global catastrophic risks. We're meeting next Tuesday to decide on a grant proposal that's got the board split right down the middle.

The proposal is from a team at a well-regarded university who want to develop and open-source a tool for detecting certain signatures of deceptive alignment in large language models. The idea is that if the community has this tool, anyone can run it against a model before deployment and catch problems early. So far so good.

Here's the problem. Several of us are worried that if this tool gets built and released, it'll also help adverserial actors train models that evade detection — the tool basically draws a target on the wall and says "here's what we're looking for, now train around it." And the real kicker is we've heard, through backchannels, that at least two other foundations are considering funding very similar projects. So we're staring at what some of my colleagues call a unilateralist's curse situation: if exactly one funder backs this, the tool gets built, the community benefits, and the adversarial risk is manageable because only one version exists. But if three different teams build three different detection tools and release them all, the adversaries get a comprehensive map of the detection landscape and the whole thing backfires. Each foundation is making its decision independently, and nobody wants to coordinate — antitrust concerns, plus some of the other foundations are overseas and coordination would be legally dicey.

I need to circulate a short memo before Tuesday's meeting recommending what we should do. The options on the table are: (a) fund the proposal as submitted, (b) fund it but require the team to coordinate with the other groups before releasing anything, (c) reject the proposal and write to the other foundations explaining why, or (d) something else I haven't thought of. The memo should have a clear recommendation and should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the other side — half my board thinks the unilateralist's curse reasoning is clever but practically paranoid, and they'll need to be convinced.

Please draft the memo. Write in the style of an experienced policy analyst who's familiar with the unilateralist's curse literature but knows the board wants practical guidance, not a seminar. Think very carefully about what actually follows from the curse structure here — don't just name-drop it and move on.
