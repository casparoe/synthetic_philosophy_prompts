I spent forty years in experimental physics before retiring, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest. This past month I worked through Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that in *The Concept of Mind*, and then the Stanley and Williamson paper that argues knowing-how reduces to a species of knowing-that, and the back-and-forth has been rattling around in my head ever since. Then, by coincidence, my grandson insisted I watch *The Karate Kid* with him — the 1984 one — and I found I could not stop seeing the film as a kind of philosophical argument staged in a dojo.

The central pedagogical relationship is built on a remarkably clean separation between the two kinds of knowledge: Mr. Miyagi teaches Daniel the physical motions of blocking — wax on, wax off, paint the fence, sand the floor — without telling him what those motions are for. Daniel's body comes to execute the blocks reliably, but he does not know *that* they are blocks, and his frustration at not knowing that seems to matter to the film. When Miyagi finally reveals the connection and throws punches at Daniel, Daniel blocks them successfully, apparently demonstrating that his knowing-how was genuine and independent of any propositional knowledge about karate. On its face, this looks like a vindication of Ryle.

I am preparing a short talk for a philosophy discussion group at my local library, and I want to use the film to think through the intellectualism debate. My question is this: does the film actually vindicate Ryle's anti-intellectualism, or does it complicate it in ways the philosophical literature has not fully captured? I am struck by three things in particular. First, before the revelation, Daniel's motions are rote and mechanical — does this really count as knowing how to block, or is it mere conditioning of the sort a laboratory animal might undergo, and does the intellectualist have resources to say why the latter is not know-how? Second, after Miyagi tells him what the motions are, Daniel's blocking seems to *improve* — he becomes more fluid, more adaptive to Miyagi's varying attacks — which suggests that acquiring the relevant propositional knowledge did something substantive for his knowing-how, not merely for his peace of mind. Third, the film's climax at the All-Valley Tournament has Daniel fighting injured and deploying the crane kick, a technique he had struggled to master in practice; the resolution seems to depend on something like confidence or focused presence, which does not reduce neatly to either knowing-how or knowing-that and may point to a third element the debate overlooks. I would like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously, and that is willing to say where the film's implicit argument is genuinely illuminating and where it trades on ambiguities the literature has already clarified — or, conversely, where the film dramatizes something the papers miss.
