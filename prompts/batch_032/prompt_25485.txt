I'm a high school junior and I've been asked to lead a twenty-five-minute discussion on the Ship of Theseus for our philosophy club next week. The other students are bright but haven't studied any philosophy formally — they'll be coming in cold. I've read a few explanations online and I think I understand the puzzle myself, but I have no idea how to structure the session so it actually lands. I don't need someone to explain the paradox to me; I need a teaching plan.

Specifically: what has to be established first, before I even mention the ship, so that the question feels genuine rather than like a word game? What's the one version of the example I should build the whole discussion around, and what features does it need to have — and what should I deliberately leave out of it? I'm worried about misconceptions. I expect some students will say "it's the same ship because we call it the same ship" and think that closes the matter. Others will probably treat it as a practical question about legal ownership or registration. I also suspect someone will propose "it's the same if most of the original material is still there" and not see why that just pushes the problem back a step. At what point in the sequence should I anticipate and defuse each of these? Finally, what's one question I can ask near the end that will tell me whether they've genuinely grasped the philosophical problem — as opposed to just being able to retell the story? And is there anything I should openly refuse to settle, so they don't walk away thinking philosophy is about finding the one right answer?
