Here's basically what the post said, paraphrased from memory:

Newcomb's problem is this thought experiment where a superintelligent Predictor who can see the future puts two boxes in front of you. Box A is transparent and always has $1,000. Box B is opaque and contains either $1 million or nothing. The Predictor has already made its prediction about what you'll do. If it predicted you'll take both boxes, it left B empty. If it predicted you'll take only B, it put the million in. The Predictor is basically never wrong. The paradox is there are two ways to reason about it. One-boxers say take only B, because the Predictor almost certainly foresaw that and you'll get a million; if you take both, the Predictor foresaw that too and you'll get just $1,000. Two-boxers say that by the time you're standing there, the money is already set — your choice can't retroactively change what's in B — so you should take both boxes no matter what, since $1,001,000 > $1,000,000 and $1,000 > $0. The post then claimed that philosophers have mostly settled on two-boxing as correct because dominance reasoning is airtight, and that the real upshot of the paradox is that it exposes a problem about free will: if the Predictor can foresee your choice, your choice wasn't free to begin with.
