I've been trying to work out what I think about a question that's been bothering me: how much of my origin is essential to my existence — that is, which changes to the circumstances of my conception would mean the resulting person wasn't me? I went through a series of cases and recorded my intuitive verdicts, and now I'm trying to figure out whether there's a coherent principle behind them. Here they are:

1. If my parents had conceived a child a month earlier than they actually did, that child would not have been me. A different egg and a different sperm were involved; that child would have been a sibling, not me.

2. If the very same sperm and egg that produced me had fused a few seconds later than they actually did, the resulting person would still have been me. A trivial difference in timing doesn't seem like it could make me a different person.

3. If the zygote that became me had split into identical twins, neither twin would have been me. I would simply not have existed; two other people would have existed instead. This feels right even though I'm not sure I can articulate what distinguishes this case from case 2.

4. If the embryo that became me had been gestated by a surrogate rather than by my biological mother, the resulting person would still have been me. The womb doesn't seem essential to who I am.

5. If a scientist had sequenced my genome and synthesized an embryo with identical DNA from scratch in a lab, the resulting person would not have been me — even if they were then raised in exactly my circumstances. My origin is tied to a particular reproductive history, not just a genetic sequence.

6. If my parents had used IVF, and the very embryo that became me had been the one implanted and carried to term, the resulting person would have been me. But if a different embryo from the same batch had been implanted instead, that person would not have been me. The particular embryo matters, not just the fact that it came from my parents' gametes.

7. Here's the case I can't settle: suppose the same sperm and egg that produced me had fused, but the resulting zygote had been cryopreserved for twenty years and only then implanted and carried to term. Would that person have been me? My gut says yes — a delay, even a long one, shouldn't matter if a few seconds doesn't. But then I worry about consistency: if a delay doesn't matter, why does the splitting in case 3 matter? In both cases the zygote's trajectory diverges from what actually happened; I need a principle that treats delay and splitting differently, and I'm not sure what that principle would be.

Is there a single principle — or a small set of principles — that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about the cryopreservation case? And if no principle can accommodate all seven without becoming ad hoc, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why?
