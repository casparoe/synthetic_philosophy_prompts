I'm a quantitative trader; I've spent fifteen years in options market-making. My working vocabulary is built around bid-ask spreads, inventory risk, adverse selection, delta hedging, no-arbitrage bounds, and the difference between a quoted price and a fair value. I have no formal training in philosophy, but a colleague recently mentioned that philosophers have something called Dutch book arguments, which are supposed to show that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the probability axioms — and, in a separate move, that rational updating must follow conditionalization — on pain of being vulnerable to a set of bets that guarantees a sure loss. "Sure-loss set of bets" sounds to me like it belongs to the same family of ideas as arbitrage, so I want to know how much of the argument survives when I force it through the conceptual machinery I actually use.

Restate the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization, in the vocabulary of market microstructure and quantitative trading, as precisely and honestly as you can. Do not smuggle market-making assumptions into the translation and then declare the original argument defective on that basis; give the argument its best version first, then map it. After the translation, give me the residue report. Specifically: which parts of the argument map cleanly onto concepts I work with daily (arbitrage, fair pricing, no-arbitrage bounds, the way a market maker updates quotes after a trade), which parts you had to idealize or distort to make the translation go through, and which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. Then tell me whether the residue points to something the philosophical argument genuinely depends on that my framework cannot express — and, if so, whether that dependence is a weakness of the argument or a limitation of my framework. I want to know whether the normative force of Dutch book arguments is genuinely arbitrage-theoretic, in which case I should find them compelling, or whether it depends on assumptions about betting dispositions that no actual liquidity provider would grant, in which case I want to understand whether those assumptions are defensible idealizations or whether they quietly beg the question.
