Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science who meet at a conference reception and discover they disagree about inference to the best explanation. One holds that explanatory reasoning is a fundamental, irreducible form of inference: when we judge that one hypothesis explains the evidence better than another, we have a direct rational license to infer that it is more likely to be true, and no amount of Bayesian machinery can replicate this license without presupposing it. The other holds that inference to the best explanation is nothing over and above Bayesian updating: explanatory virtues like simplicity, scope, and coherence are heuristics that track posterior probability, and any putatively explanatory inference can be reconstructed as an application of Bayes's rule with appropriate priors and likelihoods. The irreducibilist should press the Bayesian with curve-fitting cases: two equations fit the same data perfectly, one simple and one wildly complex, and we all infer the simple one — yet the likelihoods are identical, so the Bayesian must encode the difference in the priors, which looks like dressing an explanatory judgment in probabilistic clothing. The Bayesian should press back with diagnostic reasoning: a doctor who infers strep throat because it best explains the symptoms is manifestly doing Bayesian inference with base rates and conditional probabilities, and calling it "explanation" adds no epistemic work. Each must respond to the other's central example rather than pivoting, and each must clarify what they take "explanation" to pick out. By the end, the dialogue should reveal whether their disagreement is substantive — about what rationality itself demands — or whether they are talking past each other with different conceptions of explanation.
