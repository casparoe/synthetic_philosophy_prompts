The debate about the analytic–synthetic distinction has been running since at least the 1950s, and while the literature is vast, it is not obvious that the central disagreement has been resolved — or even that the participants agree on what they are disagreeing about. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" attacked the distinction as an unempirical dogma; Carnap, and later Grice and Strawson, pushed back by arguing that Quine had mischaracterized what the distinction was supposed to do; Kripke's introduction of the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori reshuffled the terrain by showing that analyticity and necessity come apart; and more recently, neo-Carnapian, inferentialist, and cognitive-scientific approaches have tried to rehabilitate something like the distinction under different descriptions — as a matter of inferential role, of conceptual possession conditions, or of psychological primitives. And yet, decades later, it is hard to point to a settled consensus on whether there is a principled analytic–synthetic distinction, what it would be a distinction between, or whether Quine's arguments actually succeeded against the targets he named.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a verbal dispute about how to use the words "analytic" and "synthetic"? A substantive disagreement about the structure of language or knowledge that could in principle be settled by further philosophical or empirical work? A clash of methodological commitments — about what counts as an explanation, about the role of idealization in semantics, about whether semantics should be answerable to epistemology — that makes the two sides effectively incommensurable? Or is it several different disagreements that got lumped together under a single heading, such that progress requires disentangling them rather than resolving any one of them?

In your diagnosis, engage with what at least some of the major parties actually say — you need not survey everyone, but ground the diagnosis in specific moves and commitments that appear in the debate. And then tell me: given your diagnosis, what (if anything) would count as progress? A new distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding from linguistics or psychology, a reframing of the question — or should we conclude that the question was ill-posed from the start and move on? If you think the impasse is permanent, say why no conceivable development would break it.
