So I'm in this philosophy of science course and we spent like three weeks on the evolutionary psychology wars. You know the drill: critics say evo psych is just reverse-engineering just-so stories — for any trait you find, you can tell a plausible adaptive story about the EEA, but that doesn't make it true. Defenders say that's a caricature, they do make testable predictions and test them, and anyway every historical science from geology to cosmology does the same kind of inference to the best explanation.

Here's what's bugging me: this debate has been running since the 90s and as far as I can tell, nobody has changed their mind about anything. Both sides seem to agree on what good science requires — falsifiability, evidence, not just plausibility. And yet they look at the same body of work and one side sees rigorous science and the other sees storytelling. My hunch is that the real disagreement isn't about methodology at all but about something else — maybe what counts as a satisfying explanation, or maybe prior commitments about human nature, or maybe whether adaptationism is a reasonable default. But I'm not confident about that.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a factual dispute about how much evo psych research actually tests its hypotheses versus just tells stories? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence — like, how much support you need before an adaptive hypothesis counts as established? Is it a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is it partly verbal — are they using "adaptation" or "test" differently? And crucially: given your diagnosis, what would actually move this debate? A better methodology? A different question? Or is permanent stalemate the expected outcome?
