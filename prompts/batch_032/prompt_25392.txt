I am a machine learning researcher working on large language model evaluation, alignment, and interpretability. My working vocabulary includes behavioral evaluation, mechanistic interpretability, RLHF, reward modeling, the distinction between what a model outputs and what it internally represents, and the intentional stance we inevitably adopt when we talk about models as having beliefs, goals, or world-models. I have no formal background in philosophy of mind.

I have encountered the claim that Daniel Dennett's method of heterophenomenology is an important proposal for how to study consciousness scientifically, and from what I can gather it sounds suspiciously like what my field already does when we study language models: we collect their outputs, we treat those outputs as data to be explained rather than as transparent windows into an inner reality, we construct a picture of what the system seems to believe on the basis of those outputs, and we then try to explain why it produces those outputs in terms of its underlying mechanisms. If a language model says it sees a red triangle, we do not take that as evidence that it has qualia; we take it as a text string whose production we need to explain mechanistically. This appears to be exactly what heterophenomenology recommends for human subjects.

Please restate heterophenomenology — as precisely as you can — in the vocabulary I actually use: model outputs, behavioral evaluation, mechanistic explanation, imputed world-models, and the intentional stance toward artificial systems. I want a systematic translation, not an analogy. For each core commitment of the method, tell me what it maps onto in my framework and whether the mapping is exact or approximate.

Then give me the residue report. In particular:

Is there anything in heterophenomenology that my framework simply cannot say — and if so, is that because my framework is missing a genuine distinction (for instance, something about the first-person perspective that does not reduce to the relationship between outputs and mechanisms) or because heterophenomenology is drawing a distinction that collapses once you try to operationalize it?

Does this mean we have already absorbed the methodological lesson and the philosophers are simply catching up, or does heterophenomenology contain a further claim — about consciousness itself, not just about scientific method — that we have not yet engaged with and that my vocabulary may be unable to express?
