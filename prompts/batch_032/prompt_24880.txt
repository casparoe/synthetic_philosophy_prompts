I'm teaching an introductory ethics module on virtue ethics and the situationist critique, and I plan to use William Golding's Lord of the Flies as an extended case study. The situationist challenge—drawing on Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, Milgram's obedience experiments, and the broader claim that situational factors swamp putatively stable character traits in predicting behavior—threatens virtue ethics at its foundation: if there are no robust dispositions, talk of virtues and vices as explanatory categories looks hollow. The novel seems to dramatize precisely this. A group of English schoolboys, presumably the products of ordinary moral education, descend rapidly into cruelty and tribalism once the structures of civilization are removed. Yet the novel also gives us Piggy and Simon, who appear to resist the situational pull, and Ralph, who wavers between decency and complicity.

I want a reading of the novel through this philosophical lens that goes beyond treating it as a mere illustration. Please address three questions. First, does the novel ultimately vindicate the situationist view that character collapses under situational pressure, or does it preserve space for something like stable virtue? Second, how might a virtue ethicist respond—by arguing the boys never possessed genuine virtue in the first place, or by granting that virtue requires favorable external conditions to manifest? Third, identify a scene or character arc that complicates both the situationist reading and the virtue-ethical reply, and say what that complication adds to the philosophical debate that the empirical studies alone don't capture. Keep the response to 500 words.
