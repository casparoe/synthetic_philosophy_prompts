I've been reading about this thing called the problem of old evidence in Bayesian thinking. From what I gather, the issue is: if you already know some fact, then according to Bayes' rule it can't really count as evidence for a new theory, because the probability of something you already know is 1, and multiplying by 1 doesn't change anything. But that seems crazy — like when Einstein came up with general relativity, one of the big things in its favor was that it explained the weird precession of Mercury's orbit, which astronomers had known about for decades. Everyone treated that as evidence for the theory, and it seems like they were right to. So my question is: what are the most important implications of this problem for how an ordinary person should evaluate theories they come across? Like if I'm reading about some new idea that claims to explain something we already knew, should I be more skeptical of it compared to a theory that predicted something genuinely surprising? And does this problem mean there's something wrong with how we normally think about evidence, or is it more of a technical glitch in the math that doesn't really matter for everyday reasoning?
