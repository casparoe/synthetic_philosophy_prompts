I am presenting a paper next month to a department colloquium at a university with a strong philosophy of religion cluster and several early modern specialists. My thesis is that the core of Hume's argument against belief in miracles on the basis of testimony can be detached from his broader empiricist epistemology and reconstructed as a probabilistic argument whose force does not depend on accepting Hume's more general skeptical or naturalistic commitments. Specifically, I argue that the maxim Hume proposes — that no testimony suffices to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it endeavors to establish — can be formulated in Bayesian terms, and that so formulated it places a rational constraint on miracle-belief that even a theist who rejects Hume's theory of ideas, his critique of causation, and his inductive skepticism ought to acknowledge. I will grant, for the sake of argument, that miracles are metaphysically possible and that divine action could in principle override natural regularities; my claim is only that testimonial evidence for any particular miracle will, given the structure of the case, almost always be epistemically insufficient.

The audience will include: two faculty who have published on Hume's philosophy of religion, one of whom regards Section X of the Enquiry as the weakest part of Hume's corpus and the other who defends it as his most durable contribution; three philosophers of religion, at least one of whom is a practicing Christian who has written on the epistemology of religious belief and Reformed epistemology in particular; a historian of probability who works on the early modern period; and several graduate students working on early modern philosophy or philosophical theology. I expect the room to be broadly sympathetic to theistic belief but professionally committed to philosophical rigor.
