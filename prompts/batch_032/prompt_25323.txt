so i'm running this workshop for the local rationalist meetup — it's about 18 people, mix of software engineers, a couple data scientists, one or two philosophy grad students who read lesswrong, 90 minutes total. the topic is algorithmic fairness and i want to do an activity where they actually *feel* the impossibility results, not just hear about them and go "huh, neat theorem."

here's what i'm picturing: give them a concrete decision scenario — maybe loan approvals or pretrial release — with actual numbers on cards or a handout, two groups with different base rates, and tell them to design a decision rule that's "fair." every time they satisfy one fairness intuition (say, equal error rates across groups) they break another one (say, calibration). i want them to discover the tension themselves before i ever name the formal criteria.

can you design the full thing? i need:
- the scenario and materials (what numbers, what format — cards, worksheet, spreadsheet?)
- the rules and structure (groups? pairs? whole-room?)
- the sequence of prompts or constraints i introduce, and in what order, so they don't just stare at the data confused
- a debrief that maps what happened to the formal results — calibration, equalized odds, statistical parity, the Kleinberg et al. / Chouldechova impossibility theorem, etc.
- what predictably goes wrong: e.g. one guy who knows the literature spoiling it for his group, or groups trying to redefine the problem ("well we should collect more features") rather than engaging with the tradeoff, or people getting stuck on the idea that the data itself might biased and refusing to proceed
- and an honest note on what the activity *can't* capture — e.g. the proofs themselves, or the fact that real-world deployments involve additional complications like feedback loops

the goal is that by the end they understand the impossibility isn't some technical edge case but a deep conceptual tension, and that "make the algorithm fair" is not a coherent instruction you can just hand to an engineer.
