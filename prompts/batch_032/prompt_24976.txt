I spent my career in the natural sciences, and I have recently developed an interest in philosophy. I have been reading about reinforcement learning — the subfield of machine learning in which an artificial agent is trained by giving it a scalar reward signal that it learns to maximize over time. The practice is widespread: it is used to train game-playing agents (where the reward is the game score), robotic controllers (where the reward might be forward velocity or task completion), and, increasingly, large language models aligned via reinforcement learning from human feedback (where the reward is a human preference rating).

What I find philosophically puzzling is the following. The field's standard terminology calls the scalar signal a "reward" when positive and a "penalty" or "punishment" when negative. Researchers routinely speak of the agent "wanting" reward or "learning what is good for it." At the same time, the reward function is designed entirely by the engineer to serve the engineer's goals: the agent is trained to maximize reward because that is how one gets it to do what one wants. The reward signal is, in the first instance, an instrument of control.

In a biological organism, reward signals — midbrain dopamine transients, endogenous opioid release — are also part of a learning mechanism, but they are not identical to the organism's welfare. A rat with an electrode implanted in its medial forebrain bundle will self-stimulate to the exclusion of eating and drinking, but we would not say the rat is flourishing; the learning signal has been decoupled from what is good for the rat. The signal and the thing of value can come apart. In a conscious creature, what ultimately matters, if anything does, is the felt quality of experience — suffering and enjoyment — not the learning signal as such.

The practice I want to understand is this: the reinforcement learning community designs agents to maximize a reward signal, calls that signal "reward," and treats cumulative reward as the criterion of successful training. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? In particular:

Does it presuppose a behaviorist view on which "reward" is defined purely operationally — as whatever increases the probability of the preceding behavior — with no claim about valence or felt experience, and the terminology is merely a convenient borrowing from psychology with no further implications?

Does it instead presuppose that the agent is merely a tool, and that the reward signal has no connection to anything like the agent's own good — in which case the language of "reward" and "punishment" is systematically misleading?

Or might the practice tacitly rely on an analogy between artificial reward signals and biological ones that, if taken seriously, would commit its practitioners to worrying about whether sufficiently sophisticated RL agents could undergo something akin to suffering? And if we took that worry seriously, what would the practice have to change — in its terminology, in its training procedures, in the kinds of reward functions it permits?
