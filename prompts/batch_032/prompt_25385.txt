So I'm a philosophy professor — epistemology, mostly, with some philosophy of mind on the side — and I've found myself in a situation that's making me feel like a bit of a hypocrite. For the last year or so I've been using one of the major LLMs pretty heavily in my workflow. At first it was just, you know, "summarize this paper I don't have time to read properly" or "give me five possible objections to this argument I'm drafting." But it's crept. Now I'm having it generate full lecture notes, draft referee reports, even sketch out entire paper outlines that I then fill in and polish. And here's what's bothering me: my output is objectively better than it was two years ago. I'm more prolific, my arguments are tighter, I'm catching objections I would have missed. But I have this nagging sense that I understand less than I used to. The other day a grad student asked me a follow-up about a move in one of my recent papers — something the model had originally suggested as a reply to an objection — and I realized I couldn't really explain why it worked. I'd endorsed it, I'd put my name on it, but I didn't fully grasp it. It was like the understanding was located somewhere outside me and I was just... pointing at it.

So here's my question, and I'd love a properly philosophical take on this rather then generic advice: is there a principled distinction between using a cognitive tool that genuinely extends your capacities and using one that replaces them in a way that leaves you diminished? And more practically — should I stop, scale back, or am I just being a luddite about this? My colleague in the sciences laughed and said this is exactly what people said about calculators in math class, and that I'd get over it. But that feels wrong to me and I can't quite articulate why. If there's a difference between a calculator and what I'm doing, what is it?
