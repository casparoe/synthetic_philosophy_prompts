I've been covering the immigration debate for years, and one argument I keep running into at policy conferences and in op-ed pages goes like this: the developed nations are lifeboats floating on a sea of global poverty. Each lifeboat has a carrying capacity — roughly, the resources, infrastructure, and social cohesion needed to maintain a decent standard of living for those already aboard. The developing world's population are swimmers in the water, and if the lifeboats take everyone aboard, they capsize and everyone drowns. So the only ethical course is to keep most swimmers out, while perhaps tossing some supplies over the side. The person making this argument — typically an economist from a libertarian or restrictionist think tank — then concludes that restrictive immigration policies aren't cruel; they're the only responsible way to prevent a collapse that would harm everyone, current residents and would-be migrants alike.

I recognize the debt to Garrett Hardin's 1974 lifeboat ethics essay, but the version I hear now is usually stripped of Hardin's more aggressive Malthusian framing and presented as common-sense triage. I'm trying to figure out how much work the lifeboat metaphor is actually doing here. It seems to import a lot of assumptions that don't announce themselves: that nations are discrete vessels with fixed capacities, that would-be migrants are uniformly desperate swimmers with nothing to contribute, that the choice is binary — take everyone or take no one — and that the only relevant moral consideration is avoiding the worst collective outcome. But I don't trust my own suspicions enough to dismiss the argument outright, and I've seen the analogy persuade thoughtful people who aren't reflexively anti-immigrant.

I'd like a systematic audit of the analogy: what maps onto what in the real world, where the mapping breaks, which features of the lifeboat scenario the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, and whether the policy conclusion — that restrictive immigration is ethically mandatory — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. I'm especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the intended use and which are merely cosmetic. If there's a better analogy that captures what's actually at stake in immigration policy without smuggling in a verdict, I'd want to know that too.
