I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I recently got cornered at a party by a friend who does evolutionary biology, and she started telling me about this debate in the philosophy of biology that's apparently been running for decades. The question is what it *means* to say that a biological trait has a "function." On one side you've got the etiological account: a trait's function is what it was selected for — what it did in the ancestral environment that caused it to be preserved by natural selection. The heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is what hearts did that made organisms with hearts out-reproduce organisms without them. On the other side you've got the causal-role account: a trait's function is whatever it actually does now, in the organism's current organization, that contributes to some larger systemic capacity we're trying to explain. The heart's function is to pump blood because in the current circulatory system, that's the causal contribution it makes — never mind the evolutionary backstory. My friend said the two accounts give different answers in edge cases (vestigial traits, exaptations, traits that were selected for one thing but now do another), and philosophers have been arguing about which account is "right" for ages.

I nodded along, but in my head I was doing what I always do: translating into code. A function in a codebase has a name, a signature, maybe a docstring, and some intended purpose — that's the etiological side: what the developer *meant* it to do, what the spec promised, what the commit message from three years ago says. But it also has actual runtime behavior — what it really does when executed, what downstream effects it has on the rest of the system, what you'd discover if you traced every call site and every side effect. That's the causal-role side. Sometimes these align; sometimes they don't. You get bugs where the function doesn't do what it was "selected for." You get bitrot where the system around a function changes so that its causal role drifts away from its original purpose. You get functions that were written to do X but turn out to be load-bearing for Y, and no one knows until they try to refactor. You get dead code that still technically has causal effects (it runs, it allocates, it returns) but contributes nothing anyone cares about — vestigial functions. You get functions whose original purpose is lost to history because the developer left and the commit messages are garbage, and all you have is the current behavior to go on. So here's what I want: do a systematic, honest translation of the etiological vs. causal-role debate about biological functions into the vocabulary of software engineering. Use whatever concepts you need — functions, methods, specs, tests, refactoring, technical debt, runtime behavior, static analysis, call graphs, deprecation, backwards compatibility, whatever. Restate the philosophical positions, the standard arguments, and the counterarguments in software terms as faithfully as you can. Don't just draw a loose analogy and call it a day; I want to see how far the mapping actually goes.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto software concepts, and which parts had to be distorted or couldn't be expressed at all? I'm especially curious about a few pressure points. One: biological functions have no author — no one sat down and wrote a spec for the heart. Natural selection isn't an agent with intentions, so the "etiological" story in biology is historical and population-level in a way that a developer's intent is not. Two: in biology, the "specification" and the "implementation" aren't separate documents — the only record of what a trait was "supposed" to do is the trait itself and its selective history. Three: biological systems have no clean distinction between a function's interface and its implementation; everything leaks. Are these gaps just limitations of my software vocabulary, or do they point to something the philosophical debate itself is fuzzy about? Take a definite position — don't just lay out interpretive options and leave me to pick. I want to know whether my instinct that this whole debate reduces to "intent vs. behavior" is basically right, or whether the biologists and philosophers are tracking something my framework genuinely cannot see.
