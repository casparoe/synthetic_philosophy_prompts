So I've been going down this rabbithole with AI safety stuff lately — not my day job but I read papers on the weekend — and I got stuck on this thing about "low-impact AI" or "impact measures." The basic idea is you want to build an AI that does what you ask but doesn't cause big side effects. Like, if you tell it "bring me a coffee" it shouldn't dismantle the solar system to do it. People talk about defining a baseline (what would have happened if the AI hadn't acted) and then penalizing divergence from that baseline.

But I started sketching out an argument and now I can't tell if it's profound or stupid. Here it is:

(1) To decide whether some change in the world counts as a "side effect" rather than just part of doing the task, you need to know which features of the world matter and which don't. If I ask you to clean my desk and you throw away a winning lottery ticket, that's a side effect; if you throw away a candy wrapper, it's the task. The difference is about value.

(2) Knowing which features matter, in general, requires a specification of what humans value — not just a few examples but the whole thing, because any feature you leave out could be the one that distinguishes a side effect from an intended effect in some scenario you haven't thought of yet.

(3) We don't have a full specification of human values, and getting one is basically the hard problem of AI alignment.

(4) So "avoid side effects" can't be defined without solving the whole alignment problem first. Impact measures don't give you a shortcut; they just smuggle the value judgment into the choice of baseline and the choice of which divergences to penalize.

(5) But here's the thing: humans follow instructions like "tidy up this room but don't touch anything on the desk" all the time, and we don't have a full specification of each other's values. My roommate can clean the kitchen without me having to specify that he shouldn't set the apartment on fire as a "side effect" of taking out the trash. So clearly something like "avoid side effects" is operationalizable without a complete value specification.

(6) So either (1)–(4) is wrong somewhere, or human commonsense reasoning about side effects is doing something the argument says is impossible — in which case the argument is missing something important about how we actually carve up intended vs. side effects.

I keep going back and forth on which premise to reject. Part of me thinks (2) is too strong — maybe you only need a partial specification, or maybe you can learn the distinction from examples without ever representing values explicitly. But then I worry that's just kicking the can: if your training data encodes implicit value judgments, haven't you just moved the full specification into the data? Another part of me thinks the whole framing is wrong and "side effect" isn't a value concept at all, it's a causal one — a side effect is just an effect that wasn't part of the plan, regardless of whether it's good or bad. But that can't be right either, cause we don't call it a "side effect" when the AI does something extra that happens to be wonderful; we just call that a bonus.

Anyway, I'd love someone to walk through this argument and tell me which step breaks and what it costs to break it. I'm especially interested in whether the thing humans do when they understand "don't cause side effects" is fundamentally different from what the impact-measures people are trying to formalize, or whether it's the same thing and the argument just has a hidden false step.
