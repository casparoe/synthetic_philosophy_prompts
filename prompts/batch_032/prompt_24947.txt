I'm in a moral philosophy seminar this term, and we've been working through the literature on the moral difference between lying and misleading. The standard move in much of what we've read is to say that lying — asserting something you believe to be false — is presumptively worse than merely misleading, because the liar takes on a distinctive kind of responsibility for the falsehood that the misleader sidesteps. But I've been testing this against my own reactions to a range of cases, and I can't find a principle that fits all of them. I'm hoping you can help me extract one.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. **The murderer at the door.** A killer asks whether my friend is inside. I say, "No, she's not here," when she is. That feels permissible. But so does saying, "I saw her heading toward the library an hour ago," which is true but misleading because she came back. I don't feel a moral difference between these two — both are fine.

2. **The job interview.** I'm asked whether I have experience managing a budget. I say, "Yes, I managed a six-figure budget in my last role," when I only assisted someone who did. That feels clearly wrong. If instead I say, "I worked closely with a six-figure budget in my last role," that also feels wrong — but noticeably less wrong than the lie.

3. **The used car.** A buyer asks whether the car has ever been in an accident. I say, "No, never," when it was in a fender-bender before I owned it. That feels wrong. If I instead say, "I've had it for two years and it's been completely reliable," that also feels wrong — but here I don't feel the same gap between the two. The misleading statement feels just as bad as the lie, maybe because I'm exploiting the buyer's likely inference so deliberately.

4. **The friend's haircut.** A friend asks what I think of their new haircut, which is genuinely unflattering. I say, "It looks great!" That feels fine — it's social kindness. If I say, "Wow, you really went for it!" that also feels fine. No moral difference, and neither registers as a real wrong.

5. **The academic CV.** A CV lists a paper as "forthcoming in *Philosophical Review*" when the author knows it was rejected. That feels like a serious wrong. If the CV instead says, "Revise and resubmit at *Philosophical Review*" when the author received a desk rejection and has no realistic prospect of revision, that also feels like a serious wrong — and if anything, it feels worse, because it's more carefully engineered to mislead while preserving deniability.

6. **The dying relative.** This is the case I keep going back and forth on. My terminally ill aunt asks whether the doctors think she's improving. The doctors have said her condition is stable but that the long-term outlook hasn't changed. Saying "Yes, they think you're improving" would be a lie and feels wrong. But saying "They say your condition is stable" is true and misleading — it invites her to infer improvement without my having said it — and I can't tell whether that's better, worse, or the same. Part of me thinks the misleading statement is kinder because it doesn't force me to say the false thing outright, but another part thinks that's exactly what makes it more manipulative.

Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or most of them? If some verdicts have to go, which ones and why? And what does the principle say about the dying relative case? I'm particularly interested in whether the principle can explain why the gap between lying and misleading seems large in some contexts (the interview) and nonexistent in others (the used car, the CV), and whether that variation is defensible or just noise in my intuitions.
