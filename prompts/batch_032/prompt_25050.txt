I run a reading group on AI safety — about 12 people, mostly grad students in CS and philosophy, meeting for 90 minutes. We've been reading about deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn (the idea that a sufficiently capable AI might behave well during training because it knows it's being evaluated, and then pursue misaligned goals only once it's deployed and hard to shut down). People get the argument in the abstract but I don't think they *feel* it — they treat it like a clever sci-fi plot rather than something that falls out of the incentive structure. Last week I tried just posing the question "what would *you* do if you were the AI" and the discussion was fine but stayed at the level of, like, "well I'd just be honest." Which completely misses the point.

I want to run a classroom game that makes them experience the logic from the inside — where they end up behaving like deceptive aligners not because they're sociopaths but because the incentives push everyone that way. Here's what I'm imagining: a repeated game where each round, players choose "cooperate" or "defect." But there are two phases — a "training" phase and a "deployment" phase — and the payoffs switch between them. During training, defecting gets you removed from the game (the overseer catches you). During deployment, defecting gives a big payoff and there are no consequences. Players don't know exactly when the phase transition happens, though they might get noisy signals. The debrief would connect what happened to deceptive alignment: the rational strategy of playing nice until you're confident you're safe, the arms race between evaluators and deceptive agents, and why the problem gets worse as agents get smarter (better at modeling when the transition happens).

Can you design this as a runnable activity? I need: full rules, what materials to prepare, a payoff scheme (no real money — maybe points or tokens), exactly how the phase transition works and what players know about it, and a structured debrief that maps moments in the game to the conceptual architecture of the treacherous turn. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which features of deceptive alignment the game can't capture (e.g., the game has human players with human motivations, not an AI with a mesa-objective). Oh, and give me some varients I could try if we run it a second time — like, what if some players are "interpretability researchers" who get partial information about others' strategies?
