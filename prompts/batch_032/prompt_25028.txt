When a self-driving car causes a collision, the emerging legal consensus routes liability to the manufacturer under product liability law, not to the human occupant under negligence or criminal law. Yet the same legal frameworks require that a human remain behind the wheel, attentive and ready to take over, and prosecutors have charged occupants who were plainly not supervising — watching a movie, asleep — with serious offenses. So the law seems to say both that the car is responsible and that you are. What theory of moral agency and responsibility does this two-part practice presuppose? Is it a coherent theory, or does it try to have things both ways? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in the liability framework if we adopted instead a view on which autonomous systems can be full moral agents — or, at the opposite extreme, a view on which they are mere instruments and the human supervisor always bears full responsibility. Finally, identify which assumptions your analysis depends on most heavily and how your conclusions would shift if those assumptions were relaxed.
