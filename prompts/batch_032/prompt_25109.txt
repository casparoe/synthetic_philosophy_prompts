ok so i've been going down a rabbit hole reading about this thing called illusionism about consciousness — the idea that phenomenal consciousness (like, the "what it's like" of experience, qualia, the redness of red) doesn't actually exist, it's just a kind of introspective illusion. like we have mental states and we mistakenly represent them to ourselves as having these spooky phenomenal properties they don't really have.

and i came up with this argument that seems to show the whole thing is self-defeating, but im not sure if i'm missing something because smart people seem to take this view seriously. here's the argument:

(1) if illusionism is true, then when i introspect and think "i'm in pain right now," what's happening is that introspection is presenting a mental state to me as having a phenomenal property (painfulness) that it doesn't actually have. introspection is systematically misrepresenting the nature of my own mental states.

(2) if introspection systematically misrepresents our mental states in one domain (phenomenal properties), we should be suspicious of its reliability in other domains too. like if a debugger consistently showed you wrong values for one class of variables, you'd stop trusting it for everything.

(3) if we can't trust introspection, then we can't trust introspective beliefs in general — including the belief that we have beliefs, that we have mental states with content, that we're conscious in any sense at all.

(4) but illusionism itself is a theory about the nature of mental states. it's not like we discovered it with an fMRI machine. the motivation for illusionism comes from reflecting on what consciousness seems like and finding that picture incoherent or incompatible with physicalism. that's introspection-adjacent reasoning at minimum.

(5) so illusionism, if true, gives us reason to distrust the very cognitive processes that lead us to entertain illusionism in the first place.

(6) a theory that undermines the reliability of the processes used to arrive at it is self-defeating.

(7) therefore illusionism is false, or at least can't be rationally believed.

i feel like (2) is the weak link — maybe the misrepresentation is specific to phenomenal properties and doesn't leak into other introspective judgments? but then what's the principled firewall between "this mental state has a phenomenal feel" and "this mental state has content" or "i am having a thought right now"? they all come through the same introspective channel. if that channel is lying about one thing, why trust it about the rest?

anyway i'm a software engineer not a philosopher so i'm probably reinventing a wheel here. where does this argument actually go wrong, if it does? and if it doesn't go wrong, what's the best illusionist reply?
