I'm a distributed systems engineer — I spend my days thinking about consensus protocols, event ordering, leader election, and the tradeoffs between consistency and availability. I recently tried to read some Dennett on consciousness, and I kept finding myself translating his arguments into the vocabulary I actually think in. The Cartesian theater, as Dennett describes it, sounds a lot like the architectural mistake of assuming a single-threaded event loop with a central dispatcher that everything must funnel through — a single point of serialization where sensory inputs are projected, ordered, and presented to some internal observer. And his alternative, the multiple drafts model, sounds like it's describing something closer to a distributed, eventually-consistent system: multiple parallel streams of processing running concurrently across different subsystems, no central coordinator, no single canonical ordering of events, and what we call "conscious experience" is just whichever draft happens to win some local competition at the moment the system is probed. The homunculus — the little man in the theater watching the screen — maps pretty naturally onto the fallacy of reifying a coordinator process when you haven't actually explained how coordination works.

I'd like you to do a systematic translation of this debate into the vocabulary of distributed systems — event ordering, consistency models (linearizability, sequential consistency, eventual consistency), single source of truth versus multi-writer architectures, leader election, gossip protocols, vector clocks, and whatever else from our toolkit seems relevant. But the real deliverable is the residue report: which claims from Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model translate cleanly, which ones you had to stretch or distort to fit, and which ones have no counterpart at all in the distributed-systems vocabulary. And then the hard question: does the residue point to something the distributed-systems framework is too impoverished to capture — something genuinely distinctive about phenomenal consciousness that isn't just an engineering problem — or does it point to the philosophical debate being less precise than it presents itself as being? I want to know whether my intuition that Dennett is basically describing an eventually-consistent multi-agent architecture is insight or category error.

Please also separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among philosophers and cognitive scientists working on this. If there are live disagreements about whether the multiple drafts model actually escapes the homunculus problem, or about the empirical adequacy of the model as a description of real neural processing, flag those rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
