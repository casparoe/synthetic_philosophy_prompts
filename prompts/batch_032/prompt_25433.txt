A long-form article I read on rational decision-making summarizes Pascal's mugging like this:

"Pascal's mugging is a thought experiment that shows expected utility theory is broken. Here's the setup: a mugger approaches you and claims that if you don't hand over your wallet, he will use his magical powers to torture a trillion trillion people for eternity. The chance he's telling the truth is astronomically small — say, one in 10^30 — but the disvalue of eternal torture for that many people is so enormous that the expected utility of handing over your wallet is still negative if you refuse. So expected utility theory says you should give him your wallet, which is obviously absurd. The standard fix is to adopt a bounded utility function, which just means capping how much utility or disutility you assign to any outcome, and once you do that the problem goes away. But bounded utility has its own problems: it implies you'd turn down a guaranteed chance to save an arbitrarily large number of lives for a tiny cost, which seems monstrous. So the real lesson of Pascal's mugging is that we should reject expected utility theory entirely and accept that some low-probability possibilities are simply not worth thinking about, no matter what's at stake."

I've seen this argument referenced in several places but I don't know enough decision theory to tell whether this summary is fair to the original problem and the state of the debate. Which parts accurately capture the problem and the standard responses, which parts are misleading or conflate distinct issues, and does the conclusion — that we should reject expected utility theory — actually follow from anything the summary gets right?
