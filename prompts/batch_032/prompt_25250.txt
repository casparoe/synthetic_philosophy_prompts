I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about personal identity, and I want to see whether it lines up with the Buddhist no-self view (anatta) or whether I'm smuggling in a self somewhere. Here are my gut reactions on cases.

Teleporter case: I step into a machine, it scans me down to the quantum state, destroys my body, and beams the information to Mars where an exact replica is assembled. I think the person on Mars is me. No hesitation.

Fission case: Same machine, but a malfunction creates two identical copies, one on Mars and one on Venus. My gut says neither of them is me — I don't survive fission. The original me is gone, and there are now two new people who are psychologically continuous with me but aren't me.

Gradual replacement: Over thirty years, surgeons replace each of my neurons one by one with silicon chips that perform the identical function. At the end, no biological brain tissue remains. I think I'm still me throughout. This doesn't bother me at all.

Brain transplant: My brain is transplanted into a new body. I wake up in the new body. That's me.

Upload: My brain is destructively scanned and a perfect simulation of my mind runs on a computer. I think the simulation is a copy of me, not me. I'm dead, and something that thinks it's me continues. This feels different from the teleporter and I'm not sure why, and that bothers me.

Total psychological break: I suffer complete amnesia, my personality changes radically, my values and memories are gone. Same body, same brain. I think this is still me — just a very different version of me, the way I'm a different version of me from who I was at age five.

Stream of consciousness gap: I fall into a coma for ten years with zero brain activity, then wake up. Still me.

Okay so here's what I notice: I seem to care about some kind of continuity, but not purely psychological continuity (otherwise the upload would be me, and fission wouldn't bother me the way it does). I also don't think I'm just my body (otherwise the brain transplant would fail and gradual replacement might bother me). There's something about the continuity of the underlying substrate — the same brain, or a causally continuous replacement process — that matters. But I can't quite articulate it.

Here's what I want: first, tell me what principle or small set of principles best captures these verdicts. Be honest about which ones the principle forces me to give up, if any. Second, apply that principle to a case I haven't mentioned: suppose a meditator, after years of practice, reports that upon close inspection they find no stable self — just a stream of impermanent sensations, thoughts, and volitions arising and passing. They say the sense of being a persisting "I" was always an illusion, and now they see through it. On whatever principle you extracted from my verdicts, is this meditator the same person they were before the insight? And third, here's the real question: does the principle I seem to hold actually commit me to the existence of a self in a way that Buddhism would reject? Or could a Buddhist no-self theorist accept my principle and just say that what I'm tracking with "same person" isn't a self but a causally continuous series — like a flame passed from candle to candle? I want to know whether my gut on these cases is secretly at odds with anatta or whether the two can be made to fit.
