I'm a doctoral student writing a dissertation chapter on the philosophical anthropology implicit in the design of social media platforms, and I want to use Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public — especially the analysis he develops in the second half of *Two Ages* — as my diagnostic framework. I need a sharp, committed analysis I can respond to and build from.

Here is the practice I want you to examine. Take four design features that are now so ubiquitous across major platforms that users hardly register them as contingent choices:

**1. The engagement-optimized feed.** Content is served in an endless, algorithmically sorted stream. What you see is not determined by chronology or by your own deliberate navigation but by a model trained to maximize time-on-platform and interaction — where "interaction" includes outrage, compulsive scrolling, and other forms of attention that the user may not reflectively endorse.

**2. The quantification of social response.** Likes, retweets, follower counts, view counts, and similar metrics render approval and attention numerically visible, comparable across posts and across persons, and updated in something close to real time.

**3. The flattening of content types.** A friend's wedding photograph, a news report, a stranger's polemic, a commercial advertisement, and a meme all appear in the same visual format, in the same feed, with the same structural weight. The platform does not distinguish among them by genre, epistemic status, or relational context.

**4. Expression without commitment.** You can post, delete, edit, and repost; you can signal a stance, an identity, or an emotional reaction without having to sustain it, act on it, or live with its consequences in embodied social life. The architecture makes expression cheap and revocable.

For each of these four features, I want two things:

First, reconstruct the implicit philosophical anthropology the feature presupposes. What view of the human person — of what a self is, what authentic communication consists in, and what a worthwhile life looks like — does the design take for granted, quietly promote, or make difficult to sustain? Which rival views of the self does it rule out or marginalize?

Second, bring Kierkegaard's analysis to bear. Which of his specific diagnostic concepts — the public as an abstraction, leveling, reflection without passion, chatter, the loss of decisive action and inwardness, the spectator relationship to one's own life — illuminate what this feature does to the person who inhabits it? Be precise: do not just gesture at "Kierkegaard would have hated social media." Show how particular concepts from his critique of the nineteenth-century press map onto particular design features of twenty-first-century platforms, and note where the fit is imperfect.

Then step back and answer a synthetic question: do these four features collectively presuppose a consistent picture of the self, or do they pull in different directions? If they are inconsistent, identify the tensions and say which feature does the most structural work. If they are consistent, name the unifying picture and say whether Kierkegaard would have recognized it as a new technological form of the pathology he diagnosed in the bourgeois public sphere, or as something genuinely novel that his categories cannot fully capture.

I want a committed response. Do not lay out interpretive options and leave me to choose; tell me which reading you think is strongest and defend it with reasons. Where a design feature is philosophically underdetermined — where more than one view of the self could license it — flag that, but still take a stand on which view is actually doing the work in the platforms as they currently operate. Where you think Kierkegaard's framework needs modification to handle the digital case, say so and propose the modification.
