I serve on my university's standing committee on academic freedom and scholarly conduct. A faculty member in philosophy has proposed a multi-year research project investigating whether certain widely-held moral convictions — for instance, that familial obligations carry greater moral weight than impartial duties to strangers — are better explained as culturally transmitted biases than as tracking genuine moral reasons. A group of faculty and students has petitioned to block the project, arguing that its methodology is question-begging: to classify a moral conviction as a "bias" rather than a legitimate preference or judgment is already to take a substantive normative stance, so the project cannot claim to be doing neutral descriptive work. The researcher replies that every empirical inquiry into bias faces a version of this circularity worry, that the alternative is to insulate moral convictions from scrutiny altogether, and that the petitioners' objection is itself an instance of the very bias the project aims to study. The committee must issue a written opinion: should the project proceed, be blocked, or proceed with conditions? The opinion must engage seriously with the underlying philosophical difficulty — that there may be no value-neutral way to distinguish a moral preference from a moral bias — and must offer a concrete, implementable recommendation with reasons the losing side can recognize as fair.
