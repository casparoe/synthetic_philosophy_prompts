For my high school's philosophy symposium, I'm writing a staged dialogue to be performed at the opening session. The topic is moral relativism, and I want the dialogue to go beyond the usual talking points. Please write a dialogue between two characters—Mara (who defends a sophisticated version of normative moral relativism) and Kal (who defends moral universalism)—in which they debate the following specific question: Does the fact of persistent, deep cross-cultural moral disagreement give us good reason to accept moral relativism, and can relativism adequately account for the phenomenon of moral progress?

The dialogue should be about 900 words. Both characters should be equally competent and well-read; neither should be a strawman. They should genuinely respond to each other's points rather than talking past each other. In particular, Mara should not simply assert that different cultures disagree and therefore there is no objective morality—she should address the universalist's best counterarguments. Likewise, Kal should not simply dismiss relativism as "anything goes"—he should engage seriously with the relativist's epistemological challenge.

The dialogue should touch on at least the following moves:

- The relativist's argument that persistent moral disagreement is best explained by the absence of objective moral facts, and the universalist's reply that disagreement in other domains (e.g., history, science) does not force relativism there.
- The universalist's argument that moral progress (e.g., the abolition of slavery, the expansion of rights) presupposes a standard independent of any particular culture's norms, and the relativist's attempt to explain progress in framework-relative terms.
- The question of whether the relativist can consistently criticize the moral practices of other cultures (e.g., foot binding, honor killing) without implicitly appealing to a universal standard.
- The relativist's challenge that the universalist owes an account of how we could ever access mind-independent moral truths, given that all our moral reasoning begins within some cultural framework or another.

The dialogue should end not with one side conceding, but with each character identifying the deepest remaining point of disagreement—something that would need to be resolved for further progress between their positions.
