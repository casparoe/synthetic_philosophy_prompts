I am trying to work out what I actually think about the claim that artifacts can have politics — that technical objects can embody, enforce, or require particular forms of social order — by going case by case and seeing whether a stable principle emerges from my verdicts.

Here are the cases and what my gut says about each.

Robert Moses's low-hanging overpasses on the Long Island parkways, designed to prevent buses from passing under them and thereby to exclude people who relied on public transit from Jones Beach: these have politics. The exclusion is not a side effect; it is built into the physical form of the thing.

A nuclear power plant: has politics. The technology seems to require centralized, hierarchical control, expert authority, and security arrangements that resist democratic oversight in a way that a distributed technology like rooftop solar does not. The political arrangement is not just one option among others — it appears to be demanded by the artifact's operating requirements.

A kitchen knife: does not have politics. It can be used to prepare dinner or to harm someone, and nothing about its form pushes toward one social arrangement rather than another. It is politically inert.

A handgun: has politics. It makes lethal force available to anyone who can pull a trigger, which shifts power away from physical strength, numbers, or social authority and toward speed and willingness. That seems like a fact about the artifact itself, not merely about how some people happen to use it.

A public park bench segmented by metal armrests that make lying down impossible: has politics. The design encodes a decision about who may use public space and for what — it is an answer to the question of whether homeless people may sleep here, rendered in steel.

A speed bump: here I hesitate. It forces every driver to slow down, which is a constraint on behavior, but it does not feel political in the way the bench does. Perhaps because the constraint serves a shared good — safety — and applies uniformly? But the bench also applies uniformly: no one may lie down on it. I cannot identify what distinguishes them, yet I feel a difference.

A staircase at a building entrance with no ramp: has politics. Even if the builder gave no thought to wheelchair users, the artifact itself encodes a norm about whose presence is expected and whose is not. The exclusion is in the object, not just in the absent-mindedness of its designer.

The TCP/IP protocols underlying the internet: I want to say they do not have politics. They were designed to be content-neutral and to route around damage or blockage. But the fact that they route around blockage — that they treat censorship as damage and work around it — sounds like a political property. I am genuinely torn.

A social media platform's algorithmic feed that optimizes for engagement: has politics. The optimization target is not neutral; it systematically amplifies outrage and polarizing content, and this shapes public discourse in ways that are politically consequential regardless of whether anyone intended that outcome.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If no single principle gets them all, which verdicts should I overturn and why? And what does the principle you arrive at say about a case I have not listed: a municipal water supply system whose pipes were made of lead because lead was cheaper at the time of installation, and which has since leached lead into the drinking water of the predominantly poor and minority neighborhoods it serves?
