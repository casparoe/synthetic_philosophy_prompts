I retired from physics a few years ago and have been spending my time working through philosophy of science and formal epistemology. I have arrived at a set of views about induction, simplicity, and probability that I would like someone to audit for consistency before I commit to them in writing. I will state them as plainly as I can, and I would be grateful if you would go through them and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revisions would be. I want a committed assessment, not a survey of options: tell me what you think the problems are and what I should give up, and why.

Here are the views.

First, Solomonoff induction is the correct normative theory of inductive inference for an ideal agent with unlimited computational resources. It tells us how prior probabilities should be assigned and how they should be updated on evidence.

Second, the universal prior — the probability distribution over computable sequences given by Solomonoff's construction, which assigns higher probability to sequences with shorter programs — is the uniquely correct objective prior. There is no other prior an ideal reasoner should use in the absence of evidence.

Third, Kolmogorov complexity is the right formal measure of the simplicity of a hypothesis, and simplicity understood this way is what justifies assigning higher prior probability to some hypotheses than to others.

Fourth, Goodman's new riddle of induction shows that any purely syntactic or formal measure of simplicity is inadequate for grounding inductive inference. The predicate "grue" can be made syntactically symmetric with "green" by choosing an appropriate primitive vocabulary, so no formal measure can distinguish them without presupposing the very inductive dispositions it is supposed to justify.

Fifth, in actual scientific practice, where we face finite data and computational limits, we should use model selection criteria like the Minimum Description Length principle or the Bayesian Information Criterion. These are reasonable practical approximations to what ideal induction would recommend.

Sixth, the fact that Solomonoff induction is uncomputable does not undermine its status as a normative ideal. We accept normative ideals in other domains — expected utility theory, for instance — that we cannot fully compute in practice, and this is not generally taken to refute them.

Please do not hedge. I want to know which of these I should revise and in what order of priority. Write your response in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
