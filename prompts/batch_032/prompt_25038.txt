I am giving a department colloquium next month defending a particular reading of Hume's argument against miracles. My thesis is that the argument in Section X of the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding is best understood not as an a priori proof that testimonial evidence for miracles can never suffice, but as a piece of applied epistemology: Hume is showing how a reasonable inquirer should discount testimonial evidence in light of uniform background experience, and the so-called everlasting check is a practical maxim about how heavily to discount, not a demonstration that no amount of testimony could in principle overcome the presumption. On this reading, the argument is more resilient than its critics suppose. In particular, it survives the familiar charge that it would also rule out believing testimony about unprecedented but genuine events — the first report of a black swan, or the discovery of radioactivity — because Hume's framework has resources for distinguishing between mere violations of past regularity and violations of well-confirmed laws of nature. I also argue, more tentatively, that this reading has downstream bite for how we should evaluate certain contemporary scientific controversies in which anomalous findings are reported against a backdrop of overwhelmingly uniform prior evidence.

The audience is a philosophy department with strong clusters in formal epistemology and philosophy of science, plus several historians of early modern philosophy. The epistemologists include Bayesians (some of whom have written on the probability of miracles and the role of priors), non-Bayesians who work on the epistemology of testimony, and at least one person who thinks Hume's argument is simply a confused version of a point Bayes made better. The philosophers of science include people who have thought hard about the epistemology of anomaly, the theory-ladenness of observation, and Kuhnian paradigm change. The Hume scholars will care about textual fidelity and will catch any sloppiness about what Hume actually says. I expect about thirty-five people, and the Q&A will run roughly twenty-five minutes.
