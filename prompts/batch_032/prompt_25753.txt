A friend who works in pain research explained pain asymbolia to me using the fire-alarm analogy. Here is roughly how they put it. A fire alarm has two functional parts: the detection mechanism that registers smoke and the blaring siren that makes everyone evacuate. Pain works the same way. The sensory-discriminative component — the location, the burning or stabbing quality, the intensity — is the detection mechanism: it encodes information about what is happening and where. The affective-motivational component — the unpleasantness, the "hurt," the urge to escape — is the siren: it makes the information matter to you and drives action. In pain asymbolia, the detection mechanism still works. Patients can describe the sensation in precise detail — where it is, how intense, whether it burns or throbs — but the siren has been disconnected. They register the signal without any impulse to withdraw or any sense that the experience is bad. My friend drew a strong conclusion from this: the analogy shows that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation itself. It is a separate evaluative layer added by the brain, which is precisely why it can be selectively lost while the sensation remains intact.

I find the analogy helpful for getting a grip on the phenomenon, but I suspect it is doing more argumentative work than my friend acknowledges. I would like you to audit it. In particular, I want to know what features of an alarm system the analogy quietly imports into our picture of pain that may not belong there, and whether the conclusion — that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy for what pain asymbolia reveals, I would like to hear that too.
