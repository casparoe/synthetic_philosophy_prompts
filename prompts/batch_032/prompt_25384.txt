I've been wrestling with an argument that seems to show that rational people cannot persistently disagree about anything — and since they plainly do, something has to give. The argument runs as follows:

(1) A person is rational only if they update their beliefs by conditionalizing on evidence according to Bayes' rule. Their credence in a proposition after learning evidence E is their prior credence conditional on E.

(2) Any two rational agents who are willing to discuss a topic can be represented as sharing a common prior probability distribution over some sufficiently rich space of possible worlds. If they started with different priors, those priors would themselves reflect different evidence they received earlier, and conditionalizing on that earlier evidence from a shared ur-prior would recover their current priors. So the appearance of different priors is just the appearance of different evidence at one remove.

(3) When two people honestly discuss a proposition and each tells the other their current credence, those credences become common knowledge between them: each knows the other's credence, each knows the other knows, and so on.

(4) Aumann's agreement theorem shows that if two agents share a common prior, update by conditionalization on partitions representing their private information, and have common knowledge of their posterior credences for some event, then those posteriors must be equal.

(5) Therefore, any two rational people who honestly discuss any proposition should converge to exactly the same credence in that proposition.

(6) But rational people persistently disagree — about politics, about philosophical questions, about economic policy, about the interpretation of complex data — even after extensive, honest discussion. They do not converge.

(7) So either the people in (6) are not in fact rational, or at least one of premises (1) through (3) is false. And if one of them is false, then something we thought was a requirement of rationality — Bayesian updating, the availability of a common prior, or the transparency of honest discussion — isn't.

I find the conclusion hard to accept, but I cannot locate the mistake. The premises each seem individually plausible. Tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives: what does each standard response give up, and which response leaves the least damage to our picture of rational belief and disagreement?
