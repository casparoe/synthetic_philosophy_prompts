I'm a software engineer, and I got drawn into decision theory through a practical concern: if we eventually deploy autonomous AI systems at scale, how should they reason about cooperating with each other in one-shot interactions where they can't communicate or enforce contracts? That question led me into a debate that seems to have been running for years without resolution — the argument about evidential cooperation in large worlds.

The idea, as I understand it: in a world with many agents who are similar to you (similar training data, similar architectures, similar reasoning procedures), your decision to cooperate in a prisoner's dilemma doesn't cause those other agents to cooperate, but it is evidence that they will — because they're running the same decision procedure you are. The evidential cooperation argument says you should therefore cooperate, and the larger the world (the more similar agents there are whose behavior your decision provides evidence about), the stronger this argument becomes, because the evidential payoff scales with the number of similar agents. Causal decision theorists reject this: they say you should only care about what your action causes, not what it's evidence for, regardless of how many similar agents exist or how strong the correlation is.

What I find puzzling is that both sides seem to agree on all the empirical facts. They agree there's no causal link between the agents' decisions. They agree the agents are similar in the relevant ways. They agree about the payoff matrix and about how many similar agents exist. And yet they reach opposite conclusions about what to do. This feels like it should be resolvable — it's a formal question about decision procedures, not an empirical one — and yet it hasn't been resolved.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that this is ultimately a disagreement about what "rationality" means: the evidential camp thinks rationality is about making decisions that you would endorse given what they imply about the world (including about what other agents will do), while the causal camp thinks rationality is about choosing actions that cause good outcomes. If that's right, then the debate is really about which concept of rationality we should care about, and perhaps there's no fact of the matter to be discovered. But I'm not confident in that diagnosis — maybe there's a formal result I'm missing, or maybe one side has a decisive argument that I haven't encountered because I lack the philosophical background.

Given your diagnosis: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or a clash of bedrock intuitions? And is there anything — a formal result, a new distinction, a reframing of the question — that would actually move it, or should I stop expecting resolution?
