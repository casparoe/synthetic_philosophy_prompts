I've been thinking lately about I.J. Good's theorem on the value of information — the proof that, for an expected-utility maximizer facing a decision problem, cost-free information can never have negative expected value. The argument is almost embarrassingly simple: whatever signal you receive, you can always choose to ignore it and act exactly as you would have on your prior, so the option to acquire information can only expand your set of available strategies, never contract it. And yet the theorem sits strangely alongside ordinary life. We decline medical tests whose results we would rather not know, we ask friends not to spoil a film, we scroll past the headlines. Some of this can be explained within the framework — information can enter the utility function directly, so that knowing a painful truth is itself a cost, and we may not trust ourselves to act optimally given what we learn, which means the "ignore the signal" move isn't genuinely available to us. But a great deal of information-avoidance seems to reflect something deeper: that we are not the kind of agent the theorem describes, and the gap between its clean normative guarantee and our messy reluctance to know feels like it says something important about the relationship between formal rationality and being a person.

I'd like you to write a villanelle about Good's theorem on the value of information. The two refrains should carry the argument: one refrain should state or evoke the theorem's core claim (that free information never lowers expected utility), and the other should capture the human impulse to avoid information (the turning-away from truths that would, by the theorem's own logic, help us act). The villanelle form — with its two refrains circling back and trading places across the tercets — should enact the tension between the normative pull of the theorem and the psychological reality of information-avoidance. When both refrains finally meet in the closing couplet, I want the poem to have shown why the theorem is correct and yet somehow fails to compel, without resolving that tension. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the theorem and its standard caveats (direct utility of knowledge, distrust of one's own future decisions, computational limits) would recognize the argument, but it should also succeed as a poem for a reader who has never encountered I.J. Good.
