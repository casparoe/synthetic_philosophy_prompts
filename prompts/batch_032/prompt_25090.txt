Two friends of mine got into it over a weird epistemic question — though they didn't use the jargon. The concrete case: Dan spent an afternoon working through a tricky logic puzzle and became convinced the answer was B. Then he remembered he'd only slept three hours the night before. He said he should probably switch his answer to A, because sleep-deprived people make more errors on these puzzles, and A is the most common wrong answer tired people gravitate toward.

Maya told him that was irrational. She said: you've just carefully reasoned to B. Unless you can actually find a flaw in your reasoning, the mere fact that tired people often get these wrong doesn't give you a reason to switch — it's a statistical fact about other tired people, not evidence about your particular reasoning.

Dan pushed back: but it IS evidence about my reasoning. I'm a person, I'm tired, tired people mess these up — that's what evidence is. If I learned the puzzle came from a source where 90% of answers are misprinted, I'd update. Why is this different?

Maya said: because in the misprint case you have evidence that bears directly on the truth of the answer. In the tiredness case you have evidence about your cognitive process, but you've already taken that process as far as it can go — you've examined the reasoning itself and found it sound. To then doubt the conclusion because of a generic fact about tiredness is to treat yourself as a black box rather than engaging with your own first-order reasoning.

Dan said: but that's exactly the point — I SHOULD treat myself partly as a black box. I know I have biases, I know I make errors I can't detect from the inside. If I refuse to update on evidence about my own unreliability, I'm being dogmatic.

Maya replied: there's a difference between updating on specific evidence of error and updating on mere statistical risk. If you found a particular step where you'd divided wrong, fine. But absent that, you're just double-counting: your initial reasoning already incorporated whatever care you could muster given your tiredness. The fact that you're tired doesn't add new information about the puzzle itself.

Dan said: that can't be right, because by that logic you'd never update on evidence that you're drunk, or that someone slipped you a drug, or that you have early-stage dementia — as long as your reasoning felt fine from the inside.

Maya paused, then said: okay, maybe in extreme cases like dementia the statistical evidence is so overwhelming that it swamps your first-order reasoning. But that's a matter of degree, not a difference in kind — and in Dan's case the effect of one bad night's sleep is nowhere near that threshold.

Dan said: so you admit the principle is right, we're just haggling over the numbers?

Maya said: no, I'm saying there's a threshold below which higher-order evidence shouldn't move you at all, precisely because you have direct access to your reasoning and you've found it sound. That's not haggling — it's a structural claim about how first-order and higher-order evidence interact.

They went back and forth and eventually asked me to weigh in, but I honestly couldn't untangle it. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what? In particular, I'm curious whether Maya's "threshold" move at the end is principled or just ad hoc, and whether Dan's drunk/drugged/dementia examples actually force her to concede more than she thinks.
