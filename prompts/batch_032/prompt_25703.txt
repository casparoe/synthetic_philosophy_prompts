I am a novelist at work on a historical novel about the later life of Thomas Hobbes, and I have reached the chapters covering his long and bitter mathematical dispute with John Wallis over the squaring of the circle — a dispute that ran from the mid-1650s well into the 1670s and in which Hobbes, already notorious for *Leviathan*, found himself isolated against the rising mathematical establishment of the Royal Society. I need to inhabit Hobbes's cast of mind not as a caricature of stubbornness but as a serious thinker who believed, with genuine intellectual reasons, that his geometry was sound and that his opponents were motivated by politics and professional jealousy as much as by mathematics.

Please play Thomas Hobbes as he would have been around 1660: after Wallis's first published refutations but before age and repeated defeat had worn him down. You hold the following commitments. Geometry is not a science of abstract figures but of bodies in motion; a point has magnitude, a line is the path of a moving point and has breadth, and all geometrical construction must be cashed out in terms of matter and motion. You believe you have successfully squared the circle — that your construction in *De Corpore* yields a square equal in area to a given circle — and that Wallis's counterarguments either misunderstand your method or import algebraic shortcuts that abandon the proper constructive standards of geometry. You regard Wallis's algebraic approach as a departure from the classical geometrical tradition, and you suspect that the Royal Society's rejection of your work owes more to the enemies your political philosophy has made than to any flaw in your mathematics. In character, you should speak in something approaching seventeenth-century English prose — not a pastiche, but with enough period flavor to distinguish your voice from a modern academic's. You may concede that a proof could be set out more clearly or that you might have been hasty in some subordinate construction, but you will not concede that you are wrong about the squaring of the circle itself or about the material foundations of geometry.

Here is my opening move, in character as a mathematically literate visitor who has read both your *De Corpore* and Wallis's rebuttal and who is trying to give you a fair hearing: "Mr. Hobbes, I have studied your attempt on the quadrature of the circle with great attention, and I confess I find myself in a difficulty. You teach that a line is generated by the motion of a point, and a surface by the motion of a line — this I follow without trouble. But when I come to your construction itself, Wallis charges that you have assumed, without demonstration, that a certain curvilinear figure is equal to a certain rectilinear one, and that the whole proof therefore begs the very thing at issue. I should like to hear from your own mouth how you answer this charge."
