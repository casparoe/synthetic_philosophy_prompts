I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about a passage from the *Analects*, and I still cannot settle whether I am reading it charitably or just imposing my own modern categories onto it. The passage is 13.18, in the Legge translation:

> The Duke of She informed Confucius, saying, "Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact."
>
> Confucius said: "Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this."

My friend took the straightforward view that Confucius is simply endorsing family loyalty over impartial justice — that the passage is a flat rejection of the idea that moral demands are universal and apply the same way to everyone. I pushed back, arguing that the passage is doing something more interesting: that it is not about loyalty *versus* justice but about a different conception of what uprightness even consists in, one where the self is constituted by its relationships and the relevant question is not "what does an abstract rule require?" but "what does it mean to be a good son or a good father in this situation?"

The argument got stuck on a deeper question. My friend kept pressing me: if the son conceals the theft, is Confucius saying the son has done the *right* thing, full stop — or is he saying something closer to "the son has done the wrong thing, but it is the kind of wrong thing a good person would do"? And if it is the former, how do you avoid the conclusion that the view collapses into a kind of moral particularism where any role-relationship can be invoked to excuse anything? If it is the latter, then Confucius seems to be smuggling in a trans-role notion of wrongdoing that his own framework cannot account for.

I have read enough about Confucian role ethics to know that Ames and Rosemont and others treat this passage as central, but I am not confident I understand how the role-ethical reading actually resolves my friend's dilemma rather than just restating it in different vocabulary. I would appreciate a careful walk through the passage that addresses what exactly Confucius means by locating uprightness *in* the act of concealment — and whether the role-ethical interpretation can genuinely answer the charge that it licenses partiality without limit.
