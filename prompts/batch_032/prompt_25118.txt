ok so heres the thing — ive been going back and forth on the surprise exam paradox for like two weeks and i think i've landed on a position that most people would find pretty annoying. the teacher announces there'll be a surprise exam next week. the student does the backward induction thing: cant be friday (no surprise by thursday night), so not friday, then not thursday for the same reason, etc., concludes no exam can happen. then the teacher gives it on wednesday and boom, it's a surprise.

the standard move is to say the student's reasoning has a flaw — maybe the self-referential nature of "surprise" breaks something, maybe the induction step fails when you get to a certain number of days, maybe the student cant legitimately eliminate friday unless they know theyll still know the announcement is true on thursday night. ive read versions of all of these and none of them feel like they actually stick.

so my position is: the student's reasoning IS valid. the paradox is real. the fact that in real life we get surprised anyway doesnt refute the reasoning — it just shows that the reasoning depends on assumptions about perfect self-knowledge that we dont actually satisfy. like the paradox shows something true about ideal reasoners, and the fact that we non-ideal reasoners get surprised isnt a counterexample, its just evidence that were not ideal.

- and honestly — is my position unfalsifiable? have i just defined things so that no possible observation or argument could touch it? and if so, is that actually a problem for a view about ideal reasoning, or is that just what philosophy looks like sometimes?
