I'm teaching a unit on free will to advanced undergrads and I want to give them a decision tree that sorts them into a position on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Start by presenting a standard Frankfurt-style case — you know, Jones decides to kill Smith, Black is waiting in the wings with a brain implant to make Jones do it if he wavers, but Jones does it on his own and Black never intervenes. First question: is Jones morally responsible for killing Smith?

From there, branch based on their answers and follow-ups. The leaves I need covered: the straightforward "PAP is false, Frankfurt wins" position; the flicker-of-freedom view (there's still some alternative possibility, just a very thin one); the dilemma objection in both its horns (either the signal is deterministic and begs the question, or it's indeterministic and leaves a relevant alternative); source incompatibilism (Frankfurt shows source matters more than alternatives, but that doesn't help compatibilism); and at least one revisionist take that keeps PAP by reinterpreting what "could have done otherwise" means.

Build it from the logical space, not from a list of named views — figure out what the diagnostic questions are and what combinations of answers are possible, then locate the actual positions in that space. If there are combinations nobody defends, flag them and say whether they're empty because they're incoherent, just wildly unattractive, or maybe underexplored. Also flag any two leaves your tree can't really pull apart.

Phrase every branching question so a student can answer it without knowing which position it's steering them toward. No "do you accept semi-compatibilism?" type questions. Keep it to maybe 10 leaves max. And don't worry about being exhaustive — if there are obscure views you can't fit, just note what got left out.
