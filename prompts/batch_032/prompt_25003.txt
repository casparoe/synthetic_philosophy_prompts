I'm a journalist working on a long-read about free will, and I've been talking to philosophers across the spectrum. One of them walked me through something close to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. The version I got was this: to be ultimately morally responsible for what you do, you'd have to be ultimately responsible for the state of mind that produced the action. But to be responsible for that state of mind, you'd have to be responsible for the prior state that produced it. The regress only stops if you are causa sui — the cause of your own character and mental makeup — and that, the argument says, is impossible. So nobody is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything.

When I described this to a colleague who isn't a philosopher, he pushed back with an analogy that I've since heard from several people in roughly the same form. He said: imagine you inherit a house you didn't build. At the outset, nothing about it is your doing. But over decades you renovate — you knock down walls, add windows, replace every fixture, repaint every room. Eventually every square foot of the house reflects a decision you made. You didn't lay the foundation, but you are now responsible for the house as it stands. Character works the same way: you start with raw material you didn't choose, but through reflection, effort, and self-discipline you reshape yourself across a lifetime, and at some point the person you are is genuinely your own creation. The regress, on this view, isn't infinite — it just takes time.

I can feel the pull of this analogy, and I understand why it shows up so often in conversation. But I also suspect it's doing a great deal of quiet work and may be importing features of house renovation that Strawson's argument is precisely designed to deny. I'd like you to audit it for me systematically. 

First, give me the correspondence table: what in the analogy maps to what in the target domain of character and moral responsibility? Be explicit about what plays the role of the inherited house, the renovations, the renovator, the passage of time, the completed house, and anything else you think the analogy implicitly posits.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in whether the renovator — the entity that surveys the house, forms intentions about it, and executes changes — corresponds to something that actually exists in the target, or whether the analogy smuggles in a homunculus that Strawson would say is exactly what's at issue. But don't stop there: any structural disanalogy that matters for the argument's intended use should go on the table.

Third, give me a verdict: once the disanalogies are made explicit, does the conclusion — that we can become responsible for our own characters through gradual self-modification — survive the audit? Or was the analogy carrying a conclusion that the target domain can't support on its own? If there's a better analogy that does the same work more honestly, I'd like to hear that too.
