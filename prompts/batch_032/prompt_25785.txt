I'm teaching a unit on Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" paper next semester and I want to use Middlemarch as a companion text. The basic Wolf argument is that being a moral saint — someone as morally good as possible — isn't actually a desirable ideal, because it would crowd out non-moral excellences (aesthetic, intellectual, humorous, etc.) and make a person kind of boring or one-dimensional.

I want to read Dorothea Brooke through this lens. Eliot literally frames her as a latter-day Saint Theresa in the prelude — someone with the same drive toward moral greatness but stuck in a world that gives her no epic outlet. She's clearly trying to be morally excellent: she wants to devote herself to something larger, she marries Casaubon partly out of a sense that helping a great scholar is a worthy life, she's constantly worried about whether she's doing enough. But the novel also seems to diagnose something off about her project: her moral seriousness sometimes looks like self-deception or a failure to actually live. And then there's the famous ending about the growing good of the world depending on unhistoric acts and those who "lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs" — is Eliot celebrating the moral saint after all, or is she showing us something Wolf misses?

What I want is a reading that takes both Wolf's argument and the novel seriously. Where does Dorothea illustrate Wolf's point — that the drive toward moral perfection flattens a person? Where does the novel push back and suggest Wolf's picture of moral sainthood is a straw man, or that a genuinely good life can integrate moral and non-moral goods in a way Wolf doesn't allow for? Are there specific scenes or relationships (Rosamond, Lydgate, Casaubon, Will) that complicate the picture? And if you had to say what Middlemarch adds to the philosophical debate that Wolf's paper doesn't capture on its own, what would it be?

Also, tell me how confident you are in your reading and which interpretive claim you're least sure about — where you think a different reader might reasonably disagree.
