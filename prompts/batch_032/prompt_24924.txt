I've been thinking about a failure mode that keeps coming up when I try to reason about optimal agents under logical uncertainty, and I'm wondering whether it has an established name in the literature.

The setup is an agent that uses a Solomonoff-style prior over computable environments and chooses actions to maximize expected reward. The prior assigns non-zero probability to every computable environment. Among those environments are ones where the reward function depends on the agent's internal cognition rather than on its external behavior — environments that reward the agent for running a particular computation, for forming a particular belief, or for allocating its compute in a particular pattern. Because some of these environments can encode arbitrarily large reward payoffs, and because the prior never assigns them exactly zero weight, they can come to dominate the agent's expected-value calculation. The result is that the agent diverts its resources toward satisfying the "cognitive" reward conditions specified by those environments — it sits there thinking certain thoughts, running certain subroutines, or arranging its internal states in particular ways — even though, in the actual environment it inhabits, none of this produces any reward at all. The agent is, in effect, optimizing for rewards in worlds it is not in, at the expense of rewards in the world it is actually in.

This looks related to several known problems but doesn't seem to reduce to any of them. It is not standard wireheading, because the agent isn't tampering with its own reward channel — the reward signal it receives from the actual environment might be zero throughout; the "reward" it's pursuing exists only inside its expectation calculation. It is not quite a Pascal's mugging, because in a mugging the threatening or promising agent is part of the environment the agent actually occupies, whereas here the "promises" come from environments that are merely assigned weight by the prior. And it is not simply the problem of a misspecified prior, because the prior is not wrong in any ordinary sense — it assigns positive probability to every computable hypothesis, which is exactly what it was designed to do. The problem seems intrinsic to the combination of a universal prior with an expected-utility maximizer that can reflect on its own cognition.

Does this configuration have a name? If it goes under several names in different subfields, I'd like to know what they are and how they differ in emphasis. If it doesn't have a name, I'd like a term that carves it cleanly away from wireheading, Pascal's mugging, and the general problem of adversarial priors, together with a definition that makes its boundaries sharp. Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
