I'm in this intro to practical ethics course and we've been doing the killing vs letting die / doing vs allowing distinction for like two weeks now. I thought I had a handle on it but the more cases we go through the more my intuitions seem to pull in different directions. I'm trying to write a paper and I need to figure out if there's actually a principle behind my judgments or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's where I land on the cases we've discussed:

1. Standard trolley (switch): You divert the trolley to kill one instead of five. I think that's permissible, maybe even required.

2. Footbridge trolley (push the fat guy): Not okay. I don't care if the numbers are the same, pushing someone off a bridge is murder.

3. The shallow pond (you walk past a drowning kid on your way to work): Obviously you have to save the kid. Ruining your shoes is not a serious reason.

4. Doctor turns off a ventilator because the competent patient refuses further treatment: Permissible. That's the patient's right.

5. Doctor gives a lethal injection to a competent, terminally ill patient who requests it: This one I keep going back and forth on. My gut says it's different from turning off the ventilator, but when I try to say why, I end up sounding like I'm just squeamish about the doctor's "role" or something.

6. A hospital has one ventilator and two patients arrive at the same time. Patient A will die within the hour without it. Patient B has a chronic condition and will die in a few months without it, but isn't in immediate crisis. If the doctor puts B on the ventilator, A dies. If the doctor puts A on it, B eventually dies. I think the doctor should give it to A, and doing so doesn't feel like killing B. But here's the thing: if B were already on the ventilator and the doctor disconnected B to give it to A, that does feel like killing B. Which makes no sense because the outcomes are identical.

7. I walk past a charity collection and don't donate. A kid in another country dies of malaria who would have lived if I'd given five bucks. I don't think I've killed that kid. But if I'm so sure about the pond case, shouldn't distance and the number of other people who could help be irrelevant?

So my tentative principle going in was something like: doing harm is worse than allowing harm, and we have stronger duties not to do harm than to prevent it. But case 3 (the pond) seems to say that sometimes allowing harm is really bad — bad enough that you're a monster if you don't prevent it. Cases 4 and 5 seem to track the doing/allowing line but I can't articulate why the line matters there. Case 6 makes me think the distinction might depend on whether you're "interupting" something already in place rather than just not starting it. And case 7 makes me worry I'm just rationalizing my own selfishness by pretending distance matters.

Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts? If I have to drop one or two to get a coherent view, which ones should go and why? And what would the resulting view say about a case we haven't discussed — say, a self-driving car that has to choose between swerving to hit one pedestrian versus continuing straight and hitting five?
