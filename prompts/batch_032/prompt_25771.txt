I'm taking an upper-division course on Nietzsche this semester, and I'm developing a paper that reads Harold Ramis's film *Groundhog Day* (1993) through the lens of the eternal recurrence. I'd like comments on my argument — in particular, on whether I'm being faithful to Nietzsche and whether I'm giving the film a fair reading.

My central claim is that the film dramatizes something close to the eternal recurrence but ultimately resolves it in a way that Nietzsche would find evasive. In *The Gay Science* §341, the demon tells you that "this life as you now live it and have lived it you will have to live once again and innumerable times again; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unspeakably small or great in your life must return to you, all in the same succession and sequence." The test is whether you can affirm your life exactly as it is, down to the last detail, repeated infinitely. Phil Connors, by contrast, gets to revise: he moves through hedonism, despair, and eventual moral self-improvement across his repetitions, and the film treats this arc as redemptive. The film's logic seems to be that if you get enough chances, you can *become* someone whose life is worth affirming. Nietzsche's logic is that you don't get another draft — the question is whether you can say yes to the one you already have.

Where I think the film is more interesting, and more Nietzschean, is in its middle section. Phil's repeated suicides — waking up again each time despite every attempt to end it — capture something of the psychological weight Nietzsche thought the thought would carry: the possibility that it would crush you rather than elevate you. But the film resolves this by letting Phil escape the loop once he becomes sufficiently virtuous, which strikes me as closer to a moralized teleology than to *amor fati*. Nietzsche's affirmation isn't conditional on self-improvement; it's an affirmation of what already is, including everything one might wish were otherwise.

I have two main worries about my argument. First, I may be unfair to the film. One could argue that Phil's transformation isn't about escaping the recurrence but about learning to affirm the *one* day he actually has — February 2nd — and that the final escape is just a narrative concession to the genre, not the film's philosophical point. On this reading, the film is more Nietzschean than I'm giving it credit for. Second, I'm worried I'm flattening Nietzsche. I'm leaning heavily on the existential-test reading of the eternal recurrence, but I know there is a significant interpretive tradition — Paul Loeb's work, for instance — that takes Nietzsche's cosmological claims about recurrence seriously rather than treating the doctrine as a mere thought experiment. If Nietzsche meant the recurrence literally, does that change what the film gets wrong, or does it open up a different way of reading the film altogether?

I'd appreciate comments on whether my reading of the film is defensible, whether my use of Nietzsche holds up under the interpretive pressure I'm acknowledging, and what I might be missing in the relationship between the two.
