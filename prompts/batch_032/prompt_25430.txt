For the better part of a decade I have been covering disputes in systematics and philosophy of biology, and I keep returning to the species problem because it strikes me as the purest example of a scientific debate that should have been settled by now — and has not been. The empirical picture has only grown richer: we now have whole-genome phylogenies, fine-grained ecological data, population-genetic models of speciation with and without gene flow, and detailed case studies of ring species, hybrid zones, and cryptic species complexes. And yet the conceptual landscape is, if anything, more fragmented than it was when Mayr and Dobzhansky were writing. The literature now contains something like two dozen species concepts — biological, phylogenetic, ecological, evolutionary, genotypic cluster, cohesion, recognition, and on — and the people who work on this do not seem closer to consensus than they were thirty years ago. It is not that anyone denies the phenomena; it is that the same phenomena get carved up differently depending on which concept you start with, and there is no agreed-upon meta-framework for choosing among the concepts. The result is a peculiar situation: a field with more data than ever and no convergence.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test, is that the impasse is not a factual disagreement at all, nor even a straightforward verbal dispute, but something closer to a disagreement about what kind of work the species category is supposed to do for us — a disagreement about the job description, not the candidate. But I can think of at least four other diagnoses that have been offered in the literature and at the conferences I have attended: (a) it is a genuine empirical question that will be resolved once we understand the speciation process well enough; (b) it is a verbal dispute that could be dissolved by agreeing to use different terms for different concepts and getting on with the biology; (c) it is a case of incommensurable theoretical frameworks, where each species concept embeds assumptions that make the others invisible or irrelevant from within; (d) the category "species" is a family-resemblance or cluster concept, and the search for necessary and sufficient conditions was the mistake all along. I want you to give me your best diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, with reasons drawn from what the participants actually argue — not a survey of the concepts themselves — and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new kind of data, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question, or an honest acknowledgment that no resolution is possible and why. I also want you to flag how confident you are in each major claim you make, and to single out the point you are least sure of.
