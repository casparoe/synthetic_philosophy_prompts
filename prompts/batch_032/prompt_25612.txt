I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading the scientific realism literature with great interest. The argument I find most troubling—and most compelling—is the pessimistic meta-induction: the claim that the historical record of empirically successful theories that were later rejected as false gives us reason to expect that our current best theories will likewise turn out to be false.

I want to examine this argument through a case I know firsthand: the transition from Newtonian gravitation to general relativity. Newton's theory was spectacularly successful for more than two centuries. It predicted planetary orbits, the tides, the oblateness of the Earth, and the return of Halley's comet. Yet general relativity revealed it to be, strictly speaking, false: it posited instantaneous action at a distance and absolute space, and it gave incorrect predictions for the perihelion advance of Mercury. At the same time, Newtonian gravity was not simply discarded. It survives as a low-energy, weak-field limiting case of GR, and its central mathematical structure is recoverable from the Einstein field equations.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the pessimistic meta-induction. Does the Newton-to-Einstein transition strengthen the argument, weaken it, or require us to refine what the argument claims? In particular, address the realist response that appeals to the preservation of approximate truth or structural continuity across theory change, and say whether this episode gives the realist a satisfactory reply or merely forces the anti-realist to formulate the induction more carefully. Keep your answer to at most 600 words.
