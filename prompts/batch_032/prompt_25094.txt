Before I read any more proposed solutions to the problem of superrationality, I want to know what a solution is supposed to look like. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of superrational choice in symmetric games with common knowledge of rationality must deliver. Include the following.

Which games it must get right. Prisoner's Dilemma is the flagship, but what else — Stag Hunt, Chicken, the symmetric Battle of the Sexes, asymmetric variants where payoffs differ but the structure is isomorphic? And what does "getting it right" mean in each case? The output cannot just be a strategy profile; it has to be a story about why that profile is uniquely rational for agents who know each other to be rational, not merely an equilibrium that happens to be Pareto-superior.

Which paradoxes and puzzles from the existing literature it must dissolve or at least address. At minimum: the "I know you'll cooperate so I'll defect" regress (if the reasoning that recommends cooperation depends on the other's cooperation, the temptation to defect re-enters at the level of reasoning about reasoning); the problem of embedding — why the reasoning that works in the one-shot PD does not recommend cooperating with a rock or a simpleton who happens to mirror your move by brute causal mechanism; and the tension with causal decision theory, which seems to rule out the very pattern of choice superrationality demands. Add any others you take to be central.

Which results from classical game theory it must be consistent with and which it is allowed to overturn. For instance, must it preserve the existence of Nash equilibria in the games it addresses? Must it respect the standard result that defection strictly dominates cooperation in the one-shot PD, or is it permitted to reject dominance reasoning under common knowledge? Be explicit about what gets kept and what gets revised.

The relationship it must specify between superrationality and ordinary individual rationality. Are they two distinct modes of reasoning that agents switch between depending on what they know about each other? Does one reduce to the other? Or is superrationality simply what individual rationality becomes once the fact of common knowledge is properly taken into account — so that the apparent conflict was always an artifact of an incomplete specification of the decision problem?

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. That is the part I most want to see: if some of these requirements are in tension, or if satisfying one forces you to abandon another, say so and explain the conflict.

Then give a one-paragraph scorecard for how Hofstadter's original proposal, evidential decision theory, and the program-equilibrium approach do against the list. I am less interested in the scores themselves than in which desiderata do the most work to separate the contenders.

Keep the whole thing to at most 800 words.
