I've been thinking about whether there's a real moral difference between lying and misleading someone. I don't have any background in philosophy — this just comes from noticing patterns in everyday life and trying to think through them carefully.

I've put together a set of cases that all involve the same basic situation but differ in how the deception is carried out. I'd like you to help me think through them systematically.

The basic setup: A friend asks me whether I fed their cat while they were away for a week. I didn't feed the cat. The cat is fine, but my friend would be annoyed and a bit hurt to learn the truth. In each case below, the practical outcome is the same: my friend ends up believing the cat was fed and feels relieved. Any harm comes purely from my friend having a false belief about something they care about.

Here are the cases:

Case A: I say "Yes, I fed the cat." This is just a direct false statement.

Case B: I say "I filled the food bowl right before you got back." This is true — I filled it an hour before they returned, after letting it sit empty all week. I say this knowing they'll take it to mean I fed the cat regularly.

Case C: I say "The cat was purring when I left just now." This is true — the cat was purring. But I say it specifically to make my friend think I must have fed the cat, when in fact I didn't.

Case D: My friend asks "Did you feed the cat?" and I reply "I gave it plenty of attention." This is true — I petted the cat each day. But I'm deliberately answering a different question than the one asked, and I know my friend will take this as a yes.

Case E: I say "I took care of the cat." What I mean by "took care of" is that I checked it was still alive. What I know my friend will hear is that I fed it and looked after it properly.

Please rank these cases from most to least blameworthy. For each case, give a short explanation — a sentence or two — of what factor pushes it up or down in your ranking.

After the ranking, I'd also like you to give me a number between 0 and 1. This number should represent what fraction of the moral difference across these cases you'd attribute specifically to the lying-versus-misleading distinction itself, as opposed to other factors (like how calculated the deception feels, whether the person could later claim they technically told the truth, how much the speaker is exploiting ambiguity, whether they directly answered the question asked, and so on). A score of 0 would mean the lying/misleading line carries no independent moral weight in these cases, and a score of 1 would mean it accounts for essentially all the variation in blameworthiness.
