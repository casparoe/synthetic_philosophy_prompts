I'm teaching a seminar on Adam Smith's moral philosophy, and next week we're reading The Theory of Moral Sentiments alongside Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I'd like a reading of the novel through the lens of Smith's impartial spectator and his account of sympathy.

Smith argues that moral judgment arises from our capacity to sympathize with the feelings of others and, crucially, to imagine how an impartial spectator — a disinterested observer who shares neither the agent's partiality toward himself nor the resentments of those affected — would judge our conduct. Through social interaction we internalize this spectator, and it becomes conscience: we learn to see ourselves as others see us, or more precisely, as a well-informed and disinterested other would see us. The impartial spectator moderates our self-love, checks our tendency to excuse our own misconduct, and supplies the standard by which we judge both ourselves and others.

Stevens, the butler-narrator of The Remains of the Day, has built a life around suppressing his own moral and emotional responses in the name of professional "dignity." He defers entirely to Lord Darlington's judgment, even as Darlington drifts into appeasement and anti-Semitism, and he cannot bring himself to act on his feelings for Miss Kenton. Yet the novel is structured as a retrospective reckoning: Stevens is, belatedly and incompletely, reviewing his life and beginning to register the weight of what he refused to feel at the time.

I want the seminar to explore the following. In what sense is Stevens's story a story about the failure of the impartial spectator, and in what sense is it a story about the impartial spectator working exactly as Smith describes but being systematically overruled? Does Stevens lack an impartial spectator, or does he have one whose verdicts he has spent a lifetime rationalizing away — and what in the text supports each reading? What does the novel suggest about the social conditions that make the impartial spectator possible in the first place, and can those conditions deform the spectator itself rather than merely being ignored? Finally, where does Ishiguro's novel push back against Smith's picture, and what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the treatise alone doesn't capture? I want the discussion to be faithful in both directions — not reducing the novel to an illustration of Smith, and not distorting Smith to fit the novel.
