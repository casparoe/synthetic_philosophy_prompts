I'm a novelist — mid-career, a couple books out, not famous — and I'm working on something new that's been tying me in knots. The book plays with narrative time in ways I don't fully have a theory for: scenes that loop, a narrator who might be experiencing events out of order, that kind of thing. So I've been going back to the obvious touchstones, and I just finished rereading Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five* for the first time since college.

Here's the thing. The Tralfamadorian view of time — all moments exist at once, stacked up like the Rocky Mountains, nobody really dies because they're alive in all the other moments — that's basically a block universe, right? Eternalism, B-theory, the whole package. And Vonnegut seems to offer it as a kind of consolation: if every moment just *is*, then nothing is ever really lost, and "so it goes" is the only sane response to death. But I've also been reading about the thermodynamic arrow of time — entropy increase, the Second Law, the fact that there's an objective physical difference between past and future that isn't just a trick of human psychology. And I can't shake the feeling that these two pictures are in tension, but I can't articulate exactly how.

What I'd love is a reading of the novel through the lens of the philosophy of time, specifically the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow. Where does the Tralfamadorian eternalism genuinely conflict with the arrow of time, and where is the conflict only apparent? Does the novel have resources to push back against the physicist who says "entropy increases, the past is objectively different from the future, your block universe doesn't erase that asymmetry"? And here's the part I care about most as a writer: does the *form* of the book — the way Billy Pilgrim slips between moments, the way the narrative structure itself refuses chronological order — do something that the Tralfamadorian *philosophy* as stated can't? I'm trying to figure out whether Vonnegut is offering a coherent metaphysical picture, or whether the novel's power comes precisley from the fact that the philosophy and the form are pulling in different directions, and the book knows it.
