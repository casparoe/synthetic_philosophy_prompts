I'm a software engineer who works on static analysis tools — abstract interpretation, symbolic execution, that kind of thing. I'm pretty comfortable with soundness, completeness, fixed points, abstract domains, and the fact that any nontrivial static analysis has to approximate (thanks Rice's theorem, you beautiful bastard).

I recently stumbled on this thing in epistemology called "the problem of logical omniscience." From what I gather, standard possible-worlds models of belief imply that an agent believes all logical consequences of their beliefs — which is obviously nuts for any real agent, since we don't all walk around knowing whether P=NP just because we believe the axioms of arithmetic.

This sounds to me like it's exactly the same issue as soundness vs. completeness in program analysis: an ideal reasoner would be sound and complete (believes all and only the logical consequences of their beliefs), but real agents are more like real static analyzers — they have to approximate, they're resource-bounded, they're incomplete. Am I right that this is basically the same problem, just with modal operators instead of abstract transformers?

Can you restate the problem of logical omniscience and the main families of solutions (bounded rationality approaches, impossible worlds semantics, awareness logics, whatever else there is) entirely in the vocabulary of program analysis? I want a systematic translation, not an analogy. For each solution approach, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all.

Then the residue report: is there anything the epistemologists are worried about that has no expression in how we think about static analysis? And if so, is that because we're missing a normative dimension — like, what an agent *ought* to believe, not just what they *can* compute — or because their problem dissolves once you accept that all real reasoners are resource-bounded and the interesting question is how they approximate?
