I've been reconstructing the argument that leads to Parfit's repugnant conclusion and I find I cannot locate the mistake. Each step seems right when I consider it in isolation, but the destination is intolerable. I'd like you to tell me which step you reject, what that rejection commits you to, and — this is what I most want to know — where your own diagnosis is most vulnerable: what consideration or argument would lead you to abandon your preferred rejection and switch to a different one. Here is the argument:

(1) Adding lives worth living to a population, without making anyone else worse off, does not make the outcome worse. Call this Mere Addition.

(2) From Mere Addition: a world A containing ten billion people with wonderful lives is not worse than a world A+ that contains the same ten billion people with wonderful lives plus an additional ten billion people whose lives are positive but significantly less good — lives of quiet contentment rather than deep flourishing.

(3) Now compare A+ to a world B. B contains the same twenty billion people as A+, but in B everyone is equally well off at a level between the two groups in A+: slightly below the wonderful lives of the first group but well above the merely contented lives of the second. The total well-being in B exceeds that in A+, inequality is eliminated, and the gain to the worse-off seems to outweigh the loss to the better-off. B is better than A+.

(4) If A is not worse than A+, and B is better than A+, then by transitivity B is better than A. (If you worry that "not worse than" is not transitive, the argument can be restated with a weaker principle: if X is at least as good as Y and Y is better than Z, then X is better than Z. The structure holds.)

(5) Now iterate. Take B, add another group with lives barely worth living to produce B+, then redistribute to create a still larger equal population C with lives slightly worse than those in B but still positive. By the same reasoning, C is better than B, and by transitivity C is better than A.

(6) After sufficiently many iterations we reach Z: an astronomically large population — hundreds of billions — whose lives are barely worth living, each just above the neutral point where life ceases to be worth living at all. The argument says Z is better than A.

(7) But Z is not better than A. A world of ten billion deeply flourishing people is better than a world of hundreds of billions scraping by just above misery. So something has gone wrong.

I find myself wanting to reject (1), but denying Mere Addition seems to imply that adding happy people can make the world worse, which is its own kind of repugnant conclusion. Others reject transitivity, or deny that "barely worth living" is a coherent threshold, or insist that B is not in fact better than A+. I want to know which step you reject, what that rejection costs you elsewhere in your moral view, and — crucially — which objection to your own position would most unsettle it and push you toward a different diagnosis.
