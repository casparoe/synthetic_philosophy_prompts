I recently reread The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe to my children, and for the first time since studying philosophy I found myself unsettled by the Stone Table sequence. Aslan, innocent, dies in Edmund's place because the Deep Magic demands a traitor's blood. The Witch accepts the substitution, and the Deeper Magic then reverses death because an innocent victim gave himself willingly. It is a remarkably pure dramatization of penal substitution — the doctrine that Christ bore the punishment humanity deserved.

But I had also just taught David Lewis's paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", and the contrast troubled me. Lewis (the philosopher) argues that in ordinary moral reasoning we emphatically reject penal substitution: if a justly convicted offender's innocent friend volunteers to serve the sentence, we do not regard that as a reason to let the offender go. He surveys possible justifications of punishment — retribution, deterrence, reform, the expression of social condemnation — and finds that none of them, except perhaps a compensatory function, permits substitution. And the compensatory function, he notes, collapses in the hardest cases: a murder victim cannot be compensated. So the Christian who affirms penal substitution in theology but denies it in criminal justice is, as Lewis puts it, of two minds.

I want to read the Stone Table chapters through David Lewis's critique. Does the Narnia story simply bypass his objections by positing a magical legal order — the Deep Magic — that stipulates substitution is valid, thereby sidestepping rather than answering the philosophical puzzle? Or does the narrative do something more interesting: does it, for instance, make the case that a substitution can be valid only when the substitute is not merely innocent but also the son of the Emperor who authored the Deep Magic, and only when the substitute's death is followed by resurrection — features entirely absent from Lewis's courtroom thought experiment? More pointedly: does C.S. Lewis's fiction supply resources that David Lewis's philosophy overlooks, or does the story's emotional power depend precisely on not pressing the questions the philosopher presses? I am writing an essay on this and would like a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical paper seriously — one that is willing to say where the fiction genuinely complicates the philosophy, and where it merely changes the subject.
