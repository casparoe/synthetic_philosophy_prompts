I am a third-year philosophy PhD student trying to settle on a dissertation topic within work on the meta-problem of consciousness. By "the meta-problem" I mean, roughly, the problem of explaining why we are disposed to judge that there is a hard problem of consciousness — why we find it so natural to think that phenomenal experience resists physical explanation, even if physicalism is true.

I have about three years and a background in philosophy of mind with some cognitive science coursework. I cannot run experiments, but I can engage seriously with empirical literature.

Here is how I see the current landscape. One axis of disagreement concerns the relationship between the meta-problem and the hard problem itself. Some hold that a solution to the meta-problem would effectively dissolve the hard problem, or at least shift the burden of proof decisively against non-physicalist views. Others hold that the two problems are largely independent: you could fully explain why we have dualist intuitions and still face the hard problem untouched. A second axis concerns the kind of explanation we should be after. Some pursue cognitive-architectural explanations: what features of our introspective mechanisms, self-models, or predictive processing generate the intuition of a private, ineffable, non-physical qualitative realm? Others pursue evolutionary or teleofunctional explanations: why would creatures like us develop the concept of phenomenal consciousness, and what role does that concept play in our cognitive economy? Still others pursue conceptual or semantic explanations: perhaps phenomenal concepts themselves — their inferential role, their mode of presentation, their relation to demonstratives or recognitional capacities — are the source of the trouble, and the meta-problem is really a problem about concept possession. A third axis is methodological: some treat the meta-problem as continuous with empirical psychology and think progress will come from studying introspection, source monitoring, agency detection, or mind perception; others treat it as a largely a priori project that does not require new data.

Given this landscape, which two or three lines of work would you prioritize for someone in my position, and in what order? I want a defended ordering, not a survey. For each line of work you recommend: explain why it is upstream of other questions in the area — if we settled it, what else would fall into place? — how tractable it is for someone with my skill set, and what a successful three-year contribution would look like concretely. I also want to know which approaches you would deprioritize and why — for instance, because they are already crowded, because they depend on empirical results unlikely to arrive soon, or because they are downstream of something else that needs to be settled first. If there is a promising line of work I have not listed, I would like to hear about that as well.
