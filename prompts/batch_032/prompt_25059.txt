But I want to know if I'm flattening it too much.

Here's what I think I understand. Expressivists say moral sentences like "lying is wrong" don't express propositions that can be true or false — they express non-cognitive attitudes, like disapproval. So the meaning of "lying is wrong" is something like BOO(LYING). The Frege–Geach problem is that "lying is wrong" also shows up embedded: "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong." In the antecedent, the speaker isn't disapproving of lying — they might even think lying is fine. But modus ponens still goes through: from that conditional plus "lying is wrong," you validly infer "getting your little brother to lie is wrong." So the semantics has to assign the same meaning to "lying is wrong" whether it's asserted or embedded, but BOO(LYING) doesn't compose under the conditional — what does BOO(LYING) → BOO(GETTING-YOUR-BROTHER-TO-LIE) even mean?
