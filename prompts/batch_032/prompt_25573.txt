I'm taking a constitutional theory seminar this term, and we've spent the last several weeks on the idea of constitutional precommitment — the thought, developed most prominently by Jon Elster in his work on Ulysses and the Sirens and by Stephen Holmes in his writings on the paradox of self-binding, that a political community can rationally tie its own hands in advance, much as Ulysses had himself lashed to the mast, to prevent future majorities from succumbing to dangerous or myopic temptations. The central puzzle, as our instructor keeps pressing us on, is how to make sense of the claim that a democracy can legitimately disable its own future democratic choices without thereby ceasing to be democratic.

I want to use this framework to examine a particularly stark and, I think, undertheorized instance of constitutional precommitment: Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, the so-called eternity clause. This provision declares that certain elements of the constitutional order — the principles of human dignity laid down in Article 1, the democratic and federal principles of Article 20, and the division of the Federation into Länder — are permanently shielded from amendment. Not merely subject to a supermajority hurdle, but categorically off the table, with no provision for the clause's own repeal. The Federal Constitutional Court has treated this as a genuine and enforceable legal limit on the amending power, not merely hortatory language. The drafters' motivation is clear enough: having witnessed the Weimar Republic's legal self-destruction, they wanted to ensure that no future majority could dismantle the constitutional core through formally lawful procedures. But the theoretical questions this raises for precommitment theory seem to me much harder than the standard treatments acknowledge.

Please analyze the eternity clause through the lens of precommitment theory in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each point, identify either a way in which the theory illuminates the clause — what it explains, justifies, or clarifies — or a way in which the clause exposes a tension or limitation in the theory, particularly where the analogy between individual and collective self-binding breaks down. I am especially interested in the intergenerational problem: Ulysses chose his own ropes and gave his own orders, but later generations of Germans were simply born into a constitutional order they had no hand in making irrevocable.
