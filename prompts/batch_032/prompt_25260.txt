I'm in this interdisciplinary reading group — mostly philosophers and a few biologists — and we've been working through some papers on reductionism. Every single meeting, when someone argues that some phenomenon (consciousness last week, this week it was protein folding) can't be fully explained by its parts, someone else nods sagely and says "well, the whole is more than the sum of its parts," and the conversation just... stops. Like it's a settled argument. Last week I finally pushed back and asked what that actually *means*, and the guy who said it got kind of flustered and said something about "nonlinear interactions" and changed the subject.

So now I'm fixated on this. "The whole is more than the sum of its parts" — it's obviously doing real argumentative work for people, but I can't tell if it's saying something trivial (like, of course a pile of bricks isn't a house) or something radical (like, there are properties of wholes that can't in principle be derived from facts about parts). And I suspect different people in the room mean completely different things by it. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan might be making, from the boringly true to the wildly controversial, and say for each one whether it's plausible and whether it actually does the work people want it to do — namely, blocking a reductionist explanation? I'd especially like to know which reading, if any, actually rules out the kind of reduction where you can in principle derive the behavior of the whole from the laws governing the parts plus the arrangement, even if it's computationally impossible in practice. And if none of them do, what *should* someone say instead if they want to push back on reductionism without just gesturing?
