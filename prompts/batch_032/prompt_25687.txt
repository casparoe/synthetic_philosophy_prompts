I've been following the Solomonoff induction / Occam's razor discourse on LessWrong and related blogs for years and it feels completely stuck. The basic shape of the debate, as I see it: Group A says Solomonoff induction is the "correct" formalization of Occam's razor — it gives you a universal prior that converges on the true distribution for any computable environment, and the arbitrary choice of UTM washes out in the limit. Group B says the UTM-dependence doesn't wash out for any finite amount of data a real agent ever has, the prior is uncomputable so it can't be a normative standard (ought implies can, or at least ought implies approximable), and anyway you're smuggling in a very specific metaphysics by treating the world as the output of a program. Group C says both sides are missing the point: Solomonoff induction isn't meant to tell you what to believe, it's a formal result about the limits of prediction, and confusing that with epistemology is the whole problem.

I genuinely can't tell whether this is a factual disagreement (about what happens with finite data under different UTMs), a philosophical disagreement about the nature of normative epistemology (does a standard have to be computable to be binding?), or just three groups using "induction" to mean different things. Diagnose this. And if your diagnosis is that it's a mix of all three, say which part is doing the most work and whether anything — a theorem, a conceptual distinction, an empirical result — could actually move anyone.
