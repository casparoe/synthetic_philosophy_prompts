In many theistic religious communities — across Christianity, Judaism, and Islam — the problem of evil receives two sharply different treatments depending on context. In academic theology, apologetics, and philosophy of religion, the problem is met with theodicies: systematic arguments that aim to show that the existence of God is compatible with the existence of suffering. The free-will defense, soul-making theodicies, greater-goods theodicies, and skeptical theism all belong to this project. Yet in pastoral contexts — at a funeral, at the bedside of a dying child, in the immediate aftermath of a disaster — those same clergy and theologians often set theodicies aside entirely. They do not explain the suffering. They sit in silence, they lament, they offer presence without justification. A chaplain who would assign a soul-making theodicy in a seminary course will not offer it to the parents who just lost their son.

What philosophical commitments does this dual practice presuppose? One possibility is that it presupposes a division of labor: theodicy answers the intellectual problem of evil but is pastorally inappropriate because the grieving person is not in a position to receive it — a view about the ethics of speech rather than about the adequacy of theodicy itself. A stronger possibility is that the practice presupposes that theodicy fails as an answer to the problem of evil in its existential form, and that the academic project and the pastoral practice rest on incompatible understandings of what the problem of evil actually is. Still another possibility is that the practice is philosophically underdetermined: several different theories of evil, of God, and of the aims of religious language could all license the same duality.

Reconstruct the implicit theory — or the range of theories — that could make sense of this duality. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice leaves the underlying philosophy open. In doing so, please separate what is settled or widely agreed among philosophers of religion who have examined this territory from what is genuinely contested, and flag any place where you are presenting one side of a live debate as if it were the consensus.
