I'm a TA for an undergraduate course in political philosophy, and in two weeks I have to lead a fifty-minute discussion section on what makes consent valid — not the applied-ethics checklist (informed consent forms, IRB protocols), but the underlying philosophical question of what consent is doing such that deception, coercion, and incompetence each threaten it, and whether they threaten it in the same way or in genuinely different ways. The students will have read a short piece by Heidi Hurd, so they will have encountered the idea that consent is a normative transformation — it turns a wrong into something permissible — but I doubt they'll have internalised what that means for the structure of the problem. I don't need you to explain the material to me. I need a plan for how to sequence the fifty minutes so the distinctions build rather than sit next to each other as a list of three conditions.

Specifically: what idea has to be in place before the session can get anywhere — I suspect it's the distinction between consent as a mental state and consent as a communicative act, but I'm not sure whether that has to come before or after the normative-transformation point, and I don't know how to introduce it without ten minutes of abstract preamble. What single running example or scenario should carry the whole session, and what features does it need to have? I want one case I can return to and vary — change the kind of deception, change the kind of pressure, change the agent's capacities — without switching scenarios, so that by the end the students can see how the three threats relate rather than treating them as a list. Which misconceptions do students predictably form at each stage? I'm especially worried about: (a) 'any deception invalidates consent,' which collapses the distinction between fraud in the factum and fraud in the inducement before they even know those terms exist; (b) 'coercion is just feeling pressured,' which erases the difference between threats and hard choices; and (c) 'competence is a bright line,' which makes them think the problem is diagnosis rather than threshold. At what point in the sequence should I head each of these off, and how? What should I explicitly refuse to settle at this level — I'm guessing the ontology of consent (whether it's a mental state, a speech act, or a relational stance) is too much, and perhaps the question of whether hypothetical consent does any normative work at all — and how should I flag that without making the session feel incomplete? Finally, what one question can I pose in the last five minutes that will tell me whether they've grasped the structure of the problem rather than just memorised three conditions?
