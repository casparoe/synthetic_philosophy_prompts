So I'm a novelist — literary fiction, character-driven stuff — and I've stumbled onto this thing in experimental philosophy called the Knobe effect that's been keeping me up at night. The gist: when people evaluate whether someone acted *intentionally*, their judgment flips depending on whether the outcome was morally bad or good. Classic experiment: a CEO approves a profit-boosting project. In one condition, it harms the environment as a side effect; the CEO says he doesn't care, and subjects overwhelmingly say he *intentionally* harmed the environment. In the other condition, it *helps* the environment as a side effect; the CEO again says he doesn't care, and now subjects say he did *not* intentionally help. The action, the indifference, the causal structure — all identical. The only difference is the moral valence of the side effect. And that difference rewires our whole attribution of agency.

I'm trying to build a scene around this. My protagonist is a product manager at a mid-size tech company — not evil, not heroic, just someone navigating quarterly targets and office politics. I want the reader to experience the asymmetry from the inside, to catch themselves making the very judgment the Knobe effect describes, without me ever mentioning philosophy.

So here's what I'm asking: write a short story, maybe 800–1200 words, centered on a workplace decision where a character's action produces both an intended outcome and a morally freighted side effect. The story should be structured so that a reflective reader would notice the Knobe asymmetry in their own reaction to the character. Dont signal it. Dont moralize. Just construct the situation with enough precision that the effect does its work on the reader quietly.

Think very carefully about how you set up the two sides of the decision and how you manage the reader's moral sympathies. Respond in the style of an expert — I need the psychological architecture to be sound, not just a story with a lesson.
