I'm a retired physicist — spent my career in condensed matter, nothing fancy — and I've gotten really into Buddhist philosophy the last few years, especially Nāgārjuna and the Madhyamaka stuff on emptiness. Here's what I keep chewing on and I'd love to hear someone work through it properly.

Nāgārjuna's tetralemma says that for any claim you can examine, none of the four options — it's true, it's false, it's both, it's neither — ultimately hold up under analysis. Everything is empty of inherent nature. Now, I can't help noticing that when you look at quantum mechanics, especially the measurement problem, you get these situations where a system seems to resist being pinned down in any of those four boxes. A superposition isn't quite "the electron is here," it isn't quite "the electron is not here," and it's not straightforwardly "both" or "neither" in any classical sense. And then there's the whole business with quantum logic where the distributive law fails.

So here's my question: if Nāgārjuna were alive today and sat down with a solid understanding of quantum mechanics — not the pop-science version, the real formalism — what would he make of it? Would he see quantum phenomena as a kind of empirical illustration of what he was getting at with emptiness, or would he think physicists are making a category mistake by trying to map his dialectical method onto physical systems? I'm genuinely torn. Part of me thinks he'd say "yes, this is exactly what I meant — even the most basic properties of matter resist being captured by conventional conceptual frameworks." But another part thinks he'd say the physicists are still trying to describe an ultimately real world out there, just with wierder math, and that misses the whole point of emptiness.

I'm not asking for a reading list — I want someone to actually do the work of thinking through what position he'd likely take and why, given what we know of his arguments in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā.
