My younger brother was in a car accident last month and declared dead by neurological criteria after two examinations twenty-four hours apart. His body remained on a ventilator for three more days while the organ donation team did their work. During those three days his chest rose and fell, his skin was warm, his wounds continued to heal, and when I held his hand it felt like holding a sleeping person's hand. The nurses spoke of him in the past tense and referred to him as "the donor." The death certificate lists a date two days before his heart stopped.

I am not questioning the medical judgment or the donation — his organs saved four people, and he would have wanted that. But I am struggling with a philosophical question that has become urgently personal: what theory of life and death does this practice presuppose? The same body was dead for the purpose of extracting vital organs, yet alive enough that those organs remained viable, wounds healed, and a ventilator could drive respiration. What must we believe about what it means to be alive — or to be dead — for this to be coherent? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
