I've been defending the view that self-deception is a real thing — not just a sloppy label for motivated reasoning or wishful thinking or whatever, but a genuine psychological phenomenon where a person ends up sincerely believing something they have good reason to think is false, and their own agency is part of the causal story. I think the two standard paradoxes (the static one: how can you simultaneously believe p and not-p? and the dynamic one: how can you intentionally bring about a belief you know is unwarranted, since the intention seems to require you to already know the belief is false?) have workable resolutions. I'm not married to any particular resolution — could be a partitioning story, could be a temporal story where the deception unfolds in stages, could be a deflationary account where the "intention" isn't full-blown agential intention but something weaker. But I am committed to the bottom line: self-deception happens, it's psychologically real, and it's philosophically coherent.

Are there cases my view is committed to describing a certain way, where if the description fails, the view fails?
