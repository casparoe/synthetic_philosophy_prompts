I've spent a lot of time over the past few years thinking about when an action is morally required and when it's just especially good — the kind of thing we admire but don't think someone has failed at if they don't do it. I've kept a running list of cases and my gut reactions to them, and I'm trying to figure out whether there's a single principle behind my judgments or whether I'm just inconsistent. I'd like you to help me work through this.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **The pond.** A child is drowning in a shallow pond. You can wade in and save them, but it will ruin your suit. Verdict: you have a duty to save the child. This isn't heroic; it's basic.

2. **The kidney.** You learn you're a tissue match for a stranger who will die without a kidney transplant. The surgery carries real risks and requires weeks of recovery. Verdict: donating would be supererogatory — deeply admirable, but not something you've failed at if you don't do it.

3. **The ten percent.** You earn a comfortable salary in a wealthy country. You could give ten percent of your income to organizations that reliably save lives in very poor countries — bed nets, deworming, that sort of thing. Verdict: I cannot settle on one. Some days it feels like a duty, some days it feels supererogatory. This is the case that bothers me most.

4. **The 911 call.** You witness a car crash. Calling 911 costs you nothing. Verdict: duty, obviously.

5. **The grenade.** A soldier throws themselves onto a live grenade to absorb the blast and save their squad. Verdict: supererogatory. No one is required to do this.

6. **The lonely neighbor.** Your elderly neighbor has no family nearby and is clearly lonely. You could visit for an hour each week. It's not an emergency and the cost is modest but ongoing. Verdict: supererogatory, though I'd think less of someone who never did it.

7. **The doctor after hours.** A doctor at a rural clinic has a patient who will deteriorate badly unless someone stays three hours past the end of the shift to stabilize them. No one else is available. Verdict: duty. It comes with the role.

8. **The mugging.** You're walking home at night and see someone being attacked. Calling the police is a duty, but physically intervening would put you at serious risk of injury. Verdict: intervening is supererogatory.

9. **The life's work.** Someone spends their entire career working in disaster relief, living modestly and taking real risks, for decades. Verdict: the whole shape of the life feels supererogatory. But I'm not sure why it feels different from a whole life of doing many small duties — if each individual act were a duty, would the whole life still be supererogatory? Or does the fact that it feels supererogatory mean I don't really think the individual acts are duties?

What I'm stuck on is this: cases 1 and 8 both involve helping a stranger at some physical cost to yourself, but one feels like a duty and the other feels supererogatory. You might say the difference is the level of risk — but then case 2 involves serious risk and feels supererogatory, while case 7 involves no physical risk and feels like a duty because of the role. And case 3 involves no physical risk at all, yet I can't decide. So I suspect I'm using at least two or three different factors — magnitude of sacrifice, immediacy of the need, whether you have a special role, whether you're the only one who can help — but I can't state how they combine.

What I'm asking for: Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures most of these verdicts? State it as precisely as you can. Tell me which of my verdicts it preserves and which it overturns. Tell me what it says about case 3 — the ten percent — and about case 9. And if you have to overturn some of my verdicts, tell me which ones and why I should let them go. I'd also like you to test your principle against a case I haven't listed — one that might reveal a weakness in it.

Please think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert moral philosopher working through the problem step by step. I'm less interested in a label for the view than in seeing the reasoning laid out clearly.
