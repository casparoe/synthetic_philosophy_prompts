I'm in a behavioral ecology class and we were going over the handicap principle — Zahavi's idea that signals need to be costly to be honest, like peacock tails. Someone in discussion said "so this is basically just 'talk is cheap' with equations" and the instructor nodded along and said "right, the lesson is that only costly signals can be trusted."

I'm pretty sure they're collapsing some distinctions that matter. But when I tried to push back I realized I couldn't articulate exactly what the different versions of the claim would be. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "only costly signals are honest" could be making? I'm thinking about: does the handicap principle say cost is necessary for honesty, sufficient for honesty, or just that cost can stabilize honesty at equilibrium under certain conditions? Does it apply to all signals or only to signals of underlying quality (as opposed to, say, signals about intentions)? And what about cases where the cost is only paid if you're lying — like the risk of being found out — which isn't paid upfront the way a peacock's tail is? Give me a menu of the possible readings and say which ones the actual theory supports and which are overextensions people make when they turn it into a slogan.
