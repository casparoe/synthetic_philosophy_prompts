heres a midterm answer from my undergrad ethics class. the question was:

"Explain what the 'paradox of supererogation' is, and discuss how it bears on act utilitarianism and on Kantian deontology. Which theory handles it better, and why?"

Here's what the student wrote:

"The paradox of supererogation is the puzzle that arises when we consider acts that seem morally praiseworthy but not morally required, like jumping on a grenade to save your squad. If such an act is morally better than what duty demands, then why doesn't duty demand it? It seems like morality should require us to do the best thing. But if we say the act is required, then we lose the category of the supererogatory entirely, which seems to conflict with our ordinary moral thinking.

Act utilitarianism has a well-known problem with supererogation. Since act utilitarianism says the right action is always the one that maximizes overall utility, there is no conceptual space for doing more than what is required — you're always required to do whatever produces the most good. So act utilitarianism simply cannot accommodate supererogatory acts; every act is either required or impermissible.

Kantian deontology, by contrast, handles supererogation quite naturally through its distinction between perfect and imperfect duties. Perfect duties (like the duty not to lie) admit of no exceptions, but imperfect duties (like the duty of beneficence) are duties that we must fulfill but have latitude about when and how much. This latitude is exactly what supererogation consists in: going beyond the minimum that an imperfect duty demands. So, for example, giving a large portion of your income to charity is supererogatory precisely because the imperfect duty of beneficence only requires some reasonable amount of helping others. Kantianism therefore has a ready-made framework for supererogation and handles the paradox much better than act utilitarianism does."
