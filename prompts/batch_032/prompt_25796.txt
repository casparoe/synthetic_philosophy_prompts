Two friends of mine, both in the same philosophy of science seminar, got into it over coffee about whether Bayesianism has a fatal problem with old evidence. The issue came up cause they were discussing how Einstein used the known anomaly in Mercury's perihelion as evidence for general relativity — something astronomers had known about for decades before GR.

Dan said it's a devastating problem: if you already know some piece of evidence E, your credence in E is 1, and Bayes' theorem says P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E). If P(E) = 1, then P(H|E) = P(H). So old evidence literally cannot confirm a theory. But it obviously does — Mercury's orbit confirmed GR. So Bayesian confirmation theory is broken at its core.

Maya said this is too quick. She said what actually happens when a scientist uses old evidence is they consider what the evidence would have looked like if they didn't already know it — they subtract it from their background knowledge and then see whether the theory predicts it. So the formalism can be saved by asking what your credence would have been without E.

Dan shot back that "subtracting" evidence you already know is psychologically impossible — you can't just pretend you don't know something — and even if you could, there's no principled way to decide what to replace it with. Your credences would be totally unconstrained.

Maya said that's a practical problem, not an in-principle one, and anyway scientists do this kind of counterfactual reasoning all the time — it's basically what "this theory would predict X" means.

Dan said that's exactly his point — scientists do it all the time, and Bayesianism can't represent it, so Bayesianism fails as a descriptive account of how confirmation actually works in science.

Maya paused and said maybe the real issue is that we shouldn't think of confirmation as updating on known facts at all — maybe confirmation is about explanatory relationships, not credence changes. But Dan said that just abandons Bayesianism rather than saving it.

They went back and forth a while and never resolved it. Can you walk through their arguments and say who has the better case at each step, and whether either of them is missing something important?
