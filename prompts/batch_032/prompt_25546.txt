I've been trying to build a gears-level model of what makes privacy violations wrong — not just a list of things that happen to be illegal, but the underlying normative structure. The method I'm using is bog-standard reflective equilibrium: collect my intuitive verdicts on a range of cases, then try to extract a principle that fits them, revising either the principle or the verdicts where they clash. The problem is I've now got eight cases on the table and I can't find a principle that doesn't either look gerrymandered or force me to abandon a verdict I'm not willing to give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A hospital uses fully anonymized patient records for a study that improves treatment protocols. Verdict: clearly fine, no objection.
2. A data broker scrapes public records — property sales, voter registration, marriage licenses — and compiles a detailed profile on me that it sells to marketers. Verdict: creepy and wrong, even though every individual piece of information was already publicly available somewhere.
3. A friend tells me something embarrassing about a mutual acquaintance that the acquaintance told them in confidence. Verdict: wrong.
4. A stranger takes a photo of me on a busy city sidewalk and posts it on a public Instagram. Verdict: mildly annoying, but not a wrong.
5. My neighbor sets up a chair by their second-floor window and watches me in my living room every evening through my uncurtained window. They never trespass or use equipment beyond their eyes. Verdict: a serious violation.
6. The NSA collects telephony metadata on every domestic phone call — numbers dialed, call duration, time of day — but never records content. Verdict: deeply troubling, though I find it harder to articulate why this is worse than case 2 than I expected.
7. An ex-partner publishes a detailed account of our sexual relationship online, none of it false. Verdict: a grave wrong.
8. A coworker, without asking, tells our manager about a health condition I mentioned to them casually over lunch. I never said "keep this between us." Verdict: a genuine violation, though less severe than case 3.

The tensions I can't resolve: Cases 2 and 4 both involve information that was already public, but I judge them differently. Cases 3 and 8 both involve sharing information disclosed in a social context, but 3 feels worse even though the practical stakes in 8 are higher. Cases 4 and 5 are both observation from a legally-accessible vantage point, yet my verdicts are opposites. And case 6 feels worse than case 2, but in case 2 the information is more granular and more personal — so "quantity of data" or "sensitivity of data" can't be doing all the work.

What I'm after is a principled account of what makes a privacy violation wrong that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases. If no single principle gets all eight, tell me which verdicts I should reconsider and why. And please apply your preferred account to a case I keep going back and forth on: a social media company trains a large language model on all public posts on its platform, including mine; the model can sometimes reproduce near-verbatim snippets of people's posts in response to adversarial prompts, but it never outputs anything that wasn't already publicly posted. Is that a privacy wrong, and if so, why?
