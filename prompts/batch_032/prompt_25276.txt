For my philosophy class, I've been collecting a friend's verdicts about which behaviors count as genuine weakness of will (akrasia). So far she's ruled on five cases:

1. Alex resolves to quit smoking, judges that quitting is best, but at a party accepts a cigarette while thinking "I really shouldn't." She says: akratic.
2. Brianna consistently prefers $50 today to $100 in a month, says she simply discounts the future heavily, and never regrets these choices. She says: not akratic.
3. Carlos plans to study for an exam, ends up watching TV, all the while thinking "I should be studying, this is a mistake." She says: akratic.
4. Daphne has severe clinical depression and stays in bed despite wanting to get up and believing she should. She says: not akratic.
5. Eli decides in advance that he'll make an exception to his diet for birthday parties, and at a party eats cake consistent with that plan. She says: not akratic.

Next week we're discussing a new case: Fatima chooses $50 today over $100 in a month. At the moment of choice, she says "Rationally I know I should wait, but I just want the money now." The next day, asked whether she regrets it, she says "Not really — I got to enjoy the money immediately, and that's worth more to me than an extra $50 later."

Predict what my friend will say about Fatima. Identify the rule or rules that fit her first five verdicts equally well, explain where they diverge on Fatima, and give your best guess about which rule she's actually running — with reasons. Then describe a further case that would cleanly settle which rule she holds. Finally, tell me what consideration or evidence would change your mind about your prediction: what would make you switch from thinking she'll call Fatima akratic to thinking she won't, or vice versa?
