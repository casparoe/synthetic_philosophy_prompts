I'm a fourth-year PhD student putting together my dissertation proposal on the metaphysics of time, and I'd like an outside audit of whether my core commitments actually hang together before I build three chapters on top of them. I've been living inside this view long enough that I may be missing tensions that would be obvious to someone standing a step back. The proposal is due in six weeks, and I'd rather discover problems now than at the defense.

Here's the package I've settled into. (1) Presentism: only the present moment and its contents exist; the past is gone, the future is not yet. (2) Endurantism: objects persist by being wholly present at each moment they exist — no temporal parts, no four-dimensional worms. (3) Truthmaker maximalism: every true proposition has something in reality that makes it true. (4) There are true propositions about the past — it's true that I ate oatmeal this morning, and it's true that Caesar crossed the Rubicon — and these need truthmakers. (5) The truthmakers for past truths are present tensed facts: the world right now instantiates fundamental, irreducible tensed properties like having-been-such-that-p. These aren't reducible to present traces, records, or causal consequences. (6) Temporal passage is real and irreducible: the moving now is a fundamental feature of reality, not something that can be analyzed away in B-theoretic terms. (7) The special theory of relativity is physically correct, and its denial of absolute simultaneity is a genuine metaphysical constraint — I don't think we get to bracket physics when doing metaphysics of time. (8) The future is open: there are no true propositions about future contingent events. The past is fixed: there are true propositions about all past events. This asymmetry between the open future and the fixed past is, for me, one of the main attractions of presentism over eternalism. (9) We have genuine knowledge of the past; historical knowledge isn't a useful fiction or a mere coherence among present records.

I'm not equally attached to all nine, but I'd rather not tip my hand about which ones I'd sacrifice first. What I want is a ranked audit: which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and for the genuine tensions, how severe are they and what's the cheapest revision? If the whole framework rests on a mistake I haven't noticed, say so plainly — I'd rather scrap it now than defend it badly.
