I've been turning over a puzzle about when you're entitled to say something flat-out — not about lying or politeness, just the basic rule that separates claims you're in a position to make from claims you aren't. I ended up with an argument that seems to show no rule works, and I can't find the flaw.

Here's the argument:

(1) Suppose the rule for proper assertion is: you may say that p only if you know that p. Call this the knowledge rule.

(2) I do not know that my train will arrive at 3:00 tomorrow. I checked the schedule, the line is reliable, and I have no particular reason to expect trouble. But I cannot rule out a mechanical failure or a sudden strike. If knowing something means being absolutely certain of it, with no uneliminated possibility of error, then I do not know the train will arrive at 3:00.

(3) Yet it is plainly fine for me to tell someone, "My train gets in at 3:00 tomorrow." If the knowledge rule says that statement is improper, then the knowledge rule is too demanding.

(4) So the rule must be weaker. Suppose instead: you may say that p whenever your evidence makes p overwhelmingly likely. Call this the evidence rule.

(5) Now take a fair lottery with ten million tickets and one winner. My evidence that my ticket will lose is overwhelming — the odds are 9,999,999 to 1 against me. That is far stronger evidence than I have about the train, which is late maybe one trip in fifty.

(6) So by the evidence rule, I may properly say, "My ticket will lose."

(7) But that seems wrong. Even before the drawing, it feels like I am not in a position to flat-out say my ticket will lose. If I say it and then happen to win, I did not merely turn out to be mistaken — I said something I should not have said. And the oddness does not depend on the outcome; the statement seems improper either way.

(8) So the evidence rule is too weak, and the knowledge rule is too strong. We are stuck with no workable norm.

I can see a few places the argument might slip. Maybe step (2) is wrong because knowledge does not require certainty, and I do know the train will arrive even though I cannot rule out every far-fetched scenario. But if I fix that, the puzzle just moves: why can I know the train will arrive but not know my lottery ticket will lose, when the evidence for the ticket losing is so much stronger? Or maybe step (7) is wrong, and it actually is fine to say your ticket will lose — our reluctance is just a kind of superstition about tempting fate. But that feels like I am giving up something obvious.

I want to know: which step or steps in this argument should I reject, and what does rejecting them cost? If the argument is valid and the real work is choosing which premise to abandon, tell me what each choice commits me to. Also, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and which part of it you are least sure about.
