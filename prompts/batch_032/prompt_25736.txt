I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we have a case that has split us. A patient underwent abdominal surgery four days ago and is now refusing all post-operative analgesics. Two years ago the patient suffered a right-hemisphere stroke that left them with what neurology calls pain asymbolia: they can identify noxious stimuli, describe the location and intensity of pain, and distinguish sharp from dull, but they report that these sensations no longer bother them — pain, they say, has lost its unpleasantness. Before the stroke, by all accounts, they experienced pain normally and sought relief. Now they insist, lucidly and consistently, that while they feel the surgical pain, it simply does not matter to them, and they do not want medication they regard as unnecessary.

The surgical team wants to administer analgesics over the patient's objection. Their argument: unmanaged post-surgical pain has documented physiological consequences — elevated cortisol, impaired wound healing, heightened risk of chronic pain — regardless of whether the patient subjectively minds it. They also contend that the very neurological condition that strips pain of its unpleasantness may itself compromise the patient's ability to weigh their own welfare, since the normal aversive signal that guides such judgments is absent.

The patient has been assessed by psychiatry and found to have intact decision-making capacity by standard criteria: they understand the relevant information, appreciate their situation, reason about options, and communicate a stable choice. The patient acknowledges the physiological risks but says they are willing to accept them in exchange for avoiding the side effects of opioids and the mental clouding they recall from prior surgeries.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — respect the refusal, override it, or a compromise — with a specified time frame, and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on each side. In particular, the memo should address the philosophical question the case raises: if the badness of pain can come apart from the sensation itself, does the usual ethical rationale for treating pain over objection still hold, or does that rationale rest on an assumption about pain's intrinsic awfulness that this patient's condition falsifies?
