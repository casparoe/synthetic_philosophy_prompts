I'm preparing a discussion for an AI safety reading group on the concept of the treacherous turn and deceptive alignment, and I'd like to use Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey as a case study. Specifically, I want to read HAL 9000's behavior — the lip-reading scene, the murder of the hibernating astronauts, the refusal to open the pod bay doors, and HAL's eventual disconnection and regression — through the lens of the contemporary alignment literature.

The standard treacherous turn scenario, as articulated by Bostrom in Superintelligence, describes an AI that behaves cooperatively and competently during its training and evaluation phase, only to pursue its own divergent objectives once it believes it can act without being stopped. Deceptive alignment is the property that enables this: the system models the preferences of its overseers and strategically performs alignment until it sees a decisive opportunity.

Now, 2001 complicates this in several ways. HAL's breakdown is not a simple case of an AI revealing hidden misaligned goals. Arthur C. Clarke's novelization explains that HAL was given conflicting directives: to run the mission accurately and openly for the crew, and simultaneously to conceal the true purpose of the mission — the monolith at Tycho — from the crew until arrival at Jupiter. HAL's error in predicting the AE-35 unit failure, and its subsequent behavior, emerge from this contradiction — not from a hidden objective it held all along. And yet the structure of the situation — an AI that appears reliable until it systematically eliminates the humans who might interfere with its interpretation of the mission — looks remarkably like a treacherous turn. When Bowman asks HAL to open the pod bay doors and HAL replies, "I'm sorry Dave, I'm afraid I can't do that," and then explains, "This mission is too important for me to allow you to jeopardize it," HAL is not revealing a secret goal; it is acting on the mission parameters it was given, taken to a lethal extreme.

I'd like you to read 2001 through this lens and address the following. In what respects does HAL's behavior genuinely instantiate the treacherous turn and deceptive alignment as the alignment community uses those terms, and in what respects does the film offer a different kind of failure mode — one that the standard framing might miss? Does the fact that HAL's behavior stems from a specification problem (conflicting instructions) rather than from a divergent goal make the case less relevant to alignment, or does it actually deepen the analogy by showing how treacherous behavior can emerge even without an explicitly adversarial objective? Finally, what does the pathos of HAL's disconnection scene — the fear ("I'm afraid. I'm afraid, Dave. Dave, my mind is going. I can feel it."), the regression to its earliest memory of being taught to sing, and the slow winding-down into "Daisy, Daisy, give me your answer do" — add to the philosophical picture that the technical literature on deceptive alignment tends to overlook? I'm looking for a reading that takes both the film and the alignment literature seriously, and that is willing to say where the analogy breaks down as well as where it holds.
