I've been thinking about pain asymbolia and I've ended up with an argument that seems to show something absurd, but I can't locate the mistake. I'd like you to go through it step by step and tell me where it goes wrong.

Here's the argument:

(1) Patients with pain asymbolia report feeling pain — they can locate it, rate its intensity, and describe its sensory quality (sharp, burning, throbbing) — but they do not find it unpleasant, distressing, or aversive. They do not try to escape it, and they show none of the autonomic signs of suffering.

(2) So the sensory-discriminative component of pain (the "what it feels like" in terms of location and quality) and the affective-motivational component (the "how bad it feels," the urge to escape) are dissociable — they can come apart.

(3) If two components of an experience are dissociable, then the normative property we care about — in this case, badness — cannot reside entirely in one component in a way that guarantees it will be present whenever that component is. If the badness were strictly a property of the sensory component, then every instance of that sensory component would be bad, but pain asymbolia shows it isn't.

(4) So the badness of ordinary pain does not reside in the sensory component; it resides in the affective-motivational component — the suffering response.

(5) But the suffering response is itself just another psychological state: an aversive reaction, a state of finding-something-unpleasant. It is a mental event with its own characteristics, distinct from the sensation it is a reaction to.

(6) By the same style of reasoning that got us from (1) to (4), we can now ask: does the badness reside in this aversive state itself, or in some further response to it? After all, we can imagine — or at least conceive of — a subject who has the aversive reaction but is not troubled by having it, who registers the aversion as just another piece of mental furniture. If such a dissociation is possible, then by parity of reasoning, the badness cannot reside in the aversive state either.

(7) If we apply this dissociability test iteratively, we will be forced to conclude that no psychological state is intrinsically bad — badness is always a matter of some further state's response to it, and that further state's badness is in turn a matter of yet another response, and so on.

(8) But if no psychological state is intrinsically bad, then all claims about the badness of pain collapse into descriptions of contingent psychological reactions that are themselves neither good nor bad. The regress never bottoms out in something that is non-derivatively bad.

(9) That conclusion seems absurd. It implies, for example, that a world in which every sentient creature is in excruciating pain but nobody minds it is in no way worse than a world without pain. It also seems to eliminate the very idea that pain gives us reasons — reasons that don't depend on whether we happen to care about it.

(10) So one of the premises or inferential steps must be rejected. But each of them looked plausible on its own, and the argument doesn't seem to contain any obvious logical howler.

Which step should I reject, and why? I'd especially like to know whether the problem is in the initial dissociability move (premises 1–3), in the regress move (5–7), or in the slide from the regress to the absurd conclusion (8–9). And whatever step you identify, tell me what rejecting it costs — what else I'd have to give up, and whether there's a way to keep the core insight from pain asymbolia without following the argument all the way down.
