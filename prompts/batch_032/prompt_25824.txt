**Question:** "Explain Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. What exactly do these results demonstrate, what assumptions do they depend on, and what are their consequences for the project of giving a truth-conditional semantics to indicative conditionals?"

**Student's answer:**

David Lewis, in his 1976 paper "Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities," proved a series of results showing that the probability of an indicative conditional cannot be identified with the corresponding conditional probability. The target is the thesis that P(If A, then C) = P(C | A), often called Adams's Thesis or the Equation. Lewis showed that if we assume both that indicative conditionals express propositions and that the Equation holds, the probability function collapses to triviality: it can assign positive probability to at most two mutually exclusive propositions. Since real agents distribute credence over many more possibilities than that, the Equation cannot hold for any realistic probability function.

Lewis's first proof uses only the Equation and standard probability theory. It proceeds by expanding P(A → C) via the law of total probability over C and ¬C, substituting via the Equation, and deriving that P(C | A) = P(C) whenever both P(A ∧ C) and P(A ∧ ¬C) are positive. Lewis's second proof adds an assumption about conditionalizing on conditionals themselves and reaches the same triviality conclusion through a different route. Both proofs converge on the same result: the Equation is incompatible with conditionals expressing propositions, at least if we want probabilities to behave normally.

Lewis himself took the results to show that the Equation is false—conditionals do express propositions, but their probabilities don't reduce to conditional probabilities. Others, notably Dorothy Edgington and Ernest Adams, drew the opposite conclusion: the Equation is non-negotiable, so conditionals don't express propositions at all. On their view, the "probability" of a conditional just is your conditional credence, and there is no proposition whose probability that is. This divide remains unresolved, and the triviality results are widely regarded as one of the most important negative results in philosophical logic—they force everyone to give up something plausible, but there's no consensus on what.
