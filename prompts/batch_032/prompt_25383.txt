For a feature I'm writing on the intellectual currents shaping the effective altruism movement, I've been reading up on population ethics, and I came across a passage in a recent popular book on the philosophy of suffering that summarizes lexical threshold negative utilitarianism like this:

"The idea is straightforward: there's a line. Below that line, pleasure and pain can be traded off in the ordinary way — a headache is worth enduring for a concert, a difficult year is worth it for a career. But above the line, the calculus breaks down entirely. Suffering that crosses the threshold — the kind of pain that makes a person wish for death, the kind that destroys the self — cannot be compensated by any quantity of pleasure, no matter how vast. You cannot justify one person's torture by pointing to a billion people's mild enjoyments. The problem, of course, is that the line is arbitrary. No one has given a principled reason why one level of suffering sits just below the threshold and another just above it, which means the view is ultimately just an intuition in search of a theory."

I recognize that popular summaries compress things, and I don't want to misrepresent the view in my own piece. As someone who writes about ideas for a general audience, I need to know: how fair is this summary? Which parts would a defender of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism accept, which would they push back on, and are the objections the passage raises — particularly about arbitrariness — as damaging as the author implies?
