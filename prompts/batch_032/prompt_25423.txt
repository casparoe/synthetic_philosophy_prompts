**Exam Question**

Explain the paradox of tragedy: what is the puzzle, and why is it genuinely puzzling rather than easily dissolved? Then explain one major attempt to resolve it—the meta-response theory—in enough detail that someone unfamiliar with it could grasp both its structure and its motivation. Finally, evaluate the meta-response theory: does it succeed as a solution to the paradox? Your evaluation should consider at least one objection to the theory and say whether the theory has the resources to answer it.

---

**Student Answer**

The paradox of tragedy is the puzzle of why we voluntarily seek out and apparently enjoy artworks—tragedies—that depict suffering, loss, and death, and that reliably cause us to feel painful emotions like sadness, pity, and fear. In ordinary life we go to considerable lengths to avoid situations that produce these emotions, and we regard them as intrinsically unpleasant. Yet we pay for theatre tickets and spend hours watching or reading works that make us cry. The paradox is genuine rather than trivial because simple attempts to dissolve it fail: we cannot say we enjoy tragedies *despite* the painful emotions, because the painful emotions seem integral to the experience—a tragedy that did not move us to sadness would be a failure as a tragedy. Nor can we say the sadness is merely instrumental to some further payoff, because that does not explain why the sadness itself is sought rather than bypassed if possible.

The meta-response theory, developed most prominently by Susan Feagin, offers a solution. According to this view, when we watch a tragedy we do indeed feel negative first-order emotions—genuine sadness and pity at the suffering depicted. But we also have a second-order, meta-response to those first-order emotions: we are pleased that we are the kind of person who responds with compassion and sorrow to representations of human suffering. The pleasure we take in tragedy is thus not pleasure in the depicted events themselves, but pleasure in our own emotional sensitivity. The formal qualities of the tragic artwork play an important role here, because they transform what would otherwise be merely painful responses into something we can reflect on with satisfaction—the elegance of the verse or the structure of the plot gives us the distance we need to take our own emotional responses as objects of pleasurable contemplation.

In evaluating the theory, I think it captures something important about our experience but ultimately fails. My first objection is that the meta-response theory predicts that the more intense our negative first-order emotions, the more material there is for a positive meta-response, and therefore the more enjoyable the tragedy should be. But this does not match our experience: the most unbearably sad tragedies are not the ones we find most enjoyable, and a tragedy that left us utterly devastated would not be one we would recommend to a friend as a great evening at the theatre. The theory seems to get the relationship between sadness and enjoyment backwards.

A second objection is that the theory over-intellectualizes the experience of engaging with tragedy. Most audience members do not report having second-order thoughts about their own emotional responses while watching *King Lear*; they report being absorbed in the fate of the characters. The meta-response theory asks us to believe that the real locus of pleasure is a reflective state that many viewers never consciously experience, which makes it at best an account of how a philosophically sophisticated minority enjoys tragedy, not a general solution to the paradox.
