I'm organizing a monthly discussion group for people in the rationalist orbit—mostly programmers, a couple of graduate students in non-philosophy fields, all broadly familiar with the Sequences and with expected-utility reasoning as a normative ideal for decision-making. Next month I want to spend an hour on L.A. Paul's concept of transformative experience and the challenge it raises for the standard model of rational choice. I've read the book; the group hasn't. I'm not looking for a summary of the argument. I need a teaching plan.

What I want from you is the dependency structure and the misconception map. Specifically:

- What has to be in place before the problem even registers as a problem? I assume the standard picture—rational choice as maximizing expected utility over a known preference ordering—needs to be on the table, but is there something more specific that needs to be foregrounded first, like the distinction between uncertainty about outcomes and uncertainty about one's own future values?

- What's the right carrying example? Paul's vampire case is vivid, but for this audience it risks coming across as a toy problem that doesn't bite on real decisions. I'm considering using the decision about whether to have children instead—it's closer to home and harder to dismiss, but it also brings in emotional weight that might derail the conceptual point. Which example should anchor the session, and what does it need to have and lack to work?

- What misconceptions will this particular audience predictably form, and at what point in the sequence should I head each one off? My guesses: (a) collapsing the problem into ordinary uncertainty about future preferences and thinking it's just a case where you need better priors; (b) treating it as a problem about the unreliability of testimony from people who've undergone the experience, and therefore soluble in principle by better polling; (c) reducing it to standard incommensurability of values and reaching for a Pareto-frontier or bargaining framework. Are there others I'm missing, and does the order I've listed them match the order they'll arise?

- What's the dependency order among the core conceptual pieces? Does the first-personal vs. third-personal knowledge distinction need to come before the expected-utility framing, or does the framing have to be fully set up before the distinction can land? - What should I explicitly bracket and refuse to cover? I'm inclined to set aside the epistemology of disagreement as applied to testimony from the transformed, and also the question of whether the argument overgenerates to all decisions under uncertainty. Are those the right cuts, or am I walling off something that needs to be addressed for the core argument to go through?

- At the end of the hour, what single question or scenario should I put to the group to check whether they've understood the problem itself, as opposed to just finding it intuitively plausible or unsettling?

Assume the group has the conceptual vocabulary of someone who's read up through the decision-theory Sequences but has no exposure to academic philosophy of mind or epistemology. The goal is for them to see exactly where expected-utility reasoning runs into a structural obstacle, not just a practical one.
