Philosophers have proposed several families of explanation for the paradox of tragedy — the puzzle of why we willingly seek out and apparently enjoy artworks that evoke sadness, fear, or pity. Below are six candidate resolutions. Please rank them from most to least convincing, and give a sentence or two justifying each placement.

1. **Catharsis:** We enjoy the purgative release of negative emotions that have accumulated in us; tragedy functions as a kind of emotional drainage.
2. **Conversion:** The aesthetic frame transforms the character of the emotions themselves — sadness-in-art is not the same phenomenal state as sadness-in-life, and the former is intrinsically pleasurable.
3. **Control:** We take satisfaction in experiencing negative emotions from a position of safety and mastery, much as we enjoy the fear induced by a roller coaster or a horror film.
4. **Meta-emotion:** What we actually enjoy is not the first-order sadness but a positive second-order response — we are pleased by the fact that we are moved, or by the intensity and richness of our own emotional engagement.
5. **Compensation:** The displeasure of tragic emotions is real but is outweighed by concurrent pleasures — the beauty of the language, the elegance of the composition, or the intellectual satisfaction of the plot.
6. **Value pluralism:** The experience is genuinely painful, but we pursue it because we value things other than pleasure — emotional depth, self-understanding, or insight into the human condition — and tragedy delivers these at a price we judge worth paying.
