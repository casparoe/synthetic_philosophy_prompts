I serve on my university's Faculty Senate Committee on Academic Standards. Six months ago we reviewed a tenure case that has since become a quiet crisis for the committee. The candidate's file met every quantitative benchmark we use — publications in high-impact-factor journals, citation counts above the department median, an h-index comfortably on target — but the external reviewers, reading the actual work, described it as methodologically competent yet substantively hollow: a single finding recombined across many papers, each sliced to the minimum publishable length, citing and cited by the same small network of collaborators. The committee felt we had no choice but to recommend tenure, because our own policies had made the metrics the standard and given us no clear warrant to override them. Several of us left that meeting ashamed.

The provost has now asked us to produce a formal recommendation on whether and how the university should revise its use of bibliometric indicators in hiring, tenure, and promotion. Draft the committee's memo. It should recommend a concrete policy — retain the current metrics, abandon them entirely, or modify how they are used (e.g., make them advisory rather than decisive, supplement them with structured qualitative review, or change which metrics count and how they are weighted). The memo must engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side: if you recommend abandoning metrics, address the concern that qualitative review invites cronyism and idiosyncratic bias; if you recommend keeping them, address the concern that any metric used to make high-stakes decisions will be gamed until it measures only the skill of gaming it. The memo must also be actionable — specify who would implement the change, on what timeline, and what it would cost in money or administrative effort. The provost will circulate it to the deans, several of whom have built their departments' reputations on precisely these numbers and will need to be persuaded, not lectured.
