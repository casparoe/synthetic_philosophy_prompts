So I've been going back and forth on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness for like two years now—ever since that Frankish paper made the rounds on the forum—and I keep noticing that discussions between illusionists and realists totally talk past each other because they're using different criteria for what would count as a successful theory. The realist says "you haven't explained the thing that most needs explaining" and the illusionist says "you're asking me to explain something that doesn't exist, that's like asking a theory of combustion to explain phlogiston." And then everyone just retrenches.

I want to step back and get clear on the actual desiderata. If someone is going to defend illusionism—the view that phenomenal consciousness, the "what it's like"-ness, qualitative feels, whatever you want to call it, is an illusion and doesn't actually exist as we introspectively seem to find it—what does their theory actually have to deliver? Not just "explain why we're mistaken," but the full spec. What are the phenomena it has to account for? Which cases does it have to get right? Which distinctions can't it collapse without losing explanatory power? I'm thinking of things like: the apparent difference between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness (illusionists often want to collapse this, but then they need to explain why the distinction felt so compelling and why it maps onto things like blindsight); the fact that we can talk about, reason about, and compare our "experiences" (the illusion has to be causally efficacious in some sense, or at least the mechanisms that generate the illusion do); the meta-problem of consciousness—why we think there's a hard problem at all; altered states from meditation or psychedelics where the "illusion" seems to change in structured ways; the fact that pain motivates action in a way that mere propositional knowledge doesn't, and an illusionist owes an account of that difference; and the apparent unity of the phenomenal field. I'm sure I'm missing some.

What I'm especially interested in is which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are just intuition-pumps that realists have smuggled into the criteria. Like, is "preserve the intuition that there is something it is like to be me" a genuine desideratum, or is that exactly the thing the illusionist is trying to debunk and demanding they preserve it begs the question? And I want to know if there's some subset of the requirements that are jointly unsatisfiable—if so, that's itself a result about the viability of the position, and I'd rather know that upfront than spend another two years going in circles. If you want to do a quick scorecard for the main variants (Dennett-style, Frankish's strong illusionism, Graziano's attention schema, maybe the predictive processing crowd) against the list, that's fine, but I mostly care about the criteria themselves and the reasoning behind each one. The scoring is secondary.
