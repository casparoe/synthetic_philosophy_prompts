I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral responsibility and the justification of legal punishment. I've been following the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso — their exchange in *Just Deserts* (2021) and the surrounding literature — and I find myself thinking that they may be talking past each other more than either acknowledges. Dennett insists that we can preserve a defensible notion of moral responsibility and desert without libertarian free will, and that abandoning desert would be morally and politically disastrous. Caruso counters that no one ever *basically deserves* to suffer, that retributive attitudes rest on a fiction, and that we should replace the whole apparatus of blame and punishment with a public-health quarantine model focused on rehabilitation and incapacitation. But I suspect that when each says "desert" or "moral responsibility," they are picking out different things, and that the real dispute — once the verbal fog clears — is narrower than it appears.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement that Dennett and Caruso could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of claims both can accept. Cover determinism, the brain sciences, the forward-looking functions of legal punishment (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation), which kinds of "free will" are worth wanting, and what should actually happen in practice to people who commit crimes. I suspect this list is longer than either tends to emphasize in debate.

2. **Disagreements table.** For each genuine point of dispute, state the claim neutrally, then give Dennett's position in wording he would accept, then Caruso's position in wording he would accept. Aim for the right grain size — not so coarse that the entries reduce to "Dennett: yes, Caruso: no," and not so fine that we disappear into epicycles about Frankfurt cases or manipulation arguments. Include at minimum the disagreement over basic desert, the disagreement over whether forward-looking justifications are sufficient, and the disagreement over the social consequences of publicly abandoning desert.

3. **Cruxes.** Identify the sub-questions such that, if we could settle them, at least one side would revise its view. For each crux, specify what kind of evidence or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and record what each side predicts that evidence would show.

4. **Verbal disputes.** Flag any parts of the exchange that you think are merely verbal — where a term like "free will," "moral responsibility," or "desert" is being used in different senses by the two parties, and the appearance of disagreement dissolves once the senses are distinguished. If you conclude the entire debate is verbal, say so and explain why; if not, say which parts are verbal and which are genuine, and state what remains after the verbal disputes are set aside.
