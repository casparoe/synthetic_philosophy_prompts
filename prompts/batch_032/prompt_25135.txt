I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about a puzzle he brought up, and I have been turning it over ever since. The puzzle, as he presented it, goes like this. Picture two adjacent chambers of gas at the same temperature, separated by a wall with a tiny trapdoor. Now imagine a minuscule intelligent creature stationed at that door, capable of observing the molecules buzzing around. Whenever a faster-than-average molecule approaches the door from the left chamber, the creature opens it; whenever a slower-than-average one approaches from the right, it does the same. Over time, the left chamber heats up and the right chamber cools down — a temperature difference emerges from an initially uniform gas, and the creature never seems to push or pump anything. My friend insisted this showed that the second law of thermodynamics has a loophole. I argued that the creature must expend something in order to measure the molecules and decide when to open the door, and that this expenditure would turn out to balance the books. We went back and forth for hours and never settled it.

What has stayed with me, though, is not the physics of the particular puzzle but a more general pattern I now realize I have encountered in several different contexts. The pattern is this: someone proposes a setup in which simply knowing something — knowing which molecules are fast, knowing which trades will be profitable, knowing which argumentative move an opponent will make — seems to let you extract work, or money, or advantage, from a system without putting in any corresponding effort or energy. The knowledge is treated as if it were free, sitting outside the system and costing nothing, and the whole force of the puzzle depends on that assumption. I have seen versions of this move in discussions of whether a sufficiently clever trader can beat an efficient market without taking on risk, in arguments about whether consciousness could have evolved without imposing a metabolic cost, and in debates about whether an ideal reasoner with perfect self-knowledge can avoid certain paradoxes. In each case the structure is the same: information is positioned as a costless resource, and the interesting question is whether that positioning is legitimate.

I am trying to find out whether this recurring pattern has a recognized name — not just the original thermodynamic puzzle, but the broader argumentative move of treating information as a free resource and then extracting value from it. I am less interested in a bare label than in understanding how the pattern has been analyzed: is it one thing or several? Does the thermodynamic version have a name of its own, and is it considered fundamentally the same as the economic or computational versions? If the pattern as I have described it does not map neatly onto any established concept, I would be grateful for an attempt to define it and distinguish it from whatever lies nearest.
