Ok so my philosophy study group has been going in circles on this and I think we've built an argument that can't be right but I can't find the crack. Here's the argument:

(1) You're only morally responsible for what you yourself intentionally do (or negligently fail to do, but negligence still requires that you individually could have done otherwise).

(2) When people act together — like carrying a piano, or running a company that pollutes — what gets done isn't something any single person intended to do on their own. Each person intends their little part, or intends that *we* do it, but no one intends *I will bring about the full outcome*.

(3) So no individual intentionally brings about the full collective outcome.

(4) Therefore, from (1) and (3), no individual is morally responsible for the collective outcome.

(5) But the group isn't a person either — it doesn't have a mind or intentions in the literal sense, so it can't be responsible.

(6) So *no one* is responsible.

(7) But that's insane. When a company's board votes to dump toxic waste, or when three guys rob a store together, someone's responsible. The argument seems to prove that joint action makes responsibility vanish into thin air.

I feel like premise (1) is the one to attack — maybe you can be responsible for things you do together with others even if you didn't individually intend the whole thing. But then what does that do to the rest of our moral framework? If I'm responsible for what *we* do, am I responsible for everything any group I'm part of does? Where's the line? And if I bite the bullet and say groups literally *are* responsible agents, doesn't that mean I'm positing some spooky group mind?

Basically: which premise do you reject, and what do you have to pay to reject it?
