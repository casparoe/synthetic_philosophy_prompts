I've been in a social epistemology reading group for two years and the testimony debate — reductionism vs. anti-reductionism — has become groundhog day. The reductionists (broadly following Hume) say that believing someone's word is justified only if you have inductive evidence that they're reliable: you've seen enough people tell the truth, or you've got background reasons to trust this particular speaker on this particular topic. The anti-reductionists (broadly following Reid) say testimony is a basic source of justification, like perception or memory — you don't need independent reasons to trust what people tell you, any more than you need independent reasons to trust your eyes. And then the reductionists say: but people lie, misremember, and bullshit all the time, so treating testimony as basic is epistemically reckless. And the anti-reductionists say: but you literally couldn't acquire the inductive base the reductionist demands without already trusting testimony, because most of what you know about reliability patterns comes from other people's reports. And then the reductionists say: that's a genetic point about how we acquire concepts, not a justificatory point about what makes a particular belief justified. And then the anti-reductionists say: fine, but when a stranger gives you directions, you don't run a reliability check, you just believe them, and that seems perfectly fine. And round we go.

It feels like we've mapped the dialectical space completely and everyone is just picking their favorite starting intuition and building a fortress around it. But I can't tell whether that means the debate is (a) a verbal dispute about the word "justification" where one side means "inferential support" and the other means "entitlement," (b) a substantive empirical disagreement about how much checking ordinary cognition actually does, (c) a clash of methodological commitments about what epistemology is supposed to be — a description of our actual practices or a normative theory of what we ought to do — or (d) something else entirely. My hunch is it's mostly (c) dressed up as (a) and (b), but I've been wrong about these things before.

Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. I want you to engage with the moves each side actually makes (not just the caricatures), say which of (a)–(d) or some other option best captures the impasse, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis. If your answer is "nothing will move it and here's why that's fine," that's an acceptable answer too — but you have to earn it.
