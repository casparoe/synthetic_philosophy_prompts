I've spent a lot of the last year thinking about a tension I keep noticing in conversations about institutional reform. A friend of mine works in local government and tells me about zoning codes that everyone agrees produce bad outcomes — housing shortages, segregation, sprawl — but that nobody can seem to change, partly because there's always someone who says these rules were put in place for a reason, and until you can tell me exactly what that reason was and why it no longer applies, you shouldn't touch them. Another friend works in software and finds this attitude maddening; his view is that if you can measure the harm an institution is doing right now, you don't need to write a history of its origins before you fix it. I've come to think of this as a deep disagreement between what I'll call the Chestertonian and the rationalist-reformer temperaments — not just about specific policies, but about where the burden of proof should sit when we face an inherited practice whose original rationale is obscure.

I'd like you to draft the document that would come out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed Chestertonian (who holds that inherited institutions and practices often encode distributed knowledge that no single mind can reconstruct, and that the presumption should be strongly in favor of keeping them until their function is understood) and a committed rationalist reformer (who holds that institutions should be judged by their current, measurable effects, and that age and obscure origins do not by themselves constitute reasons to preserve something).

I want the claims they can both sign after hashing everything out — not truisms, but substantive points about institutional knowledge, path-dependence, the costs of change, and the limits of both traditions that survive the back-and-forth. Then I want the precise statements of where they still differ, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as fair. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. And for each crux, I want a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what that evidence would show, recorded in advance. Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
