I've been working through a retired federal judge's memoir — she spent three decades on the bench — and I've been trying to reverse-engineer her sentencing philosophy from the cases she describes in the most detail. Here are five where she is clearest about her reasoning:

(a) A twenty-year-old first offender who stole electronics to fund a heroin addiction. She imposed a treatment program and probation, writing that incarceration would be counterproductive.

(b) A pharmaceutical executive who knowingly shipped contaminated drugs that caused several patient deaths. She imposed a substantial custodial sentence, citing the gravity of the harm and the defendant's position of responsibility.

(c) A man who committed a serious assault while in an acute psychotic episode. She ordered mandatory hospitalization rather than incarceration, on the grounds that he lacked the capacity to be held to the usual standard.

(d) A career burglar with a dozen prior convictions who expressed no remorse. She imposed a lengthy sentence, explicitly invoking the need to protect the public.

(e) A woman who killed her husband after years of severe domestic abuse. She sentenced well below the guideline range, emphasizing the coercive context in which the act occurred.

Now, the case I cannot settle: a man who committed an armed robbery at nineteen, served a short sentence for it, and then led a completely law-abiding life for the next thirty years — steady employment, a family, community involvement. Last year, DNA evidence from a cold case matched him to a second robbery he committed during that same teenage period. He is now fifty, employed, a father of three, and by all accounts poses no danger to anyone. He has never been charged for this second robbery until now.

If the judge were to decide this case consistently with her earlier ones, what would she do? I can construct at least two distinct principles that fit her five verdicts equally well — one that treats desert as a legitimate and non-negotiable ground of punishment, and one that is purely forward-looking, concerned only with deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation. The two principles point in different directions on the new case. Which principle does the pattern of her verdicts better support, what would each predict, and is there a further case — real or hypothetical — that would cleanly separate them?
