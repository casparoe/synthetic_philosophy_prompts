For the better part of a decade now, I have been covering philosophy of mind and aesthetics for a general-interest magazine, and there is one debate I keep returning to that seems to be running in place: the so-called paradox of fiction. The puzzle, in its simplest form, is that we routinely have strong emotional responses to fictional characters and events — we weep at the end of a novel, flinch during a horror film, feel genuine elation at a fictional reunion — despite knowing perfectly well that none of it is real. But if emotions require beliefs about the existence and actuality of their objects, how can we have emotions about things we know do not exist? The literature has been working on this since at least the 1970s, and the main camps were staked out long ago: some argue that we temporarily suspend our disbelief or somehow bracket our knowledge that the events are fictional; others claim that what we feel are not genuine emotions but something like quasi-emotions that run on imagination rather than belief; still others reject the premise that emotions require existence-beliefs at all and treat our responses to fiction as continuous with our responses to counterfactual scenarios, daydreams, and hypotheticals. There are more exotic proposals too — simulation theories, theories that treat fiction as a kind of game of make-believe, theories that redescribe the phenomenon at the level of neural processing.

What frustrates me, as someone who has now interviewed proponents of every major position, is that the arguments on all sides are well-rehearsed and the empirical facts — what people report feeling, what brain scans show, what developmental psychology tells us about children's engagement with pretend play — are largely uncontested. And yet nobody moves. I have started to suspect that the impasse is not really about the evidence or even about the logic of the arguments, but about something deeper: a disagreement about what would count as an explanation of an emotional response in the first place. Some philosophers seem to want a conceptual analysis that preserves the rationality of our responses to fiction; others want a psychological mechanism; others seem content with a deflationary story that shows the paradox dissolves once we stop asking the wrong question. If that diagnosis is right, then the debate is not stalled because it is hard — it is stalled because the participants are answering different questions under a single heading. But I would like to hear that diagnosis tested, and I would like to know: if I am wrong about the diagnosis, what would actually move the debate? A new experimental paradigm? A sharper distinction between types of emotion? Or is this one of those philosophical problems where the best outcome is clarity about why it will not resolve?
