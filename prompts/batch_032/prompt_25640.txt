I've been reading Hayek lately and keep running into this analogy: the market is like a distributed mind. Prices are like beliefs, the price system is a kind of cognitive apparatus that aggregates information no single person could possess, and the market as a whole "knows" things. People in my discussion group (I'm a retired physicist, got into philosophy a few years back) use this to argue we should defer to market outcomes over expert panels or central planning—the market's epistemic reach is just broader, so it's epistemically arrogant to override it.

Then I came across the no-trade theorems—Milgrom and Stokey, the whole literature—and now I'm stuck. The rough idea, as I understand it: if everyone's rational and shares a common prior, purely informational trade shouldn't even happen. My willingness to sell you an asset already signals my private information, so you should update your valuation downward, and we end up with no trade. But obviously real markets are full of trading.

So here's what I want. Audit the "market as mind" analogy. Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of perception, belief, inference, memory? Where does the mapping break, and do the no-trade results mark one of those breaks? And the bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, can the analogy still support the conclusion that we should defer to markets over experts, or was it carrying that conclusion from the start?
