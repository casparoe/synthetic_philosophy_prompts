I was reading this trade book on American philosophy — the kind journalists like me reach for when we need a quick intellectual history fix — and the author sums up pragmatist theories of truth in a paragraph I dog-eared because something about it felt slippery. Here's the passage as I remember it:

"William James and C.S. Peirce founded pragmatism together, and on the question of truth they were basically on the same page. Both held that truth is not about matching your beliefs to some mind-independent world — that's a philosopher's fantasy. Instead, a belief is true if it works. James put it bluntly: true ideas are those we can assimilate, validate, corroborate, and verify; false ideas are those we cannot. Peirce said the same thing in more academic language, defining truth as the opinion fated to be agreed upon by all who investigate. The core insight is identical: truth is whatever is useful to believe. That's why James could say that if religious belief makes someone's life richer and more meaningful, then for that person, God exists — and Peirce, despite his scientific temperament, never really disagreed. The pragmatist theory of truth is, at bottom, the claim that the true and the useful are the same thing."

Now, I've read a little James — enough to know he did say some provocative things about religious belief and the "cash value" of ideas. And I know Peirce was so annoyed by how James was understood that he renamed his own view "pragmaticism" (a name, he said, "ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers"). So lumping them together as if they held the same view sets off alarm bells. But I don't know Peirce well enough to say where exactly this summary goes wrong, or whether the James it presents is even fair to James. Can you go through this paragraph and tell me what a careful Peircean would reject, what a careful Jamesian would push back on, and whether the author's claim that they're "basically on the same page" survives scrutiny? I'm less interested in scoring rhetorical points against the book and more in understanding what each of them actually thought truth was.
