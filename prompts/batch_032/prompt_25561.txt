I'm a junior taking a philosophy of decision theory course this semester, and we've spent the last few weeks on L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience. The puzzle, as we've been discussing it, is this: for a certain class of decisions—having a child, changing careers, undergoing a religious conversion, taking a psychedelic drug—you cannot know what it will be like to have made the choice until you've already made it, because the experience changes your preferences, your values, and even the self that does the valuing. Standard expected utility theory seems to break down here: you can't assign a utility to an outcome whose qualitative character is epistemically inaccessible to your pre-transformation self. Paul's positive proposal is that we should sometimes choose transformative experiences on the basis of their revelatory value—we want to discover what it's like, even if we can't evaluate it in advance.

I've been trying to think through how this framework applies to a case that's even more radical than the ones Paul discusses, and I keep coming back to Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival*. Louise Banks, a linguist, learns the written language of the heptapods, and the process fundamentally rewires her cognition: she begins to experience time non-linearly, perceiving her entire life—including her future daughter's terminal illness and death—as a simultaneous whole. By the end of the film, she chooses to have Hannah despite knowing the grief that awaits, and the film presents this as an affirmation rather than a tragedy.

But I'm not satisfied with just mapping Paul's categories onto the film. I think *Arrival* might actually pose a challenge to the transformative experience framework that I haven't seen articulated. Specifically: Paul assumes that after a transformative experience, you're still fundamentally an agent who makes decisions in something like the standard way—you just have different preferences. But Louise's transformation seems to change the very structure of her agency. If you experience your own future as already fixed—if the distinction between deciding and remembering what you will do collapses—does the concept of a decision still apply? And if not, can we even call Louise's experience transformative in Paul's sense, or is it something else entirely, something that decision theory as a framework may not be equipped to analyze? I'd love a reading of the film that takes Paul's theory seriously but is willing to say where the film pushes beyond it, rather than just using the film as an illustration.
