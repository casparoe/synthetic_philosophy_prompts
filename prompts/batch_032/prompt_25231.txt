ok so i've been stuck on this for like two weeks and it's making me question every estimate i've ever made at work. i'm a software engineer, mostly backend stuff, and we do a lot of planning that involves probability — like "what are the odds this refactor breaks something" or "how likely is it we hit the Q3 deadline." i started thinking about how i actually come up with those numbers and now i have an argument that seems to prove way too much. here it is:

(1) whenever i assign a probability to some event — say, a 20% chance this deploy causes an outage — what i'm really doing is putting the event in a bucket (a "reference class") and reporting the frequency in that bucket. like, i'm implicitly saying "deploys of this type fail 20% of the time."

(2) but any given event belongs to a ton of different reference classes, and they give different frequencies. this deploy is: a friday deploy (maybe 8% failure rate), a deploy touching the payment module (maybe 25%), a deploy i personally wrote (maybe 5%), a deploy during Q4 (maybe 15%), a deploy on this specific repo (maybe 12%), and so on — basically infinite classes.

(3) there's no objective rule for picking which reference class is the right one. you can say "pick the narrowest one" but narrowest along what dimension? "deploys touching the payment module on fridays in Q4 that i wrote" is narrower than any of the above but might have zero or one data point.

(4) so any probability i assign is just a reflection of an arbitrary choice of reference class.

(5) if probability estimates are arbitrary, they can't rationally justify decisions. i can't say "we should delay the deploy because there's a 25% chance of failure" if that 25% is just whichever bucket i felt like picking.

(6) but we DO use probability estimates to make decisions all the time, and it works — or at least it works well enough that we're not just flipping coins. we ship software, we manage risk, we allocate engineering time, and the people who are good at this genuinely outperform the people who aren't.

(7) so something between (1) and (6) has to give.

i keep going back and forth. sometimes i think (3) is false and there IS a rule — like, use the reference class that gives you the most accurate predictions — but that seems circular because i need probabilities to know which class is most accurate. other times i think (4) doesn't follow from (2) and (3) but i can't explain the gap. and sometimes i think the whole thing shows that probability isn't "out there" in the world at all, it's just a measure of my ignorance, but then i don't know what that means for the estimates i have to give my manager on monday.

where does this argument actually go wrong? if you have to reject a premise, which one and what does it cost?
