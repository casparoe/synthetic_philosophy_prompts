I've been going back and forth with a buddy about Pascal's mugging — you know the one, where some guy on the street says "give me five dollars or I'll simulate a googolplex of conscious beings being tortured for a trillion years," and since you can't assign literally zero probability to him being able to do that, the expected disutility of refusing is astronomical, so you should hand over the cash. My friend thinks it's just a cute paradox with no relevance outside a philosophy seminar. I'm not convinced. It feels like the basic move — "you can't prove me wrong, and if I'm right the stakes are basically infinite, so you have to act as if I'm right" — shows up all over the place once you start looking. I tried to come up with my own examples but kept getting stuck on cartoonish stuff like doomsday cults and Nigerian prince emails, which feel too on-the-nose.

So here's my request. Give me exactly five examples of realistic scenarios (politics, business, personal life, tech, whatever) that have the same structural features as Pascal's mugging. Specifically, each should involve: (i) a claim you can't definitively disprove, (ii) stakes that are so enormous they'd swamp any ordinary cost-benefit calculation, and (iii) a strong intuition that you should reject the claim anyway despite what the expected-value math says. Each example should be just a sentence or two. Number them 1 through 5, no introduction, no conclusion, no commentary — just the five items. And please don't just give me variations on the standard mugging scenario; I want examples where the Pascal-ish structure is hiding in something that looks like a perfectly normal decision problem.
