I've been working out my views on the norms of assertion and I want a consistency check before I write them up. Here's the package I'm considering:

(a) The norm of assertion is knowledge: you should assert that p only if you know that p.

(b) Knowledge is externalist — whether you know p depends on factors like reliability and truth that can be entirely outside your first-person perspective. You can think you know and be wrong; you can know without being able to tell that you know.

(c) For a norm to genuinely govern a practice like assertion, it has to be action-guiding: an agent must be able to determine, from their own perspective and without further empirical investigation, whether they are complying with it. A norm you cannot deliberately follow is not really a norm for that practice.

(d) The knowledge norm gets the right result in lottery cases: you should not assert "my ticket lost" on the basis of the astronomical odds alone, and the explanation is that you don't know it lost — which is exactly what the knowledge norm says.

(e) There are cases where a testifier asserts p without knowing p — perhaps without even believing p — but the hearer can still permissibly assert p later on the basis of that testimony, because the testimony was reliable and true and the hearer thereby came to know p. (Think of a teacher who sincerely rejects some theory but teaches it accurately under institutional pressure; a student can learn it from them and later assert it.)

I want to know: which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing everything as equally problematic. I'm especially interested in whether (c) forces me to give up either (a) or (b), and whether (e) is actually compatible with (a) or whether I'm helping myself to something that (a) rules out.
