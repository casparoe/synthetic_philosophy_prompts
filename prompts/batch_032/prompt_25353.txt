I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about dirty hands in politics, going by my gut on a bunch of cases. Here's where I've landed so far:

1. The mayor of a city authorizes torturing a suspect who they're nearly certain knows the location of a bomb set to go off in a school. The suspect talks, the bomb is defused, kids are saved. I think what the mayor did was wrong — torture is wrong — but I also think it was the right thing to do. Both things at once. That's the whole point of dirty hands, right?

2. A president lies to the public about the location of troops during an ongoing military crisis, to prevent enemy forces from learning where they are and to avoid mass panic. I think this is fine. Not even wrong-but-necessary. Just the right call, no residue.

3. A senator takes an envelope of cash from a defense contractor to fund their re-election campaign, knowing they need to win to cast the deciding vote on a climate bill that will save millions of lives. I think this is just corrupt. Not dirty hands, not tragic, just wrong. But I'm not sure why I'm so confident about this one when I'm not about the torture case — both involve doing something clearly wrong for a genuinely greater good.

4. A defense minister orders a drone strike on a terrorist leader, knowing with near-certainty that it will kill three civilians in the building next door. The strike succeeds and prevents an attack that would have killed hundreds. I think this is grim but justified. I'm not sure whether I want to say it was "wrong" in the way the torture was. It feels different, but I cant articulate why.

5. A prime minister covers up a cabinet member's extramarital affair to prevent a scandal that would topple the government and kill a major public health reform. I think this is not okay. It feels like personal loyalty dressed up as public interest. But I worry I'm just rationalizing — if the reform really would save lives, why is this different from the drone strike?

6. A police commissioner authorizes warrantless surveillance of a neighborhood where intelligence suggests an attack is being planned, without meeting the legal threshold for a warrant. The surveillance uncovers the plot and prevents the attack. I think this is troubling but probably permissible in genuinely exigent circumstances. But I'm not sure if it's "wrong but necessary" or just "justified."

7. A head of state orders the assassination of a foreign leader who is orchestrating a genocide that will kill hundreds of thousands. I think this is justified, and I also think the person who gives the order should carry the weight of it — should lose sleep, should not feel clean. But I dont know if that means I think it was wrong, or just that it was terrible.

So my question is: is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? What distinguishes the cases where I feel the "wrongness persists" (like the torture case) from the ones where the act feels justified without remainder (like the troop lie) or the ones that just feel corrupt (like the bribe)? And what does that principle say about a case I haven't listed — say, a public health official who knowingly understates the severity of an outbreak to prevent panic, which then allows them to control it more effectively?

Also, please separate what's genuinely contested among people who work on dirty hands from what's basically settled. I don't want to walk away thinking there's a consensus where there isn't one.
