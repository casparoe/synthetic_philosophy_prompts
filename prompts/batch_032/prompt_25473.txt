A friend who's been reading Berkeley handed me this argument and now I can't shake it:

(1) Try to conceive of a tree existing completely unperceived — no one seeing it, hearing it, thinking about it, period.
(2) But in the very act of trying, you're conceiving it. So the tree you're picturing is being perceived — by you.
(3) So what you've actually conceived is a perceived tree, not an unperceived one.
(4) Therefore you can't conceive of an unperceived object.
(5) If you can't conceive of X, then X is impossible (or at least you have no grounds to claim X is possible).
(6) So mind-independent objects are impossible.

But here's the problem: by exactly the same reasoning, I can't conceive of a conversation happening in a language I don't understand, or a planet orbiting a star I've never heard of, or a time before I was born. In each case the act of conceiving makes it an object of my mind, so the argument says I'm not really conceiving the thing I claim to be conceiving. That's absurd — obviously I can conceive of those things. So something has gone wrong.

But I can't find the slip. Give me exactly five distinct candidate diagnoses — which step fails and why. Each should be a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
