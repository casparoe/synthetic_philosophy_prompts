Government-issued identification documents — passports, driver's licenses, national ID cards — almost universally include a sex or gender marker, typically M or F. But jurisdictions differ radically in what they take this marker to represent and what they require to change it. Some treat the marker as recording sex assigned at birth and make it effectively immutable or changeable only with proof of surgical alteration. Others treat it as recording gender identity and allow amendment on the basis of self-declaration alone. Still others have introduced a third category, X, while leaving the underlying logic of the binary markers unclear. Meanwhile, the same government that permits self-identification on a driver's license may continue to use birth-assigned sex for determining sports eligibility, prison housing, or the sex listed on a birth certificate — sometimes within the same agency. The result is a patchwork in which a person can be legally male for one purpose and female for another, or can change their marker on one document but not another, without any obvious statement of what the marker is supposed to be tracking in the first place.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments this practice — or set of practices — rests on. What theory of sex and gender does a system presuppose when it puts an M or an F on an ID at all? What does each rule for changing that marker presuppose about whether gender is a biological kind, a social role, a psychological state, or something else? Are the different rules used by different agencies within the same government even mutually consistent, or do they implicitly rely on incompatible views of what gender is? And if a government wanted to make its practices coherent — to have its IDs, its sports policies, its prison assignments, and its birth certificates all presuppose the same metaphysics of sex and gender — what would it have to change, and what would the resulting system actually look like?
