I'm a GP at a practice that's gotten a bit of a reputation for being, let's say, open-minded about prescribing for what you might call enhancement rather than treatment — modafinil for healthy people pulling all-nighters, beta-blockers for musicians with performance nerves, that kind of thing. The other doctors and I have realized we have no consistent way of deciding which requests to say yes to, and it's starting to cause real tension. One colleague thinks we should just say no to anything that isn't treating a diagnosed condition; another thinks if it's safe and the patient wants it, why not. I'm somewhere in the middle and I'd like something more principled than my gut.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run through in a ten-minute consult. It should be grounded in whatever the philosophy of medicine has actually worked out about the therapy/enhancement distinction, but it has to be usable by someone who last read philosophy in undergrad. Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to clinical judgment because the question is really one of values rather than facts.

Then run it on three cases:

(1) A college student with no ADHD diagnosis asking for Adderall during finals week.
(2) A short but otherwise healthy twelve-year-old boy — predicted adult height 5'2", no growth hormone deficiency — whose parents want growth hormone.
(3) A recently widowed seventy-year-old who doesn't meet the criteria for clinical depression but wants an SSRI to "get through this."

I need you to actually commit to a specific procedure and defend where you drew the lines — don't just lay out the philosophical options and leave me to pick. And flag honestly where the procedure gives an answer I'm probably not going to like.
