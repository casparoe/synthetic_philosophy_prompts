I'm a junior, and something's been bothering me about a controversy at my school that I think might have a philosophical angle. Our student council president proposed moving the annual talent show from Friday to Thursday so it wouldn't conflict with the divisional basketball championship. She knew, and openly acknowledged, that this change would have two side effects: the jazz band wouldn't be able to perform because they have mandatory Thursday rehearsals, and the art club would finally get to display their work in the lobby, which they can never do on Fridays because of early building closure. She didn't move the show in order to exclude the band or to include the art club — those were just foreseeable consequences of the one decision she made for scheduling reasons.

Here's what I can't stop thinking about: in the hallway conversations and even in the school paper's coverage, everyone talks as though she intentionally excluded the jazz band. People say things like "she did that on purpose" and "she knew exactly what she was doing." But nobody — literally nobody — says she intentionally included the art club. They say the art club "got lucky" or that it was "a nice side benefit." Yet her state of knowledge and her degree of control were identical in both cases: she knew each outcome would happen, she could have avoided each by not moving the show, and she moved it anyway for an unrelated reason.

I get that excluding the band is bad and including the art club is good, but why should the moral valence of an outcome affect whether we describe someone as having brought it about intentionally? That seems like a confusion — either she did both intentionally or neither. I want to understand what's going on here before I bring it up in my philosophy club. Could you give me exactly five distinct hypotheses about why people's judgments might be asymmetric in this way, each stated in a sentence or two, as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion?
