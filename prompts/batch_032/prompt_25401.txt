I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Value and Choice" — about eighteen students, one eighty-minute session per week — and we're about to reach the unit on the formal structure of comparative value. Last week I stayed late after seminar arguing with a colleague from the economics department who holds that the transitivity of "better than" is simply a constitutive principle of practical reason: anyone who denies it, he said, hasn't grasped what a comparative judgment is. I pushed back with spectrum arguments — the kind where you construct a long chain of pairwise comparisons, each adjacent pair seems forced, and yet the endpoints appear to reverse — and he countered that all I had shown was that our judgments get noisy near indifference, not that the underlying betterness relation itself could be intransitive. We spent an hour on it and neither of us moved an inch, but I came away convinced that the issue is harder to settle than the textbooks make it sound, and that students need to encounter the pressure of the problem directly, not just read about it.

I want to design a structured in-class activity for the session before we read the theoretical papers on transitivity and spectrum arguments. The activity should take about thirty-five to forty minutes, leaving the rest of the session for a debrief. The core idea is to get students making actual pairwise comparisons — between options, outcomes, states of affairs, whatever works — and to have them discover, through their own choices or judgments, that they can be led into a cycle, or else that resisting the cycle forces them to posit a sharp break somewhere in the chain that they cannot independently defend. The students should not be told what the activity is about until the debrief; the rules, incentives, or roles should be arranged so that they run into the intransitivity (or the pressure to deny it) on their own. The debrief should then map specific moments in the activity onto the formal question — distinguishing, for instance, between intransitivity in revealed preference, in comparative judgment, and in the betterness relation itself, and saying what would have to be true for the activity to bear on each. I would also appreciate an honest note on what predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs this: where students tend to resist the setup, how they try to escape the cycle without confronting the philosophical issue, and whether the activity's structure accidentally lets them off the hook in a way that needs to be patched in discussion.
