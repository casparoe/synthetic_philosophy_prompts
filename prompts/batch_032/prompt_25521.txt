something that's been bugging me and i'd love a philosopher's take on it. in the us and a bunch of other liberal democracies, there's this weird asymmetry in how we treat religious reasons in official decision-making. a legislator can get up on the floor and say "i'm voting for this bill because my faith demands it" and sure, some people might disagree, but nobody thinks they've violated a basic norm of democratic deliberation — it's considered totally within bounds. but if a federal judge wrote "i'm ruling for the plaintiff because scripture commands it" in a judicial opinion, that would be a scandal of the first order, probably grounds for reversal and judicial discipline. and yet both of them are state actors making decisions that bind other people. so what i want to know is: what theory of public reason, or of the proper place of religion in public life, does this asymmetry presuppose? is there actually a coherent theory that makes sense of it, or is the practice just inconsistent — two different intuitions that haven't been reconciled? and if we took the best theory seriously, would it push us toward policing legislative speech more like we police judicial speech, or loosening up what judges can say, or something else? i'm less interested in the black-letter constitutional answer (i know about the establishment clause) and more in the underlying political philosophy that would justify or undermine the distinction. also, does it matter that legislators are elected and judges aren't, or is that a red herring?
