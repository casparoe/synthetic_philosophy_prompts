I teach a graduate seminar on global priorities research — about 18 students, one 90-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework without my having to lecture it at them. Design a multi-round resource-allocation game in which small groups act as a foundation deciding how to distribute a fixed budget across a set of candidate cause areas.

The cause profiles should be constructed so that the sequence of rounds forces participants to discover the multiplicative logic themselves. In the first round, groups should see only descriptions of the problems and their scale, and they should predictably fund the biggest, most salient ones. In the second round, they receive new information about what other funders are already doing — some of the problems they chose turn out to be crowded, and they must confront the idea that marginal impact depends on how many others are already working there. In the third round, they receive evidence about intervention effectiveness — some of the remaining candidates turn out to be nearly intractable despite being large and neglected, and they must confront the idea that a problem's being big and ignored does not matter if you cannot make progress on it. The sequence should lead someone in the room to say something like "we should be looking for problems that are big, solvable, and ignored" before the facilitator names the framework.

Give me the full setup: the cause-area cards with their profiles (specify scale, solvability, and current funding levels in concrete terms the groups can use), the budget constraint, the rules for each round, the information revealed between rounds, a scoring rule that rewards groups for maximizing expected impact without using real money, and a detailed debrief that maps what happened in each round onto the three components of the framework and shows how they combine multiplicatively rather than additively. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — groups that fixate on a single criterion and refuse to update between rounds, groups that treat the scoring rule as the point rather than the reasoning, the risk that one cause area is too obviously the right answer and short-circuits the discovery, and the risk that the facilitator accidentally names the framework too early — and how to handle each. Be honest about what the activity cannot capture: in the real world, all three parameters are deeply uncertain and contested, whereas the game hands participants clean numbers; the game also cannot reproduce the fact that real cause prioritization involves comparing problems that differ in kind, not just in degree, so the commensurability the scoring rule assumes is itself a substantive philosophical commitment the facilitator should flag.
