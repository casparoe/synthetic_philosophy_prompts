I've been working through an argument that seems to force a deeply uncomfortable choice, and I would appreciate a rigorous diagnosis. The argument is not mine — it emerged from a reading group on animal ethics — but I find I cannot easily locate the flaw, and each way out seems to exact a price I am reluctant to pay.

Here is the argument, reconstructed as clearly as I can:

(1) If an existing being's death is compensated by the creation of a new being who will live an equally happy life, then the death does not constitute a net harm (the Replaceability Thesis).

(2) Most animals raised for food live lives that are, on balance, worth living — the pleasures they experience outweigh the sufferings.

(3) In standard agricultural practice, each animal killed is replaced by a new animal who will likewise live a life worth living; the total quantity of animal welfare is maintained or increased.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), killing animals for food does not constitute a net harm, and the practice is permissible — at least on any broadly consequentialist framework.

(5) The reasoning from (1)–(4) does not appeal to any property unique to nonhuman animals. It relies only on the Replaceability Thesis and the claim that the beings in question live lives worth living and are replaced. So if the reasoning is sound for animals, it is sound for any being who satisfies those conditions — including humans.

(6) Thus, the Replaceability Thesis would permit killing and replacing happy humans.

(7) But it is not permissible to kill and replace happy humans.

(8) Therefore, either the Replaceability Thesis is false, or there is some morally relevant property that distinguishes humans from animals and blocks the inference from (4) to (6) without relying on bare species membership.

I regard (7) as fixed. I am also committed — though less fixedly — to the view that bare species membership is not a morally relevant property. That leaves me two main options: reject the Replaceability Thesis at (1), or identify a property at (8) that blocks parity.

Rejecting (1) seems to require holding that death is an uncompensable harm — that no amount of new happiness can make up for the loss of an existing life. But this view has its own difficulties. If death is an uncompensable harm, does that mean bringing new beings into existence is always wrong, since every life ends in death? Or does it mean only that death cannot be compensated by a different being's happiness, which would push us toward a person-affecting rather than impersonal axiology — and is that a stable position?

On the other hand, identifying a parity-blocking property at (8) seems to require finding a trait that all humans possess and no animals possess, which is morally relevant to the permissibility of killing and replacing. Candidates include: the capacity for future-directed desires, autobiographical memory, or the ability to conceive of one's own death. But each of these runs into the problem of marginal cases — some humans lack the trait, and some animals may possess it to some degree.

Where does the argument go wrong? If you had to give up one premise, which would it be, and what does your preferred resolution cost?
