I've been reading L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience and I've basically come around to her view — that there's a genuine, distinctive problem for rational choice when you're facing a decision whose outcome would change you in ways you can't anticipate from the outside. You can't know what it's like to have a child, or to become religious, or to lose your hearing, until you've actually undergone it, and worse, you can't even know what your future preferences will be because the experience might change those too. So the standard model of weighing expected utilities against your current preferences just doesn't have the traction it's supposed to. I find this pretty convincing, and it seems to capture something real about why big life decisions feel so intractable.

But I'm worried I've just fallen for a seductive story. So I want to know: what would count against this view? Not just the standard objections I've already heard (like "we make these decisions all the time and it's fine" or "you can just ask other people what it's like"), but the serious stuff. What observations, arguments, or cases should move me away from Paul's position? I want the defeaters ranked from "this would be fatal" to "this is awkward but survivable," and I want to know which ones a committed defender of the view would actually concede as counting against them versus which ones they'd dismiss as missing the point. And here's what I'd personally find most persuasive: show me that the supposedly distinctive epistemic problem either collapses into ordinary uncertainty that the standard model already handles, or that it proves too much and would rule out decisions we clearly should be able to make rationally. Also, be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it just the nature of philosophical claims about the limits of decision theory?

One more thing — I'm especially curious wether there's a good empirical angle here. Like, if we got better at predicting how our preferences would shift after major life changes (through longitudinal studies or whatever), would that dissolve the problem, or does Paul's point cut deeper than that?
