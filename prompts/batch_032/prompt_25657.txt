I'm preparing a unit for my advanced undergraduate seminar on self-knowledge and self-deception, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* as a literary case study. The novel is narrated by Stevens, an aging English butler who, over the course of a road trip through the West Country, revisits his decades of service to Lord Darlington — a man who, it gradually and painfully emerges, was a Nazi sympathizer and collaborator. What interests me philosophically is the way Stevens's narration seems to exhibit something structurally akin to Moore's paradox. As you know, Moore's paradox concerns sentences of the form "p, but I don't believe that p." Such sentences are not logically contradictory — both conjuncts can perfectly well be true — yet they are absurd to assert. The standard diagnosis is that assertion expresses or conventionally implies belief, so asserting p while simultaneously disavowing belief in p generates a kind of pragmatic incoherence. But Stevens's narration is subtler than the textbook examples. He never says anything as bald as "Lord Darlington was a great man, but I don't believe he was." Instead, he asserts things — about Darlington's nobility, about the dignity of his own professional choices, about his feelings for the former housekeeper Miss Kenton — while the texture of his narration, his digressions, his conspicuous omissions, his moments of involuntary emotional leakage, and his repeated returns to scenes he claims not to find troubling all seem to betray that he does not fully believe what he is asserting, or that he believes something he cannot bring himself to state.

I'd like you to read the novel through the lens of Moore's paradox and the surrounding philosophical literature on the norms of assertion, the nature of belief, and the transparency of mind. In particular: does Stevens's narration genuinely instantiate something like a Moore-paradoxical structure, or is the analogy superficial? One might object that Moore's paradox concerns occurrent, second-person assertion — I say to you, "p, but I don't believe that p" — whereas Stevens's narration is retrospective, interior, and addressed to no one in particular (or perhaps to himself). Does that difference matter to whether the paradox applies? And if the analogy does hold, does the novel suggest that the standard philosophical diagnosis of Moore's paradox — that it violates a norm of assertion — is incomplete, and that the real drama is not about assertion at all but about something like self-constitution, or the stories we need to tell to make our lives and our sacrifices bearable? I want a reading that takes both the philosophy and the novel seriously, that does not reduce Ishiguro to an illustration of a philosophical point, and that is willing to say where the novel complicates or resists the philosophical framework. If you think the Moore's paradox lens is the wrong one — that what Stevens exhibits is better understood through, say, Sartrean bad faith, or Freudian repression, or some epistemic failing that has nothing to do with the norms of assertion — I'd like you to say so plainly and explain why, rather than forcing the fit.
