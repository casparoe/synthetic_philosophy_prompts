I'm a neuroscience graduate student, and the Mary's room thought experiment has been coming up repeatedly in our lab discussions about consciousness. I've been testing my intuitions on a range of cases where someone gains a new kind of experience after having only propositional knowledge, and I'd like to know what principle captures my verdicts — or which ones I need to give up.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Mary learns all the physical facts about color vision while confined to a black-and-white room, then sees red for the first time. She learns something new: what it's like to see red. This is factual knowledge she lacked.

2. Someone studies everything there is to know about bicycle physics, the neuroscience of motor control, and the mechanics of balance — but has never been on a bike. When they finally learn to ride, they acquire something new, but it's a skill, not factual knowledge. This feels different from Mary's case.

3. Someone memorizes a complete neuroscientific account of what happens in the brain during gustation, then tastes a durian for the first time. They learn what durian tastes like. This feels like knowledge, not just a skill — it patterns with Mary, not the bicycle.

4. A blind person learns all the physical facts about echolocation — the acoustics, the neural processing, everything — and then learns to echolocate by making clicks and interpreting the echoes. I'm torn. Part of me says they learn what echolocation is like, which would be knowledge. Another part says it's just a skill, like the bicycle. I can't settle this one.

5. Someone learns all the physical facts about the neurochemistry of romantic love, then falls in love for the first time. They learn what it's like. This patterns with Mary and the durian — it's knowledge.

The tension I can't resolve is this: cases 1, 3, and 5 feel like acquiring factual knowledge, but case 2 feels like acquiring a mere skill. Yet I can't identify a principled difference between learning what it's like to ride a bike and learning what it's like to see red. Both involve a new experience underdetermined by the physical facts. So either the bicycle case is more like Mary than I think, or Mary is more like the bicycle than I think.

Is there a coherent account that delivers all my verdicts, or must I overturn one? Please take a definite position — I want a theory, not a survey. And please tell me what your theory says about case 4, since I can't settle it myself.
