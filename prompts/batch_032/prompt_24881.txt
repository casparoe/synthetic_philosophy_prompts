I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've been going down the anthropic reasoning rabbit hole for a few months now. The thing I keep coming back to is the SSA vs. SIA debate — self-sampling vs. self-indication. I've read the Bostrom stuff, the Manley paper, the back-and-forths, and honestly it feels like the debate has been frozen for years. Everyone knows the moves: SSA gives you the doomsday argument, SIA gives you the presumptious philosopher, and then people just sort of pick their poison based on which conclusion they find less crazy. But nobody seems to be able to say why the other side is *wrong* in a way that the other side would accept.

What I want to understand is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a disagreement about what counts as evidence — like, whether your own existence is evidence for more people existing? Is it a disagreement about the right reference class, and if so, is that an empirical question, a conventional choice, or what? Or is it something deeper, like a bedrock intuition about what "I" even means in a probabilistic context, where neither side can argue the other into submission because there's no shared premise to appeal to?

I have a working hypothesis that the whole thing might be a verbal dispute dressed up as a formal one — that SSA and SIA are really answering different questions about what we're trying to do when we reason under uncertainty about our own location. But I'm not confident about that, and I'd rather hear someone else's diagnosis before I commit to it. So: diagnose the impasse. What would actually count as progress here — a new thought experiment, a formal result, a conceptual distinction that reframes the whole thing — or is this just one of those debates where we should expect smart people to disagree forever?
