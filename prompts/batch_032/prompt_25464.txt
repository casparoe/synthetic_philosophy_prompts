The sorites paradox arises from the vagueness of predicates like 'heap'. Consider a single grain of sand: clearly not a heap. Now consider the principle that if n grains do not make a heap, then n+1 grains also do not make a heap — adding one grain cannot turn a non-heap into a heap. By repeated application of this principle, we can conclude that no number of grains makes a heap, not even a million. But a million grains clearly is a heap. So we have a paradox: plausible premises lead by apparently valid reasoning to a contradiction.

The epistemicist response, developed most prominently by Timothy Williamson, denies the inductive premise — the claim that if n grains don't make a heap then n+1 grains don't either. According to the epistemicist, there is in fact a sharp boundary between heaps and non-heaps. There is some particular number k such that k grains do not make a heap but k+1 grains do. The reason the paradox seems compelling is that we cannot know where this boundary lies. Vagueness is thus a matter of ignorance: 'heap' has a precise extension, but we are necessarily unable to identify its boundaries.

This response has the significant virtue of preserving classical logic. Unlike supervaluationism, which must reject bivalence for borderline cases, or many-valued approaches that introduce intermediate truth values, epistemicism allows us to keep all the standard logical principles. The law of excluded middle holds: for any number of grains, either it is a heap or it is not. The epistemicist simply insists that one of these disjuncts is true, even though we cannot tell which.

However, epistemicism faces a serious objection. If the meaning of 'heap' fixes a precise cutoff — say, 37 grains — then meaning must be able to draw a distinction between 37 and 38 grains. But competent speakers of English cannot distinguish these two cases: if you showed them 37 grains and 38 grains and asked which is the heap, they would not give consistent answers. Since meaning is constituted by speakers' dispositions to apply terms, and speakers have no disposition that distinguishes 37 from 38, meaning cannot fix a cutoff at 37. The epistemicist's sharp boundary is therefore not determined by anything in our linguistic practice, which makes the view mysterious: where does the boundary come from, if not from how we use the word?

Williamson responds that meaning is determined by use in a holistic way, not by individual application dispositions for each case. But this response is unsatisfying because it merely gestures at holism without explaining how use could determine a specific number. If our use of 'heap' is equally consistent with the cutoff being at 37, 38, 39, or any number in that region, then holism does not pick out one of them. The epistemicist is left saying that there is a fact of the matter about which number is the cutoff, but this fact floats free of anything we do or could know. For this reason, epistemicism does not resolve the sorites paradox so much as replace it with a mystery about how meaning works. A satisfactory resolution should not require us to accept unknowable sharp boundaries that are ungrounded in linguistic practice.
