Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* is often taught as a play about the ethics of political assassination, but it is rarely read against the most influential medieval treatment of the question: Aquinas's discussion of tyrannicide. Aquinas draws distinctions that the play seems to both anticipate and complicate — between the tyrant who usurps power and the legitimate ruler who rules tyrannically, between action by public authority and by private citizens, and between the justice of removing a tyrant and the prudential question of whether the removal will make things worse. Read the play through Aquinas's framework. Where do Brutus, Cassius, and the other conspirators map onto Aquinas's categories, and where does the play resist that mapping? Does the play's aftermath — the civil war, the rise of the triumvirate, the fates of Brutus and Cassius — function as an argument about the prudence condition in particular? And does Shakespeare ultimately vindicate Aquinas's caution, or does the play leave room for a view of tyrannicide that Aquinas's categories cannot capture? I am writing an essay that uses the play to teach the philosophical issues, and I want a reading that takes both the drama and the scholastic distinctions seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other.
