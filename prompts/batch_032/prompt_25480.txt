I've been turning over an argument that seems to prove that Black's famous two-sphere universe is impossible, and I cannot find the exit. I'm sure something is wrong with it — the conclusion is too strong — but the friends I've tried it on either reject different steps or tell me the whole thing is question-begging without saying exactly how. Here is the argument:

(1) For any two distinct concrete objects, there must be some explanation of why they are two rather than one — that is, there must be a ground of their distinctness. (Restricted Principle of Sufficient Reason)
(2) The only candidate grounds of distinctness are: (a) a difference in intrinsic qualitative properties, (b) a difference in relational qualitative properties, or (c) a primitive non-qualitative thisness (a haecceity) possessed by each object.
(3) In Black's scenario — a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of exactly the same size, composition, temperature, and color, separated by two feet — the spheres share all their intrinsic qualitative properties by stipulation.
(4) They also share all their relational qualitative properties: each is two feet from an iron sphere of the same kind, each is at the center of a universe containing exactly one other such sphere, and so on. Any relational description true of one is true of the other, because the setup is perfectly symmetric.
(5) Primitive thisnesses are mysterious: they are properties that make an object the very object it is, yet they are not analyzable in qualitative terms, and positing them seems to give up on explaining distinctness rather than providing an explanation. So (c) is not a genuine ground.
(6) Therefore none of the candidate grounds of distinctness is available in Black's scenario.
(7) So, by (1), the two spheres cannot be genuinely distinct. The scenario describes a single sphere, misdescribed as two.
(8) But the scenario is perfectly coherent. We can imagine it, describe it without contradiction, and reason about it. If it were impossible, that impossibility should be detectable from the description alone, yet generations of philosophers have treated it as at least conceptually possible.

The conclusion (7) seems intolerable, but each premise looked plausible when I wrote it down. I am particularly unsure about (4): does the symmetry of the description really guarantee that the spheres share all relational properties, or does the fact that there are two of them already give each a relational property the other lacks (e.g., being-two-feet-from-sphere-A versus being-two-feet-from-sphere-B)? But that way of individuating relational properties seems to presuppose the distinctness we are trying to ground, which would make the argument circular in a different way. And I worry that rejecting (1) means giving up on the Principle of Sufficient Reason altogether, which feels like a large price.
