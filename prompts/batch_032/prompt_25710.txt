I'm helping to organize a small philosophy conference on free will and moral responsibility, and one of our invited speakers is a philosopher who has spent much of their career defending the Frankfurt-style argument against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. Their position, in brief, is that Frankfurt cases show PAP is false: an agent can be morally responsible for what she does even if she could not have done otherwise, because the presence of a counterfactual intervener who would have forced her to act had she wavered does not undermine her responsibility when she acts on her own. I have been asked to moderate the Q&A after their talk, and I want to do better than the usual round of questions that simply invite the speaker to restate the argument. The audience will be mostly faculty and graduate students who already know the basic moves, so the questions need to engage the debate at its current pressure points — the places where the literature has pushed hardest against Frankfurt-style cases in the last decade or two.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for me, along with follow-ups? I want the questions to press on at least the following issues, though you should also include whatever else you think a well-prepared interlocutor ought to raise. First, the flicker-of-freedom strategy: even if Frankfurt cases eliminate the ability to do otherwise in the coarse-grained sense, they seem to leave in place some alternative possibility — a flicker, a sign, a neural event — and the question is whether that flicker is robust enough to ground responsibility, or whether it is too thin to do the work. Second, the dilemma that Robert Kane, David Widerker, and others have pressed: either the prior sign that triggers the intervener is causally sufficient for the action (in which case the case presupposes determinism and begs the question against the incompatibilist), or it is not causally sufficient (in which case the agent retains alternative possibilities after all, and the case fails to eliminate them). Third, a question about what the argument actually establishes: even if Frankfurt cases show that PAP is false, does that fact advance compatibilism, or might PAP be false for reasons that are neutral between compatibilism and incompatibilism — and if the latter, what is the dialectical payoff? Fourth, the scope question: do Frankfurt cases work equally well for omissions, for negligence, for cases where the agent acts from a conflicted or akratic will, or are they persuasive only for a narrow class of deliberate actions performed wholeheartedly? And fifth, I would like at least one question that connects the Frankfurt literature to the manipulation arguments — Mele's zygote argument, Pereboom's four-case argument — and asks whether the speaker's defense of Frankfurt cases commits them to saying anything uncomfortable about manipulated agents.

For each question, anticipate the reply the speaker is most likely to give — the standard move, the well-rehearsed distinction, the response they have given in print — and include a follow-up that pushes past it, the way a good moderator keeps the exchange from ending at the first answer. Please do not include any generic or biographical questions: no "what drew you to this topic," no "what are you working on now," no "what advice would you give graduate students." The questions should all be substantive and should show familiarity with the state of the debate. Write your response in continuous prose only — no bullet points, no numbered lists, no headings, no bold or italic text, just paragraphs the way one would write a letter or an email.
