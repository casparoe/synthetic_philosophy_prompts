I've been sitting in on a metaphysics reading group that's been working through the literature on dispositions, and last week two members got into a protracted argument that none of us could resolve. The issue was the distinction between finks and masks. For context: a fink is something that removes a disposition when its stimulus condition occurs — the classic case being a wire connected to a safety device that cuts the current the instant the wire is touched, so that the wire, though live, ceases to be live before it can deliver a shock. A mask, by contrast, leaves the disposition intact but prevents it from manifesting — like a dose of poison that remains lethal but whose effect is blocked by a simultaneously administered antidote. The poison is still poisonous; its manifestation is merely interfered with.

The disagreement started when David proposed the following case: a patient has a cardiac condition that makes him disposed to suffer ventricular fibrillation whenever his heart rate exceeds 180 beats per minute. He has an implanted defibrillator that detects the arrhythmia and delivers a shock within seconds, restoring normal rhythm before any loss of consciousness occurs. David claimed the defibrillator is clearly a mask. The disposition to fibrillate is still there — the heart tissue itself hasn't changed, and the arrhythmia does in fact begin — but the manifestation (sustained fibrillation leading to collapse) is blocked by the device.

Elena pushed back. She said that if we take the disposition in question to be the disposition to undergo sustained, dangerous fibrillation, then the defibrillator isn't merely blocking a manifestation — it's altering the causal basis of the disposition itself. The implanted device is part of the patient's overall cardiac system, and a system with a working defibrillator simply isn't disposed to sustained fibrillation in the way an unprotected heart is. So the defibrillator is functioning as a fink: the very occurrence of the stimulus (the arrhythmia) triggers the device, which removes the disposition before it can fully manifest.

David replied that this move proves too much. By Elena's reasoning, you could redescribe any mask as a fink just by incorporating the masking factor into the "system" and redefining the disposition. The antidote becomes part of the poison-plus-antidote system, which isn't disposed to kill — so the antidote is now a fink, not a mask. He said the whole distinction collapses if we allow this kind of redescription.

Elena answered that the difference is that the defibrillator responds to the very stimulus that triggers the disposition — the arrhythmia itself — whereas an antidote doesn't respond to the poison's entering the bloodstream; it's just already present, passively blocking the mechanism. She said this temporal and causal link between stimulus and countervailing intervention is what makes something a fink rather than a mask, and her case has it while David's redescription of the antidote doesn't.

David said this just relocates the problem: now we need a theory of what counts as "responding to the stimulus," and we'll find borderline cases all the way down. A slowly administered antidote that activates only once a certain concentration of poison is detected — is that now a fink? What about a packing material that deforms progressively under impact, so that it "responds" to the striking? He argued that if the distinction can't be drawn without appealing to another distinction that is itself blurry, then the original contrast is doing less work than the literature assumes.

I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move. At each stage, who has the better argument, and why? And by the end, does either position emerge as stronger, or does the exchange reveal a genuine instability in the fink/mask distinction itself — a problem the literature may not have adequately addressed?
