I've been teaching an upper-level philosophy of mind course this semester, and we've spent the last three weeks on the conceivability argument against physicalism — the argument that because we can conceive of a world physically identical to ours but lacking phenomenal consciousness (a "zombie world"), such a world is metaphysically possible, and therefore physicalism is false. My sharper students have pushed back hard on the step from conceivability to metaphysical possibility, and I want to give them a genuinely new way of testing that step. Not by rehearsing the standard Kripkean moves about water and H2O, which they've already dissected, and not by appealing to the usual physicalist rejoinders about phenomenal concepts, which they find question-begging in the other direction. I want a parallel case from entirely outside the philosophy of mind — a kind of stress test for the inference itself.

Please construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. Design a scenario in which some state of affairs is conceivable in the sense relevant to the zombie argument: it must be coherent on careful, reflective consideration, not ruled out a priori, and not obviously contradictory on its face. Yet there must be a strong, independent reason to think that state of affairs is metaphysically impossible — where the reason it is impossible is not simply that some natural-kind term rigidly designates a thing whose essence excludes the scenario, à la "water is not H2O." The case must not involve consciousness, phenomenal experience, or any proprietary concept from philosophy of mind; draw it from metaphysics, mathematics, the foundations of physics, or another domain where modal claims can be made precise. The structural analogy to the zombie conceivability argument should be tight: you are looking for a scenario that seems coherent when we reflect on it carefully, that we cannot rule out from the armchair alone, but that we nonetheless have excellent reason to believe could not obtain — where the reason it could not obtain is not obvious on the face of the description itself.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following. First, explain why the state of affairs counts as conceivable in the relevant sense — what would an ideal reasoner say about it, and why can't they spot the impossibility a priori? Second, explain why it is nevertheless metaphysically impossible, and identify what kind of necessity (nomological, metaphysical, mathematical, or something else) is doing the work. Third, lay out the structural analogy to the zombie argument in detail: what in your case corresponds to the physical facts, what corresponds to the phenomenal facts, and where the two arguments run in parallel step by step. Finally, address the question of disanalogy head-on: what is the best move a defender of the conceivability argument could make to show that your case is not a genuine parallel — that the inference from conceivability to possibility remains safe in the zombie case even if it fails in yours? Be candid about whether that defensive move succeeds or whether it exacts a cost elsewhere in the anti-physicalist's position.
