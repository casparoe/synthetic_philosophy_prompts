I'm teaching a unit on the problem of divine hiddenness next semester — Schellenberg's argument that a perfectly loving God would make his existence sufficiently clear to anyone who sincerely seeks him, so the persistence of nonresistant nonbelief is evidence against God's existence. I want to pair the philosophical texts with the Coen brothers' film *A Serious Man*. If you haven't seen it: Larry Gopnik is a Jewish physics professor in 1960s Minnesota whose life falls apart — his wife leaves him for a pompous widower, his tenure case is in jeopardy, his brother won't get off his couch — and he keeps going to rabbis for guidance and getting nothing back but platitudes, shaggy-dog stories, and silence. The film is a dark comedy, but its also the most sustained cinematic treatment of divine hiddenness I know. I'd like a reading of the film through the lens of the philosophical debate. Where does the film dramatize Schellenberg's case, and where does it complicate or resist it? Does it take a position on whether Larry's failure to encounter God is evidence against God's existence, or is it doing something else? And what does the film add that the arguments alone miss — something about the texture of the experience, maybe? Also, tell me which assumptions your reading depends on and how it would shift if you dropped them.
