She's been going through cases trying to figure out when this actually works, and she's got a set of verdicts that I think are pretty consistent but I can't quite nail down the rule she's running.

Here's what she says so far:

- Two AIs debate whether a code patch fixes a buffer overflow. Judge is a senior software engineer. Verdict: works.
- Two AIs debate which of two macro models better explains the 2008 crash. Judge is an econ PhD. Verdict: works, though she says it might take a while.
- Two AIs debate whether some specific AI alignment proposal actually solves the problem it claims to. Judge is a philosophy PhD who doesn't know machine learning. Verdict: debate fails — the philosopher can't tell when a technical argument is subtly wrong.
- Two AIs debate the tax implications of a complex statute. Judge is a first-year law student. Verdict: fails.
- Two AIs debate a factual claim where one side has a dataset the other doesn't. Judge is a domain expert. Verdict: works — the side without the data gets caught out when they can't answer specific questions.
- Two AIs debate whether a 500-page proof is correct. Judge is a mathematician who hasn't read the proof. Verdict: fails — the judge can't verify steps in real time and both sides can make plausible-sounding moves that the judge can't check.

The judge is a research engineer at an AI lab — they train models every day, they understand loss functions and reward shaping and training dynamics, but they've never read the alignment literature and don't know the technical definitions of things like "deceptive alignment" or "gradient hacking."

I'm trying to figure out what she'll say. But more importantly, I want to know what rule she's actually running. I can think of at least two rules that fit all her verdicts so far, and they give different answers on this new case. What are those rules, which one fits better, and what further case would cleanly settle which one she's using?
