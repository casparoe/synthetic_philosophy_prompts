**Exam Question:**

Susan Wolf, in her 1982 paper "Moral Saints," argues that moral sainthood is not an ideal to which we should all aspire. Reconstruct the central argument of the paper, making sure to (i) explain the distinction between the Loving Saint and the Rational Saint, (ii) clarify the role that non-moral values play in Wolf's critique, and (iii) identify what you take to be the most serious objection to her view and assess whether it succeeds.

---

**Student's Answer:**

Wolf's paper challenges the intuitive idea that being as morally good as possible is always desirable. Her central argument is that a moral saint—someone who is as morally good as can be—would have to be so single-mindedly devoted to moral concerns that they would lack the capacity to develop or appreciate non-moral goods, and that this makes moral sainthood an unattractive personal ideal.

Wolf distinguishes two kinds of moral saint. The Loving Saint is someone who genuinely cares about others and acts from a sense of impartial benevolence; their own happiness simply coincides with promoting others' welfare. The Rational Saint, by contrast, does not naturally take joy in moral action but instead disciplines themselves to do the right thing out of a sense of duty, overriding their own inclinations for the sake of morality. Wolf's key move is to argue that both types are problematic, though for different reasons. The Loving Saint seems more appealing at first glance, but Wolf argues that even the Loving Saint's life is impoverished because their happiness is entirely parasitic on others' well-being—they have no independent sources of fulfillment. The Rational Saint is worse off still, since they are engaged in a constant struggle against their own desires, which makes their life one of perpetual self-denial.

The role of non-moral values is central to Wolf's critique. She argues that a life worth living must make room for non-moral excellences and pursuits—things like artistic achievement, athletic skill, culinary appreciation, deep personal friendships, and intellectual curiosity pursued for its own sake. The moral saint cannot properly engage with these goods because moral considerations will always take priority. If a moral saint reads a novel, it must be to become more empathetic; if they play a sport, it must be to build character or serve others. The point is not that these non-moral goods are equally weighty as moral ones, but that a life without them is missing something important—something that makes a human life rich and admirable in a well-rounded way. Wolf therefore concludes that moral sainthood is not a rational ideal; a person can be perfectly wonderful without being a moral saint, and indeed a moral saint would fail to be wonderful in certain important respects.

The most serious objection to Wolf's view comes from utilitarianism. A utilitarian might reply that Wolf has simply described the correct moral ideal and then complained that it is demanding. If morality really does require us to maximize aggregate well-being, then the fact that this ideal is personally unappealing does not count against it—morality is not in the business of making our lives attractive. Wolf anticipates something like this objection when she considers the "point of view of the universe" argument, and she responds that the impersonal standpoint is not the only standpoint from which to assess a human life. But I think this response is insufficient. The utilitarian can grant that there are other standpoints while maintaining that the moral standpoint is the one that matters when we are asking what we morally ought to do. Wolf's argument seems to show at most that moral sainthood is personally costly, not that it is morally optional. So while Wolf succeeds in making us feel the strangeness of the moral saint's life, she does not decisively refute the utilitarian claim that we are nonetheless obligated to approximate it.
