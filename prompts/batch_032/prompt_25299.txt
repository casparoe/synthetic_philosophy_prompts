When we say someone is "irritable," what do we mean? The natural answer is: if you poke them, they'll snap. Philosophers call this a dispositional property — a property defined by what would happen under certain stimulus conditions. Fragility is the textbook case: something is fragile if it would break if dropped.

But this simple conditional analysis has problems. In the 90s, people like C.B. Martin and David Lewis pointed out that dispositions can be "finked." A fink is something where the very stimulus that's supposed to trigger the disposition also destroys it. Imagine a glass wired to a sensor: the moment something tries to drop it, the sensor instantly reinforces the glass so it won't break. The glass never breaks when dropped. But intuitively it's still fragile — it's just that the test condition itself keeps intervening.

Then there are masks. A mask doesn't destroy the disposition; it just blocks the manifestation. The glass is still fragile, but you've wrapped it in bubble wrap. Drop it, it doesn't break — but the fragility hasn't gone anywhere, it's just prevented from showing.

Here's the move I want to make. When my son's teacher says he's "not trying" because he won't sit still and finish the worksheet, she's treating the classroom as a neutral test of his dispositions. But what if the classroom is functioning as a mask? What if he has the disposition to focus, but the environment systematically prevents it from manifesting? And here's the trickier part — what if some of the things we do to help (medication, accommodations) are finks rather than masks? That is, they don't just reveal the underlying disposition by clearing away obstacles; they actually change the disposition itself while the intervention is active. And maybe we don't always know which is which, and maybe it matters for how we think about what someone is "really" like.

I want to argue that a lot of everyday trait attribution relies on a naive conditional analysis that ignores finks and masks, and this has real stakes — for kids in school, for performance reviews at work, for how we judge people's character generally. We treat a disposition's failure to manifest as proof it was never there, when the environment might be finking or masking it.

But I'm worried I'm stretching the concepts. In the metaphysics literature finks and masks are discussed for things like fragility and solubility — physical dispositions with well-defined stimulus conditions. Psychological traits are messier. Maybe the whole thing is a category mistake and I'm just dressing up a mundane point about "environment matters" in fancy metaphysics jargon. Or maybe the analogy is fine but I'm getting the fink/mask distinction wrong in how I apply it. Honestly I've read like four papers and I'm not sure I've fully grasped the difference in edge cases.
