When the negative points out that moratoriums also carry risks (medical breakthroughs delayed, climate mitigation stalled, economic harms from forgone innovation), his response circles back to the same phrase, as if the proverb itself tells us which risks to prioritize and which to tolerate.

I'm starting to suspect that "better safe than sorry" is doing a lot more work than it can actually support, but I'm not sure how to pin down exactly what's wrong. The phrase seems to slide between several different claims depending on how hard you press it. Could you unpack the distinct precise principles that "better safe than sorry" might be expressing — from the most modest and nearly tautological to the strongest and most demanding — and evaluate each one? For each reading, I'd like to know whether it's plausible as a general principle, whether it actually resolves the tension between the risks of action and the risks of inaction, and whether it genuinely supports a moratorium on emerging technologies. I'm trying to figure out whether my teammate has a real argument or just a rhetorical device that sounds like one.
