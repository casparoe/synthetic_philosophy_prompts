I spent my career in experimental physics, and since retiring I have been reading my way through metaphysics — philosophy of time, in particular. One thing I cannot get past is the presentism–eternalism debate. Presentists hold that only the present moment and its contents exist; the past is gone and the future is not yet. Eternalists hold that past, present, and future are all equally real — the block universe. Both sides accept the same physics. Everyone agrees that special relativity tells us there is no privileged foliation of spacetime into a cosmic "now," and that the relativity of simultaneity makes trouble for any view that picks out a single present as ontologically special. The empirical facts are not in dispute, and haven't been for a century. And yet the debate rolls on, with sophisticated arguments on both sides — about truthmakers for past-tensed statements, about the rate of time's passage, about how to interpret the growing block as a compromise — and neither camp seems to move. I have watched this for several years now and I honestly cannot tell whether the two sides are having a substantive disagreement about the world or whether something else is going on.

My working hypothesis, coming from a field where persistent disagreement usually meant someone was measuring the wrong quantity, is that presentists and eternalists are answering different questions under the same heading — perhaps one side is doing descriptive metaphysics (what does our ordinary concept of time commit us to?) and the other is doing revisionary metaphysics (what picture of time does our best physics recommend, ordinary concepts be damned?). But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I can imagine alternatives: perhaps it is a verbal dispute about the word "exists," or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, or a case where the question itself is ill-posed because "existence" does not have a univocal meaning across tensed and tenseless contexts. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, supported by what each side actually says, and an honest answer about what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a new way of framing the question — or else an argument that we should not expect progress and should stop expecting it. Please end with a one-sentence summary of your diagnosis as the final line of your reply.
