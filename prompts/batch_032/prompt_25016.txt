I've been stuck on this thing from my political philosophy class and it's making me a little nuts, so I'm hoping someone can walk me through it. We read about the discursive dilemma — you know, the List and Pettit thing — and the way the prof set it up, it looked like an argument that majority rule just can't produce coherent group judgments, full stop. Which seems like it would mean parliaments and courts and basically every committee that's ever existed are secretly irrational, and that can't be right. But I tried to lay the argument out step by step and I genuinely can't find the exit. Here's my reconstruction:

(1) A group forms its collective position by taking majority votes on the relevant propositions.

(2) Sometimes the propositions are logically connected. For instance: a three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable. Liability requires both (A) that the defendant owed a duty of care, and (B) that the defendant breached it. So the conclusion (C = liable) is equivalent to A ∧ B.

(3) There are two ways to run the vote. Procedure 1: vote directly on C. Procedure 2: vote on A and B separately, then let the group's position on C be whatever follows from the majority's positions on A and B.

(4) These two procedures can come apart. Here's the classic case:

- Judge 1: A true, B false → votes not liable
- Judge 2: A false, B true → votes not liable
- Judge 3: A true, B true → votes liable

Majority on A: 2–1 for true. Majority on B: 2–1 for true. So under Procedure 2, the group affirms A, affirms B, and therefore ought to affirm C. But under Procedure 1, the group votes 2–1 against C. So the group's collective judgment depends on which procedure you pick, even though the underlying individual votes are the same.

(5) So majority rule can yield a group that affirms each premise but denies the conclusion that follows from them. That's straightforwardly inconsistent.

(6) An inconsistent set of beliefs is irrational. If a group holds inconsistent beliefs, the group is irrational.

(7) Therefore majority rule is not a rational procedure for forming group judgments.

(8) But majority rule is the only game in town — any alternative (giving someone a veto, weighted voting, etc.) either collapses into majority rule in disguise or is arbitrary.

(9) So no democratic body can be guaranteed to form rational collective judgments.

The conclusion of (9) feels absurd. Real institutions do form judgments, and we criticize them when they're inconsistent, which presupposes they can be consistent. But every premise seems individually plausible and I can't see which one breaks. If I reject (1), I need some other aggregation rule that doesn't have the same problem, and I don't know what that would be. If I reject (6), I'm saying groups don't need to be logically consistent, which seems like giving up on the whole idea of group rationality. If I reject (4), I'm just denying arithmetic. Where does this argument actually go wrong? And whatever move you make to block it, what else do you have to pay — like, does your solution force you into a weird position about what group beliefs even are, or about whether some propositions are off-limits for majority voting?
