A friend of mine who has been reading popular philosophy books summarized the sorites paradox for me like this:

"Here's the sorites paradox. Take a heap of sand. If you remove a single grain, it's still a heap — one grain can't make the difference between a heap and a non-heap. But if you keep applying that rule, you end up forced to say that a single grain of sand is a heap, which is absurd. So the paradox proves that vague words like 'heap' are logically inconsistent. The only way out is to say there's some exact number of grains where it stops being a heap — but obviously no such number exists, because that's not how the word 'heap' works. So the real lesson is that ordinary language is riddled with contradictions we just ignore, and if we want to be rigorous we should replace vague terms with precise ones."

I have a feeling this isn't quite right — I know there are philosophers who think vague language is perfectly fine and that the paradox doesn't refute it — but I can't put my finger on exactly where my friend's summary goes wrong or what the alternative responses are. Can you go through it and tell me which parts a philosopher of language working on vagueness would accept, which are oversimplifications or misrepresentations, and whether the conclusion about replacing vague language with precise terms actually follows from the accurate parts?
