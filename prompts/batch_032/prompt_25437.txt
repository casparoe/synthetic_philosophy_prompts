I'm in a high school philosophy seminar, and over the semester our teacher has given us verdicts on a series of cases about when inequalities are unjust and when society owes people compensation. I've been keeping track, and I think I can see a pattern, but I'm not sure I've nailed down the rule she's running. Here are her verdicts:

1. A child is born with a rare genetic disorder requiring lifelong expensive treatment. She says the cost should be socially covered — this is a clear case of bad brute luck.

2. An adult with a stable middle-class job quits to start a restaurant, knowing that most new restaurants fail within two years. The restaurant fails and he loses his savings. She says society has no obligation to compensate him — he took a calculated gamble and it didn't pay off.

3. A woman grew up in a neighborhood with severely underfunded schools, unstable housing, and exposure to violence. She developed a substance use disorder as a teenager and is now in her late twenties, unable to hold a job. Our teacher says society does have an obligation to help her, because the background conditions that shaped her vulnerability were not things she chose.

4. Someone is born into a wealthy family and inherits a large fortune at twenty-one without having done anything to earn it. She says the resulting inequality is unjust and the state is justified in taxing that inheritance heavily.

5. Someone from an ordinary background works eighty-hour weeks for fifteen years, builds a successful company, and becomes quite wealthy. She says this inequality is not unjust and should not be taxed away at nearly the same rate as the inheritance case — the wealth traces to effort and choice, not luck.

6. A person with average opportunities takes a career risk that most people in their position would consider reasonable — going back to school in their thirties for a field with decent but not guaranteed prospects. The gamble doesn't pay off and they end up worse off than before. She says they are owed partial but not full compensation — more than the restaurateur but less than the person with the genetic disorder.

7. Someone of average talent and background chooses to work part-time as a barista to devote most of their time to painting, knowing they will never earn much. They live modestly but happily. She says no redistribution is owed to them — they chose a life with less money in exchange for more time doing what they love.

Next week we're discussing a new case, and I want to see if I can predict her verdict. The case is: Henry is born with a strong genetic predisposition to impulsivity and poor long-term planning — this is well-established in his family history and confirmed by his own lifelong pattern. He grows up in an average home with decent schools and no unusual adversity. As an adult, he makes a series of impulsive financial decisions — credit card debt, a car loan he can't afford, quitting jobs on a whim — and ends up in poverty. The impulsivity made it genuinely much harder for him to make prudent choices than it is for most people, but he was never legally incompetent and no one forced his hand on any particular decision.

What do you predict her verdict will be, and why? More importantly, I want you to identify the rule or rules that could consistently generate her first seven verdicts, say which rule best fits the pattern, and tell me what that rule predicts for Henry. If more than one rule fits her track record equally well and they give different predictions for Henry, tell me which further case would distinguish them. Finally, please separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested — I don't want a contested view presented as if it were the consensus.
