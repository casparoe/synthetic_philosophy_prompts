I've been teaching a grad seminar on decision theory and I've come around to the view that the Ellsberg paradox genuinely undermines Savage's sure-thing principle as a normative requirement — not just as a descriptive fact about how people choose, but as a normative challenge. The standard setup: an urn with 90 balls, 30 red and 60 that are black or yellow in unknown proportion. Most people prefer betting on red over black (known 1/3 vs. unknown between 0 and 2/3) and also prefer betting on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow (known 2/3 vs. unknown between 1/3 and 1). That pattern can't be represented by any single probability distribution over the three colors, so it violates the sure-thing principle. The natural interpretation is ambiguity aversion — people prefer known probabilities over unknown ones, and I think this is a legitimate rational attitude, not a bias to be corrected. Subjective expected utility theory just doesn't have the resources to capture it, and that's SEU's problem, not the agent's.

What I want is a clear-eyed map of what would count against this view. Not the surface-level stuff every textbook mentions — I know about the two-urn version, I know people sometimes violate the pattern when the setup is made more transparent, I know about the competence hypothesis and the comparative ignorance effect. I want the deeper challenges. What experimental results would genuinely embarrass the ambiguity-aversion-as-rational story, the ones that make even sympathetic researchers pause? What theoretical results — Dutch book arguments, representation theorems that recover ambiguity neutrality from seemingly innocuous axioms, dynamic consistency problems — put real pressure on it? Which of these do defenders of the view actually concede as counting against them, and which do they dismiss as question-begging or as imposing the very SEU framework that's in dispute?

And here's what I'm most curious about: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Between all the available machinery — different weighting functions for different sources of uncertainty, α-maxmin, variational preferences, smooth ambiguity models, the whole menagerie — is there any possible choice pattern that a committed ambiguity-aversion theorist would accept as counting against their position? Or have they built in enough degrees of freedom that the view doesn't really risk anything anymore? If it's been insulated that way, is that a problem for a normative theory, or is it just what you'd expect from a view about rationality — that it's more like a framework than a falsifiable hypothesis? I want an honest assessment, even if the answer is uncomfortable.
