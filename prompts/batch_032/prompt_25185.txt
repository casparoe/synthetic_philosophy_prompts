I'm teaching a small philosophy of probability seminar this semester — mostly undergrads, a couple of grad students auditing — and we've been going through Bertrand's paradox for the last two sessions. You know the setup: you've got a circle, an inscribed equilateral triangle, and you ask "what's the probability a random chord is longer than a side of the triangle?" Depending on how you generate the chord — picking two random endpoints on the circumference, picking a random radius and a random point along it, or picking a random point inside the circle as the midpoint — you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4. The students are hooked, but now they're totally tangled up about what the paradox actually *means*. Some think it's a straightforward refutation of the principle of indifference. Others think indifference is fine, you just need to apply it to the right parameterization (but which one?). A couple of the sharper students are leaning toward the subjectivist line that there's no fact of the matter about the "right" prior — the paradox just shows that ignorance doesn't pick out a unique distribution. And one guy in the back keeps insisting it's all just a confusion about different physical sampling methods and there's no deep philosophical problem at all.

I want to give them a taxonomy — like a decision tree or flowchart — that maps the logical space of responses to the paradox. The tree should start from the most fundamental dividing question (something like: does the paradox show the principle of indifference is broken, or can it be salvaged?) and then branch on further commitments until you hit named positions. The leaves I definitely want included are: (a) the strict frequentist rejection of indifference, (b) Jaynes's invariance / group-theoretic solution, (c) the subjectivist "it's a feature not a bug" view, (d) the dissolutionist view that the three methods are answering three genuinely different questions, and (e) the idea that indifference just needs to be applied to the "natural" or "privileged" partition (I'm thinking of something like the maximum entropy approach, though I'm not sure if that collapses into Jaynes or is genuinely distinct). If there are other important positions I'm missing, please add them — just flag which ones are yours so I know what I didn't think of.

For each branching question, phrase it so a student can answer without already knowing the philosophical labels — I want the tree to sort people by what they believe about the paradox, not by which philosopher they've heard of. And for any combination of answers that doesn't map to an actual defended position, mark it as empty and say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't cleanly seperate and tell me what extra question would split them.
