I recently had a long argument with a friend about the sunk cost fallacy, and it left me unsettled. He was adamant that honoring sunk costs is never rational, and afterward he sent me a passage from a popular behavioral economics book he's been reading. Here is the summary he relied on:

"The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to throw good money after bad. Once you've invested time, money, or effort into something, you feel you have to see it through — but this is always irrational. The only thing that should matter for any decision is future costs and future benefits. What you've already spent is gone no matter what you do, so it can't be relevant. People who finish books they hate, stay in bad relationships, or keep funding failing projects are just letting their emotions override their reason. The cure is simple: when making a decision, ask yourself what you would do if you were starting fresh today, and do that."

I pushed back with several examples: someone finishing a PhD to avoid acquiring a reputation for quitting, a country persisting in a costly war so that adversaries don't learn they can outlast it, a parent continuing a child's piano lessons so the child learns that commitments matter. My friend dismissed all of these as rationalizations — he said the PhD student is just dressing up the fallacy in respectable clothes, the country is committing the same error at a geopolitical scale, and the parent is using the child as a proxy for their own psychological hang-ups.

I suspect his summary is too sweeping, but I don't know enough decision theory or philosophy of rationality to say exactly where it goes wrong — or whether I'm the one who is confused. Could you go through his summary and tell me what a philosopher or decision theorist would accept, what they would push back on, and whether my counterexamples actually challenge the core claim or simply miss the point?
