So I've been reporting on decision theory and behavioral economics for years, and the argument that's really gotten under my skin is L.A. Paul's thing about transformative experiences. The way she sets it up, some decisions — having a kid is the classic one, but also stuff like emigrating, converting religions, or even getting a cochlear implant if you've been deaf your whole life — are "transformative" in a specific technical sense. You can't know what the outcome will be like from the inside before you do it, because the experience is epistemically new. And on top of that, the experience might change your core preferences, so the person evaluating the outcome afterwards isn't even the same person, preference-wise, as the one making the choice. So the whole apparatus of expected utility theory — list outcomes, weight by probability, compare — just doesn't have a grip. You're making a decision without knowing the values you'll use to asses it.

I find this basically persuasive, and it's made me look at a lot of life decisions differently. But I want to stress-test it properly. What would actually count against this view? Not the stuff people always say — "we manage somehow" or "just use testimony from people who've done it." I mean: what empirical findings would undermine it? If we got really good at predicting hedonic adaptation, or if developmental psychology showed that core preferences are way more stable across major life transitions than Paul assumes, would that do it? What about formal results — has anyone shown that the problem can be tamed within some extension of decision theory, like with imprecise probabilities or regret-based reasoning? And what would a defender of the view have to concede as counting against it, even if they think the view survives?

Also, I worry about whether the view has been insulated in a way that's a problem. If the claim is "you can't make a fully rational decision about transformative experiences" but then you add "though you can make a good-enough one based on X" or "the irrationality is only in this narrow technical sense," at what point has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know whether the view actually risks anything — whether there's a possible world where we'd look at the evidence and say "yep, transformative experience isn't the problem Paul thought it was."
