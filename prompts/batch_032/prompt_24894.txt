I keep noticing a pattern in arguments that I don't have a name for, and it's driving me a bit nuts. Here's what happens. Someone makes a claim like "there's a reason to preserve this forest" or "there's a reason to believe the defendant is guilty." Not "I have a reason," not "you have a reason," not "the court has a reason" — just that the reason is sort of... out there, floating, not attached to any particular person. When I push on this and ask "a reason for whom?" the response is usually something like "it's just what the balance of considerations supports" or "any rational agent would see it."

So far this might just be agent-neutral reasons, I get that. But here's the specific thing I keep seeing: later in the same argument, the person will treat this unattached reason as if it automatically generates a reason *for some specific agent* — and they don't seem to notice they've crossed a gap. Like: "There's a reason to reduce emissions" slides into "therefore Norway has a reason to cut its oil production." Or "there's a reason to think the butler did it" slides into "therefore the jury ought to convict." The first claim is about reasons floating free; the second is about reasons had by particular people in particular epistemic or practical situations. And the slide is never argued for — it's as if the existence of a free-floating reason is treated as sufficient for anyone and everyone to have it.

I've seen versions of this in climate ethics, in epistemology (especially discussions of what "the evidence supports"), in everyday moralizing, and even in arguments about what "the market" or "the science" says. It's not exactly the naturalistic fallacy or the is-ought gap, because both claims are normative. It's not exactly the agent-neutral/agent-relative distinction either, because the issue isn't whether the reason's content makes essential reference to an agent — it's about whether the reason attaches to anyone at all in the first claim, and then how it magically attaches to someone specific in the second. It also doesn't seem to be quite the same as the "view from nowhere" or an impersonal standpoint, because those are stances you can deliberately adopt; this is more like an unnoticed equivocation that does real argumentative work.
