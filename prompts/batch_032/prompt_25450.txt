Health insurance systems routinely cover interventions aimed at restoring normal functioning — insulin for diabetes, SSRIs for major depression, cochlear implants for profound deafness — while denying coverage for interventions that would enhance functioning beyond the normal range, even when the same technology is covered for a therapeutic purpose: growth hormone for diagnosed deficiency is covered; for a short-but-healthy child it is not. The same therapy/enhancement distinction organises anti-doping rules in sport, the approval pathways of drug regulators, and the professional remit of medicine itself.

Reconstruct the implicit theory that this distinction, as it operates across these institutions, presupposes. Address the following in particular. (1) Does the practice strictly presuppose a substantive account of normal human functioning — a theory of human nature — or is it compatible with a welfarist view on which the only thing that matters is whether an intervention makes someone better off? (2) What theory of the health/disease boundary is presupposed, and which philosophical accounts of that boundary (naturalist, normativist, hybrid) can sustain the practice and which cannot? (3) Are the commitments of the practice consistent across insurance, sport, and drug regulation, or do these domains implicitly rely on different and potentially incompatible theories? (4) If the therapy/enhancement distinction turned out to be philosophically indefensible, what would have to change in at least one of these institutions, and what would the practical costs of that change reveal about what the distinction was really doing all along?
