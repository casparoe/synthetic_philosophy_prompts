Okay so I've been following some local politics and I noticed something weird about how we do public debate. When someone on the city council argues against a zoning change by saying "this is what my faith teaches about stewardship of the land," everyone gets uncomfortable and basically tells them they need a secular reason. But when someone else argues for the same zoning change by saying "this maximizes aggregate welfare in the long run according to utilitarian principles," nobody blinks. Both are comprehensive moral views. Both are controversial — plenty of reasonable people reject utilitarianism. But only the religious one gets flagged as out of bounds.

This seems to be a pretty general feature of liberal democratic practice, not just my city council. Courts, legislatures, regulatory agencies — there's this norm that coercive laws need to be justified with reasons that are "public" or "accessible," and religious reasons fail that test. But secular comprehensive doctrines (utilitarianism, Kantianism, libertarianism, etc.) sail right through, even though they're no more accessible to citizens who don't already accept their starting points. A Rawlsian Catholic who accepts public reason is supposed to translate her religious convictions into terms others could endorse; a Rawlsian utilitarian doesn't have to translate anything. That's the asymmetry I'm poking at.

So here's what I want you to do. Reconstruct the theory of political legitimacy that this asymmetric practice presupposes. In particular: (1) What is the principle that's supposed to rule out religious reasons but not secular comprehensive ones? (2) Is that distinction actually defensible, or does the practice rest on a mistake about what makes a reason "public"? (3) What would the practice have to look like if we took the asymmetry seriously and applied it consistently — would secular comprehensive doctrines also need translation into something thinner, and what would that thinner thing even be?

- "practice_description": a one-sentence description of the practice
- "presupposed_principle": the principle of legitimacy the practice seems to presuppose (one sentence)
- "asymmetry_rationale": the best rationale you can reconstruct for why the practice treats religious and secular comprehensive doctrines differently (one sentence)
- "is_defensible": a boolean, true if you think the asymmetry is ultimately defensible, false if not
- "consistent_version": what the practice would look like if the underlying principle were applied consistently (one sentence)
- "rival_theories": an array of named alternative theories of political legitimacy that would license a different practice, each with a one-sentence gloss on what they'd change
