**Question:**

Explain the bootstrapping problem for process reliabilism and the related problem of easy knowledge. Are these two problems distinct, or is one a special case of the other? Do they succeed as objections to reliabilism, or does the reliabilist have adequate responses? Be precise about the structure of each argument.

**Student's answer:**

Process reliabilism holds that a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a reliable cognitive process — one that tends to produce true beliefs. This is an externalist theory: the subject need not know or have reasons to believe that the process is reliable; it just has to be reliable in fact.

The bootstrapping problem, introduced by Jonathan Vogel in "Reliabilism Leveled" (2000) and developed further in "Epistemic Bootstrapping" (2008), is meant to show that reliabilism licenses epistemically circular reasoning. The classic example: suppose Roxanne owns a gas gauge that is in fact reliable, though she has no independent evidence of this. She looks at the gauge, sees it reads "F," and forms the belief that the tank is full. According to reliabilism, this belief is justified because perception of a reliable gauge is a reliable process. Now Roxanne can reason as follows: "The gauge reads F, so the tank is full. Therefore, on this occasion, the gauge read accurately." By repeating this across many occasions, she can amass evidence that the gauge is reliable — concluding that it tends to give accurate readings. But intuitively, this reasoning is objectionably circular: Roxanne is using the gauge to establish its own reliability. So reliabilism seems to permit an illicit form of bootstrapping.

The easy knowledge problem, also introduced by Stewart Cohen (2002), is essentially the same problem in a different guise. Cohen points out that if reliabilism allows basic perceptual knowledge — e.g., I can know that there is a red table in front of me just by looking, without first establishing that my vision is reliable — then by the closure principle (if S knows P and knows that P entails Q, then S knows Q), I can deduce that I am not in a skeptical scenario. For instance: I know the table is red; I know that if the table is red, then it is not a white table illuminated by red light; therefore I know I am not being deceived by red lighting. This yields "easy" knowledge of anti-skeptical propositions. The bootstrapping problem and the easy knowledge problem are therefore two descriptions of the same underlying difficulty: reliabilism makes it too easy to acquire knowledge of the reliability of our cognitive faculties.

Do these problems succeed as objections to reliabilism? I think the bootstrapping problem is the more serious of the two. The reliabilist's best response to the easy knowledge problem is to reject the closure principle that the argument relies on — and indeed, many reliabilists (following Dretske and Nozick) do reject closure. Once closure is abandoned, the easy knowledge problem dissolves. But the bootstrapping problem does not depend on closure; it depends only on reliabilism's claim that a belief can be justified simply by being the output of a reliable process, plus ordinary deductive inference. So rejecting closure does not help with bootstrapping.

For bootstrapping, the reliabilist might try a different response: deny that Roxanne's track-record reasoning actually generates justification, because her inference to "the gauge is reliable" depends essentially on premises that are themselves justified only if the gauge is reliable. The reasoning is circular in a way that prevents the acquisition of new justification. However, this response risks making reliabilism collapse into a kind of internalism, because it seems to require that the subject have independent reasons for trusting the process — which is exactly what reliabilism was designed to avoid.

Ultimately, I think the bootstrapping problem shows that reliabilism is false, or at least incomplete. A theory of justification needs to say more than just that the process is reliable; it needs to rule out circular track-record arguments as justification-defeating, and doing so seems to require internalist constraints on what the subject must antecedently believe or have reason to believe.
