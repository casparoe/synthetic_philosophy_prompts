I recently had a health scare — nothing terminal, but enough to make me think seriously about what I'd be leaving behind. I noticed something odd about my own reactions. I wasn't just worried about the people I'd miss or the experiences I wouldn't have. I was worried about whether the things I've spent my career building — a research program, a set of institutions — would continue and flourish after I was gone. Not because I'd be around to feel proud or disappointed, but because it seemed to matter in itself whether the projects I'd invested in kept going or collapsed. And when I imagined, as a thought experiment, that everyone died a week after I did — that humanity simply ended — that felt like a much worse outcome than a world where I died but humanity continued, even though in both scenarios my own life is identical and I'm not around to experience the difference.

I mentioned this to a colleague in the philosophy department, and she said something like "oh, that's the X problem" — but I've forgotten the name she used, and I'm embarrassed to ask again. I've tried searching, but I keep landing on the non-identity problem or Parfit's work on future generations, which isn't quite it. What I'm after is the specific idea that the survival of humanity (or of one's projects, or of the things one values) after one's death matters to one's own life going well, or matters in a way that standard accounts of prudential value have trouble capturing. Does this phenomenon or argument have an established name? If so, what is the nearest literature, and how exactly does it differ from related ideas like the non-identity problem, Parfit's future-generations arguments, or the rationality of caring about posthumous reputation?
