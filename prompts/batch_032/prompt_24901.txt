In Anglo-American criminal trials, when inadmissible evidence is accidentally presented to a jury—a hearsay statement, a defendant's prior conviction improperly mentioned—the judge issues a curative instruction: the jury is told to disregard what they just heard. If the evidence is deemed too prejudicial to be set aside by instruction alone, the judge may declare a mistrial. Both practices seem to presuppose that a juror can, upon being told to do so, set aside a belief they have already formed and reason exclusively from the remaining, admissible evidence.

What theory of belief, or of epistemic agency, does this practice presuppose? Does it require doxastic voluntarism—the view that we can choose our beliefs directly, by an act of will—or can a weaker account of indirect control (looking away, redirecting attention, deliberately weighing countervailing evidence) suffice? And if a juror finds themselves in a state of epistemic akrasia—believing the defendant is guilty on the basis of the excluded evidence while simultaneously judging that the admissible evidence, taken alone, does not warrant that conclusion—does the practice have the resources to say what has gone wrong, or does it simply insist that the juror not act on the akratic belief? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the curative instruction, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely hopes for, and say whether the practice is coherent if we accept that belief is not under direct voluntary control.
