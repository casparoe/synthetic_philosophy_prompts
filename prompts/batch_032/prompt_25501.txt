I am a novelist working on a book whose central question is whether a civilization ought to announce itself to the wider universe. In the course of my research I keep running into the dark forest hypothesis — the idea, drawn from Liu Cixin's Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy, that the cosmos is like a forest at night: every civilization is a silent armed hunter, and if you so much as strike a match you get shot, because the only rational response to discovering another intelligence is to destroy it before it destroys you.

What interests me is that people in my circles — writers, technologists, people in the effective altruism and AI safety communities — routinely deploy the dark forest as an analogy that does real argumentative work. They use it to argue against METI, to explain the Fermi paradox, and increasingly to argue that we should treat advanced AI systems as dark-forest adversaries: reveal nothing, assume the worst, preempt if you can.

I want to audit this analogy properly before I build a novel around it. Please lay out the full mapping: what in the real world corresponds to the forest, the hunters, the darkness, the gunshots, the silence? Where does the analogy break? Which features of the dark-forest scenario does it quietly import that might not hold for interstellar civilizations, or for AI, or for both? And once the disanalogies are on the table, do the practical conclusions people draw from the analogy — do not broadcast, do not trust, preempt — actually survive, or was the analogy carrying more weight than it can bear?
