I've been reading in the philosophy of computation for a couple of years now, and there's one thing that keeps bothering me: the debate about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis seems to have been running in place for decades, and I can't tell whether that's because the question is genuinely hard or because it's somehow ill-posed.

The basic situation, as I understand it, is this. On one side, you have people who treat the thesis — roughly, that every effectively computable function is Turing-computable — as something like an empirical hypothesis about the limits of physical computation, one that could in principle be falsified by discovering a physical process that computes a non-Turing-computable function. On another side, you have people who treat it as a definition or a conceptual analysis of the informal notion of effective calculability, so it's not the kind of thing that could be falsified by a physical discovery. And then there are people who say it's a theorem once you accept a particular formalization, people who say it's an axiom we adopt for methodological reasons, and people who say the whole framing is confused because "effective computability" isn't a single notion waiting to be analyzed — it's a family resemblance concept, or it's theory-laden from the start, or the question of its limits is simply the wrong question to ask. What frustrates me is that these positions have been on the table since at least the 1980s — arguably since the 1930s, if you look at how Church and Turing themselves understood what they were doing — and yet the debate doesn't seem to have progressed. Everyone agrees on the mathematical facts: the equivalence of the various formalisms, the absence of a counterexample, the existence of oracles and hypercomputation as formal notions. Everyone agrees on the history. And yet the disagreement persists. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about mathematics or physics at all, but about what counts as an adequate justification for a foundational claim of this kind — a disagreement about philosophical methodology that the participants haven't fully surfaced. But I'm not confident in that diagnosis, and I'd like to hear it tested.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement with a hidden empirical or formal question at the bottom, a verbal dispute about the word "thesis," a clash of methodological standards that can't be adjudicated from neutral ground, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as progress?
