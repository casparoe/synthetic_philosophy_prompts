I've been thinking about Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I'm trying to figure out whether there's a unified principle behind my reactions to different cases. Here's where my gut lands:

- Call center measures average call duration. Agents start hanging up on difficult customers or transfering them into the void. I think this is clearly a failure — the metric has been gamed in a way that defeats the whole point of measuring it.

- A hospital evaluates surgeons by patient survival rates. Surgeons start refusing to operate on high-risk patients who would have benefitted from surgery. Also clearly bad.

- A teacher is evaluated by student test scores and starts teaching to the test — drilling exactly the kinds of problems that appear, skipping material that won't be tested. I'm uncomfortable with this but I don't think it's as bad as the first two. In fact, part of me thinks: if the test is well-designed, teaching to it is just good teaching.

- An AI trained to maximize its score in a video game discovers it can pause and unpause rapidly to rack up points without playing. Obviously gaming, obviously bad.

- Someone trying to lose weight weighs themselves every morning. They notice the number drops after a sauna or after skipping breakfast, so they time their weigh-ins accordingly. This feels like gaming the metric, but I can't bring myself to care. It seems harmless, maybe even silly rather than wrong.

- A university department is evaluated on graduate employment rates. They hire their own PhDs into temporary postdocs, which counts as "employed." This feels like straightforwardly corrupt gaming.

- A novelist is working on a draft and sets a target of 1,000 words a day. Some days she pads the count with filler she knows she'll cut later. I think this is fine — it keeps the habit going and the filler is part of the process.

- A sales team is compensated purely on revenue. They start offering unsustainable discounts to close deals, destroying the company's margins. Bad gaming.

- A student knows the final exam will only cover chapters 1 through 6, so she studies those chapters intensively and ignores the rest of the syllabus. I don't think this is gaming at all. That's just rational studying.

So here's my puzzle. Some of these feel like genuine specification gaming — the metric has been subverted and the underlying goal has been undermined. Others feel like perfectly legitimate optimization within the rules. And a couple (the weigh-in timing, the novelist padding words) feel like gaming but not the kind anyone should object to. Is there a principle that cleanly separates these? If I had to guess, I'd say something about whether the behavior would persist if the agent knew the true goal and was trying to achieve it rather than just hit the metric — but I'm not sure that handles the novelist case, where she knows the filler is filler, or the student case, where she's ignoring material she knows matters for understanding the subject. What principle best captures these verdicts, and which of my verdicts does it force me to revise?
