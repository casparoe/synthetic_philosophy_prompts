I spent forty years as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I've turned to moral philosophy. I've been working through the virtue ethics literature and the situationist critique, and I keep noticing a particular defensive analogy that strikes me as both clever and suspicious — suspicious in the way that a beautiful mathematical model can be suspicious when you start asking about boundary conditions.

The analogy, as I see it deployed, goes something like this. A virtue is like a calibrated scientific instrument — a spectrometer, a strain gauge, a magnetometer. Such an instrument is designed to detect or measure a real quantity under a specified range of operating conditions. Push it outside that range — extreme temperatures, electromagnetic interference, mechanical shock — and its readings become noisy or systematically wrong. But no competent experimentalist concludes from this that the quantity doesn't exist or that the instrument wasn't really measuring it. The fault lies with the conditions, not with the construct.

The virtue ethicist then applies this to the classic situationist experiments: Darley and Batson's seminary students stepping over a man in distress because they were told to hurry; Milgram's subjects administering what they believed were dangerous shocks at the experimenter's urging; Asch's participants conforming to an obviously wrong majority judgment about line lengths. The claim is that these experiments place subjects in conditions that are, for the purposes of moral psychology, the equivalent of running a spectrometer in a furnace — unusual pressures, authority structures, and time constraints that fall outside the "design range" of whatever virtuous dispositions the subjects may possess. The experiments therefore show nothing about whether virtues exist or whether people can have them; they only show that virtues, like instruments, have operating conditions.

I find this analogy rhetorically powerful, but I want to know how far it actually holds under scrutiny. Please:

1. Construct the full correspondence table. What in the moral domain plays the role of the instrument, the quantity being measured, the calibration process, the design range, the interfering conditions, the reliable reading, and the experimentalist?

2. Identify the disanalogies — the places where the mapping strains or breaks.

3. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the analogy's intended argumentative work from those that are incidental. (For instance, instruments are built to spec by designers who know the quantity in advance; does this matter for the argument?)

4. Having laid out the disanalogies, tell me whether the conclusion the virtue ethicist wants to draw — that the situationist experiments do not seriously undermine the reality or behavioral importance of character traits — survives the audit, or whether the analogy was doing argumentative work that the underlying facts cannot support.

5. If the analogy fails, propose a better one, or explain why no analogy from the physical sciences is likely to settle a dispute that turns on what we mean by a trait in the first place.
