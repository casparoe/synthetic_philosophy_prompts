As part of a metaethics workshop, I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers who disagree about the wrong kind of reasons problem. One of them, call her the bifurcationist, holds that the distinction between the right kind and the wrong kind of reasons for an attitude — roughly, between considerations that bear on the attitude's correctness and considerations that bear on the benefits of having it — tracks a genuine normative joint, and that the WKR problem, though difficult to formulate precisely, cannot be dissolved by any unified theory of reasons. The other, call him the reductivist, holds that the WKR problem is an artifact of an overly narrow conception of reasons, and that a properly expansive account of normative reasons — one that takes all reasons to bear on the question of what to do, believe, or feel, without a primitive bifurcation — can handle the canonical cases without remainder.

They have agreed to produce a joint document with the following structure.

First, a numbered list of claims both can sign. These should include: agreement on the canonical cases that motivate the WKR problem (e.g., the demon who will punish you unless you admire him, the grant that depends on your believing the grantor's work is excellent); agreement on what a satisfactory account must explain — the data points, the intuitive judgments, the structural features of the cases; and agreement on which proposed solutions have been ruled out, or at least on what their shared desiderata rule out.

Second, a disagreements table with the following columns: the precise statement of the disagreement, in wording each side would accept for its own position; why it matters — what downstream philosophical question turns on it; and whether either side suspects the disagreement is partly verbal, and if so, how they propose to test that.

Third, a crux list: the sub-questions such that, if both sides came to agree on the answer, at least one side would change its view. For each crux, include a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, each side's prediction about what the case or argument shows, and what each side would count as a serious concession — the thing they would have to give up if the other side's prediction held up.

The document should be written so that both parties could sign it without reservation about the fairness of the presentation. It should not resolve the disagreement but should make it exact. Please draft this document.
