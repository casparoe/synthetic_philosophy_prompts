I'm a high school junior taking a world philosophy elective. We spent two classes on the School of Names and Gongsun Long's "white horse is not a horse" argument, and I'm supposed to write a final paper on it. The problem is that every treatment I've found either treats Gongsun Long as obviously correct (once you understand him) or as playing trivial word games. I don't think either is right, and I want my paper to show the dispute as genuinely hard.

Please write a dialogue (roughly 900–1,200 words) between two characters who disagree about whether a white horse is a horse. One defends something like Gongsun Long's position; the other defends the common-sense view. Neither should be a strawman — each should make moves that force the other to refine their position, and the dialogue should contain at least two turns where a character genuinely concedes part of the other's point before pushing back. The setting can be anything you choose (ancient China, a modern classroom, a dream, whatever), but the arguments should stay philosophical rather than historical. By the end, I want a reader to feel the pull of both sides and understand why the question is harder than it first looks.

Then, in a section titled "Commentary on the Philosophical Choices" (roughly 500–700 words), walk me through the key decisions you made in constructing the dialogue. In particular, address: which interpretation of Gongsun Long's argument you gave the defender (e.g., is the core issue about how terms pick out their referents, about the identity conditions of kinds, about the relationship between universals and particulars, or something else?), which objection you gave the critic and why you thought it was the strongest one available, and where in the dialogue you placed the turning-point — the moment the disagreement stops being about horses and becomes about something deeper — and why you placed it there. I'm less interested in whether you got Gongsun Long "right" historically than in whether the philosophical structure of the dialogue is honest to the difficulty of the problem.
