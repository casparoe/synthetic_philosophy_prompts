The U.S. Endangered Species Act protects "species" from extinction, but it also extends protection to subspecies and, for vertebrates, to "distinct population segments." This looks like a pragmatic compromise, but it raises a puzzle: if the Act were genuinely committed to species as the units of conservation, why protect mere parts of species? And if populations are what matter, why limit population-level protection to vertebrates? Meanwhile, the Act's administrators must decide, in practice, whether a given population counts as a species, a subspecies, or a distinct population segment — decisions that turn on taxonomic judgments. But conservation biology has no single agreed-upon species concept: the biological species concept (reproductive isolation), phylogenetic species concepts (monophyly), and morphological and ecological concepts all carve up the living world differently, yielding different counts of what exists to be protected. What theory of species does the Act's framework presuppose? Does it commit to any particular species concept, or is it agnostic — and if agnostic, is that agnosticism stable once listing decisions force administrators to take sides? Does the inclusion of subspecies and distinct population segments reveal that the Act isn't really in the business of protecting species at all, but something else — evolutionary significant units, perhaps, or whatever humans happen to value? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics and say what would have to change in conservation law if we adopted a different species concept.
