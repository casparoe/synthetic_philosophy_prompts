Spent like three hours last night arguing with my friend about van Fraassen's Reflection principle and we got absolutley nowhere. You know the setup: your current credence in P should equal your expected future credence in P, so in some sense you should treat your future self as an expert. My friend kept hammering the obvious counterexamples — you know you're about to take a drug that'll make you irrationally certain you can fly, or you know you'll be severely sleep-deprived and your judgment will be garbage. And I kept saying those aren't really counterexamples, they're just cases where you know your future credence won't track the evidence, so of course the principle doesn't apply. And he'd fire back: but then the principle isn't a principle — it's just a rule of thumb with a giant "unless you have reason to distrust your future self" escape hatch, at which point what work is it doing? And around we went.

But here's the thing — this isn't just us being stubborn. The whole literature feels stuck in exactly this loop. Christensen patches it with Rational Reflection (your credence should equal your expected *rational* future credence), Briggs has her version, and then someone cooks up a case where you know you'll be rational by your future lights but not by your current ones, and we're back to square one. Everyone agrees on the probability formalism, everyone agrees on the cases, nobody moves.

My hunch — and this is what I tried to say to my friend around hour two — is that the disagreement isn't really about credences at all. It's about what we even want from a "rationality principle." One side wants something exceptionless and formal, like a logic. The other side is fine with a defeasible norm that captures something genuine about good reasoning even if it has edge cases. If that's right, the impasse is a meta-normative disagreement about what counts as a principle, not a first-order disagreement about how to update.

But I'm not confident in that diagnosis. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal, substantive, are the two sides answering different questions under the same heading, or is the whole question just ill-posed? And given your answer, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a new conceptual distinction, or should we just admit the question was badly framed from the start?
