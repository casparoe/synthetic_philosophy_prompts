I am a software architect. For the past year I have been the primary caregiver for my mother, who has early-stage dementia, and I have found myself repeatedly reaching for my professional vocabulary to make sense of the experience — thinking about the relationship as a kind of protocol, the asymmetry of our capacities as something like a degraded API, my own exhaustion as a resource-exhaustion problem. A friend recommended I read some care ethics — Gilligan, Noddings, Tronto — and while I can tell it is getting at something real, the philosophical vocabulary sits uneasily with me. I would like you to restate the core claims of care ethics in the vocabulary of a software architect as faithfully as you can. Use the concepts I actually work with: synchronous and asynchronous communication, statefulness and statelessness, coupling and decoupling, graceful degradation, idempotency, polling versus event-driven architectures, technical debt, and the distinction between an interface specification and its implementation. For each major concept — the caring relation, engrossment, motivational displacement, the particularity of the cared-for, the network of care relations, the critique of impartiality and of principled thinking — give me the closest analogue in my vocabulary, even if the fit is imperfect. Then give me the residue report: which claims translated cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression at all in the vocabulary of a software architect. I want to know whether the residue points to something my conceptual toolkit is genuinely missing about human relationships, or whether care ethics is making claims that look substantive only because they have not yet been forced through a translation this unsympathetic.
