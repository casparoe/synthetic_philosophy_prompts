I've been teaching a graduate seminar on concepts of liberty this term, and over the weeks I've found myself increasingly convinced that the republican conception of freedom — freedom as non-domination, associated with Pettit, Skinner, and the neo-republican revival — is not merely a useful supplement to the standard positive/negative distinction but a genuinely distinct and superior account of what political freedom consists in. The core claim, as I understand it, is that you are unfree not only when someone actually interferes with your choices, but when someone has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily, even if they never exercises it: the slave with a benevolent master is still unfree, the worker subject to at-will dismissal is unfree even if the boss never fires anyone, and the citizen living under a constitution without effective checks on executive power is unfree even during periods of restraint. This, the republican says, is something negative liberty (freedom as the absence of interference) misses, and something positive liberty (freedom as self-mastery or participation in collective self-rule) overcorrects for. I want to take this view seriously enough to know what would count against it.

I'm not asking for the standard objections that a republican theorist already has rehearsed replies to — the claim that non-domination is just negative liberty with a wider notion of interference, or that it's hopelessly vague, or that it licenses an illiberal perfectionism. I want to know what a reflective defender of the view should regard as genuine defeaters: the kinds of evidence, arguments, cases, or theoretical developments that would make a reasonable republican say, "if that's right, the view is in serious trouble." What would the world have to look like — empirically, conceptually, normatively — for non-domination to turn out not to be a distinct third concept after all, or to be distinct but not especially valuable, or to be valuable but not in the way republicans think? Which of these defeaters are live possibilities, and which are merely logical ones? I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the view, as developed in the literature, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of normative political theory.
