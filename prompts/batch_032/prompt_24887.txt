I'm a PhD student in public health, working at the intersection of harm-reduction policy and sexual health outcomes. My dissertation examines how the criminalization of specific sexual practices affects population-level metrics — STI transmission rates, care-seeking delays, mental health comorbidities — through the mediating variable of stigma. The conceptual toolkit I work with daily includes distinctions like primary versus secondary harm (where the secondary harms of an intervention can exceed the primary harms of the behavior), stigma as a social determinant of health, the iatrogenic effects of punitive policy, and the proportionality principle in public health law. Recently, a colleague in the history of political thought pointed out that Jeremy Bentham made arguments about the decriminalization of sodomy and other so-called "sexual irregularities" in the late eighteenth century — arguments that were too radical to publish in his lifetime — and suggested that they anticipate some of the conceptual architecture of contemporary harm reduction. I've now read a fair amount of this material: Bentham's core moves include the claim that consensual sexual acts produce no direct harm to the participants (and thus fail the threshold condition for criminalization), that the real suffering in these cases is manufactured by the law itself and by the social opprobrium it licenses, that mere "offense to the sensibilities of others" cannot survive utilitarian scrutiny once you trace the full ledger of pleasures and pains, and that the irrationality of existing sexual prohibitions traces back to an ascetic prejudice he called the "principle of antipathy."

I'd like you to perform a systematic translation: restate Bentham's arguments about sexual morality — as they appear in these unpublished manuscripts — using the conceptual vocabulary of contemporary public health ethics and harm reduction. Map his distinctions onto ours where you can. Use the terms I listed above, and introduce others from the public health literature as needed. Do this as faithfully as possible; don't smuggle public health assumptions into Bentham and then call them his.

Then give me the residue report. Which of Bentham's claims translate cleanly into the public health framework, which translate only with distortion or stretching, and which have no expression at all in our vocabulary? I'm especially interested in whether Bentham's hedonic calculus — the idea that you can, at least in principle, sum pleasures and pains across persons and arrive at a policy verdict — has any genuine counterpart in how public health thinks about trade-offs (QALYs, DALYs, cost-effectiveness thresholds), or whether this is a point where the frameworks diverge in a way that can't be papered over. And I want your verdict on the direction of the residue: where something fails to translate, is that because the public health vocabulary is impoverished relative to Bentham's, or because Bentham's framework smuggles in commitments — about the commensurability of pleasures, about the neutrality of the calculator — that public health has good reason to have left behind?
