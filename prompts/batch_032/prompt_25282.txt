I'm a graduate student in philosophy preparing for a seminar on mental causation, and I've found myself going down a rabbit hole that I'd like some help thinking through. We've been working through Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument — the claim that if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, then mental events appear to be excluded from having genuine causal efficacy, since positing an additional mental cause would amount to objectionable overdetermination. The seminar has focused on contemporary responses: non-reductive physicalists who try to carve out a role for mental causation via supervenience or realization, interventionists who reframe the problem in terms of difference-making rather than sufficiency, and so on. But I keep wondering how earlier philosophers — whose metaphysical frameworks differ so radically from the assumptions Kim works with — would have diagnosed the problem.

In particular, I've been thinking about Spinoza. His metaphysics, as laid out in the *Ethics*, seems to cut across Kim's argument at an unusual angle. Spinoza holds that there is exactly one substance — God or Nature — with infinitely many attributes, of which thought and extension are the two accessible to us. The order and connection of ideas, he insists, is the same as the order and connection of things: every mode of extension has a corresponding mode of thought, and causal relations in the physical domain are mirrored exactly by causal relations in the mental domain, because they are ultimately expressions of the same underlying causal structure under different attributes. This is neither straightforward dualism nor straightforward physicalism, and it is certainly not the kind of non-reductive physicalism that Kim targets. On one reading, Spinoza simply sidesteps the exclusion problem: there is no competition between mental and physical causes because they are not distinct causal sequences that could overdetermine an effect — they are one and the same causal order, conceived under different attributes. On another reading, though, Spinoza's parallelism might be seen as giving up on mental causation in any robust sense. If the mental is just the physical order expressed in the idiom of thought, does a mental event genuinely *cause* anything, or does it merely accompany a physical cause that does all the real work? And if Spinoza were to bite that bullet, could he still make sense of human agency, or does his system collapse into a kind of epiphenomenalism about the mental that he himself would resist?

I'd like you to write an essay predicting how Spinoza would have responded to Kim's causal exclusion argument, had he encountered it in something like its contemporary form. The task is not to summarize Spinoza's views in a vacuum, but to extrapolate: given his actual metaphysical commitments — substance monism, attribute parallelism, the conatus doctrine, the rejection of teleology, and his account of adequate and inadequate ideas — what would he make of an argument that says mental causation is threatened by the causal closure of the physical? Consider both how Spinoza would diagnose the assumptions built into Kim's framing (e.g., does the exclusion argument presuppose a kind of dualism about causal relata that Spinoza would reject at the outset?) and what positive account of the causal role of the mental he would offer or decline to offer. Make the case for a specific predicted response and justify it by reasoning through his system, not merely by gesturing at broad affinities.
