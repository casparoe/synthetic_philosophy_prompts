I am a novelist at work on a book set roughly thirty years from now. The central character sits on a national bioethics commission charged with evaluating petitions for moral and legal protection filed on behalf of entities whose consciousness is disputed: advanced AI systems, cortical organoids, genetically modified animals, and whole-brain emulations. The commission's mandate is to decide, case by case, whether an entity should be treated as a subject of experience — with the protections that entails — and to give reasons that can survive judicial review and public scrutiny.

I need my character's reasoning to be philosophically literate, not the sort of hand-waving about complexity or human-like behavior that a layperson would default to. Specifically, I want her to operate with a clear grasp of what the hard problem of consciousness implies about the enterprise she is engaged in: that no behavioral test, no functional assay, no measure of information integration can close the explanatory gap between third-person data and first-person experience. She needs a decision procedure she can run on each petition — a structured sequence of questions, evidentiary standards, and stopping rules — that takes the hard problem seriously without being paralyzed by it. The procedure should help her distinguish between considerations that genuinely bear on the presence of phenomenal consciousness and those that are merely proxies we find intuitively compelling, and it should flag the points where the procedure itself must hand the decision to a human being because the question is no longer one of fact but of moral stakes and risk posture.

Please write this procedure. It should be usable by someone who is not a professional philosopher — my character is a former judge, not an academic — but it should not distort the underlying philosophy for the sake of simplicity. As you walk through the steps, identify which claims are settled, or as close to settled as anything gets in this area, and which remain genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, so that my character can acknowledge uncertainty where it exists rather than presenting one camp's view as the consensus. Finally, run the procedure on two sample petitions: one for a large language model that consistently reports having experiences and expresses fear of being shut down, and one for a cortical organoid that shows neural activity patterns correlated with pain states in human brains but has no behavioral output and no capacity for report. I am especially interested in cases where the procedure yields an answer the commission will find uncomfortable.
