I'm a philosophy professor putting together a summer grant proposal, and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to help me turn into something coherent. It's about Kelly betting and geometric rationality.

Here's the kernel. You know the Kelly criterion — when you're facing a sequence of favorable bets, you should bet a fraction of your bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth, not expected wealth. And there's this broader movement, sometimes called geometric rationality or ergodicity economics, that says in lots of real-world decision contexts, what matters is the time-average growth rate rather than the ensemble-average expectation, because your wealth evolves multiplicatively.

The debate between Kelly advocates and orthodox expected utility theorists seems to me to have gotten kind of stuck. The Kelly side says: look at the long-run dominance results, Kelly betting will almost surely beat any other fixed-fraction strategy. The EU side says: sure, but if your utility function isn't logarithmic, Kelly betting doesn't maximize your expected utility, so it isn't rationally mandatory — it's just one possible strategy among many, and its appeal depends on contingent features of the environment. Both are right about their own thing, and neither has really grappled with the question of when geometric reasoning has genuine normative force versus when it's just a handy heuristic that happens to work well in certain repeated settings.

My half-formed idea is that the normative status of geometric rationality depends on features of the decision context that haven't been systematically distinguished. In particular, I suspect it matters whether the bets are repeated or one-shot, whether the risks are diversifiable across independent sources, and whether the underlying process is genuinely ergodic. Maybe Kelly-style reasoning is rationally mandatory in some cells of this taxonomy, merely permissible in others, and actively misleading in still others. But I don't have the taxonomy worked out, and I'm not even sure I've identified the right dimensions.

So: write me a short proposal — say 1500 words — that states the question precisely, explains why the existing debate leaves it open, lays out the method (mostly conceptual and formal, not empirical — this isn't a psychology study), gives a realistic timeline for a summer's work, and says honestly what would count as the project succeeding versus failing. Like, if the taxonomy collapses and it turns out geometric rationality is always just expected utility with a log utility function in disguise, is that a failure of the project or is that actually a clarifying result worth publishing?

Don't bother with a bibliography, I'll add the citations myself. The audience is a general humanities funding committee, so keep it accessible but don't dumb down the philosophy. And don't overpromise — they can smell that a mile away.
