I'm trying to get clear on what we should even want from a theory of how to act under consequentialist cluelessness — the problem that the long-run downstream consequences of our actions are radically unpredictable, so the consequentialist calculus seems to break down before it gets started. By "cluelessness" I mean the phenomenon pressed in a now-familiar line of argument: when I choose a career, or decide whether to have a child, or pick what to eat for lunch, the ripple effects propagate through an unknowably complex causal network, and I have no idea whether the net consequences centuries out are positive or negative. If consequentialism tells me to maximize expected value over all consequences, but I cannot form any justified expectation about the sign, let alone the magnitude, of those consequences once the causal chain goes long enough, then the theory seems to give me nothing to go on.

Before I evaluate any particular proposal — expected value with a temporal discount, satisficing, robust-choosing, rights as side-constraints on the consequentialist framework, cause-prioritization heuristics, and so on — I want the full specification of what such a theory has to deliver.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of decision-making under cluelessness must get right, which cases it must handle, which distinctions it cannot collapse, and which results from decision theory or moral philosophy it must remain consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, how the main families of response fare against the list.

Some things I particularly want addressed: the distinction between cluelessness that is merely epistemic (we could in principle know if we had better models) and cluelessness that is in-principle (chaotic amplification, butterfly effects, non-computability); whether a theory must give action-guidance in every case or can sometimes say "no determinate answer" without collapsing into nihilism; whether the theory must respect the original consequentialist motivation — that consequences are what matter — or can revise that commitment beyond recognition and still count as addressing the problem rather than abandoning consequentialism; and whether the theory must handle all sources of cluelessness uniformly or can treat different sources (causal complexity, value-uncertainty, non-identity of future people) differently.
