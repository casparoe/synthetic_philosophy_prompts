I run a reading group on decision theory and practical rationality — about 20 people, mostly software engineers and graduate students in quantitative fields, many of them longtime readers of LessWrong and similar forums. We meet for 90 minutes. I want to run a session on money pump arguments and diachronic rationality where participants feel the force of the idea rather than just discussing it abstractly.

Design a classroom activity — a game with rules, incentives, and a structured debrief — that makes participants experience being money-pumped. The activity should have them form their own preferences over a set of options, then discover that those preferences, if intransitive, allow a "market maker" (played by me) to extract value from them through a series of individually advantageous trades. By the end they should understand why transitivity isn't just an aesthetic requirement on preference orderings but a vulnerability if violated.

Some constraints and desiderata:

- The options should be described in terms that feel natural to this group — ideally involving trade-offs between two attributes (e.g., "expected value" and "worst-case floor" for project proposals, or "precision" and "recall" for classifier designs) so that intransitive preferences arise organically from the structure of the comparisons rather than from confusion or inattention. The three options should be constructed so that in each pairwise comparison a different attribute carries the larger difference, encouraging a dimension-rotation effect that produces cycles in a substantial fraction of participants.
- The stakes should be in tokens or points rather than real money, but the extraction should feel real — participants should be able to watch their token balance decline as the pump operates, and the numbers should be large enough that losing, say, 15–20% of their starting endowment stings a little.
- The activity should work even if only a subset of participants exhibit intransitive preferences; the debrief should use both the pumped and the non-pumped as data points rather than treating the non-pumped as a failure of the demonstration.
- No lecture up front. Participants should encounter the problem through the rules and their own choices before anyone names "money pump" or "transitivity." The phrase "money pump" should appear for the first time in the debrief.

Please provide: (1) the full setup — what materials I need to prepare, what the three options are (with exact numbers), how preferences are elicited, and what participants are told about what we're doing before we begin; (2) the rules of the market phase — how trades work, what the transaction cost is, the protocol for how I approach each participant, and what I say when they ask why I'm offering these particular trades; (3) a timeline for a 90-minute session with approximate minute marks; (4) a detailed debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical architecture — the distinction between synchronic preference structure and diachronic choice, the difference between merely having intransitive preferences and being exploitable by a trader who knows them, the foresight response (refusing to trade when you notice the cycle), and what normative conclusion we should actually draw; (5) an honest account of what predictably goes wrong — participants who refuse to trade out of suspicion, participants who revise their preferences mid-game when they see where things are headed, participants who don't produce cycles, participants who try to arbitrage the market maker — and how to handle each in the moment and in the debrief; and (6) which feature of the philosophical money pump argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll need to supply myself in discussion, with a note on how to introduce it without undermining what participants just experienced.
