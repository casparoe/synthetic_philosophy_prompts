I'm a journalist working on a piece about experimental philosophy for a general audience. I recently read a popular psychology book that summarizes the Knobe effect in the following way:

"Knobe's experiments revealed that whether we classify an action as intentional depends on whether we morally approve of its outcome. In the now-famous CEO scenario, subjects read about a chairman who approves a business plan knowing it will harm the environment, while explicitly stating he doesn't care about the environmental effects — he just wants to make money. When the plan goes through and the environment is harmed, most subjects say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally. But in a parallel scenario where the plan helps the environment and the chairman expresses the same indifference, subjects deny that he helped the environment intentionally. The asymmetry is inescapable: we attribute intentionality when the outcome is bad but withhold it when the outcome is good. What this shows is that the concept of intentional action isn't a neutral descriptive tool for navigating the world — it's a covert moral judgment. We reach for 'intentional' when we want to assign blame, and we set it aside when there's nothing to condemn. The folk concept of intentional action turns out to be a moral concept through and through."

Before I rely on this as background, I'd like to know how fair the summary is. Which claims would a philosopher working on the Knobe effect accept, which would they push back on, and what does the effect actually license us to conclude about the relationship between moral judgment and the concept of intentional action?
