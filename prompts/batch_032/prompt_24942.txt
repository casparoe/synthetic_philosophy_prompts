I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of decision theory and ethics, and I've spent the better part of the last year trying to figure out where I stand on expected value fanaticism. The position, as I've come to understand it, is roughly this: if you accept that the rational choice is the one that maximizes expected value, and you refuse to place any principled upper bound on either the probability scale or the value scale, then you are committed to letting vanishingly small probabilities of arbitrarily large payoffs dominate your practical reasoning. A one-in-a-googolplex chance of preventing an astronomical amount of suffering would, on this view, outweigh the certain prevention of a single headache — and indeed would outweigh any finite amount of ordinary suffering prevented with certainty. I find myself drawn to the view because the alternatives seem to involve either an unmotivated cap on value (why this bound rather than that one?) or a departure from expected value maximization that looks ad hoc when examined closely. And I'm not satisfied by appeals to what we would or wouldn't do in practice; the view is making a claim about the structure of practical rationality, not about human psychology.

What I want — and what I haven't found a clear treatment of — is a systematic map of what would count against this position. Not a list of objections I can bat away, but an honest survey of the evidence, arguments, formal results, and conceptual considerations that should genuinely move someone who currently accepts fanatical expected value reasoning. I want to know which defeaters would be decisive (they would force me to abandon the view), which would be merely awkward (they would require significant qualification but leave something recognizable intact), and which are often presented as defeaters but on closer inspection beg the question or miss the point. I'm especially interested in whether the view makes any predictions that differ from its rivals in a way that could in principle be checked — or whether it has been insulated against empirical disconfirmation entirely. If the latter, I want to know whether that insulation is a problem for a view about the norms of practical reason, or whether it's exactly what we should expect. And I'd like to know which of these costs the view's defenders would themselves concede as genuine, as opposed to what critics claim the view is committed to.
