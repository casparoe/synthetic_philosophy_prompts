So I'm in this year-long interdisciplinary workshop on scientific classification — we've got a philosopher of biology, a psychiatrist, a chemist, and a few of us from clinical psychology. For months now, two of them have been going at each other about whether the sciences actually "carve nature at its joints." Maria (philosopher of biology) is a realist: she thinks when a science matures, its categories converge on real divisions out there — she points to the periodic table, to molecular biology, to how independent lines of evidence triangulate on the same groupings. David (historian of psychiatry) is what I'd call a pragmatic conventionalist: his go-to moves are the DSM's shifting categories, how "planet" got redefined in 2006, the fact that biologists can't agree on a single species concept, and he says our kinds are tools built for human purposes, not discoveries of pre-existing seams.

This hit me personally because I'm writing my disseration on complex PTSD and the fight over whether it should be a distinct diagnosis in the ICD-11. (It got in, barely, and the arguments on both sides were basically this debate in microcosm.) So I care about getting the philosophy right — it's not just seminar-room stuff for me.

Our workshop organizer has finally asked me to draft the document these two should have produced months ago: an adversarial-collaboration summary. I want a numbered list of claims both Maria and David would actually sign; then their remaining disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would genuinely move at least one of them; and for each crux, what kind of evidence or argument both would agree bears on it, plus what each predicts. I have my own guesses about the cruxes (something about whether convergence across methods counts as discovery vs. just stabilization of a convention, and maybe whether the "joints" metaphor itself is doing illicit work), but I want you to identify the real ones, not just confirm mine. And if any part of their fight turns out to be verbal — if they're using "natural kind" in different senses and don't realize it — say so and say why.
