I'm working through the Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments for a paper and I want to check whether my current commitments form a coherent package before I start writing. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I am a computationalist about the human mind: all human cognition is in principle realizable by a Turing machine. The mind is the software of the brain, and nothing essentially non-computable is going on.

(2) I accept Gödel's first incompleteness theorem in its standard form: for any consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system that contains enough arithmetic to represent primitive recursive functions, there is a true Π₁ sentence — a Gödel sentence — that the system cannot prove.

(3) I think Lucas was onto something genuine: a human mathematician who understands a formal system F and has good reason to believe F is consistent can, by reflecting on Gödel's construction, see that F's Gödel sentence is true, even though F cannot prove it. This seems to me like a real cognitive achievement that F cannot replicate, and it is the phenomenon any adequate philosophy of mind must account for.

(4) I also think the standard Lucas argument — which moves directly from (2) and (3) to the conclusion that the human mind is not a Turing machine — fails. The standard objection is correct: the human's inference to the truth of G_F depends on the premise that F is consistent, and establishing that premise is non-trivial in a way the second incompleteness theorem makes vivid. The argument either equivocates on "consistent" or begs the question.

(5) However, I find Penrose's reformulation more troubling than the standard refutations suggest it should be. His idea, as I understand it, is that for any proposed mechanistic model M of my own mathematical reasoning, I can — if I accept that M is sound — see that M's Gödel sentence is true, thereby transcending M. And this process of reflection-and-transcendence cannot itself be captured by any mechanistic model, on pain of contradiction. I am not convinced the argument succeeds, but I also have not found a refutation that leaves me entirely satisfied; the ones I know seem to miss something about the self-reflexive structure of the move.

(6) I am not persuaded by Penrose's positive proposal — the appeal to quantum gravity and non-computable physics — and I think the anti-mechanist argument should be evaluated on its logical merits independently of whether anyone has produced a worked-out non-computable physical theory.

(7) My working view is that the human ability to recognize Gödel sentences is itself rule-governed and mechanical, but not capturable in any single formal system we can antecedently specify. It is an open-ended, system-transcending process that is nonetheless computable in principle — roughly, the "mind is a Turing machine, but we do not know which one, and we cannot use it as a fixed object of our own reasoning" response.

(8) I hold that the second incompleteness theorem is the deeper result for this whole debate, and that any adequate resolution needs to give it more weight than the first — most of the action is in what we can and cannot know about the consistency of systems we might be, not in the bare existence of unprovable truths.

Please audit this set of commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? Order them by severity and say, for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. I am especially interested in whether (5) and (7) can be held together, and whether (3) does any work for me that (7) does not already do better. I do not need a lecture on the background — I need a fine-grained map of where my own views pull against each other and what it would cost to fix it.
