I'm drafting a paper on consequentialist cluelessness and want to pressure-test my position before I commit to it in writing. Here is where I currently stand:

(1) Act consequentialism is the correct criterion of rightness: an act is right iff it maximizes expected aggregate welfare.

(2) The cluelessness problem is genuine and pervasive: for the vast majority of ordinary actions, we cannot form justified, precise credences over their long-term consequences. The causal ripple effects are too complex and too sensitive to unknown initial conditions for us to assign anything like real-valued probabilities to most long-run outcomes.

(3) A moral criterion and a decision procedure can come apart without inconsistency. The right way to deliberate need not mirror the right-making features of actions — this is the familiar point associated with Mill, Sidgwick, Hare, and others.

(4) Given (2) and (3), the appropriate decision procedure for everyday life is to follow commonsense moral rules — keep promises, don't lie, be generous within limits — since these rules have been shaped by cultural evolution and individual experience to track welfare reasonably well in the kinds of recurrent situations humans face.

(5) The correct norm for practical deliberation under uncertainty is to maximize expected utility relative to a precise, real-valued probability function. I hold this as a claim about procedural rationality, not merely as a restatement of the consequentialist criterion.

(6) When probabilities over outcomes are genuinely indeterminate — not merely unknown but indeterminate, as in Ellsberg-style ambiguity — the correct norm for practical deliberation shifts: among acts whose short-run expected welfare is comparable, we should favor the one whose worst-case long-run outcome is least bad. I take this to be a form of ambiguity aversion operating at the level of decision procedure, not a revision to the criterion of rightness itself.
