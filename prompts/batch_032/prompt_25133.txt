I recently had a long argument with a friend about the epistemology of assertion — specifically about whether the norm governing assertion is knowledge, truth, belief, or something else — and the conversation kept circling back to a real case that neither of us could quite settle: the public assertions made by U.S. and U.K. officials in 2002–2003 about Iraq's possession of weapons of mass destruction. My friend insisted the case is a straightforward failure of the knowledge norm, since the claims turned out false. I pushed back that this misses what's interesting: some officials may have believed the intelligence, some may have been reckless with it, and some may have knowingly exaggerated. The case seems to me to cut differently depending on which norm you adopt, and it might even be an argument for one norm over another. We went back and forth on whether Colin Powell's UN speech, the Downing Street memo, the aluminum tubes claims, and the Niger uranium episode each look different under a knowledge norm, a truth norm, a reasonable-belief norm, and a mere belief norm.
