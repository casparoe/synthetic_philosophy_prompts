I have been following a colleague's jurisprudence seminar and keeping a record of her judgments on a series of hypothetical cases about what qualifies as law. Her answers so far:

(1) A statute enacted through flawless legislative procedure that requires the extermination of a disfavoured minority. She says: it is law, though judges have a moral obligation to refuse to enforce it.

(2) A statute enacted through flawless procedure that imposes a tax widely regarded as unfair but not confiscatory. She says: law.

(3) A norm that has been consistently followed by courts and citizens for centuries but was never formally enacted. She says: law, as customary law.

(4) A statute enacted through flawless procedure but never promulgated or made accessible to the public. She says: not law.

(5) A statute enacted through flawless procedure in which section A explicitly commands what section B explicitly forbids. She says: not law.

(6) A judicial ruling that openly departs from the plain text of a statute in order to avoid a result the court deems absurd. She says: law.

The next case she plans to discuss is this: a criminal statute, enacted through flawless procedure and duly published, that makes it an offence to engage in "conduct contrary to the moral order of the community," with no further definition or guidance as to what this covers. What should we predict she will say about this case, and which theory of law does her overall pattern of verdicts commit her to? If her existing verdicts underdetermine the answer — that is, if more than one theory fits them equally well — identify the candidate theories, say what each would predict about the new case, and explain which of her prior verdicts, if any, she would need to revise in order to be fully consistent with each.
