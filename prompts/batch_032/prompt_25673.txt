I'm leading a reading group of ten graduate students from mixed disciplines — philosophy, cognitive science, biology, computer science — on a single idea: Dennett's claim that Darwin and Turing each performed a "strange inversion of reasoning," showing how competence can arise without comprehension. We have ninety minutes. I don't want you to explain the idea to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

Please structure your response in the following five sections, with the rough word counts given:

1. Dependency structure (~300 words): What conceptual pieces must be in place before the inversion can be stated clearly, and in what order must they be introduced? For each piece, say what it is and why it is a prerequisite for what follows. I am especially interested in whether the Darwin inversion and the Turing inversion must be taught in a particular order relative to each other — can one stand alone, or does each need the other as scaffolding? If one should come first, justify the choice.

2. The carrying example (~200 words): Propose the single example or case that should carry the entire session — the one I return to each time a new conceptual piece is added. Say what features it must have and what it must lack (for instance, it must not be an artifact case that invites "but someone designed the artifact" objections). If you think the Darwin inversion and the Turing inversion require different carrying examples, say so and justify splitting them.

3. Misconception map (~400 words): List the misconceptions students will predictably bring or form, in the order they are likely to surface during the session. For each, state: the misconception itself; the point in the dependency sequence where it typically arises; how I should head it off (by framing, by example, by explicit warning, or by letting it surface and then undermining it); and whether heading it off early risks creating a different misconception. I expect at least these candidates, though your list need not be limited to them: conflating "comprehension" with "consciousness"; thinking the claim is that comprehension does not exist; thinking Darwin's inversion is just "randomness plus time" while missing the algorithmic substrate of natural selection; thinking Turing's inversion reduces to "it's just symbol manipulation" and therefore misses what is strange about it; and the "but who designed the process?" regress objection.

4. Check for understanding (~150 words): Propose one question I can pose in the final ten minutes whose answer would tell me whether they have grasped the inversion itself rather than merely memorized a slogan. The question should require them to apply the idea to a novel case, not to restate it. Explain what a correct answer would show and what specific wrong answers would reveal about which piece of the dependency structure did not land.

5. What to leave out (~150 words): Identify what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the tempting tangents, the technical details, the neighboring debates — and give me, for each, the one-sentence flag I should plant when someone tries to pull us there ("That is a real question but it is not tonight's question because…").
