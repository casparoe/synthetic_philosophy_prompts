I've been following the war in Ukraine closely — I have family on both sides of the border — and I keep running into a moral puzzle I don't have the vocabulary for. Perhaps you can help me identify whether this has a name in the philosophical literature, and if so, how the relevant distinctions are drawn.

The puzzle is this. When I read about a Russian soldier who fires on Ukrainian positions, I find myself thinking: he's a conscript, he didn't choose this war, he's been fed propaganda, and if he refuses to fight he'll be imprisoned or worse. So I'm reluctant to call him a murderer, even if the war itself is an unjust war of aggression. At the same time, I have no hesitation calling Putin and the generals who planned the invasion culpable for the deaths that result. So there seems to be an asymmetry: the leaders are blameworthy for an unjust war, but the ordinary soldiers who do the actual killing are not — or at least not in the same way.

But then I notice that this asymmetry doesn't seem to generalize. If a mafia boss orders a hit, we don't say the hitman is off the hook just because he was following orders or needed the money. If a corporation dumps toxic waste, we don't excuse the engineers who designed the system on the grounds that they were just doing their jobs. In ordinary life, "I was following orders" is not a full defence. Yet in war, it seems to carry enormous weight — and not just legally, but in our ordinary moral reactions.

I've also noticed that this isn't simply about duress. A professional soldier in a volunteer army who genuinely believes in his country's cause — even if that cause is, objectively, unjust — also seems to get a kind of moral pass that we wouldn't give to, say, a committed Nazi bureaucrat who genuinely believed in the Reich. There's something specific to the combatant role that seems to confer a moral symmetry between soldiers on both sides, regardless of the justice of their respective causes. And yet this symmetry is peculiar: if the war is unjust, every killing done in its service is a killing in service of an unjust end. How can that not taint the one who pulls the trigger?

I've tried to work this out on my own, but I keep going in circles. On the one hand, it seems absurd to say that a nineteen-year-old conscript is morally equivalent to the political leaders who launched an aggressive war. On the other hand, it seems equally absurd to say that killing someone is morally permissible simply because you're wearing a uniform and acting under orders — we rejected that line of reasoning at Nuremberg. So I suspect I'm missing a distinction, or conflating several different questions. Is there an established name for this puzzle, or for the view that soldiers on both sides are morally on a par? What are the main positions philosophers have taken on it? And what exactly is the strongest argument for the view that ordinary combatants on the unjust side are not culpable? I can feel its pull, but when I try to state it precisely, it keeps slipping into analogies I wouldn't accept in any other domain.
