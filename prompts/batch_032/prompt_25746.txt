I'm not a philosophy student or anything, just someone who finds this stuff fascinating and stays up too late reading about it. The thing I keep getting stuck on is: how is this actually different from plain old relativism? Like, if you say "all perspectives are partial and conditioned," isn't that statement itself a perspective? So either it's also partial (in which case maybe some perspectives aren't partial, and the whole thing unravels), or it's somehow magically exempt (which seems like special pleading).

I've read that Jains have this whole apparatus of syādvāda — the "doctrine of maybe" or conditional predication — where you prefix claims with "syāt" meaning "in some respects" or "from a certain standpoint." But I don't get how adding the word "maybe" to your sentences actually solves the self-refutation problem. It feels like a linguistic trick rather than a real philosophical move. Like, if I say "syāt the cat is on the mat," I haven't really said anything at all, have I? And then there's the nayavāda part, the doctrine of standpoints, where you're supposed to recognize that every claim is made from one of seven partial viewpoints. But listing out seven viewpoints doesn't tell me why the meta-level claim — the claim *that* there are exactly seven valid standpoints and that all knowledge is standpoint-dependent — isn't itself just another standpoint claim that undermines itself.

Can you explain how Jain philosophers actually handle the charge that anekāntavāda refutes itself? Like, what's the actual argumentative move they make — not just the surface-level "we're humble about truth" stuff but the deeper logical structure of how they avoid the trap?
