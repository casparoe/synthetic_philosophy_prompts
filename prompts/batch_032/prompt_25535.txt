I've been defending the view that the epistemic dependence we are developing on large language models and similar AI systems is qualitatively novel and distinctively corrosive to understanding — not merely an extension of familiar worries about testimony, the division of cognitive labor, or offloading memory to external devices. The core claim is that when we rely on AI to summarize, explain, or reason through material we could in principle engage with ourselves, we lose something that is not lost when we rely on human experts or on reference works: a grasp of the structure of a domain that comes from working through it oneself. This loss, I argue, is accelerated by three features that converge in AI systems in a way they do not elsewhere: the opacity of the system (we cannot inspect the inferential path that produced the output), its fluency (it generates confident-sounding prose that masks gaps and errors), and its lack of the social-epistemic accountability that constrains human testifiers (it cannot be challenged, shamed, or held to a norm of sincerity). I want to know what would count against this view — not just objections I would dismiss, but evidence, arguments, or developments that should genuinely move me.

Specifically: what would have to be true — empirically, conceptually, or normatively — for this position to be false or significantly weakened? Please assess each of the following possibilities, saying whether it would be decisive, merely awkward, or largely irrelevant, and why: (a) Well-designed empirical studies find no measurable difference in long-term understanding between AI-assisted and traditionally-assisted learners when controlling for engagement time, and the effect does not reappear in delayed follow-ups. (b) The opacity objection turns out to apply equally to human experts once we take seriously how little we can inspect the inferential processes of another mind — the difference is one of degree, not kind, and the degree is shrinking as AI interpretability improves. (c) AI systems become sufficiently interpretable that their reasoning paths can be audited in something like the way we cross-examine a human informant, and users routinely do so. (d) The relevant notion of understanding I am invoking cannot be made precise without collapsing into either mere knowledge-of-facts or a vague phenomenology of "getting it," and attempts to operationalize it for empirical study consistently fail. (e) The view overgeneralizes: if AI-assisted reasoning erodes understanding, then so does using a calculator for arithmetic, a spell-checker for writing, or an index for navigating a book — and since we do not regard those as epistemically problematic, the view must either carve out an ad hoc exception or retreat to a much weaker claim. (f) Historical parallels — the introduction of writing, the printing press, the search engine — show a recurring pattern of alarm about cognitive erosion followed by adaptation without the predicted loss, suggesting the present worry is just the latest instance of a generic techno-skeptical script.

Beyond these specific possibilities, I would like to know: does the position, as stated, risk anything at all, or has it been (or could it be) qualified into unfalsifiability? If it has been or can be insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it the sort of philosophical claim for which insulation from direct empirical refutation is acceptable because it operates at a conceptual or normative level? Finally, I would like an honest map of where the view's defenders would themselves concede vulnerability — as opposed to where critics locate the vulnerability but defenders do not — and an assessment of which of the defeaters a committed defender would take most seriously and why.
