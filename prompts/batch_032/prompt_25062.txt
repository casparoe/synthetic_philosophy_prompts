So in my econ class we were talking about incentives and my teacher mentioned this thing called Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. And it reminded me of this argument I overheard between two friends at lunch last week that I still can't sort out.

Basically our school started this new thing this year where 10% of your grade in most classes is "participation," and teachers are supposed to track how many times you speak up in class. My friend Diego thinks it's completely backfired. He says now kids just raise their hand to say the most obvious, empty stuff — like "I agree with what she said" or just restating whatever the teacher said two seconds ago — purely to check the box. He says it's a textbook case of Goodhart's law: the school wanted to measure engagement, but once they made it a target, people started gaming it, and now the metric is meaningless. He even said it might be worse than meaningless because it's training people to say things without thinking.

My other friend Kayla pushed back hard. She said before this policy, class discussions were just the same four kids talking all year, and now way more people at least try to contribute. She admited some of it is low-effort, but she said that's just how incentives work — you can't design a perfect one. She said Diego was letting the perfect be the enemy of the good, and that even "fake" participation sometimes turns into real engagement because once you've opened your mouth you're more likely to stay tuned in.

Diego came back with: but the policy doesn't just fail to measure engagement, it actually changes behavior in a way that undermines the whole point. He said it's like when a company ties CEO pay to stock price and then the CEO just does buybacks instead of improving the actual business. The proxy and the goal come apart. He said the participation grade is teaching everyone that what matters is looking engaged, not being engaged.

Kayla said that's way too dramatic for a 10% grade component, and that Diego was ignoring the kids who genuinely benefited — like her cousin who has social anxiety and said the policy gave her a reason to push through it, and now she actually participates for real.

Then Diego said: okay but that's a different argument — now you're saying it's good because it forces people to overcome anxiety, not because it measures engagement. You've switched from defending the metric to defending the incentive. And those are different things.

Kayla said she didn't think they were different — the metric creates the incentive, and the incentive changes behavior, and some of that behavior change is good. You can't separate them.

And that's where they left it. I feel like both of them made really solid points and I genuinely can't tell who's more right. Could you go through their back-and-forth and adjudicate it? Like, who's right about which specific claims, and what (if anything) are they both missing?
