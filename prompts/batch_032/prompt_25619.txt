I'm a hospital ethicist and I keep running into the same nightmare scenario: a patient wrote an advance directive years ago saying "no life-sustaining treatment if I have severe dementia," but now they have moderate dementia and seem content — they smile, enjoy pudding, recognize family sometimes. The family is split. Some say "follow the directive, she would hate this." Others say "look at her, she's happy, how can you withhold antibiotics from a happy person?"

I need an actual procedure I can use on the floor — not a seminar paper. Something my team (attending physician, nurse, social worker, family) can work through in under an hour. It should tell us: what questions to ask in what order, what counts as an answer that pushes us one way or the other, where we should stop and say "the directive stands" vs. "the directive is overridden," and — crucially — which steps are genuinely philosophical judgment calls that no algorithm can settle.

Ground it in whatever the literature on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self actually says: Dworkin's integrity view, Dresser's critique, the personal identity problem, whatever's relevant. But translate it. I don't need citations, I need a tool. Then run it on two cases: (1) the dementia case above — the contented patient with moderate dementia whose advance directive refuses antibiotics for pneumonia, and (2) a case where the patient's advance directive refuses blood transfusions on religious grounds (Jehovah's Witness), but the patient is now unconscious after a car accident, the directive is 15 years old, and the family says they left the faith five years ago — though there's no documentation of that. And tell me honestly: what kinds of cases will this procedure fail on, where I should just call the ethics committee and admit we're flying blind?
