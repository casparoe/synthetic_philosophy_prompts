I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on the philosophy of interpretability, and I've been puzzling over something. In mechanistic interpretability research, it is standard practice to train a linear probe on a neural network's hidden states and, if the probe successfully decodes some property P, to announce that one has "found the model's representation of P." Researchers speak of "the truth direction," "the sentiment direction," or "the Golden Gate Bridge feature" as if these are real components of the model's internal structure — things to be discovered rather than stipulated. The practice embeds a further methodological commitment: that representations are linearly accessible, or at least that linear decodability counts as evidence of representation.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Specifically: (a) Does it strictly presuppose realism about representations — that there are mind-independent facts about what a network represents — or is it compatible with instrumentalism, where "representation" is merely a useful shorthand for predictive success? (b) Does it presuppose that representations are linearly encoded, or could a nonlinear representational realist reject linear probes as a discovery method while keeping the realist vocabulary? (c) Is the practice internally consistent? For instance, if random high-dimensional subspaces can support above-chance decoding, does the practice's criterion of "linearly decodable therefore represented" overgenerate? (d) What would change in day-to-day interpretability work if the field adopted instrumentalism — would we stop talking about "finding" representations and talk instead about "constructing useful probes," or would the practice survive largely intact?

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. At the end, add a brief note indicating how confident you are in each point and singling out the claim you are least sure of.
