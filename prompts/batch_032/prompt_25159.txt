I was at a dinner party last weekend where the conversation turned to whether universities should adopt policies restricting "harmful language" — not just slurs, but also political euphemisms, coded rhetoric, and what one person called "linguistic strategies that erode the possibility of honest debate." A friend of a friend, who teaches political theory, shut down a long back-and-forth by quoting Orwell: "But if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought." His argument was that policing language isn't merely a matter of civility or sensitivity; it's a matter of preventing cognitive harm, because degraded public language degrades the thinking of those who use and encounter it. Several people nodded as if this settled things, and the conversation moved on.

I've been turning it over since, and I'm not convinced the slogan does the work he needed it to. For one thing, Orwell's essay is about cliché, vagueness, dead metaphors, and the kind of euphemism that lets officials talk about atrocities without picturing them — not about slurs or hate speech. For another, the sentence is so compressed that it could be unpacked into several quite different claims. Is it a causal claim about individual psychology — that using sloppy or euphemistic language literally impairs your capacity to think clearly? Is it a claim about collective epistemology — that a linguistic community that loses certain distinctions will, over generations, lose the ability to entertain certain thoughts? Is it a claim about self-deception — that political actors who reach for euphemisms are thereby fooling themselves about what they are doing, not just their audience? Is it the modest point that language and thought are correlated, so cleaning up your prose is a useful diagnostic for cleaning up your thinking? Or is it something stronger, almost a Sapir-Whorf thesis about linguistic determinism?

I'd like to go back to this person with a clearer sense of what the slogan could actually be claiming. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought" might express — from the trivial to the radical — and say for each whether it's plausible on its own terms and whether it actually supports the claim that institutions ought to restrict harmful speech?
