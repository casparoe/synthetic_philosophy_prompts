At a dinner after an effective altruism conference, I ended up sitting between two people who got into an extended argument about whether the movement has a systematic blind spot, and I found myself unable to sort out who was winning at each step. I took notes afterward and reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I could. I would like you to adjudicate it move by move — say who is right about which specific point, rate each move on its own terms, and then give an overall assessment of which position ends up stronger and why.

Here is the exchange, with the speakers labelled M and C.

(1) M said: The effective altruism movement prides itself on following the evidence wherever it leads, but its standards of evidence are not neutral. The movement heavily weights interventions whose impact can be quantified in something like dollars per life saved or per QALY, and that systematically favors direct-delivery interventions — bednets, deworming, cash transfers — over systemic or institutional interventions like policy advocacy, movement-building, or legal reform. The latter may have far larger expected impact, but their effects are diffuse, delayed, and hard to isolate, so they look worse by the metrics the movement actually uses. That is a structural bias, not a mere preference.

(2) C replied: You are describing a feature as if it were a bug. The insistence on measurable, quantified impact is exactly what distinguishes EA from ordinary charitable giving, which is driven by emotion, anecdote, and whichever cause happens to be in the news. Without a discipline of measurement, you are just following your priors about what seems important, and those priors are unreliable. The burden of proof should be on the person claiming that some hard-to-measure intervention is more impactful than the best measurable ones. If you cannot quantify the impact, you have no basis for claiming it is larger.

(3) M answered: That sets up a false choice between rigorous quantification and pure emotion. There are intermediate forms of evidence — comparative historical analysis, expert elicitation, qualitative case studies, mechanism-based reasoning — that are not RCTs but are also not just following your priors. The demand for a particular kind of quantitative evidence is itself a value-laden methodological choice, not a neutral epistemic standard. When you insist on that kind of evidence, you are not being agnostic; you are tilting the field toward interventions that happen to be amenable to that method.

(4) C responded: I grant that there are intermediate forms of evidence, and I think you are underestimating how much EA already uses them. GiveWell's cost-effectiveness models, for instance, incorporate all sorts of assumptions and judgments that go well beyond the RCT data. But the key point is that the more an intervention's claimed impact depends on speculative chains of reasoning, the more room there is for motivated cognition and self-deception. So it is appropriate to demand stronger evidence for interventions that claim to be orders of magnitude more effective than the best directly measurable ones. That is not bias; it is proportionate skepticism.

(5) M said: Even if I concede that each individual funding decision can be defended on its own terms, the institutional critique is about the aggregate. The movement as a whole directs enormous amounts of talent and money toward a narrow class of interventions, and the culture of the movement — what kinds of projects get prestige, what kinds of people feel welcome, what kinds of questions get asked at conferences — reinforces that narrowness. You can have a situation where every individual decision is locally rational but the collective result is a movement that systematically underweights structural change. The bias is in the composition, not in any single decision.

(6) C replied: That sounds like a coordination problem, not an epistemic one. If systemic interventions are genuinely more impactful, then someone should be able to make that case using the best available evidence, and EAs — who pride themselves on updating — would update and reallocate resources. The fact that this has not happened on a large scale is itself evidence that the case for systemic interventions is weaker than its proponents claim. You cannot simultaneously assert that systemic interventions are more impactful and that this cannot be demonstrated; if it cannot be demonstrated, on what basis are you asserting it?

(7) M answered: That is exactly the circularity I am pointing to. What counts as an adequate demonstration is defined by the very framework under dispute. If the framework sets the bar for "demonstrated impact" at a level that systemic interventions structurally cannot meet — because their effects play out over decades, through complex causal chains, with no possible control group — then the fact that they have not met that bar tells you nothing about their actual impact. It tells you about the bar. And the framework is not handed down from heaven; it was chosen by people with their own methodological commitments and career incentives.

(8) C said: But the bar was not chosen arbitrarily. It was chosen because alternative approaches — the kinds of broad-gesture reasoning you seem to be defending — have a terrible track record. The history of philanthropy and development is full of interventions that sounded transformative based on plausible-sounding causal stories and turned out to do nothing, or worse, active harm. The quantification movement in development economics exists precisely because of that history. You are asking the movement to lower its epistemic standards for a class of interventions whose proponents have not been able to meet those standards, and you are doing so on the basis of the very kind of plausible-sounding causal story that has failed repeatedly in the past. That is not a critique; it is a request for a free pass.

Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert who has thought deeply about both the philosophy of evidence and the structure of the effective altruism movement.
