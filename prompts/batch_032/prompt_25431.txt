I keep noticing a pattern in mathematical reasoning and I am trying to determine whether it has a recognized name, or whether it is simply an instance of something broader that I have not properly distinguished.

Here is a concrete instance. Consider the claim that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a^b is rational. One classic argument runs: either √2^√2 is rational or it is irrational. If it is rational, take a = b = √2 and we are done. If it is irrational, take a = √2^√2 and b = √2; then a^b = (√2^√2)^√2 = √2^2 = 2, which is rational. The argument proves that such a pair must exist, but it does not tell you which of the two candidate pairs actually works — it does not produce a specific pair. In classical mathematics this is considered a perfectly valid existence proof. But in other contexts — say, when one wants an algorithm that actually outputs the numbers — it is unsatisfying. What the argument really seems to establish is that it cannot be the case that no such pair exists; it refutes the universal nonexistence claim. Yet refuting "no such pair exists" and exhibiting a specific pair are being treated as the same thing.

I am not asking about proof by contradiction in general. I understand that proving not-P by assuming P and deriving a contradiction is uncontroversial even in settings that are careful about these distinctions. What I am asking about is the specific logical step from "not-not-P" to "P" — the move that takes the impossibility of P's falsehood as sufficient for P's truth. Does this step have a standard name? And is there a name for the practice — or the fallacy — of treating that step as costless in contexts where it is not? I am also interested in the boundary: are there principled intermediate positions between always permitting this step and never permitting it — views on which the step is legitimate for some kinds of statements but not others? If so, what are those views called, and what is the rationale for drawing the line where they do?
