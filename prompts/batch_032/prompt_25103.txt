I've been teaching the free will debate for years and lately I've gotten stuck on the Dennett/Caruso exchange about punishment. You know the one — Dennett thinks we can keep our whole practice of holding people responsible, including retributive punishment, without any libertarian free will, because what matters is that we're competent agents who can respond to reasons. Caruso says no, if determinism is true then nobody *ultimately* deserves to suffer, and retributive punishment is just vengefulness with a fancy justification. They wrote a whole book going back and forth and as far as I can tell neither of them moved an inch.

Here's what bugs me: they agree on determinism, they agree on the neuroscience, they agree that forward-looking considerations — deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation — justify *some* kinds of sanctions. The whole fight is over whether there's any room left for desert, for the idea that someone should suffer *because* of what they did, over and above whatever good consequences it produces. And they just. keep. missing. each other.

So my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute where Dennett means something thinner by "desert" and Caruso insists on a thicker, more metaphysically loaded notion? Is it a genuine normative disagreement about whether retributive harm can ever be justified? Or is it one of those cases where the two sides have different bedrock intuitions about fairness — Dennett feels in his bones that some people just deserve to be punished, and Caruso feels in his that no one does — and no argument is going to bridge that gap? I want a real diagnosis, not a summary of their views. And tell me honestly: is there anything that could actually resolve this, or is it the kind of thing where we just have to pick a side and live with it?
