In a philosophy of cognitive science seminar, a presenter argued that the computational theory of mind is fundamentally misguided because, as she put it, "the brain is not a computer — it doesn't have a CPU, it doesn't run compiled code, and neurons aren't logic gates." During discussion, someone objected that "the brain is a computer" doesn't mean any of those things, but neither side spelled out what it does mean. I want to get clear on this before the next session. What are the distinct claims that "the brain is a computer" could be used to assert, from the weakest and least controversial to the strongest and most metaphysically committal? For each reading, indicate whether it is a claim about literal hardware equivalence, about functional organization, about computational description, about simulation, or about something else — and say whether it is plausible and whether the computational theory of mind actually requires it. I am especially interested in knowing which reading the presenter was attacking and which one she ought to have been engaging with if she wanted to challenge the view seriously.
