I've been chewing on this thing in epistemology that my professor brought up — the dogmatism paradox, or whatever it's called — and I'm trying to figure out whether my gut reactions on a bunch of cases actually hang together under some principl. The basic idea, as I understand it: if I genuinely know something, then any evidence that seems to go against it has gotta be misleading, right? So in some sense I'm entitled to dismiss it. But that sounds like a recipe for just never updating on anything, which is obviously nuts. So I wrote down my immediate, unreflective verdicts on a bunch of scenarios, and now I want to know what epistemic principle — if any — captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts I need to give up if they can't all fit.

Here are the cases:

1. I know my car is parked on the street because I parked it there eight minutes ago and I can see it from my window. My roommate walks in and says "hey man, I think I just saw your car getting towed." My gut says: I should at least look out the window. It'd be irrational to just say "nope, I know it's there" and go back to my cereal.

2. I know that 2+2=4. Someone hands me a dense, ten-page formal "proof" that 2+2=5, full of notation I don't immediately see a flaw in. My gut says: I can dismiss this without working through it. I don't owe it any engagement.

3. I know my partner is faithful — we've been together years, complete trust, never a reason to doubt. A close friend I trust shows me a photo that really, really looks like my partner kissing someone else at a party last weekend. My gut says: I cannot just dismiss this. I have to investigate, even though I "know" they're faithful.

4. I know the Earth is roughly spherical. A stranger on the street presses a flat-earth pamphlet into my hand, full of diagrams and "evidence." My gut says: I can toss it in the trash without reading a word.

5. I know what I ate for lunch — it was a turkey sandwich, I remember it vividly. My coworker says "no, you had tomato soup, I sat right across from you." My gut says: I should at least pause and consider that my memory might be wrong here, not just dismiss her.

6. I know I have two hands. A philosophy grad student at a party starts in on brain-in-a-vat scenarios and says I can't rule out that my hand-experiences are simulated. My gut says: I can ignore this entirely and go get another drink.

7. (This is the one I keep flipping on.) I know my political party's candidate is the better choice — I've followed politics closely for over a decade, I've read the policy papers, I've thought hard about it. A smart friend who also follows politics closely lays out a detailed, thoughtful case that the other candidate is better, citing facts and considerations I genuinely hadn't weighed. Part of me says I have to engage seriously with her argument. Another part says: I already know my candidate is better, so her case has to be misleading somewhere, and maybe I don't need to do the work of finding where.

So: is there a single principle about when you're rationally permitted to dismiss counterevidence to something you know, that gets all of these right? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why those ones specifically? And what does your preferred principle say about case 7? Please take a definite, committed position — don't just survey the options and leave me to pick. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert epistemologist who's willing to stick their neck out and defend a view.
