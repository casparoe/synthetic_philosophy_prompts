I'm in a reading group on practical reason, and we've been stuck for weeks on the question of incommensurable values — the idea that some goods can't be placed on a single scale of measurement. One member of the group keeps reaching for the same analogy to settle things: when you're torn between, say, a career in law and a career in music, or between moving closer to aging parents and taking a dream job abroad, you're just "comparing apples and oranges." The conclusion he wants to draw is that reason falls silent in such cases. There's no fact of the matter about which option is better, because the values at stake belong to different dimensions — financial security versus artistic fulfillment, filial duty versus professional ambition — and you can't weigh them against each other. Any attempt to do so is self-deception; you just have to pick, and picking isn't the sort of thing reason can guide.

I'd like you to audit this analogy. Give me a correspondence table: what in the target domain (hard choices between values) maps onto what in the source domain (comparing apples and oranges)? Then walk me through where the mapping breaks down. In particular, I'm interested in whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions about the nature of value — for instance, that values come pre-packaged as discrete, self-contained kinds the way fruit varieties do, rather than being things we partly constitute, revise, or discover through the act of choosing itself. Also: apples and oranges can in fact be compared along many dimensions — nutritional content, cost, what you're in the mood for, which one would make a better pie. Does that fact cut against the analogy or, on reflection, actually reinforce it? And if the analogy does break, does the conclusion — that reason is silent in hard choices — survive the breakdown, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what's going on in hard choices, I'd like to hear it.
