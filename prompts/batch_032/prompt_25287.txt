Hey — I'm a journalist who covers the science beat, and I'm working on a longread about the evolutionary psychology wars. I've been reporting on this for a while and I've sort of accumulated a set of views, but I've never actually checked whether they're consistent with each other. Before I write this piece I want someone to audit my position. Here's where I've landed:

(1) The adaptationist program — the approach of assuming a psychological trait is an adaptation and then working backwards to figure out the selection pressures that shaped it — is a legitimate and productive research strategy. You've got to start somewhere, and reverse-engineering is how you generate testable hypotheses.

(2) That said, I think a very large fraction of specific evolutionary psychology hypotheses — the stuff about mate preferences, cheater-detection modules, waist-to-hip ratio preferences, sex differences in jealousy — are basically just-so stories. They're underdetermined by the available evidence and often formulated in ways that make them unfalsifiable in practice.

(3) Evolutionary psychology should be held to exactly the same epistemic standards as other historical sciences like paleontology, geology, or cosmology. No special skepticism, but also no special pleading.

(4) When an evolutionary hypothesis makes a surprising, non-obvious prediction that gets confirmed empirically, that's strong evidence for it. This is how historical sciences make progress, and evo psych should be no different.

(5) The charge that evolutionary psychology rationalizes the status quo or provides scientific cover for regressive politics is a political objection, not an epistemic one. It shouldn't affect our assessment of whether the science is good science.

(6) Cross-cultural consistency of a psychological trait is decent evidence that it has an evolved basis, but it's not decisive — convergent cultural evolution can produce similar patterns independently.

(7) The reverse-engineering approach — inferring ancestral selection pressures from the structure of present-day psychological mechanisms — can yield genuine knowledge even when we don't have independant evidence about ancestral environments. The design of the mechanism itself tells you something about what it was designed for.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest fix would be — which commitment I'd have to modify or drop, and what it would cost me. Don't just flag every pair; some of these sit together fine and I need to know which ones do. Keep it to 500 words max — I have a deadline.
