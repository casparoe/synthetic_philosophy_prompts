I'm a computer science major currently enrolled in a philosophy of logic course, and we have just finished reading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." I find the piece genuinely arresting, but I also find myself frustrated in a way that I suspect has to do with the vocabulary I bring to it. Every time I try to pin down what the problem is, I reach for concepts from my programming languages and formal methods coursework, and the whole thing starts to look either much clearer or much more confused than my philosophy classmates seem to think it is.

Here is the argument as I understand it. Achilles wants the Tortoise to accept a conclusion Z on the basis of premises A and B, where the inference from A and B to Z has the form of a simple logical step. The Tortoise accepts A and B but refuses to accept Z. Achilles then adds a new premise C, which states that if A and B are true then Z must be true. The Tortoise accepts C as well but still refuses Z, on the grounds that nothing yet compels him to move from accepting A, B, and C to accepting Z. Achilles is then forced to add premise D, stating that if A, B, and C are true then Z must be true, and so on indefinitely. The regress appears to show that if we treat the rule of inference itself as just another premise to be granted, the deduction can never get off the ground.

In my own coursework I work constantly with precisely this distinction, though we give it different names. We distinguish the object language from the meta-language; we distinguish axioms from inference rules in a deductive system; we talk about a proof checker that verifies whether a rule has been correctly applied without needing the rule itself to appear as an additional formula in the object language; and we talk about the difference between a conditional connective inside the logic and the turnstile that records derivability. I have a strong intuition that if I could restate the whole tortoise problem in this vocabulary, it would either dissolve into a simple point about the architecture of formal systems or else reveal something deeper that my framework cannot express.

Restate Carroll's regress argument in the vocabulary I have just described — inference rules, meta-language and object-language, the turnstile versus the material conditional, proof checking, and the architecture of a deductive system as it appears in a theorem prover like Coq or Lean — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical problem translate cleanly into this vocabulary, which parts you had to stretch or distort to make them fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all. Finally, I want your honest assessment: does the residue suggest that the tortoise's problem is less deep than philosophers take it to be, because the computer science framework simply dissolves it, or does it suggest that the CS framework is missing something important that the philosophical debate is tracking?
