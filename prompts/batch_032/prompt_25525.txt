So my friend has this whole thing about when you should give in to threats or pay up in weird scenarios, and I've been keeping track of her verdicts:

- Classic Pascal's mugging: stranger on the street says "give me $5 or I'll torture 3^^^3 people in a simulation." She says don't pay, obviously.
- Actual mugging: guy flashes a knife and wants your wallet. She says hand it over, no question.
- Credible terrorist threat: known group with a track record says they'll bomb a stadium unless they get $10,000. She says you take it dead seriously, pay, involve the authorities, the whole thing.
- Charity: you can give $10 to a well-vetted charity that saves roughly one life per $5,000. She says obviously donate.
- Email from an unknown sender: "I've hacked your webcam, send $200 or I'll send the footage to all your contacts." She says don't pay, it's a scam, these get sent to millions of people.
- A stranger comes up to you and says "I put slow-acting poison in the coffee at your office this morning. Give me $50 or I won't tell you which pot." You work in a medium-sized office, the guy seems agitated but you've never seen him before. She says don't pay.

Now here's the new case I'm curious about. A physicist you know personally — not a close friend but someone whose judgment you respect, you've had coffee a few times — tells you they've been going over the specs for an experiment starting next month at a nearby lab. They've calculated a roughly 1-in-3,000 chance that the experiment triggers a vacuum decay event that destroys the entire observable universe. They're not asking for money for themself; they're raising funds for an emergency legal challenge to get an injunction. They want $100 from you.

What would my friend say about this one? And more importantly — what rule or rules is she actually running? I can see at least two different principles that fit all her verdicts so far, and they point in opposite directions on the physicist case. Can you lay out what those candidate principles are, which one you think she's more likely using and why, and what further case would cleanly settle it?

Also — and this is important to me — can you flag which parts of this whole debate are genuinely unsettled among philosophers versus where there's a rough consensus? Like, I know there's a literature on Pascal's mugging and bounded utility and all that, but I don't want to walk away thinking some niche view is the standard position when it's actually contested.
