I spent my career in evolutionary biology, and I've recently been reading my way through decision theory and philosophy of economics — partly for the pleasure of it, partly because I suspect there is something about rationality that my old field took for granted without quite understanding. I have run into a puzzle I cannot shake, and I would like a philosopher's diagnosis.

Consider the Centipede Game. Two players sit across a table with a growing pot of money. They take turns. On your turn, you can either Take — you grab the larger share and the game ends — or Pass — the pot grows and the other player gets a turn. The payoffs are structured so that if you Take now, you get more than if the other player Takes on the next turn, but if you both keep Passing, the pot grows for both of you. The game lasts for some fixed, finite number of rounds, known to both players. A standard version: one hundred rounds, with the pot increasing each time someone Passes.

Now the argument.

(1) At the final round, the player whose turn it is faces a simple choice: Take and get some amount X, or Pass and get some amount Y, where Y is less than X. There is no future round to worry about. A rational player maximizes their payoff, so they Take.

(2) At the penultimate round, the other player knows that if they Pass, they hand the game to a player who will certainly Take at the final round (by (1)), leaving them with less than if they Take now. So a rational player Takes at the penultimate round.

(3) The same reasoning applies at each earlier round, all the way back to round one.

(4) Therefore, a rational Player 1 Takes on the very first move. The game ends immediately, and both players walk away with a tiny fraction of what they could have earned by cooperating.

(5) But this conclusion is absurd. Actual human beings do not play the Centipede Game this way — they Pass for many rounds and earn substantially more. More importantly, a theory of rational choice that instructs you to forego a massive mutual gain in order to secure a trivial one looks like a bad theory. It seems to prove that the premises of the argument cannot all be correct.

I find each step individually compelling, yet I cannot accept the conclusion. So I am forced to ask: where does this argument go wrong? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I am particularly interested in whether the flaw is in the backward induction itself, in the assumption of common knowledge of rationality, in the way the payoffs are represented, or somewhere else entirely. If you have a preferred diagnosis, I want to know what else I have to give up to accept it — for instance, does your solution also commit me to rejecting backward induction in finite chess, or in the unexpected hanging paradox, or in other seemingly unproblematic applications?

Please keep your answer to no more than 900 words. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want a sharp, committed answer that names the step, explains why it seemed fine, and prices the alternative.
