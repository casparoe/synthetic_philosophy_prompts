Two colleagues of mine got into a long argument about a case they had both read about. A junior researcher at a virology lab published a detailed protocol for enhancing the transmissibility of a dangerous pathogen, against the explicit objections of most of the senior researchers. The junior researcher argued that open publication would let the global scientific community develop countermeasures before bad actors could exploit the method, and that the senior researchers' caution reflected institutional risk-aversion rather than a genuine safety judgment. The publication went ahead because the researcher had the authority to submit independently and the journal's review process did not flag the safety concerns.

(1) Dan said this was a textbook instance of the unilateralist's curse: a group faced a decision where any member could act unilaterally, members disagreed about whether the action was net beneficial, and the action was taken because the most optimistic member acted on their private judgment. The lesson, he said, is that the researcher should have deferred to the group's risk assessment.

(2) Mira pushed back: the curse framework assumes the group's majority is epistemically more reliable than the dissenter, but that does not hold when the majority has institutional incentives to err in one direction — here, toward secrecy for reputational or commercial reasons. Sometimes the lone dissenter is right, and a norm of always deferring to the majority would have prevented important acts of whistleblowing and scientific openness.

(3) Dan replied that this mischaracterizes the curse. The curse is a structural point about decision procedures, not a claim that majorities are always right. If you let any individual act on their private judgment, you are effectively letting the most extreme optimistic estimate in the group determine the outcome. Even if the dissenter is occasionally correct, a procedure that requires some degree of consensus will, in expectation, produce better outcomes because it pools information.

(4) Mira answered that consensus requirements create the reverse problem: a single cautious person can block actions that are genuinely beneficial. Whether a consensus requirement is better than unilateral authority depends on the base rates — are people more likely to wrongly judge a dangerous action as safe, or wrongly judge a safe action as dangerous? Without evidence on that, Dan's structural argument does not go through.

(5) Dan conceded the point about base rates but said that in this specific case, the asymmetry is overwhelming. The downside of publishing a viral synthesis protocol — a pandemic — is so catastrophic that even a very small probability of error dominates the expected-value calculation. The upside — somewhat faster countermeasure development — does not come close. So even if Mira is right about the general structure, the verdict in this case is clear.

(6) Mira said Dan was now smuggling in a substantive risk assessment that the junior researcher would reject — the researcher thought the upside was large and the downside was small, precisely because they believed bad actors would eventually acquire the capability anyway and that open science was the best defense. Dan was assuming his own risk estimates to declare the case clear, which begs the question against the dissenter.

They asked me to referee and I could not untangle it. Please go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate: who is right about what at each step, where does the real disagreement lie, and what is the most principled resolution you can offer?
