I'm in a graduate seminar on collective epistemology, and we've been working through the discursive dilemma. After class I tried to sharpen what I think the dilemma really shows, and I ended up with an argument that seems to prove that democratic voting procedures cannot coherently produce group agents. The conclusion is obviously too strong — it would mean that every parliament, jury, and committee that uses voting is either irrational or not a genuine agent — but I've been turning it over for days and can't locate the mistake cleanly. Here's the argument as I've formalized it:

(1) For a group to count as a rational agent, its collective attitudes (beliefs, judgments, intentions) must be logically consistent. A group cannot simultaneously judge that p, that if p then q, and that not-q, any more than an individual can.

(2) If a group forms its collective judgments by proposition-wise majority voting — that is, by taking a separate majority vote on each proposition under consideration — then the group's collective judgments can be logically inconsistent even when every individual member holds perfectly consistent judgments. This is the discursive dilemma: different propositions are supported by different majority coalitions, and nothing guarantees that the resulting set of propositions will be consistent.

(3) Therefore, proposition-wise majority voting is incompatible with guaranteeing group rational agency.

(4) Any voting procedure that avoids the inconsistency of proposition-wise majority voting must do so by restricting which propositions the group votes on (as in premise-based or conclusion-based procedures) or by assigning different weights to different members' votes on different issues. Both strategies violate a core democratic principle: that each member should have an equal say on every question that comes before the group.

(5) Therefore, no voting procedure can simultaneously satisfy three desiderata: (a) equal say for each member on each question, (b) logical consistency of the group's collective attitudes, and (c) group rational agency.

(6) Democratic theory and practice are committed to all three: we insist on equal voting rights, we expect legislatures and courts to reason coherently, and we hold corporate bodies responsible as agents.

(7) Hence democratic theory and practice are incoherent — they demand something that no procedure can deliver.

I'm least willing to abandon (1) or (6), and I suspect the trouble is somewhere around (4), but every attempt I've made to weaken (4) seems to let in a different problem. I'd like you to diagnose this argument in detail.
