I teach a philosophy of language course, and one of the things that reliably puzzles my students is the relationship between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by uttering it — the Gricean distinction, roughly, between sentence meaning and speaker meaning. I want to bring this to life by examining a concrete legal practice, and I'd like you to help me think through what that practice presupposes.

Here is the practice. In American perjury law, there is a well-known doctrine — call it the literal-truth defense — according to which a witness cannot be convicted of perjury if the statement they made under oath was literally true, even if they intended to mislead the questioner and succeeded in doing so. A witness who is asked "Did you ever meet with Mr. X?" and answers "I have never been in the same room as Mr. X," when in fact they met Mr. X outdoors in a park, has not committed perjury: the statement, taken literally, is true. The witness's communicative intention — to convey that no meeting occurred — is irrelevant to criminal liability, so long as the words themselves, read in their most narrow and uncharitable sense, correspond to the facts.

Now compare this with how the same legal system handles fraud, contract interpretation, and defamation. In fraud cases, a literally true statement can still be actionable if it was crafted to create a false impression in the mind of a reasonable hearer. In contract law, courts routinely interpret terms by asking what a reasonable person in the position of the parties would have understood them to mean, not by parsing the text as though it were a logical formula. In defamation, the "substantial truth" doctrine protects a defendant whose statement was true in gist even if literally inaccurate in some detail — the mirror image of perjury's rule.

So we have an asymmetry: in perjury, the law treats sentence meaning (narrowly construed) as the thing that matters for criminal liability; in fraud, contract, and defamation, the law treats speaker meaning, or at least the meaning a reasonable hearer would recover, as what matters. The same words, spoken with the same communicative intention, can be legally innocuous in one context and legally culpable in another, depending entirely on which doctrine applies.

What I want you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory of linguistic communication — and the implicit theory of what makes a statement an assertion, a representation, or a communicative act — that this asymmetry presupposes. Specifically:

1. What view of the relationship between sentence meaning and speaker meaning does the perjury rule presuppose, and what view do the fraud and contract rules presuppose? Are these views consistent with each other, or does the legal system tacitly endorse incompatible theories of meaning depending on the context?

2. Is there a principled way to reconcile the asymmetry? For instance, one might argue that perjury is about the oath — a promise to tell the truth, where "the truth" is understood as sentence-truth — while fraud is about the relationship between two parties, where what matters is the reasonable expectations each creates in the other. Does that distinction hold up under scrutiny, or does it merely redescribe the asymmetry without justifying it?

3. What would have to change in perjury law if we adopted a thoroughly Gricean view, on which what a speaker means is primary and sentence meaning is merely a piece of evidence the hearer uses to recover it? And conversely, what would have to change in fraud law if we adopted the perjury rule's apparent commitment to sentence meaning as the sole locus of truth and falsity?

4. Finally, is there a coherent philosophical position according to which the asymmetry is exactly right — that is, a position that gives sentence meaning a privileged role in one kind of legal context and speaker meaning a privileged role in another, without contradiction? If so, what is it, and what broader view of language and responsibility does it commit one to?
