Population axiology has been stuck on the repugnant conclusion for forty years. Since Parfit introduced it in Reasons and Persons, the dialectic has settled into a familiar shape: total utilitarians bite the bullet, average utilitarians get impaled on the mere addition paradox, critical-level and critical-range views struggle with the sadistic conclusion, person-affecting views have trouble with the non-identity problem, and variable-value theories face complaints of ad-hocery. Everyone knows everyone else's moves. Conferences recycle the same arguments. Nobody converts.

I want you to diagnose this impasse. Do not adjudicate the debate or defend a view; diagnose the disagreement itself. Structure your response in three titled sections:

**1. The shape of the stalemate** (roughly one paragraph). Briefly characterize the main camps and what each would have to give up to move. Be concrete: name the views and the costs they face, not just "both sides have intuitions."

**2. What kind of disagreement is this?** (roughly two paragraphs). Is it a clash of bedrock moral intuitions that no argument can reach? A disagreement about the formal structure of the betterness relation — transitivity, separability, completeness? A dispute about what population axiology is even supposed to do: guide policy, describe an impersonal good, systematize considered judgments? Or is it a verbal dispute about "better than" that different theories operationalize differently? Give reasons for your diagnosis, drawing on what the positions actually say.

**3. What would count as progress?** (roughly one paragraph). Given your diagnosis, is there anything — a theorem, a new case, a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about well-being — that could actually move the debate? Or should we expect the impasse to be permanent? Be honest: if nothing would help, say so and explain why.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is really two debates sharing a name. One is about the formal properties of an impersonal betterness ranking over populations. The other is about what we owe to possible people given that we care about actual people in a particular way. These questions pull in different directions, and most theories try to answer both at once, which is why they all break somewhere. But I want your diagnosis, not confirmation of mine.
