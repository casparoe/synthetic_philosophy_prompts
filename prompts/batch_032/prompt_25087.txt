I retired from the lab a couple years back — did electrophysiology on rodent hippocampus, nothing glamorous — and somehow ended up in this philosophy reading group at the community college. We've been working through theories of well-being, and there's one person in the group, Diane, whose verdicts on the cases are maddeningly consistent but I can't extract the rule. She's not a philosopher by training either (she was a social worker), so she's not just reciting a theory.

Here are her calls so far, from my notes:

- Experience machine (Nozick): wouldn't plug in. Says a life inside is genuinely worse than the same experiences outside.
- Happy deluded spouse: partner is unfaithful, spouse never knows, dies content. Diane says their well-being is lower than it looks — the betrayal counts even if it never lands in experience.
- Frustrated artist: produces masterpieces, dies bitter and unrecognized, fame comes posthumously. Diane says the life went badly. The later recognition doesn't reach back and fix it.
- Contented grass-counter (the Rawls case, more or less): someone who gets deep satisfaction from counting blades of grass, does nothing else. Diane says low well-being, despite the satisfaction.
- Sacrificing parent: gives up career and pleasures so the kids flourish, finds it meaningful but not enjoyable day-to-day. Diane says this can be a high-well-being life.
- Achievement machine: like the experience machine but it gives you the false belief you've done great things. Diane says this is worse than the plain experience machine — it corrupts something extra.

So she's obviously not a hedonist, and she's not a simple desire-satisfaction theorist either. She seems to want truth, genuine achievement, and connection to matter. But I can't tell if she's running an objective list, an informed-desire account, a perfectionist view, or some hybrid.

The case we're doing next week — I'm the one who proposed it — is this: a person chooses, with full information and after real deliberation, to enter a persistent virtual world. Other real people are there too. They form genuine friendships, even fall in love. They pursue difficult projects that matter within that world — building things, teaching, whatever — and the skills are real, the frustrations are real, the relationships are reciprocated. They know it's a simulation. The life isn't maximally pleasurable; it's challenging. But it's exactly the life they want, knowing what they know. From the outside, none of it "counts" in base reality.

What would Diane say? Is this person's life going well, or is it a fancier grass-counter? I'm less interested in the answer than in what her existing verdicts commit her to, which candidate theories diverge on this case, and what further case would cleanly separate them. And since I'm new enough to this that I might be missing something basic: what's the weakest link in the reasoning? What consideration would most likely flip the answer if I've got it wrong?
