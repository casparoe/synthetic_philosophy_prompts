I'm in a history seminar that's been going back and forth on counterfactual history for weeks — you know, the "what if the Nazis had won" or "what if the Cuban Missile Crisis had gone hot" kind of thing. The professor has been pretty dismissive of the whole genre, says it's not real history, it's just speculative fiction dressed up in footnotes. Fair enough. But then last week, when we were discussing the origins of World War I, he suddenly trots out "For want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse was lost…" and uses it to argue that the July Crisis was basically a cascade of tiny diplomatic missteps — a misfiled telegram here, a minister taking a nap there — and that the war wasn't "structurally inevitable" at all but hung on these ridiculous little contingencies.

I pointed out that he'd just done exactly what he'd spent the semester mocking: reasoning counterfactually about history. He got a bit annoyed and said the proverb isn't making a counterfactual claim per se — it's just a vivid way of saying that complex systems exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions. It's a point about unpredictability, not about "what would have happened if."

I'm not convinced. I've heard "For want of a nail" deployed by chaos theory people, by Great Man theorists, by Marxist historians arguing that systemic contradictions make revolutions inevitable *unless* some random spark sets them off, and by military historians arguing the opposite — that battles are overdetermined and no single nail ever really mattered. The proverb seems to do wildly different argumentative jobs depending on who's wielding it, and nobody ever stops to say which precise thesis they're actually asserting.

So here's what I'd like: can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "For want of a nail" could be expressing — about causation, about historical necessity, about the legitimacy of counterfactual reasoning, about predictability? Give me a menu of readings, from the boringly trivial to the wildly ambitious. For each one, say whether it's actually defensible, and — crucially — which reading my professor would need to be committed to for his "WWI was a fluke of diplomatic micro-events" argument to actually go through, and whether that reading survives his own skepticism about counterfactual history. Think very carefully and respond in the style of an expert — don't just grab the first interpretation that comes to mind.
