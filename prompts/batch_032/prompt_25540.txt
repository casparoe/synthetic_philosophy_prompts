I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether there can be normative reasons that are entirely "free-floating" — reasons that count in favor of an action without being tethered to any particular agent's concerns, desires, or projects. My friend defended a broadly Humean picture: all reasons must be anchored in some agent's motivational set; there are no reasons without reasoners. I found myself wanting to resist that conclusion, but every alternative I tried seemed to collapse back into something agent-relative under pressure. Since that conversation, I have been sketching an essay that tries to press a dilemma against the free-floating view, and I would welcome comments on whether the argument has genuine force or whether I am missing an obvious way out.

Here is the core idea, still in note form. Suppose we grant that there are agent-neutral reasons — reasons whose status as reasons does not depend on which agent we are talking about. The question is what makes such reasons normative for a given agent, what gives them practical grip on her deliberation. On the first horn: if the reason's normative force comes from some feature of the agent herself — her capacity for sympathy, her recognition of value, her practical identity as a rational being — then the reason is not truly free-floating; it is anchored in the agent after all, and we have simply pushed the Humean premise back a step without refuting it. On the second horn: if the reason's normative force does not depend on any feature of the agent, then it becomes mysterious how it ever gets traction in her practical reasoning. A reason that floats entirely free of my concerns, my evaluative outlook, or my constitutive aims seems not to be a reason for me in any sense that matters to deliberation — it may describe a fact about the world, but it is not my reason. The dilemma, then, is that free-floating reasons seem either covertly anchored (and therefore not free-floating) or practically inert (and therefore not reasons in the practically relevant sense).

I suspect the weak point is the second horn: perhaps there is a way for a reason to be both unanchored and action-guiding that I am failing to see — maybe through a constitutive account of agency on which recognizing a normative fact just is being moved by it, without any need for an intermediate desire or concern. But I have not worked this out, and I worry that such accounts either beg the question or smuggle in agent-relativity through the back door. I would be grateful for comments on whether the dilemma is genuine, whether either horn can be grasped without collapsing the free-floating view, and whether the whole framing presupposes something objectionable about what it is for a consideration to be a reason.
