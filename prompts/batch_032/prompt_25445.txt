I'm drafting a paper on the foundations of probability and would like a consistency audit of my position before I commit to it in writing. Here are the six commitments I'm working with:

(1) Frequentism for scientific inference: probability statements in empirical science are about long-run relative frequencies in actual or hypothetical sequences of repetitions, and null-hypothesis significance testing, properly applied, is the right framework for controlling error rates across a career of inquiry.

(2) Subjectivism for rational credence: degrees of belief are the primitive notion, the sole coherence requirement is conformity to the probability axioms, and updating proceeds by conditionalization. I take this to be a normative theory of how credences ought to behave, not a description of how people actually reason.

(3) Propensity for single-case physical chance: the probability that this particular uranium-238 nucleus decays in the next 4.5 billion years is not merely a summary of frequencies across a reference class; it reflects a real dispositional property of that individual nucleus. Frequencies are evidence for propensities, not constitutive of them.

(4) The Principal Principle as bridge: my rational credence in an outcome, conditional on the true chance of that outcome and on admissible evidence, should equal that chance. I take this principle to be what ties (2) and (3) together — it explains why subjective credences should track objective chances — and to be what prevents my overall view from being an ad hoc patchwork of incompatible interpretations.

(5) Methodological convergence: a competently conducted frequentist analysis and a competently conducted Bayesian analysis of the same well-designed experiment should, in practice, converge on the same substantive conclusion. Persistent divergence is evidence that at least one analysis was not competently conducted.

(6) Diagnosis of the replication crisis: the replication crisis in psychology and allied fields is best explained by widespread misuse of frequentist tools — p-hacking, low statistical power, file-drawer effects, and the conflation of exploratory with confirmatory analyses — rather than by any deep flaw in the frequentist interpretation of probability itself.

Please go through these six commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. I am especially interested in the following questions: Does (4) actually do the bridging work I claim for it, or does the Principal Principle presuppose an account of chance that sits uncomfortably with propensity? Are (5) and (6) jointly compatible with the combination of (1) and (2), or do they smuggle in assumptions that one of those interpretations would reject? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each genuine tension, specify what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost.
