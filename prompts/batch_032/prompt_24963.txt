I've been trying to think through the relationship between computation and consciousness, and I've arrived at an argument that seems to force a choice between panpsychism and giving up on computationalism entirely. Neither option looks acceptable, so I need help finding the flaw.

Here's the argument:

(1) A physical system is conscious if and only if it implements the right kind of computation — the kind that, when implemented by a human brain, gives rise to conscious experience. (Call this computationalism about consciousness.)

(2) What computation a physical system implements is not an intrinsic fact about the system; it depends on how an interpreter maps the system's physical states onto the formal states of some computational description. A system only implements a computation relative to a mapping.

(3) For any physical system with enough internal degrees of freedom — a rock, a puddle, a thermostat — there exists some mapping (perhaps a highly gerrymandered one) that maps its physical states onto the formal states of a conscious-mind computation. The mapping need only be a mathematical function from physical-state-space to computational-state-space; it doesn't need to be "natural" or to respect the system's causal structure in any special way.

(4) Therefore, by (1), (2), and (3), every sufficiently complex physical system is conscious. Rocks are conscious. Puddles are conscious. Panpsychism of a particularly promiscuous kind is true.

(5) That conclusion cannot be right. A rock has no conscious experiences.

(6) So at least one premise must be false. The natural candidate is (1): computationalism is false, and implementing the right computation is not sufficient for consciousness.

But rejecting (1) seems to leave us with no explanation for why brains are conscious. If it's not the computation, what is it? The biology? The specific substrate? That looks like a retreat to dualism or mysterianism. And yet keeping (1) while trying to block the argument by rejecting (2) or (3) seems to require saying that there is a privileged, mapping-independent fact about what computation a system implements — which sounds like it requires a kind of Platonism about computation that I'm not sure I can defend.

I'd most like to keep (1) if I can, because I think computationalism is the best naturalistic theory of consciousness we have. But I can't see how to escape the rock.

Where does this argument go wrong? If it's valid, which premise should I reject and what does that rejection cost? If it's invalid, which step is the culprit and why?

Please end with a one-sentence summary of your diagnosis.
