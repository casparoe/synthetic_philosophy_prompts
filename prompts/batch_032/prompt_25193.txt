I teach an upper-level undergrad game theory course, about 25 students, one 75-minute session. We've just covered strict dominance and iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies for pure strategies, and now I want them to discover for themselves why we need to consider mixed strategies in the elimination process — that a strategy can survive pure-strategy elimination but still be strictly dominated by a mixed strategy.

Design a classroom game for this. Here's what I'm after:

- A normal-form game with 3 or 4 strategies per player, where the first round or two of elimination uses pure-strategy dominance (which they already get), but then they hit a wall — the remaining strategies can't be eliminated by any pure strategy, yet one of them is strictly dominated by a mixture of the others. The game should be symmetric or nearly symmetric so it's easier to run.
- The game is played in pairs, repeated maybe 8–10 rounds, with payoffs in points (no real money). After each round, I collect strategy choices anonymously on slips of paper and display the distribution on the board. Between rounds, pairs can discuss but can't coordinate across pairs.
- The key moment: after a few rounds, I announce that we're going to "remove" strategies that nobody should ever play, and I ask the class to vote on which ones. They'll easily eliminate the pure-strategy-dominated ones. Then I ask: "Are we done? Can any of the remaining strategies be ruled out?" This is where they need to discover the mixed-strategy dominance. The game's payoffs should be set up so that some pairs will stumble into mixing naturally (because the pure best responses cycle), and the aggregate data will show the dominated strategy getting crushed by mixtures even though no single pure strategy beats it.
- A debrief that walks through: (1) which strategies got eliminated and why (pure dominance), (2) the "wall" they hit, (3) how mixing two strategies can dominate a third even when neither does alone, (4) the formal definition of strict dominance by a mixed strategy, and (5) why this matters for iterated elimination — that you can't stop at pure strategies if you want the full power of the concept.
