Last week I watched two fellow PhD students in my department, Elena and Marcus, get into an argument that began over a first-order metaphysical question but quickly became a dispute about what rationality requires of us when we disagree. The first-order question was whether composite objects — tables, chairs, mountains — are fundamentally real, or whether only simples arranged table-wise exist and our talk of tables is a useful fiction. Elena defended the commonsense realist view; Marcus defended eliminativism. They have read the same literature, taken the same seminars, and each regards the other as a philosophical equal. After about forty minutes of back-and-forth on the metaphysics, Elena shifted ground: "We're epistemic peers on this topic, we've both thought about it carefully, and we still disagree. That fact alone should make each of us substantially less confident. If I'm rational, I should move my credence toward yours, and you should move yours toward mine." Marcus replied that he can see the specific equivocation he thinks she is making — between constitution and identity, as he put it — and the fact that she remains unpersuaded does not give him a reason to doubt his own grasp of that distinction. Elena answered that this is exactly the move every dogmatist makes: "I see the error in your reasoning, so your disagreement doesn't threaten my view." Marcus countered that conciliationism, applied consistently, would require us to abandon nearly all our philosophical beliefs, since for any controversial thesis there exists a smart, well-informed peer who rejects it — and that this counts as a reductio of the view. Elena replied that this might simply be the correct but uncomfortable conclusion, and that our reluctance to accept it is a psychological fact about us, not an epistemological argument. At that point they noticed they were late for a department talk and left it unresolved.

Please adjudicate their exchange by identifying exactly five key argumentative moves (a move may span multiple utterances or be a shared presupposition). For each move, state whether it is correct, mistaken, or partially correct, and justify your assessment in one or two sentences. The fifth move should be your overall verdict: which of the two has the stronger position, all things considered, and why. Present your answer as a bare numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion.
