I have been running an advanced reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics for about a year now, and we have spent the last several sessions stuck on the quantum suicide and quantum immortality arguments. Two members of the group have emerged as the most articulate defenders of the opposing positions. One of them — call her the Proponent — holds that the standard quantum suicide thought experiment reveals something genuinely unsettling about the Everettian picture: if the many-worlds interpretation is correct, then a rational agent who cares about their own future should expect, with subjective certainty, to survive any quantum life-or-death event, because there will always be some branch in which they do. The other — call him the Skeptic — thinks this is a conceptual mistake, and that the argument trades on an illicit slide from the existence of a branch to the rationality of expecting to find oneself in it, while ignoring the role that branch weight or measure must play in any coherent decision theory.

The two of them have been arguing past each other for weeks, and the rest of us suspect that at least some of the disagreement is terminological, while some of it runs deep. I would like them to produce the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration would yield: a joint statement that both can sign. The document should open with a numbered list of claims both accept — about the Everettian formalism, about the setup and branching structure of the quantum suicide experiment, about what the no-go theorems do and do not rule out, and about what would constitute a successful argument for or against the conclusion. Then it should contain a disagreements table. Each row should name a specific point of dispute, give the Proponent's position phrased in wording she would endorse and the Skeptic's position phrased in wording he would endorse, and identify whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal. For each substantive dispute, the table should also specify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them — and, where possible, describe a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. The cruxes I expect to see include at least the following: whether branch measure is rationally irrelevant to first-person expectation; whether the quantum suicide argument proves too much by implying one should expect to survive a quantum Russian roulette with arbitrarily unfavorable odds; and whether the move from third-person descriptions of branching to first-person credences requires a substantive bridge principle that the Everettian is not entitled to. But please do not limit the document to these if the logic of the disagreement demands others.

The document should be written in a tone that both parties can own — neither vacuous nor slanted — and it should be clear enough that the rest of the reading group can use it as the basis for our next discussion. I want to see where the genuine residual disagreement actually lies, after the verbal disputes have been cleared away, and what each side would count as a reason to change their mind.
