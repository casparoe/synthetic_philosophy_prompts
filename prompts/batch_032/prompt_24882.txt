I've been thinking about something that's bugged me for a while, and I'd love help sorting it out. It's about why we enjoy sad or disturbing art. Here are my gut reactions to a bunch of cases, and I'm trying to figure out if there's a single principle behind them.

Reading Anna Karenina: I find it deeply moving and would absolutely recommend it. The sadness feels rich, somehow. Watching a horror movie like Hereditary: I hated every minute of it and genuinely regret watching it. But my friend says it's one of her favorite films and she's rewatched it three times. A documentary about real atrocities (say, the Holocaust): I think it's important to watch, but I wouldn't say I "enjoyed" it — it felt more like a duty. A sad song (like "Casimir Pulaski Day" by Sufjan Stevens): I listen to it on purpose when I'm already sad, and it helps. But if I'm in a great mood, I skip it. A tragedy where the protagonist basically deserves their fate (Macbeth): satisfying in a grim way. A tragedy where the protagonist is completely innocent and just gets crushed by the world (like Hardy's Jude the Obscure): I found it almost unbearable, and not in a good way. A fictional story about a parent losing a child: before I had kids, I could engage with this; now I can't — I turn it off. The same story, but with a redemptive or meaningful frame (say, the death saves others): I can handle it, barely. A cheap tearjerker that's clearly manipulating you (like those ASPCA commercials with Sarah McLachlan): I resent it and change the channel.

So here's the puzzle. Some sad or distressing art I seek out and value; other sad or distressing art I avoid or endure. The difference isn't just sadness vs. fear, because some horror lands and some doesn't. It's not just fiction vs. reality, because some real stories (like certain memoirs) do work for me. It's not just whether there's a happy ending, because some of the things I value most are bleak all the way through. I keep wondering whether my own reactions even hang together.

What principle best captures where I draw the line? And does the principle force me to revise any of my verdicts — for instance, am I being inconsistent about the documentary vs. the memoir, or about Macbeth vs. Jude? I'd also love to know what your principle predicts for cases I haven't thought of, like interactive tragedy (a video game with a sad storyline) or tragedy in which I'm partly responsible for the outcome.
