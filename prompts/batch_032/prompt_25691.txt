I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been following the debate between utilitarians and their critics over the separateness of persons for years. The objection, at least as I understand it, runs something like this: utilitarianism treats the distribution of well-being across different people as morally irrelevant except insofar as it affects the total sum. It is indifferent between a small loss to one person and a large gain to another, provided the sum increases. But persons are not merely containers for well-being; they are separate lives, each with its own projects, commitments, and point of view. Aggregating across them as though they were a single super-person, the objection goes, fails to take seriously the distinction between persons — a distinction that is, for each of us, among the most fundamental features of our moral experience.

The utilitarian replies are also familiar by now. Harsanyi's impartial-observer theorem purports to show that a rational agent choosing behind a veil of ignorance would maximize expected utility, yielding utilitarianism without ignoring separateness. Parfit and others have argued that personal identity is itself less deep than we ordinarily suppose, so the line between persons is not the bright boundary the objection assumes. And some consequentialists simply bite the bullet: aggregation across persons is no more mysterious than aggregation across time-slices of a single person, and our reluctance to accept this is a cognitive bias we should overcome.

What strikes me, after reading this literature for years, is that nobody seems to move. The same arguments are refined and reissued, but the camps remain fixed. I want to understand why. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about a substantive moral fact but a clash of bedrock pictures — one in which morality is fundamentally about the impartial point of view, and one in which it is fundamentally about the relations between distinct centers of agency. But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I would like a careful one.

Please diagnose this impasse. Is the disagreement substantive or verbal? Do the two sides differ about an empirical or formal claim that could in principle be settled, or about which cases count as data for moral theorizing, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Are they answering different questions under the same name? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, a different way of framing the question — or should we expect the impasse to be permanent? Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
