Hey — I'm a journalist working on a longread about AI and knowledge, and I've gotten myself into a bit of a rabbit hole. The standard worry you hear is that as we offload more cognitive work to AI, we lose understanding: the radiologist who lets the AI read the scan, the law student who gets Claude to summarize the case, the programmer who has Copilot write the function — they're all supposedly trading genuine comprehension for convenience.

But I've been talking to some philosophers and there's a more interesting version of the skeptical reply than just "well calculators didn't ruin math." The argument goes something like this: the whole discourse conflates two different things — *having* an understanding and *being able to produce* an understanding on demand. The claim is that understanding is fundamentally a capacity to navigate a web of reasons and inferences *when the situation calls for it*, not a constant possession of explicit mental representations. And if that's right, then AI tools don't erode understanding at all — they just change which situations call for deploying it. You still need to know when the AI is wrong, how to prompt it, how to integrate its output with other things you know, and so on. The cognitive work shifts, it doesnt disappear.

I want you to make the strongest case for that position — that AI dependence doesn't erode understanding, it relocates it. Really go at it. And then, because I need to be fair, tell me the one or two places where this argument is most vulnerable. Like, what's the best counterpunch?
