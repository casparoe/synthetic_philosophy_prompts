I was reading a blog post about consciousness and the author summarized Dennett's "Quining Qualia" like this:

"Dennett's paper argues that qualia — the raw feels of experience, like the redness of red or what pain feels like — are an incoherent idea and don't exist. His central case is Chase and Sanborn, two coffee tasters who both stop enjoying the coffee they used to love. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same, but his preferences have changed — he just doesn't like that taste anymore. Sanborn says no, the coffee tastes different now, his experience itself has changed. Dennett's point is that there's no way, even in principle, to determine who's right, because there's no fact of the matter about what their experience is 'really' like. If we can't even tell whether our own qualia have changed or stayed the same, then qualia — conceived as private, intrinsic, directly apprehensible properties — can't do the work philosophers want them to do. So Dennett's conclusion is that there's nothing it's like to have an experience; 'what-it's-likeness' is a pre-scientific intuition we should abandon, and conscious states are really just complexes of behavioral dispositions and reactive attitudes."

I know Dennett is controversial but this summary feels like its flattening something. Is this a fair representation of what the paper actually argues? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what Dennett would sign off on, what he'd push back on, and whether the conclusion as stated follows from the Chase/Sanborn argument. Don't hedge — I want to know where this summary gets it right and where it goes wrong, definitively.
