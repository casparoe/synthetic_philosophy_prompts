So I'm a novelist—published a few, midlist, you know the deal—and I've somehow talked myself into giving the keynote at this interdisciplanary thing the university is running on "The Mind and the Story." My talk argues that consciousness is basically a user illusion. The brain builds a stripped-down dashboard of itself and the world, and what we call inner experience—the voice in your head, the feeling of being a self moving through time—is that dashboard. Not a magic theater, not the deep well of subjectivity literature professors get misty about. I came to this view partly *through* writing fiction, actually: the more I paid attention to how I construct character consciousness on the page, the more I started suspecting that what we experience as rich continuous inner life is a lot of post-hoc summary and narrative confabulation. The brain serves up a story about what it just did and calls it experience.

The audience is going to be maybe sixty people, and here's the thing: it's mostly writers. Novelists, poets, memoirists, some creative writing MFA faculty, a handful of editors from literary journals. These are people who have staked their whole way of seeing the world on the premise that interiority is vast and irreducible and the whole point of literature is to capture it. They are not going to be friendly.

I need a Q&A prep sheet. What are the objections I'll actually get from this crowd—not from philosophers, not from neuroscientists, but from people who spend their lives trying to render consciousness on the page? Give me seven or eight, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way a novelist would actually say them in a Q&A (not jargon, not seminar-speak), with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody in that room thinks to ask.
