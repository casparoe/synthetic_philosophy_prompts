I'm a first-year law student who wandered into a philosophy of mind seminar last term and hasn't been able to think about criminal law the same way since. The culprit is the Knobe effect. You probably know it: subjects read about a CEO who adopts a profit-increasing program that will harm the environment as a side effect, and around eighty percent say he harmed the environment intentionally. Change "harm" to "help" and the numbers flip — most say he didn't help the environment intentionally. The asymmetry is robust across cultures, ages, and vignette variations, and it suggests that folk attributions of intentional action are sensitive to the moral valence of the outcome, not just to the agent's beliefs and desires. That alone is philosophically interesting. But I started tracing out the implications, and I built an argument that now feels like a trap I can't find my way out of. Here it is:

(1) The Knobe effect is not a superficial quirk of vignette methodology; it reflects a deep feature of how the concept of intentional action works — moral considerations partly constitute, and don't merely influence, ordinary judgments of intentionality.
(2) If moral considerations partly constitute the concept of intentional action, then the concept is itself evaluative: there is no way to fully specify its application conditions in purely descriptive, non-normative terms.
(3) If the concept of intentional action is evaluative in this way, then the distinction between factual questions ("Did the defendant act intentionally?") and evaluative questions ("Was what the defendant did wrong?") is not a clean metaphysical divide but at most a pragmatic one.
(4) If that distinction is only pragmatic, then legal concepts that depend on it — mens rea categories like purpose, knowledge, and recklessness — are covertly evaluative in a way that legal doctrine systematically obscures.
(5) If mens rea concepts are covertly evaluative, then two juries could agree on every descriptive fact of a case — what the defendant did, believed, and foresaw — and yet reach opposite verdicts because they bring different evaluative frameworks to the concept of "intentional," with no purely factual basis for saying one jury got it wrong.
(6) But the law is committed to the principle that verdicts supervene on facts: if two juries agree on the facts, they ought to agree on the verdict. That commitment is not optional — it underwrites the rule of law, the prohibition on arbitrary judgment, and the very idea that like cases should be treated alike.
(7) Therefore, either the law's self-conception is incoherent, or the Knobe effect does not license the move from (1) to (2).

I find both horns of (7) unacceptable, but I can't locate the mistake. My seminar professor suggested that the inference from (1) to (2) confuses performance with competence — that the effect shows something about how people apply the concept under certain conditions, not about the concept's structure. But that reply feels thin: the effect is large, it survives reflection, and it predicts behavior in new cases, which is exactly what you'd expect if it reflected conceptual structure rather than noise. A classmate argued that the problem is at (4), because the law already acknowledges that mens rea has a normative dimension — that's why we have jury instructions, burdens of proof, and appellate review. But that response seems to concede the dangerous part: if mens rea is normative all the way down, then the law's claim to decide cases on the facts is a kind of fiction, and we're just hoping juries share enough evaluative background to keep the system running.

I want to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. I'm especially interested in whether there's a way to accept the full force of the experimental findings — no deflationary moves about performance errors or ecological validity — while denying that the concept of intentional action is evaluative in the sense required by (2). And if (2) does go through, I want to know whether (4) really follows, or whether the law has conceptual resources I'm missing that would let it absorb the Knobe effect without collapsing the fact-value distinction it depends on.
