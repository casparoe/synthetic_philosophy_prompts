"Coherence arguments for expected utility maximization come in (at least) two flavors: Dutch book arguments and representation theorem arguments. Explain the structure of each, identify the main objection each faces, and assess whether either type of argument succeeds in showing that a rational agent's preferences must conform to the expected utility axioms."

the student wrote:

Dutch book arguments (like de Finetti's and Ramsey's) work by showing that if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, there exists a set of bets each of which you'd accept individually given your credences, but which together guarantee you a sure loss. For example, if you violate additivity by assigning P(A) = 0.4 and P(not-A) = 0.7, a bookie can sell you a bet on A for $0.40 and a bet on not-A for $0.70, and you'll lose $0.10 no matter what. The argument for expected utility maximization extends this: if your preferences over lotteries violate the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (like independence), a clever bookie can construct a sequence of trades that leaves you worse off. The main objection is that these arguments assume you're willing to accept each bet or trade individually, but a rational agent might refuse the whole package once they see it — the "package principle" isn't obviously a requirement of rationality. So Dutch books show at most that incoherent credences or preferences are pragmatically defective, not that they're irrational in a deeper sense.

Representation theorems (like Savage's) take a different approach. They start with a set of axioms about preferences over acts — completeness, transitivity, the sure-thing principle, and so on — and prove that if your preferences satisfy these axioms, there exists a unique probability function and a utility function (unique up to positive affine transformation) such that you prefer one act to another just in case it has higher expected utility. Unlike Dutch books, representation theorems don't appeal to betting or pragmatic considerations at all. They're purely about the structure of preference. This means they avoid the package-principle objection entirely. The main objection to representation theorems is that the axioms are too demanding — real agents don't satisfy completeness or the sure-thing principle, and it's not clear they should. But this is just a complaint about idealization, not about the normative force of the argument. If we grant the axioms as norms of rationality, the theorem goes through. So representation theorems succeed where Dutch books fail: they give us a genuinely normative argument that rational preferences must maximize expected utility, not just a pragmatic one.
