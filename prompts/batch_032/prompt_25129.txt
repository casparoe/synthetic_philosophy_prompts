So I'm in this comparative ethics course and we just finished a unit on Buddhist ethics — we read some primary sources (like passages from the Pali canon) and some contemporary stuff by people like Damien Keown and Charles Goodman about how Buddhist ethics relates to Western categories like consequentialism and virtue ethics. I've also been active in my uni's effective altruism group for about a year now. And these two parts of my life are kind of crashing into each other right now.

Here's the situation. We're planning our big end-of-year fundraiser and our chapter president wants to run what she calls a "high-impact empathy campaign." What this actually means is we'd be putting up posters around campus with really graphic images of children suffering from preventable diseases, and the copy would be things like "You spent £4 on a coffee this morning. A child died of malaria in the time it took you to drink it." The idea is to provoke guilt and shame to get people to donate. And honestly, the data backs this up — guilt-based appeals do raise more money, and the charity we're supporting (Against Malaria Foundation) is one of the most cost-effective ones out there by QALY/DALY metrics. So from a straight consequentialist perspective this is almost certainly the right call: we cause some transient emotional discomfort in relatively privileged students, and in exchange we generate donations that prevent real deaths.

But the Buddhist ethics material has really gotten under my skin. The way I understand it — and please correct me if I'm getting this wrong — is that in Buddhist moral psychology, the ethical quality of an action depends heavily on the cetana (intention/volition) behind it and on the mental roots (hetu) that motivate it. Actions rooted in lobha (greed), dosa (aversion/hatred), and moha (delusion) are akusala — unwholesome — and they generate bad karmic consequences for the agent and also, I think, there's something about how they shape your character or your mental continuum over time. What worries me is that deliberately trying to make people feel guilty and ashamed seems like it's rooted in or at least involves dosa — you're trying to arouse a kind of mental distress in someone, an aversive state. And even if you tell yourself its for a good cause, the immediate mental action is still one of, like, causing suffering (even if minor) in another person's mind. Plus there's maybe something sketchy about the intention itself — you're treating the donors as means rather than ends, manipulating their mental states instrumentally.

On the other hand, I can imagine a Buddhist counterargument: maybe the campaign is actually motivated by karuna (compassion) for the children who are dying, and the guilt-inducing messaging is just skillful means (upaya) to get people to act on their own latent compassion. And surely the first precept is about not killing, so preventing killing (by funding malaria nets) would be aligned with it? But then I'm not sure Buddhist ethics works like that — its not a simple "maximize lives saved" calculus, because the quality of the mind matters independently.
