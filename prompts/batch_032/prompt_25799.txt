A couple of friends of mine got into an argument at a party that I still can't resolve, and it's been nagging at me. It started when someone used the word "middle-aged" and another person challenged whether that term has any real meaning. This spiraled into a full debate about vagueness — the old sorites paradox about heaps of sand and bald men, but refought on contemporary terrain.

The first, call him David, argued that vague predicates like "heap," "bald," or "middle-aged" must have sharp boundaries somewhere — we just don't know where they are. His reasoning was that if there were no fact of the matter about when a heap becomes a non-heap, then classical logic would break down for statements involving these terms, and we'd have to abandon bivalence — the principle that every proposition is either true or false. Since we rely on bivalent logic for everything from computer science to legal reasoning, he said, the cost of giving it up is too high. The boundary exists; our ignorance of it doesn't mean it isn't there. He compared it to the boundary of a mountain: we can't say exactly which rock marks its edge, but that doesn't mean the mountain has no edge.

The second, call her Elena, pushed back hard. She said David was solving a conceptual problem by positing unknowable facts, which she called metaphysically extravagant. Why should there be a precise number of hairs that separates "bald" from "not bald"? The word simply doesn't work that way — its meaning underdetermines a cutoff, and that's not a bug, it's how natural language functions. She argued that vagueness is a feature of meaning, not a limitation of our knowledge: vague terms have borderline cases where neither "true" nor "false" quite fits, and that's fine. She pointed out that the law deals with this all the time — judges interpret vague statutes without pretending there's a hidden sharp line, and the system doesn't collapse.

David replied that her view just relocates the problem. If you admit a third category — "neither true nor false" — then you get higher-order vagueness: where is the boundary between "true" and "borderline"? You either posit another sharp cutoff there or you get an infinite regress of borderlines. He said that's no better than his single unknowable boundary, and arguably worse because it multiplies the mystery.

Elena said the regress isn't vicious — it just shows that vagueness is ineliminable and that our concepts are tolerant in a way that resists sharp boundaries at every level. David said that's giving up on logic. She said it's giving up on a certain picture of logic, not logic itself.

At this point they looked at me and I had nothing useful to say. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? I'm especially curious whether David's "cost of giving up bivalence" argument actually works as a reason to believe in unknowable sharp boundaries, and whether Elena's appeal to legal practice genuinely supports her position or is just a suggestive analogy.
