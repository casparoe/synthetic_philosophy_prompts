I've been tracking a friend's verdicts on whether particular temporal illusions involve Orwellian or Stalinesque revisions — Dennett and Kinsbourne's distinction between a scenario where you consciously experience the original stimulus and then your memory gets rewritten after the fact (Orwellian), and one where consciousness itself is delayed so that the revised version is all you ever experience (Stalinesque). She's been working through the literature systematically, and here's her track record so far:

1. **Color phi.** Two differently colored dots are flashed in quick succession at different locations; subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color mid-trajectory. M calls this Stalinesque: the conscious percept of the color change is constructed only after the second flash arrives; you never consciously experienced the first dot at the first location with its original color before the revision.

2. **Cutaneous rabbit.** Taps are delivered at the wrist, then near the elbow; subjects feel a sequence of taps hopping evenly up the forearm, including at locations that were never stimulated. M calls this Stalinesque: the later taps retroactively determine where the intermediate taps are felt in conscious experience.

3. **Attentional blink.** In a rapid serial visual presentation stream, a second target (T2) is often missed when it appears 200–500 ms after a first target (T1). M calls this Orwellian. Her reason: T2 reliably elicits a P3 event-related potential — a well-established neural signature of conscious access — even on trials where subjects fail to report it. The content reaches consciousness but is overwritten before it can be consolidated into working memory.

4. **Saccadic chronostasis** (the stopped-clock illusion). When you shift your gaze to a clock, the first second appears to last longer than subsequent seconds. M calls this Stalinesque: the brain extends the percept of the post-saccadic scene backward in time to cover the saccadic gap; the prolonged duration is built into the conscious experience itself.

5. **Change blindness in the flicker paradigm.** Two versions of a scene alternate with a brief blank interval between them; subjects often fail to notice large changes. M calls this Orwellian: subjects consciously experience each scene in full; the failure is in comparing representations across the blank interval, not in the initial percept. If you probe subjects on the features of either scene individually, they can report them — the information was consciously accessible and the deficit is at the level of cross-interval comparison.

I can see at least two principles that fit all five verdicts:

- **Content Principle.** If later information alters the *content* of the conscious percept itself — what the experience is like — the revision is Stalinesque. If later information alters only whether the content is remembered or spontaneously reported, without changing the phenomenology, the revision is Orwellian.

- **Access Principle.** If the original, unrevised information can be accessed above chance under forced-choice conditions, it was conscious at some point and the revision is Orwellian. If it cannot be accessed even under forced choice, it never reached consciousness and the revision is Stalinesque.

Now here is the new case I want to predict M's verdict on. In a metacontrast masking paradigm, a target disk is flashed briefly, followed after a 50 ms stimulus onset asynchrony by a surrounding ring that serves as a mask. Subjects deny seeing the target disk at all and report only the ring. However, when forced to guess the target's location (left vs. right) in a two-alternative forced-choice task, they perform at 80% accuracy — well above the 50% chance baseline.

Under the Content Principle, this comes out Stalinesque: the mask prevents the target from entering the content of the conscious percept; subjects consciously experience the ring, not the target. Under the Access Principle, this comes out Orwellian: above-chance forced-choice performance demonstrates that the target information was consciously accessible, and the masking interferes with spontaneous report rather than with consciousness itself.
