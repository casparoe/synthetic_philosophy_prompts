I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and last week I witnessed an argument between two colleagues that I've been unable to resolve on my own. I'd like an expert to go through it move by move and tell me who is right about what.

We were discussing deontological constraints — the idea that certain act-types (like lying, torturing, or killing the innocent) are prohibited regardless of the consequences. Marcus said he finds these constraints paradoxical. His argument was this: take the prohibition on killing the innocent. Presumably the reason we have this prohibition is that innocent life is valuable and its destruction is a terrible wrong. But now consider a case where, unless you kill one innocent person, two other innocent people will be killed by someone else. If you respect the constraint, two people die; if you violate it, one dies. Marcus said the constraint seems self-defeating: it's there to protect innocent life, yet following it leads to more innocent lives lost. He claimed this shows that deontological constraints, if they are to be rationally defensible, must either be reinterpreted as strong but overrideable considerations (which collapses them into a form of consequentialism) or grounded in something other than the value of what they protect — and he doesn't see what that something could be.

Elena pushed back. She said Marcus was smuggling in a consequentialist picture of what it means to value something. Valuing innocent life doesn't have to mean maximizing the number of innocent lives saved; it can mean adopting a principle that forbids certain ways of treating innocent people. The constraint isn't a tool for producing the best overall distribution of life and death — it's a recognition that each person has a claim against being killed by me. When I refrain from killing the one, I'm not failing to value the two; I'm respecting the one's right not to be made my victim. The fact that someone else will kill the two is a separate moral fact about that other agent's wrongdoing, not a fact that transforms my act of killing into something permissible.

Marcus replied that this just pushes the question back. He said: if the constraint is grounded in the victim's right not to be killed, then presumably that right is grounded in the value of the victim's life or the badness of their death. But if that's so, then the same value and the same badness are present in the lives of the two — and now Elena's position seems to say that the right matters more when the threat comes from me than when it comes from someone else. Marcus called this agent-relativity, and said it looks suspiciously like a fetish: I'm privileging my own causal role over the actual suffering and death of real people. He said Elena owes an explanation of why my agency should matter that much.

Elena responded that agent-relativity isn't ad hoc — it's built into the structure of practical reasons. The reason I have not to kill is a different kind of reason from the reason I have to prevent killings by others. The former is grounded in my relationship to the person I would be killing; the latter is grounded in the badness of the outcome. These aren't commensurable in the way Marcus assumes. She added that we accept agent-relativity in other contexts without finding it paradoxical: there's a moral difference between my breaking a promise and my failing to prevent someone else from breaking theirs, even if the number of broken promises in the world is the same either way.

Marcus said he accepts that distinction for promises, but he denied it carries over to killing. Promises, he argued, are partly constituted by the agent's voluntary commitment — the reason I have to keep my promise is essentially tied to the fact that I made it. But the wrongness of killing isn't like that; it's grounded in what killing does to the victim, which is the same regardless of who does it. So the analogy fails, and Elena still hasn't explained why my agency should make a difference to whether a killing is permissible when the victim's suffering is the same either way.

At this point they went in circles, and I had to leave. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess each of Marcus's moves and each of Elena's responses, identify where each argument succeeds and where it fails, and say what the deepest unresolved issue between them really is. In particular, I want to know whether Marcus's challenge about the grounding of the killing prohibition — his claim that the analogy with promises doesn't hold — actually lands, or whether Elena has resources to answer it that she didn't fully articulate in the moment.
