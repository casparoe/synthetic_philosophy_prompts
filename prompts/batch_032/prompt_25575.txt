Consider the debate over whether the relation "all things considered better than" is transitive. Spectrum arguments advanced by Larry Temkin, Stuart Rachels, and others suggest it may not be: a series of pairwise comparisons, each intuitively favoring one option, can apparently yield the conclusion that A is better than B, B is better than C, yet A is not better than C. One standard reply insists that transitivity holds and that apparent counterexamples involve an unnoticed shift in the respect of comparison across the spectrum. Another reply accepts intransitivity and revises our understanding of practical reasoning accordingly — for instance, by denying that "choose the best option" is always well-defined.

Construct a thought experiment in which these two positions — call them the Transitivity View and the Intransitivity View — yield different concrete recommendations about what an agent ought to do. Constraints: the scenario must involve no more than four options; it must not rely on Parfit-style large populations or on tradeoffs between fundamentally incommensurable kinds of value (e.g., pleasure versus knowledge, or liberty versus equality); it must depict a situation an ordinary person could face, such as a hiring decision, a medical choice, or a resource-allocation problem in a small organization; and the disagreement must not reduce to a merely verbal dispute about what "better than" means — each side must genuinely prescribe a different course of action. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each view would use to reach its verdict, identify which structural feature of the case does the work in generating the divergence, and explain what would happen to the case if that feature were removed. Finally, offer the strongest reply available to the side whose verdict you find less plausible in this particular case, and say whether that reply succeeds in collapsing the practical disagreement.
