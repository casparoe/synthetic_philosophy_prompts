I've been working on a piece about the "what is life?" question — you know, the one that's been bugging biologists and philosophers since forever, and that NASA has to answer every time they design a life-detection mission. The thing is, I keep coming back to Blade Runner. I rewatched it last week (the Final Cut, obviously) and it struck me that the whole film is basically a two-hour argument that our definitions of life and personhood are bankrupt, or at least that they break down exactly where they're supposed to do the most work. The replicants are biological, they bleed, they have memories (even if they're implanted — but then, how reliable are ours?), they form attachments, they fear death, and Roy Batty's whole arc is about wanting more life, which is about as life-like a desire as you can have. And yet the world of the film treats them as disposable equipment.

I want to use the film as a way into the philosophical problem. Not as an illustration — I don't want to reduce the film to a thought experiment — but as something that dramatizes the stakes of the defintional question in a way the academic literature mostly doesn't. Here's what I'm after: a reading of Blade Runner through the lens of philosophical attempts to define life (and the related question of what makes something a person, since the film collapses those). Where does the film track the philosophical debate — e.g., is the Voight-Kampff test a kind of operational definition, and does it fail in philosophically interesting ways? Where does the film resist the philosophical framing, or show something the definitions miss? And what does the film add to the debate that the arguments alone don't get at? I'm especially interested in whether the film ultimately takes a position — does it come down on the side of "the replicants are alive/persons" or does it leave it genuinely ambiguous, and what would either answer mean for how we should think about the definitional project?
