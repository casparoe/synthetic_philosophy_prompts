I've been trying to work out what I actually mean by "alive," going case by case rather than starting from a definition. Here are my verdicts so far:

A dog: alive. An oak tree: alive. A Streptococcus bacterium: alive. A SARS-CoV-2 virion on a doorknob: not alive. A prion: not alive. A flame: not alive. A crystal growing in a supersaturated solution: not alive. A bacterial endospore in dormancy: alive. A mule: alive. A human in a persistent vegetative state, breathing unassisted: alive. A human sustained entirely by a heart-lung machine, with no brain activity: I want to say alive, but I am less sure. A synthetic cell built from scratch in a lab, metabolizing and dividing: alive. A computer program that maintains a kind of homeostasis, repairs its own code, and copies itself across networks: not alive.

I notice tensions I cannot resolve. The virus does more life-like things than the dormant spore — it has a genome, it evolves, it reproduces (with help) — yet I call the spore alive and the virus not. The mule cannot reproduce at all, but I do not hesitate to call it alive. The crystal and the flame both grow and consume resources, but I dismiss them. And I am not sure what to do with the brain-dead patient on machines: if I call that alive, am I committed to calling the computer program alive if it gets sophisticated enough?

Is there a single principle, or a small set of criteria, that captures these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I reconsider, and what does the best available principle say about cases I have not mentioned — say, a frozen embryo, or a self-sustaining alien machine found on another planet?
