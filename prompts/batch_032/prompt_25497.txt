I am a novelist currently at work on a new book — a literary novel about a family in the aftermath of a child's death. The manuscript is, by design, emotionally grueling. Early readers have told me they wept through entire chapters. One described finishing it as feeling hollowed out, though she immediately added that she couldn't put it down and has pressed the manuscript on two friends since.

Here is my difficulty. A trusted friend — not a writer, but someone whose judgment I respect — recently asked me, with genuine concern, why I would want to put readers through that kind of pain. She pointed out that people read fiction for pleasure, for escape, for comfort. If my book leaves people feeling worse than when they started, haven't I failed at something basic? And if I am being honest, I am not sure I have a good answer. I find myself drawn to writing tragic material, and I know readers seek it out — the novel is not lacking for early enthusiasm — but I cannot articulate why this is a defensible thing to do, rather than something vaguely perverse or even cruel.

I am not asking for writing advice. What I want to understand is whether there is a coherent way to think about this — some framework that explains why an audience would genuinely value an experience that produces sadness, grief, or distress, and why an artist is not acting wrongly by providing it. Or should I take my friend's concern at face value and reconsider the whole enterprise?
