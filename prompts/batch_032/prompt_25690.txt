I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this semester and we've been working through the animal consciousness literature — Nagel, Griffin, Godfrey-Smith, the usual suspects. Last week someone in my group laid out an argument that's been eating at me ever since, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a cheap trick or a deep problem. Here it is, reconstructed from my notes:

(1) For any two species A and B that are adjacent in a smooth evolutionary lineage, if A and B are extremely similar in their neuroanatomy, behavior, and ecological niche, then either both A and B have phenomenal consciousness or neither does. The thought being: it would be bizarre — a kind of biological magic — if consciousness blinked on or off between two nearly identical organisms.

(2) There is a chain of species stretching from Homo sapiens down to something like a sea sponge, where each link in the chain is an adjacent pair of the sort described in (1). You can trace it through actual evolutionary history — the tree of life is dense enough that you never have to jump between radically different organisms in a single step.

(3) From (1) and (2), by repeated application: either every species in the chain is conscious, or none are.

(4) Humans are conscious. (I'm taking this as fixed; if you want to deny it we're in a different conversation.)

(5) Sea sponges are not conscious — no nervous system, no behavior worth the name.

(6) (3), (4), and (5) can't all be true. So something has to give.

The person who presented this said the argument shows that anyone who wants to draw a line somewhere — say, at mammals, or at vertebrates, or at creatures with a centralized nervous system — is either being arbitrary or is in denial about how gradual evolution really is. But the conclusion that consciousness goes all the way down to sponges is absurd, and the conclusion that nothing is conscious (not even us) is worse. So we're stuck.

I've been turning it over and I can see a few places to push. Maybe premise (1) looks plausible only because we're focusing on adjacent pairs and ignoring that "extremely similar" isn't a transitive relation — you can have a series where each step is small but the endpoints are nothing alike. Maybe the chain in (2) has genuine gaps if you look closely enough at the actual phylogeny. Maybe consciousness really is a threshold phenomenon and there is a sharp line somewhere, even if we can't currently locate it, and we just have to live with that. But each of these moves feels like it costs something I don't want to pay — giving up (1) seems to license drawing lines wherever we feel like it, giving up (2) seems to bet against what we know about evolution, and the threshold move feels like it kicks the problem down the road without solving it.

So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't go wrong, which premise is least costly to reject, and what do you have to live with if you reject it? I want the cleanest version of the diagnosis you can give — not a literature review. Keep it under 500 words.
