I'm taking a graduate seminar on dispositions and counterfactuals, and we've spent the last three weeks working through the literature on finks, masks, and mimics — the standard counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. Our professor has a distinctive pedagogical style: she never states her own view directly, but she'll give a crisp yes-or-no verdict on any particular case we bring to her. By now we've accumulated a decent set of her rulings, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer the principle she's running before the final paper is due. She's hinted that her view doesn't map neatly onto any single published position, which makes the exercise both more interesting and more unnerving.

Here are the cases she's ruled on so far, all of the form "Does object x have the disposition D to manifest M in response to stimulus S?":

1. A dry match struck on a rough surface: has the disposition to ignite.
2. A waterlogged match (soaked through) struck on a rough surface: has the disposition to ignite — the water masks the manifestation but doesn't remove the disposition.
3. A dry match that a sorcerer would instantly douse with water upon being struck: has the disposition to ignite — the sorcerer's intervention finks the manifestation, but the match possesses the disposition before and at the moment of striking.
4. A steel nail struck on a rough surface: lacks the disposition to ignite.
5. A steel nail that a sorcerer would coat with thermite and ignite upon being struck: lacks the disposition to ignite — the sorcerer supplies the manifestation, but the nail never had the disposition; this is a mimic, not a fink.
6. A match whose head lacks the oxidizing agent, so that it is chemically inert for ignition purposes, struck on a rough surface: lacks the disposition to ignite — the intrinsic causal basis is absent.
7. A perfectly normal match struck inside a vacuum chamber: has the disposition to ignite — the vacuum masks the manifestation, but the match itself is unchanged.

The new case I want to test against her pattern is this: a match whose head contains all the usual combustible chemicals, but also contains an additional compound that, upon the friction of striking, releases a fire-suppressant gas a split second before ignition would occur, so the match never produces a flame. The suppression is intrinsic to the match's own chemistry — no external agent and no environmental condition interferes. Everything that happens is the natural causal consequence of the match's own composition.

I can see arguments both ways. On the one hand, the match contains the full causal basis for ignition — the oxidizing agent, the fuel, everything that verdict 6 treats as criterial — and the suppressant looks like an internally located mask, analogous to the water in verdict 2. On the other hand, the suppressant is part of the match's own intrinsic structure in a way that the water in a waterlogged match arguably isn't: the water is an intruder, while the suppressant is built in from the start. If what matters is the object's total intrinsic makeup, this match might be more like the defective match of verdict 6 than the waterlogged match of verdict 2. The question seems to turn on where you draw the line between a mask and an absence of the causal basis, and whether that line tracks anything deeper than intuition.

I'm less interested in a flat prediction than in (a) the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far, (b) which rule you think she's actually running and why, (c) what each rule says about the new case, and (d) what further case would best discriminate between the leading candidates — ideally a case I could actually bring up in next week's seminar.
