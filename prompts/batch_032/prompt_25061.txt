I am a journalist who covers the intersection of science, technology, and ideas for a general-interest magazine. I am working on a piece about a tension that has gone under-explored between two influential lines of thought that share some intellectual origins.

The first is the simulation argument. The core thought is that if a civilization reaches a posthuman stage capable of running vast numbers of detailed ancestor-simulations, then the number of simulated minds would vastly outnumber the original, biological ones, making it probable — conditional on reaching that stage — that we are among the simulated. The argument's trilemma says that at least one of three propositions must hold: civilizations like ours almost never reach the posthuman stage; posthuman civilizations almost never run large numbers of ancestor-simulations; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation.

The second is the case for fanaticism about the far future, associated with the broader longtermist movement in effective altruism. The idea is that the expected value of humanity's future, if we avoid existential catastrophe, is astronomically large: trillions of future lives across millions of years, perhaps spreading beyond Earth. On standard expected-value reasoning, even a tiny change in the probability of realizing that future swamps the value of almost any intervention whose effects are confined to the present or near future. This thought has been used to argue that reducing existential risk should be humanity's overwhelming moral priority.

The tension I want to explore is this: if you take the simulation argument seriously enough to assign non-trivial credence to the hypothesis that we are simulated, what does that do to the case for far-future fanaticism? Several worries arise. If we are in a simulation, the far future that matters may be the far future of base reality — and our actions inside the simulation may bear no reliable relationship to it. The simulation might be terminated, paused, or restarted long before any far-future consequences unfold. The ethical stakes might be bounded by the scope of the simulation itself, deflating the astronomical expected-value calculations. More subtly, the simulation argument might shift the reference class for anthropic reasoning in ways that undermine the probabilistic foundations of longtermist fanaticism. And there is a reflexive worry: if fanaticism about the far future motivates us to build powerful technologies to safeguard it, and those same technologies eventually enable vast numbers of simulations, then taking the simulation argument seriously might ironically increase our credence that we are already simulated — and thereby undercut the very fanaticism that drove the project.

I would like you to write an essay that works through this tension. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides: the case that the simulation argument genuinely dampens — perhaps even collapses — the case for far-future fanaticism, and the case that the two can be reconciled or that the tension is less serious than it first appears. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it. Do not end on a fence-sitting note. The essay should be accessible to an educated reader who is not a specialist in either the simulation argument or longtermist ethics, but it should be rigorous and precise enough that a philosopher working in these areas would find it worth engaging.
