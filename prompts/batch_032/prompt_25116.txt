I'm TAing a philosophy of mind course and I've got this exam answer to grade. The question was:

"Explain Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of conscious experience, using the color phi phenomenon as your central example. What does Dennett take the significance of this distinction to be for the study of consciousness?"

Here's what the student wrote:

"The color phi phenomenon is when two differently colored dots are flashed in quick succession at different locations, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color midway. Dennett uses this to distinguish two ways the brain might handle the timing of conscious experience. The Orwellian account says that you consciously experience the first dot, then the second dot, but your memory of the sequence gets revised after the fact—like the Ministry of Truth altering records. You had the veridical experiences but you remember them wrong. The Stalinesque account says that conscious experience itself is delayed by about half a second so that the brain can construct a coherent narrative before anything reaches awareness; you never consciously see the individual dots, you only ever experience the moving, color-changing dot. Dennett's point is that both accounts are empirically equivalent—they predict exactly the same verbal reports and behaviors—so we can't decide between them on experimental grounds. He takes this to show that there's no fact of the matter about the exact timing of conscious experience, which undermines the idea of a Cartesian Theater where consciousness 'comes together' at a definite moment. This is part of his larger argument for the multiple drafts model, where there's no privileged moment of conscious awareness."
