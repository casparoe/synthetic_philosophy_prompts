So I was listening to this philosophy podcast the other day — the host had a legal scholar on, and they got into this whole thing about causation by omission. The guest summarized the issue like this:

"The law routinely treats omissions as causes. If a lifeguard watches someone drown and does nothing, we say the lifeguard caused the death by failing to act. Philosophers have mostly come around to the same view: a counterfactual analysis handles omissions perfectly. You just ask whether the harm would have occurred if the person had acted, and if the answer is no, the omission was a cause. The only people who resist this are metaphysicians who think causes have to be physical events — but that's just a prejudice. Once you accept that omissions are causes, there's no real difference between causing harm by acting and causing harm by failing to act, which is why the law is right to treat them the same way."

I'm not sure I buy all of this. Something about the jump from the counterfactual test to "the law is right to treat them the same way" feels to quick to me, and I vaguely remember reading that some philosophers think omissions raise a real metaphysical puzzle, not just a prejudice. But I don't know the literature well enough to say where exactly this summary goes wrong — or if it's actually fair and my instincts are off. Can you go through it and tell me what a philosopher working on causation would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the conclusion follows from the accurate parts?
