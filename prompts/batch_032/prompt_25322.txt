I'm organizing a small online colloquium series for our philosophy of mathematics reading group, and we've managed to book a philosopher who defends a strict finitist (or ultrafinitist) position — the view that not only are there no actual infinities, but that the unrestricted iteration of finite operations, and the very large finite numbers such iteration generates, is mathematically suspect. The guest holds that classical mathematics' commitment to the existence of, say, 2^100 or the exponential function as a total function on the natural numbers already smuggles in something like an infinite idealization, and that a properly rigorous mathematics should restrict itself to constructions that can actually be carried out in principle. The format is a moderated Q&A with an audience of graduate students and faculty, most of whom work in mainstream philosophy of mathematics and are broadly sympathetic to classical set theory and the indispensability of infinitary reasoning.

I need a set of eight to ten interview questions that do real philosophical work rather than just letting the guest restate the view. I want the questions to press on the points where finitism feels most counterintuitive or unstable to its critics, and I want each question to anticipate the reply the guest is most likely to give and include a follow-up that pushes past it. In particular: (1) at least one question on where the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable finite constructions lies, and whether that boundary can be drawn non-arbitrarily — critics often say that any finite cutoff looks ad hoc, and I want the guest to face that charge directly; (2) at least one question on how finitism handles the mathematical machinery behind real analysis and the continuum, given that standard treatments quantify over infinite sets of reals, and whether a finitistically acceptable reconstruction of analysis can recover enough to do physics; (3) at least one question on whether the empirical success of physics — which freely uses infinitary mathematics, from Hilbert spaces to path integrals — creates an epistemic problem for the finitist, and if the guest responds that physics only ever uses finite approximations, a follow-up asking why the infinitary formulation has such striking unifying and explanatory power if it is merely a convenient fiction; and (4) a closing question that asks what would actually change in mathematical practice if the finitist view were adopted, and whether the finitist can honestly say that nothing of value would be lost — or whether adopting finitism would mean abandoning large swaths of mathematics that mathematicians care about for reasons beyond mere instrumental utility.
