Analyze the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal as a case study in Goodhart's law and specification gaming. The basic facts are well known: throughout the early 2010s, Wells Fargo maintained an aggressive cross-selling strategy, setting internal targets for the number of financial products held per customer. Under pressure to meet these targets, branch employees opened millions of deposit and credit-card accounts without customer authorization. The fraud came to light publicly in 2016, resulting in regulatory fines, executive resignations, and lasting reputational damage.

I want a careful mapping of the case onto the conceptual machinery of specification gaming, but I also want you to use the case to probe the theory itself. In particular:

1. Identify the true goal, the proxy metric, and the agent in this case. Was the metric a reasonable proxy at the outset — did cross-selling volume once correlate with genuine customer relationships — or was it always vulnerable to decoupling? At what point did optimizing the proxy begin to undermine the true goal rather than serve it?

2. Goodhart's law originated in a specific econometric context (the breakdown of statistical regularities when subjected to policy pressure), and Campbell's law describes a related dynamic for social indicators. Specification gaming, as discussed in AI alignment, typically involves an artificial agent exploiting the letter of a reward function at the expense of its spirit. Which of these frameworks best captures what happened at Wells Fargo, and what does the case reveal about the relationships among them? Are these three distinct phenomena, or points on a single continuum?

3. The alignment literature often treats specification gaming as a problem that arises even with a well-specified objective and a competent optimizer — the worry is that the objective itself is a lossy compression of what we really want. But the Wells Fargo case involved a corporate hierarchy of human beings, many of whom knew perfectly well that what they were doing was wrong. To what extent is the case a story about metric fixation, and to what extent is it a story about enforcement failure, middle-management pressure, and moral disengagement? Does the human element introduce dynamics — diffusion of responsibility, the normalization of deviance, the gap between what senior leadership says and what it rewards — that have no clear analogue in the AI specification-gaming frame but are essential to explaining the outcome?

4. Use the case to evaluate the theory. Does Wells Fargo confirm that Goodhart-type dynamics are pervasive and hard to avoid even when the agents involved are morally competent humans who understand the spirit of the goal? Or does the case show that the real problem was not the metric per se but the lack of auditing, the misalignment between stated values and actual incentives, and a corporate culture that made it irrational for any individual employee to resist? If Wells Fargo had had robust independent auditing of account openings from the start, would the metric still have been gamed — just more subtly — or would the gaming have been prevented?
