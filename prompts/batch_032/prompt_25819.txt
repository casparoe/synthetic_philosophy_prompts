ok so for my philosophy class we've been talking about whether the language you speak shapes how you think, and i've been trying to figure out what i actually believe by going through a bunch of examples and seeing what my gut says. here's where i'm at:

1. russian has two different words for blue — goluboy for light blue and siniy for dark blue — and apparently russian speakers are faster at telling light blue from dark blue than english speakers. my gut says yeah, that makes sense, having the words probably tunes your perception a little.

2. but then, english didn't used to have a word for schadenfreude (we borrowed it from german), and obviously english speakers could still feel that emotion before we had the word. so not having a word doesn't mean you can't think the thing. that seems obviously right to me.

3. in spanish "bridge" is masculine and people describe bridges with words like "strong" and "big," but in german "bridge" is feminine and people use words like "elegant" and "beautiful." i want to believe this but honestly i'm kind of suspicious — like maybe people are just reaching for stereotypes when the experimenter asks them, not that the grammar is actually doing something deep to their thinking.

4. there's this language called pirahã that supposedly doesn't have number words (like, not even "one" or "two" exactly), and speakers reportedly can't do exact counting tasks. if that's true, that feels like pretty strong evidence that language constrains thought — like if you don't have numbers you literally can't think numerically in a precise way. but i've also heard there's controversy about whether the data is good.

5. some languages don't use left and right for directions, they use cardinal directions like north and south even for small-scale stuff ("move your north foot"). speakers of these languages are apparently really good at staying oriented even indoors or in unfamiliar places. this seems real to me and actually pretty cool — like language is giving them a cognitive skill they wouldn't otherwise have.

6. personal one: i speak some spanish, and when i'm speaking spanish i feel a little different — maybe more outgoing or expressive. but i honestly can't tell if that's the language itself or just that i associate spanish with certain cultural contexts and social situations. my gut says it's probably the context, not the grammar.

7. in 1984 orwell has this idea of newspeak, where the government eliminates words for rebellion so that rebellious thoughts become literally impossible. i just don't buy it. people would find ways around it — they'd repurpose other words or invent new ones. you can't just delete a concept by deleting a word.

so those are my verdicts. i feel like there's some kind of pattern here — i'm not a full-on "language determines thought" person but i'm also not a "language has zero effect" person. is there a coherent principle that captures all of these, or at least most of them? and if some of my verdicts have to go to make the principle work, which ones would you overturn and why? also, what would the principle say about a case i haven't thought of — like, if a language has no future tense (apparently some don't), would that affect how its speakers think about the future?
