ok so heres something thats been bugging me. my grandmother signed an advance directive years ago saying no feeding tube no life support the works. now shes got pretty advanced dementia, shes in a nursing home, she seems honestly kind of content? she likes pudding, she smiles at the nurses, she doesnt recognize us but shes not in distress. she got pneumonia last month and the doctors followed the advance directive and didnt treat it aggressively — just kept her comfortable. she pulled through anyway but it got me thinking: the whole legal and medical system treated the document signed by her earlier self as binding, even though the person in the bed now would probably choose to live if you could explain the situation to her (which you cant really).

and heres the thing that really gets me: if my grandmother, while still competent, had written a will leaving everything to her cat and then later changed her mind and wrote a new will, the legal system would go with the later will. the most recent expression of her wishes wins. but with advance directives, the earlier self gets a veto that the later self cant revoke — because by the time the later self exists, shes not competent to revoke it. so were privileging the earlier self in one domain and the later self in another. whats going on there?

i want to understand what philosophical commitments are baked into the practice of honoring advance directives. what theory of personal identity does it presuppose? what theory of autonomy? where is the practice actually philosophically underdetermined, so you could endorse it from several different positions? and are the presuppositions internally consistent, or does the practice pull in two directions at once?
