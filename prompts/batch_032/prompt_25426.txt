I've been playing a lot of story-driven games over the past year — the kind where you make choices that affect the fates of the characters around you — and I've noticed something that's been hard to shake. In one game, I had the option to betray a companion who had trusted me for dozens of hours of playtime. I went through with it, just to see what would happen, and afterward I felt genuinely awful — not in a "that was a dark storyline" kind of way, but in a way that felt moral, like I had actually done something wrong. The character wasn't real, wasn't conscious, was just a collection of dialogue trees and animations, and yet the feeling persisted. I started asking myself: is my emotional response just a misfiring, my social instincts getting tricked by pixels? Or is something else going on — could it be that there are real moral reasons that apply to how we treat these characters, reasons that don't simply reduce to "it might make the player feel bad" or "it might encourage cruelty toward real people"?

I want you to be a sharp and resourceful defender of this view, not a caricature. Here is my opening challenge. Consider the simplest possible case: I write a ten-line program that displays a single dot on a screen. When I press a key, the dot vanishes and the word "help" prints to the console. Surely nothing I do to this dot matters morally — the idea seems absurd. But a video game character, however richly detailed, is ultimately the same kind of thing: code responding to inputs, just vastly more elaborate. If the simple case carries no moral weight, why should piling on more complexity — more lines of code, more animations, more scripted reactions — ever cross a threshold into genuine moral significance? What kind of property could complexity add that would make that difference, and why should we think it's a property that matters?
