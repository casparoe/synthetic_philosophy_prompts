Federal perjury law in the United States, as interpreted by the Supreme Court in *Bronston v. United States* (1973), draws a sharp line: a witness who makes a literally true statement under oath has not committed perjury, even if that statement was crafted to mislead the questioner and even if the witness intended to create a false impression. In *Bronston*, a witness asked whether he had ever had a Swiss bank account answered, "The company had an account there for about six months, in Zürich." That was true — the company did have such an account — but the witness personally had also held a Swiss account, and his answer was designed to leave the questioner with the opposite belief. The Supreme Court held unanimously that this was not perjury. The remedy, the Court said, was not prosecution but sharper follow-up questions from the examiner. The rule has survived decades of criticism and remains good law.

Now notice the contrast. In securities regulation, Rule 10b-5 prohibits not only false statements but also statements that are misleading by omission or half-truth — a literally true statement can still be actionable if it creates a misleading impression in context. In fraud law more generally, a half-truth that misleads can count as fraud. And in ordinary moral judgment, many people treat the difference between lying (asserting what you believe false) and merely misleading (causing a false belief through true statements) as at most a matter of degree, not a bright line.

So here is the puzzle. The *Bronston* rule seems to presuppose that there is a morally and legally significant difference between lying under oath and merely misleading under oath — significant enough that one is a felony and the other is not even sanctionable. But what exactly is the theory behind that difference? Is it a substantive view about the wrongness of lying (e.g., that lying violates an obligation to the questioner in a way that misleading does not), or is it an institutional view about the adversarial system (e.g., that the burden of precise questioning falls on the examiner, and the witness's only duty is to answer truthfully what was actually asked), or is it a rule-of-law view about the dangers of prosecuting based on implications and inferences rather than on verifiably false statements? And does the *Bronston* rule actually presuppose one of these, or is it compatible with several?
