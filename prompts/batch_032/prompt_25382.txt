[Student Answer]

Arrow's impossibility theorem is a foundational result in social choice theory that proves there is no perfect voting system. Arrow showed that any social welfare function — a rule for aggregating individual preferences into a social ranking — must violate at least one of four seemingly reasonable conditions, provided there are at least three alternatives.

The four conditions are as follows. First, Universal Domain: the social welfare function must be able to handle any possible configuration of individual preferences; you cannot restrict what preferences people are allowed to have. Second, Pareto Efficiency: if every individual prefers alternative x to alternative y, then society must rank x above y. Third, Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): the social ranking of two alternatives should depend only on how individuals rank those two alternatives relative to each other, and not on how they rank other alternatives. This is often illustrated by the idea that adding or removing a third candidate from the ballot should not change whether the first candidate beats the second. Fourth, Non-Dictatorship: there is no single individual whose preferences always determine the social ranking regardless of what everyone else thinks.

What Arrow proved is that no social welfare function can satisfy all four conditions simultaneously. In other words, any voting system that works for all possible preference profiles and respects both Pareto and IIA must be dictatorial. This is the impossibility: you cannot have all four desirable properties at once; you must give up at least one.

The theorem is most famously illustrated by the Condorcet paradox, where three voters have cyclic preferences (A > B > C, B > C > A, C > A > B) and majority rule produces an intransitive social ranking: A beats B, B beats C, but C beats A. This shows that majority rule violates the transitivity requirement that is built into the definition of a social welfare function. Because of this, Arrow's theorem is often taken to show that majority rule is inconsistent.

Does this give us reason to be pessimistic about democracy? I think the answer is no, for two reasons. First, Arrow's conditions are not all equally compelling. In particular, IIA has been widely criticized as too strong. Why should the social ranking of x and y be completely insensitive to information about how people rank other alternatives? If we relax IIA, we can have voting systems like the Borda count that satisfy the other three conditions. Second, the theorem assumes we need a complete social ranking, but in real democratic politics we usually only need to select a winner. The theorem therefore does not directly apply to most real-world elections, which ask for a single choice rather than a full ranking. For these reasons, Arrow's theorem is an important theoretical result but does not undermine the practical viability of democratic decision-making.
