I'm a journalist who covers the intersection of technology, behavioral science, and political philosophy, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on whether the algorithmic curation and A/B testing that social media platforms use to shape user behavior amounts to manipulation rather than legitimate persuasion. Please describe the most important implications of the philosophical literature on the manipulation–persuasion distinction for this question. In particular, address the view that manipulation is distinguished from persuasion by the fact that it bypasses or subverts the target's rational capacities, and walk through what this criterion would say about an opaque recommendation algorithm that learns to exploit cognitive biases in order to maximize engagement. If the algorithm's designers do not themselves understand which specific biases are being exploited at any given moment — they simply optimize a metric and observe that certain content keeps users scrolling — does this make the case for manipulation stronger, by removing any pretense of reasoned appeal, or weaker, by severing the connection to a manipulator's intent? And whatever your analysis yields on that point, what practical conclusions follow for how a democratic society ought to regulate such systems, given that they operate at scale without anything resembling individualized consent?
