I'm part of a philosophy of mind working group that has been debating pain asymbolia for the better part of a semester, and we've reached the point where everyone knows everyone else's moves so well that we're just talking past each other. We have two factions.

The "separatists" take pain asymbolia to demonstrate a clean dissociation between the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain and its affective-motivational dimension. They point to patients who, after lesions to the insula or anterior cingulate, report that they still feel pain — they can locate it, describe its burning or stabbing quality, and rate its intensity — but insist that it doesn't bother them, that they don't want it to stop, that it isn't unpleasant. For the separatists, this is close to a knockdown argument that the badness of pain is a contingent, separable response layered on top of a sensory core, not something intrinsic to the sensation itself. They often connect this to imperativist or motivational theories: pain's badness just is its imperative force (it tells you to stop doing what you're doing), and in asymbolia the imperative is somehow disconnected from the sensation.

The "intrinsicalists" push back hard. They argue that what the asymbolia patient has is not pain-minus-the-badness but a degraded or incomplete pain state — perhaps a mere nociceptive sensation that lacks the full phenomenal profile of genuine pain. For them, unpleasantness is constitutive of pain's phenomenal character: the burning quality of pain isn't a neutral datum to which an affective tag gets attached; the burning is itself awful. They worry that the separatists are tacitly relying on a two-component model of experience — sensation here, affect there — that begs the question against views on which the affective dimension inflects the sensory character itself. Some of them also argue that the asymbolia patient's reports are less probative than they seem: if you've lost the capacity to find pain unpleasant, you may have lost the capacity to fully appreciate what you're missing, so your claim that the pain is the same sensation carries less weight than it appears to.

We've decided to try an adversarial collaboration to see if we can produce something constructive rather than another round of the same arguments. I've been tasked with drafting the joint document. I need it to include:

(1) A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be substantive — not just "pain asymbolia exists" or "the patients have brain lesions" — but claims about what the case shows, what it rules out, or what it leaves open, that both camps can genuinely accept.

(2) A disagreements table. Each row should state the precise point of disagreement in neutral terms, then give each side's position in wording that side would endorse if they were writing it themselves. I want to avoid the common failure mode where one side's view is stated in the other side's vocabulary and comes out sounding obviously wrong.

(3) A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. These should be as concrete as possible — ideally questions where one could imagine further empirical or conceptual work making a difference.

(4) For each crux, a concrete test, case, or line of argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or interpretation recorded in advance. Where possible, specify what kind of evidence would count and what each side expects that evidence to show.

The document should also flag any disagreements that turn out to be partly or wholly verbal — for instance, if the two sides mean different things by "pain," "unpleasantness," or "the same sensation," that should be noted explicitly rather than letting the dispute run on a terminological confusion.

Please respond in the style of an expert philosopher of mind who is deeply familiar with the pain asymbolia literature and the surrounding debates about phenomenal consciousness, representation, and affect. Think very carefully about what formulations would be acceptable to each side — the goal is to produce a document that both camps could genuinely sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.
