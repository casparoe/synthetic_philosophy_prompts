I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Minds, Machines, and Meaning," and I'm drafting the take-home final. The course is structured in two halves: the first covers Dennett's intentional stance — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, along with his instrumentalist view that beliefs and desires are real patterns we project onto systems whose behavior is too complex to predict otherwise. The second half turns to contemporary debates about AI cognition, with a focus on large language models. I've noticed that even my strongest students tend to treat these two halves as separate units: they can explain the three stances perfectly well on a midterm, and they can discuss whether LLMs "really understand" on a discussion post, but they rarely bring Dennett's framework to bear on the LLM question in a critical way. I want the final essay question to force that synthesis.

Please write an essay question that asks students to evaluate whether the intentional stance is an adequate framework for making sense of our tendency to attribute beliefs, intentions, and understanding to large language models. The question should be unanswerable by simply reciting the definitions of the three stances; it should require students to identify a genuine philosophical tension — for instance, between Dennett's pragmatic justification for the intentional stance and the fact that LLMs lack the evolutionary and embodied history that Dennett typically appeals to, or between the predictive success of the intentional stance toward LLMs and the worry that this success might be parasitic on the original intentionality of the humans who produced the training data. Please also write a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total 25 marks). For each point, include a one-sentence note on the most common way students go wrong. The marking scheme should clearly separate what distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer — not just in terms of completeness, but in terms of the specific critical moves an A answer must make.
