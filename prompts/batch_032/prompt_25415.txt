Okay so I've been chewing on this thing that's become pretty standard in AI safety circles — the move from talking about "value alignment" to "intent alignment." The idea, as I've absorbed it from reading LessWrong and the alignment forum over the years, is roughly: getting an AI to share human values is a mess because values are fuzzy, contradictory, hard to specify, and vary across people. But getting an AI to do what its operator actually intends — corrigibly, with the AI trying to figure out what you meant rather than optimizing literally against your words — is supposed to be a more tractable engineering target. This narrowing then feeds into a bunch of research agendas: iterated amplification, debate, CIRL, etc.

What I want to know is: what philosophical commitments does this narrowing actually presuppose? Because it seems to me that it's smuggling in a pretty specific picture of what an intention is and how intentions relate to values.

Specifically, the framing seems to presuppose that intentions are in principle specifiable without first solving the value problem — that I can say "the AI should do what I intend" and this picks out a well-defined target even if my values are a tangled mess. But is that true? If I intend for the AI to "write a nice email to my boss," my intention embeds a bunch of value-laden judgments about what counts as nice, what tone is appropriate, whether honesty or flattery is more important in this context, etc. The intention doesn't seem to float free of values; it seems to be values applied to a particular situation. If that's right, then intent alignment hasn't actually dodged the hard part of value alignment — it's just pushed it inside the word "intent."

There's also a second thing. The intent-alignment framing seems to presuppose a principal-agent model: there's a human with a well-formed intention, and the AI is the agent executing it. But a lot of the classic failure modes involve the human not having a fully-formed intention at all. Like, I tell the AI to "solve climate change" — do I actually have a detailed intention about what that means, or do I have a vague desire plus an implicit hope that the AI will fill in the details in a way I'd endorse upon reflection? If the latter, then aligning to my "intent" might be aligning to something that doesn't fully exist yet, and the AI has to do a bunch of normative work to construct it. Which starts to look an awful lot like value alignment again.

So I guess my question is: reconstruct the implicit theory of intention that the intent-alignment framing needs in order to be a genuine narrowing rather than a relabeling. What view of intention — Gricean, Bratman-esque, some kind of functional-role theory, whatever — does the framing commit you to? Does it require that intentions are fully determinate mental states, or can it handle intentions as partial specifications that get filled in by the agent? And what would happen to the intent/value distinction if you instead held a view where intentions just are values-in-context, with no clean boundary between them? Would the whole framing need to be scrapped, or would it just need to be stated differently?
