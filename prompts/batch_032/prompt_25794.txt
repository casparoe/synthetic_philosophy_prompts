I'm teaching a graduate seminar that pairs Susan Wolf's essay "Moral Saints" with Dostoevsky's The Idiot. Wolf argues that a morally perfect person — a moral saint — would be unattractive and dull, lacking the non-moral excellences (artistic sensibility, wit, culinary pleasure, athletic skill) that make a human life well-rounded and desirable. The ideal of moral sainthood, she suggests, is not one we should want for ourselves or for those we love.

Prince Myshkin is Dostoevsky's attempt to portray a positively beautiful human being — a figure of radical compassion, truthfulness, and self-sacrifice. The novel follows his return to Russian society and the cascade of disasters that culminate in his psychological collapse.

I would like a careful reading of The Idiot through the lens of Wolf's argument. Specifically: Does Myshkin exhibit the kind of moral sainthood Wolf critiques, or does Dostoevsky give him a different kind of goodness that escapes her argument? Does the novel anticipate Wolf's worry — does it show us what is lost when moral considerations dominate a life? And does the novel's tragic ending vindicate Wolf by revealing the saint's inadequacy in a complex world, or does it indict the world rather than the saint? I want the reading to be faithful to both the novel and the philosophical argument, and to end by saying what Dostoevsky adds to the debate that Wolf's essay alone cannot capture.
