I need a thought experiment built to specification. Here are the specifications.

Construct a case in which a Lewis-convention — a behavioral regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, is arbitrary (an alternative regularity would have coordinated equally well), and is sustained by common knowledge and precedent — gradually acquires genuine normative force. By the end of the case, participants treat violations not merely as foolish or self-defeating (the way it is foolish to drive on the right when everyone else drives on the left) but as wrong: as something one ought not to do, where the "ought" has the character of a requirement that survives even when defection would carry no coordination penalty on the particular occasion. The norm must have come apart from the underlying coordination problem.

Constraints:

- The transition from convention to norm must be generated by features already present in Lewis's account — precedent, salience, common knowledge, the structure of the coordination game itself — not by importing independent moral considerations (no appeals to harm, rights, fairness, or substantive moral principles external to the apparatus of Convention).
- The case must be drawn from ordinary life: a workplace, a neighborhood, a family, a community group. No exotic setups, no science fiction, no stipulation that the participants are unusual reasoners.
- The normative force at the end must be genuine in the following sense: it must make sense for a participant to say "you ought to do X" even on an occasion when doing Y would, taken in isolation, coordinate equally well — the norm has outrun the coordination incentives that gave rise to it.
- The case should unfold over time so we can see the convention at three stages: (1) a mere regularity that solves the problem, (2) an entrenched expectation whose violation surprises and inconveniences, and (3) a requirement whose violation attracts attitudes like resentment, reproach, or demands for justification — attitudes that go beyond "you made things harder for yourself too."

After presenting the case, do the following:

1. Walk through what Lewis's account says about each stage. At each point, what kind of expectation do participants have, what kind of knowledge do they share, and what kind of reason does each have to conform? Use the formal elements of the account — coordination equilibrium, common knowledge, arbitrariness, precedent — to characterize what changes across the three stages.

2. Identify which specific feature of the case does the work of generating normativity from mere convention. Is it the sheer weight of precedent? The fact that expectations become relied upon in downstream planning, so that deviation harms others who reasonably depended on conformity? The way common knowledge makes deviation publicly interpretable as a signal of something beyond a mistake — perhaps indifference or hostility? Or something else?

3. Tell me what happens if you remove that feature. Does the case collapse back into a mere coordination problem, with no residual normative force? Or does the normativity survive through some other channel, suggesting the feature you identified wasn't doing the work after all?

4. Finally, say whether Lewis himself — on the most faithful reading of Convention — would accept that this case generates genuine normativity, or whether he would redescribe it as a particularly entrenched convention that still is not a norm. If the latter, identify what would need to be added to his account to capture what is happening in the case, and whether that addition would be a friendly amendment or a departure.
