I've spent a fair bit of time on LessWrong and related forums, and one thing that keeps coming up in discussions of LLM capabilities is whether these systems genuinely understand language or are merely doing something that looks like understanding. The standard move is to reach for Searle's Chinese Room or to debate the semantics of the word "understanding." But I think the more interesting lens — and the one that gets surprisingly little sustained attention — is Wittgenstein's later philosophy: language games, family resemblance, meaning as use, the private language argument, and rule-following.

Wittgenstein died in 1951, so he never encountered anything like a transformer-based language model. But his framework seems almost tailor-made for the question. On one hand, he insisted that meaning is constituted by use within shared forms of life — and an LLM has no form of life, no embodiment, no stakes, no participation in human practices beyond the statistical shadows of them captured in its training corpus. On the other hand, his family resemblance concept suggests we should not look for a single essence of "understanding" or "meaning" that either is or is not present; we should look at the overlapping similarities between cases. And his private language argument might suggest that if a system's "language" were disconnected from public criteria of correctness, it would not count as language at all — but LLMs are trained on public language and evaluated by public criteria, so the argument might cut the other way.

I want you to construct the strongest version of the Wittgensteinian case for attributing genuine linguistic understanding to LLMs, and the strongest version of the Wittgensteinian case against it, and then give your best judgment about where Wittgenstein himself would most likely have landed. Do not just gesture at the concepts — work through them in detail. What language game is the LLM playing, and who are the players? What is the relevant form of life, if any? What would it mean for the LLM to follow a rule, in Wittgenstein's sense, and does its training process qualify? How does the family resemblance concept apply to the comparison between human language use and LLM outputs — which resemblances matter and which are merely superficial? And if Wittgenstein would have been skeptical of LLM understanding, what exactly would a system need to have or do differently to cross the threshold on his account?

Respond in the style of an expert on Wittgenstein's later philosophy who is also technically literate about how transformer-based language models work. Think very carefully before answering, and flag any points where Wittgenstein's framework genuinely underdetermines the answer rather than forcing a conclusion in one direction.
