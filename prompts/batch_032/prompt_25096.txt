I am a novelist at work on my fourth book, and I have been studying Daniel Dennett's claim that the self is a center of narrative gravity — not an entity located in the brain but an abstraction that emerges from the stories told about a person, much as a physical center of gravity is an abstraction that emerges from the distribution of mass. I suspect I have been constructing characters backward: I begin with a list of traits, a backstory, and a putative inner life, and then I try to make the character cohere around that fixed point. Dennett's picture suggests I should be working in the opposite direction, letting the self precipitate out of the narratives rather than imposing it in advance.

I would like you to design a character-construction procedure grounded in this narrative-gravity view — something I can use for each major and secondary character across novels. I write literary fiction with ensemble casts of roughly four to eight significant characters per book, and I produce a novel every two to three years, so the procedure will see regular but not daily use. I have time to work through it deliberately, but it must yield outputs I can actually write from: scenes, dialogue, decisions a character makes under pressure, moments of self-betrayal or self-revision. The procedure should take as its raw materials the events of the plot, the testimony of other characters, the character's own self-narrations (whether explicit in first-person narration or implicit in free indirect style), and the discrepancies among these accounts, and it should show how a self crystallizes — or fails to crystallize — out of that material. Flag every step at which the theory runs out and I, the author, must make a judgment call. Be explicit about which kinds of characters the procedure will handle badly.

Then apply the procedure to two cases. The first is a protagonist who narrates her own story in the first person — a character whose self-narrative is unusually dominant and who may be unreliable. The second is a character who appears only in scenes reported by other characters and who never speaks in her own voice — a minor figure whose center of narrative gravity must be inferred entirely from external reports. For at least one of these cases, show me a result the procedure forces on me that I, as a novelist reaching for intuitive consistency, would resist, and explain why the resistance is itself informative.
