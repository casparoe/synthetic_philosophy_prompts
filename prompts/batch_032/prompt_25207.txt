I'm a journalist working on a long piece about the idea that constitutional constraints are best understood as a society's collective Ulysses contract — a way of binding our future selves against our predictable worse impulses. The analogy has always struck me as powerful but incomplete, and I've been testing it against my own reactions to a range of cases, trying to see whether my intuitions about personal precommitments carry over to the political ones, or whether the two domains come apart in ways that reveal something about the limits of the metaphor. I'd like you to help me figure out what principle, if any, is actually doing the work behind my verdicts.

Here are the cases and how I react to them. (1) Odysseus orders his crew to tie him to the mast and plug their own ears, so he can hear the sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. I find this entirely legitimate — in fact, it strikes me as a paradigm of rational self-governance. (2) A problem gambler signs a casino's self-exclusion program, authorizing the casino to deny her entry and to confiscate any winnings if she manages to get in. She later tries to enter, is turned away, and sues. I side with the casino. (3) Before a party, someone hands their car keys to a sober friend with the instruction, "Don't give these back to me no matter what I say tonight." At 1 a.m. the person demands the keys, insisting they're fine to drive. The friend refuses. I think the friend did the right thing. (4) A person with bipolar disorder, during a period of stability and after extensive consultation with their psychiatrist, signs an advance directive authorizing involuntary hospitalization and medication if specific manic symptoms appear. Months later, those symptoms appear, the directive is activated, and the person is hospitalized against their vigorously expressed will. I find this legitimate, though it makes me uneasy in a way the first three don't. (5) A national constitution includes an eternity clause — a provision declaring that certain articles (say, the federal structure of the state and the commitment to human dignity) may never be amended, not even by a unanimous supermajority of the legislature and the people. I find this illegitimate, a kind of dead-hand control that goes too far. (6) A person signs a contract agreeing to pay a friend five thousand dollars if they smoke a single cigarette in the next twelve months. They smoke, and the friend demands payment. I think they owe the money. (7) A person signs a contract agreeing to be confined to their apartment for thirty days, with electronic monitoring, if they fail to meet a professional deadline. They miss the deadline. I think this contract should not be enforced, even though it was signed freely and with full understanding. (8) A person establishes an irrevocable trust that pays out income only on the condition of sobriety, verified by random testing. They relapse, the trust suspends payments, and they sue to dissolve the trust. I side with the trust.

I keep going back and forth on what unifies the cases where I say yes and the cases where I say no. It is not simply revocability — Odysseus could not revoke his instructions in the moment, and that is precisely the point of the exercise. It is not simply whether the precommitment protects the person from themselves — case (7) arguably does that, but I reject it. It is not simply whether a third party is authorized to use force or restraint — case (4) involves that and I accept it, while case (7) involves something like it and I do not. It is not simply about the magnitude of what is at stake, since the gambler's financial ruin and the smoker's five thousand dollars seem to differ in degree but not in kind, and yet I accept both. I need a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures my pattern of verdicts. I am also prepared to be told that one or two of my verdicts have to go — that no coherent principle can accommodate all eight — and if that is the conclusion, I want to know which ones are the outliers and what the cleaned-up principle looks like. Finally, I would like the principle to be applied to a case I have not listed but that is very much alive in the literature: a constitutional provision that can be amended, but only by a process so demanding — say, a two-thirds majority in two successive parliaments followed by a referendum — that it effectively entrenches the status quo against all but the most overwhelming political movements. Is that closer to the self-exclusion agreement or to the eternity clause, and what does the answer tell us about whether the Ulysses contract analogy for constitutionalism is doing genuine normative work or is just a vivid picture?
