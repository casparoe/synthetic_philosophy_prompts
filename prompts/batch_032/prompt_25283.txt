I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, and I keep noticing a pattern in arguments that I don't have a good name for — or rather, I know several names but I'm not sure any of them carve the distinction at quite the right joint.

Here is the pattern. Two people are having what looks like a substantive disagreement. They argue back and forth, each marshaling examples and counterexamples, each apparently convinced the other is missing something. Then someone steps in and says: let's taboo the central word. Define what you mean operationally, or at least in terms we both already agree on. And when they do, one of two things happens. Sometimes the disagreement simply collapses — it turns out both sides agree on all the observable facts, all the predictions, and all the practical recommendations, and were just using the word to mean different things, or to express different attitudes toward the same state of affairs. Other times, the disagreement survives intact: even after cashing everything out in concrete terms, they still predict different outcomes, or recommend different actions, or assign different probabilities to the same events.

I've seen this play out in debates about whether large language models "understand" language, whether a particular policy is "fair," whether some mental state counts as a "belief," and — the case that really made me want to get clear on this — whether certain abstract objects "exist." In that last debate, one side says numbers exist, the other says they don't, but when you push both sides to say what observable difference this makes, they seem to agree on all the same mathematical facts, all the same facts about human cognition, all the same facts about which sentences are true. And yet both sides insist the disagreement is real and important, and neither seems to be making a simple linguistic mistake.

I know the LessWrong sequences discuss "disputes about definitions" and the technique of "tabooing words." I know philosophers have a concept of "verbal disputes." But I'm after something more precise. I want to know: is there an established framework for telling whether a dispute is merely verbal before you do the tabooing — diagnostic signs, structural features of the argument itself, that let you predict whether the disagreement will survive clarification? I've noticed, for instance, that merely-verbal disputes often involve terms whose extension is vague around the disputed cases, and that the disputants rarely make conflicting predictions about anything concrete. But I've also seen cases that had both those features and still turned out to be substantive, and cases that lacked them and turned out to be verbal.

And when a dispute does survive clarification, what exactly is left? If two people agree on every empirical prediction and every practical recommendation, can they still have a substantive disagreement, and if so, what is it about — what kind of fact are they disagreeing over? I'm looking for the best available account of where the line is and how to detect it, not just a label. If there's no settled account, I'd like to know what the leading proposals are and where they disagree with each other.
