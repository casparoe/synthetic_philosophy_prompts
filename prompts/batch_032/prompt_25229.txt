I am a novelist, and I find myself caught in a professional dilemma that I suspect has a philosophical structure I cannot quite name. My publisher has recently begun drawing on e-reader telemetry to track what they call the "reader completion ratio" — the fraction of buyers who reach the final page of a book. This number now feeds directly into decisions about marketing budgets, paperback print runs, and whether option clauses in contracts get exercised. Unsurprisingly, it has begun to reshape how many of us write. Chapters are getting shorter. Point-of-view shifts now cluster at chapter breaks to manufacture artificial cliffhangers. I have watched colleagues strip out subplots and simplify their prose because the data showed that readers who encountered, say, a passage of landscape description were measurably more likely to set the book down and never return. One writer I know removed a morally ambiguous ending — the kind that asks the reader to sit with discomfort — after his editor forwarded him a graph showing a sharp drop-off in the final fifteen pages of his previous novel, which had ended similarly.

I am not a purist. I have always believed that craft includes the discipline of holding a reader's attention, and I do not dispute that a book people finish is, all else equal, a book that succeeded at something important. But I also cannot shake the sense that optimizing for this particular number is producing books that are worse in precisely the ways that matter most to me: less ambitious, less surprising, less willing to risk losing a reader in order to earn something deeper. The problem does not seem to be merely that the metric is imperfect. It is that the act of targeting it appears to be making it steadily less meaningful as a signal of quality. I have heard colleagues say some version of "once you start writing for the completion ratio, the completion ratio stops telling you what you think it tells you," but that has the texture of folk wisdom dressed up as an observation, and I would like to know whether anything rigorous lies underneath it.

So here is my question. Is there a principled framework for thinking about when a proxy measure stops being a useful guide and starts being actively distorting — and does my situation fit it? Applied to my own case: am I being precious, and should I simply accept that the metric captures something real about reader satisfaction and adapt my craft accordingly, or is there a genuine structural problem with letting this number steer the work? If my framing is confused, or if I am smuggling in assumptions that do not hold up under scrutiny, I would rather you tell me that plainly than give me a polite answer that works within a mistaken premise.
