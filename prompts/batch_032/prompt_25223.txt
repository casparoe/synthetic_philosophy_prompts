I've been thinking about the socialist calculation debate for a few years now—not as an academic, just someone who runs a small manufacturing shop and got curious about how information actually moves through an economy. I've read the core arguments from both the Austrian side and the market-socialist side, and I've also followed more recent discussions about whether modern computation changes anything. I want to produce a document that both camps could sign: an adversarial collaboration summary.

The two positions are:

- Position A (the in-principle objection): The calculation problem is fatal to any attempt to replace competitive market prices with centralized allocation. The knowledge required for efficient allocation is distributed across millions of agents, much of it is tacit and cannot be articulated even by the people who hold it, and it is generated by the competitive process itself—it does not exist in advance to be gathered. No central model, regardless of computational power, can access knowledge that has not yet been created.

- Position B (the computational sufficiency view): The calculation problem identified a genuine difficulty but not an in-principle barrier. With modern computational capacity, machine learning on real economic data, and market-like mechanisms embedded within a socialist framework—iterative price adjustment, internal prediction markets for resource allocation, algorithmic planning using revealed-preference data—a post-capitalist economy could in principle match or exceed the allocative efficiency of markets, while avoiding markets' acknowledged failures.

I want the document to have these sections:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can agree on. These should cover: what the calculation problem actually is (stated in a way neither side would reject as a strawman); what markets do and don't accomplish; what past historical episodes (Soviet planning, wartime mobilization, the Chilean cybernetics experiment, large-firm internal allocation) do and don't demonstrate; where the burden of proof falls; and what both sides would count as a genuine test of the question rather than a confound.

2. A disagreements table. Each row states a precise sub-question in neutral wording, then gives each side's answer phrased so that a thoughtful holder of that position would accept it as fair. I want the sub-questions to go beyond the headline dispute—for example: whether prices are primarily a discovery mechanism or a computational shortcut; whether tacit knowledge can be elicited through clever mechanism design even if it can't be articulated; whether the experience of large corporations successfully allocating resources internally without internal markets is a relevant analogy; whether the calculation problem bites differently for an economy at the technological frontier versus one catching up.

3. A section on cruxes: the sub-questions that, if resolved, would cause at least one side to substantially revise its position. For each crux, describe a concrete test, argument, or case that both sides agree bears on it, and record what each side predicts. I want cruxes that are genuinely diagnostic—not just restatements of the original disagreement.

4. A brief note on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal—a matter of different definitions, different scope conditions, or different implicit assumptions about what counts as "planning" or "markets"—rather than a substantive clash that evidence could settle.

I'm not looking for a verdict on who is right. I want the document that two honest, well-informed people from these camps would produce after sitting down together and really trying to find the common ground before the remaining fault lines.
