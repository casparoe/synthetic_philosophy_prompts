I keep noticing a pattern in how people's contributions to discussions get treated, and I'm wondering if there's a name for it in philosophy. It goes like this: someone from a marginalized group describes an experience — say, a woman describing a sexist interaction at work. People accept the description: yes, that happened, we believe you. But then when she offers an analysis of what it means — why it happened, what it says about the workplace culture, what should be done — suddenly she's "too close to it" to be objective, and her analysis is treated as speculative or biased. Meanwhile, someone with no direct experience of the thing offers an analysis, and that's taken seriously as "having perspective" or "being able to see the big picture."

I've seen versions of this with disabled people describing accessibility problems (their descriptions are accepted, their proposed solutions are dismissed as not understanding the constraints), with people of color describing racist incidents (the incident is accepted, the claim that it's systemic is treated as overreach), and with patients describing symptoms to doctors (the symptoms are recorded, the patient's theory about what's wrong is ignored).

It's not quite testimonial injustice, because the testimony about the bare facts is believed. And it's not quite hermeneutical injustice, because the person has the concepts and is offering an interpretation — it's just being dismissed. It's like a division of epistemic labor where one group gets assigned the role of data provider and another group gets to be the theorist. Does this have a name? If not, what's the best way to characterize it and how does it relate to the standard categories of epistemic injustice?
