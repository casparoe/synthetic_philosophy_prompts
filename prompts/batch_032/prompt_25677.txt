I've been a reader of the alignment literature for a while now, and I keep returning to a particular picture that I find myself defending in conversations: that HCH (Humans Consulting HCH), combined with factored cognition, is a productive idealization of what aligned deliberation could look like. Not a literal engineering proposal, but a conceptual anchor — the thing we're gesturing at when we say that an AI system could do ambitious cognitive work while remaining aligned by keeping human judgment at every node of the decomposition tree.

The picture, briefly: you imagine a human who, when faced with a question too large to answer directly, can spawn copies of themselves to work on subquestions. Those copies can in turn spawn further copies. At the leaves, some human does a small, checkable piece of cognitive work. The claim is that this structure preserves alignment because every step of reasoning is ultimately answerable to human judgment — no process ever runs unattended — while the recursive decomposition allows the system as a whole to tackle problems far beyond what any single human could hold in their head. Factored cognition is the broader thesis that cognitive labor can be decomposed this way at all; HCH is one proposed architecture for doing it without losing the alignment guarantee.

I find this picture compelling as a regulative ideal. But I want to know what would count against it — not a list of objections, but a structured map of its exposure.

Specifically: what observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual clarifications should make me update away from the view that HCH-with-factored-cognition is a productive idealization for thinking about aligned deliberation? I want this sorted by decisiveness. What would be fatal — the thing that, if established, would make the whole framework collapse as a useful idealization? What would be merely awkward — problems that defenders could accommodate with adjustments to the picture? What has already been argued or demonstrated that a defender ought to concede counts against the view, even if they don't think it's decisive?

I also want to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't. If HCH is a good idealization, what should we expect to see in the structure of human deliberation, in the behavior of language models, or in the trajectory of alignment research, that we wouldn't expect otherwise? And conversely: are there things we already observe — about how humans actually reason together, about the failure modes of recursive decomposition in practice, about the behavior of current systems — that sit awkwardly with the HCH picture?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated against falsification. "It's just an idealization" can function as a motte that protects a bailey about the framework's practical relevance. Has that happened here? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it appropriate given that we're talking about an idealization rather than an empirical hypothesis? I'm not looking for a polemic — I genuinely want to know how exposed this position is, and whether its defenders have left themselves any way to be wrong.
