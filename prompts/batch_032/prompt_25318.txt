I was at a decision theory workshop last month—I cover this stuff for a magazine, so I end up at these things—and two of the attendees got into it at the bar afterward about evidential cooperation in large worlds. I was sitting nearby nursring a beer and taking mental notes, and I've been turning their argument over ever since.

The basic setup: if the universe is big enough (or if there's a multiverse), there are probably lots of agents out there who reason very similarly to you. The evidential cooperation idea says you should act as though you're cooperating with these agents, even when there's no causal connection between you, because your choice to cooperate is evidence that they'll cooperate too—since they're running roughly the same decision procedure.

Here's roughly how the argument went.

Maya (skeptical): "I just don't buy it. You can't cause something to happen by being correlated with it. If I decide to send a hundred dollars to some alien civilization in a causally disconnected part of the universe, that money leaves my account. Period. The alien doesn't get a signal from the ether telling them to send me a hundred dollars back."

David (defending the idea): "But if they're reasoning the same way you are, and they know that agents with their decision procedure tend to make choice X, then their choice is evidence about your choice. If you both follow the policy 'send the money iff I believe the other will send the money,' you both end up richer. The correlation does the work even without a causal channel."

Maya: "That's just magical thinking with math on top. The correlation does no work—it's a correlation. The money is gone. The alien never finds out. The end."

David: "Okay, but do you one-box on Newcomb's problem?"

Maya: "Yeah, I one-box. But that's different. In Newcomb's problem the predictor literally ran a simulation of me or scanned my brain or whatever. There's a causal arrow from my decision procedure to the contents of the box, through the predictor's model. In the alien case there's no model, no simulation, no causal arrow at all. It's just two people who happen to think alike."

David: "I don't think the simulation is doing the work you think it is. What matters is that the predictor's prediction is accurate—that there's a correlation between my choice and what's in the box. The mechanism that produces the correlation doesn't matter. If the alien and I are both running decision algorithms that correlate our choices, that's enough."

Maya: "So if I find out that someone in another country has the same breakfast preferences as me, should I start sending them money because our breakfast choices are correlated and maybe our generosity is too? Where does this stop?"

David: "That's not the same and you know it. The correlation has to run through the decision procedure itself. If you and the alien are both implementing the same policy—'cooperate with those who implement this policy'—then it's self-referential in a way that breakfast preferences aren't."

Maya: "But 'implementing the same policy' is doing an enormous amount of work. How do I know the alien is implementing my policy? I don't. I'm just guessing, or hoping. At least in Newcomb's problem there's an actual predictor who actually made a prediction."

David: "You don't need certainty. You just need the correlation to be strong enough that the expected value flips. And in a sufficiently large universe, even a tiny correlation across a huge number of counterpart agents makes cooperation the dominant strategy."

Maya: "Now you're just multiplying small probabilities by infinity and hoping the math works out. That's Pascal's mugging territory."

At that point someone changed the subject, but I've been wondering ever since: who's actually right here? Can you go through their exchange point by point and tell me where each of them is on solid ground and where they're mistaken? I'm especially curious whether Maya's Newcomb distinction holds up, and whether David's move to "large numbers make small correlations pay" is legitimate or a dodge.
