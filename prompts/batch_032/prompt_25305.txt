I'm trying to get clear on what standing to blame amounts to, and I've been collecting my intuitive verdicts on cases. I'd like you to help me extract the principle — or small set of principles — that best captures them, in a reflective-equilibrium spirit. If some verdicts have to go, I want to know which ones and why.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **Straightforward hypocrisy.** Alex regularly pads his expense reports by 20%. He learns that Beth, a colleague, padded one expense report by 10% and blames her for it. Verdict: Alex has no standing to blame Beth. This is the central, unambiguous case.

2. **Reformed wrongdoer.** Carla shoplifted occasionally as a teenager. She's now forty, genuinely regrets it, hasn't stolen anything in twenty years, and has made amends where she could. She blames Dave, a friend, for shoplifting from a local store. Verdict: Carla has standing. Reform and time restore it.

3. **Different-kind trivial wrong.** Elena occasionally litters in a very minor way — she drops biodegradable apple cores on hiking trails, reasoning they'll decompose. She blames Frank for littering a plastic bottle on a city street. Verdict: Elena has standing. The wrongs are different enough in kind and seriousness that her own conduct doesn't undercut her blame.

4. **Parental inconsistency.** Gabe tells his children a range of small falsehoods — Santa, the tooth fairy, "we're almost there" when they're not. He then blames his daughter for lying about whether she finished her homework. Verdict: Gabe's standing is compromised. Even though the lies differ in context and kind, the practice of expecting truthfulness while being casually dishonest with the same people undermines his standing to blame them for dishonesty.

5. **Counterfactual hypocrite.** Helen has never committed embezzlement, but only because she's never had the opportunity. She's confident she would have embezzled if she'd been in the same position of trust and temptation. She blames Ian for embezzling. Verdict: Helen lacks standing, or at least her standing is severely diminished. The counterfactual matters.

6. **Worse wrong of the same type.** Jack had a years-long extramarital affair. He blames Kate, a friend, for a single drunken one-night stand. Verdict: Jack lacks standing. Committing a worse wrong of the same general kind doesn't give you standing to blame someone for a lesser one — if anything, it makes his blame less appropriate, not more.

7. **Institutional role.** Laura is a judge who was convicted of tax fraud in her youth, served her sentence, and was later pardoned. In her role as judge, she sentences Mark for tax fraud. Verdict: Laura has standing to sentence Mark. The institutional role and the formal process of punishment differ from personal blame, and her reform plus the state's pardon restore her standing in a way that might not map perfectly onto the interpersonal cases.

8. **Impure motives.** Naomi visits her friend Owen when he's sick, but primarily because she enjoys being seen as dutiful and wants the social credit, not from genuine care. Owen recovers and later fails to visit Naomi when she's sick — he's busy and self-absorbed. Naomi blames Owen for not visiting. Verdict: I'm genuinely torn. Something feels off about Naomi's blame, but I can't tell whether it's a standing problem (her own visiting was defective in a way that undermines her standing to demand it of others) or whether she has standing but her blame is unseemly for some other reason. This is the case I most want help with.

My tentative principle is something like: **S lacks standing to blame T for φ-ing iff S is in no position to sincerely say to T, in good faith, that φ-ing is wrong, because of S's own relationship to φ-type conduct.** But this is vague in several places — "in no position," "φ-type conduct," "relationship to" — and I'm not sure it handles the reform case (2) and the counterfactual case (5) with the same mechanism. If reform erases the relationship to φ-type conduct, why doesn't never-having-φ'd-but-would-have also count as no relevant relationship? And if the counterfactual does create a relevant relationship, why doesn't it persist through reform?

What I'd like from you:

- A precise statement of the principle (or principles) that best captures my verdicts.
- For each clause of the principle, which of my cases motivates it.
- A clear statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, if any, and why I should give those up.
- The principle's verdict on case 8 (Naomi and Owen), and on at least one new case I haven't considered.
- An answer to the question: what exactly is lost when someone lacks standing to blame — a right, a permission, an authority, or something else? Does your principle commit you to one of these, or is it neutral?
