I'm a novelist running a week-long residential workshop for about a dozen other fiction writers, all mid-career, on the craft of character construction. One thread I want to weave through the week is the Buddhist critique of the self — the anatta doctrine — not as a doctrinal position to be accepted or rejected, but as a destabilizing lens that might change how they write persons. I've found that simply lecturing on the chariot argument or the five skandhas lands flat: people nod and then go back to writing characters with essences. What I want is a structured activity, about ninety minutes, that makes participants feel the failure of the self to be located anywhere in particular — the way each candidate for "the thing that I am" dissolves under pressure — before they hear any Buddhist terminology. I'd like them to walk away with the sense that the burden of proof has shifted: that positing a self is the move that needs justification, not denying one.

The activity should be a kind of guided inquiry or game, not a lecture. I'm imagining something where participants are forced to make choices about what constitutes a person's identity across a series of thought-experimental transformations — perhaps a Ship of Theseus-style gradual replacement, or a fission case, or a memory-transfer scenario — and discover that each criterion they reach for either fails to track what they care about or collapses into something they don't recognize as a self. The debrief would then map their moments of frustration onto the Buddhist arguments: the failure to find the self in the body, in sensation, in perception, in volition, in consciousness. But the mapping should come after the experience, not before.

I should add: I'm not a philosopher by training, and I may be misunderstanding the doctrine or setting up a straw man. If the activity I'm asking for rests on a confused reading of anatta — if, for instance, the Buddhist claim is subtler than "there is no self" and can't be captured by a parlor game about teletransporters — I'd rather you tell me that plainly and suggest what the activity could actually show, or whether a different philosophical tradition would serve my purposes better. Likewise, if ninety minutes is too short or the group too large, say so. I want something that works, not something that sounds clever on the syllabus.
