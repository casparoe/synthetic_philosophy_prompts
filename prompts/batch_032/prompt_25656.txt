I recently served on a jury in a case where the defendant's intentions were the central issue, and it made me revisit something I'd read about the Knobe effect years ago. Now I can't shake a worry that seems to follow from it, and I'd like help locating the flaw.

Here is the argument that troubles me:

(1) The Knobe effect shows that whether ordinary people judge an action to be intentional depends on the moral valence of its outcome: the same behavior is called intentional when it produces a bad side effect but merely foreseen when it produces a good one.

(2) The best explanation of this pattern is that the concept of intentional action itself is sensitive to moral considerations — it is not a purely factual, descriptive concept.

(3) If the concept of intentional action is morally loaded in this way, then we cannot use it as a neutral criterion for assigning blame or praise; we would be using a morally infused concept to determine moral responsibility, which looks circular.

(4) But legal and everyday practices of holding people responsible require a distinction between what someone did intentionally and what they did merely knowingly or accidentally, and this distinction is supposed to be factual and prior to moral evaluation — you first establish the facts, then you judge.

(5) So either these responsibility practices rest on a confusion, or the Knobe effect does not reveal what it seems to reveal about the concept of intentional action.

(6) The Knobe effect is robust: it appears across cultures, age groups, and experimental variations, and it resists obvious debunking explanations. It seems to reveal something genuine about how the concept works.

(7) Therefore, our practices of assigning responsibility on the basis of intentionality are circular in a way that should undermine our confidence in them.

I find (7) hard to accept, but I cannot see which step is the weak one. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
