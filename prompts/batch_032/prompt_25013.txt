i was sitting in on a sentencing hearing last week and the judge basically cut the guy's sentence in half because he showed remorse — broke down, apologized to the victim's family, said he'd been in a dark place. and it got me thinking: pretty much every legal system does some version of this, remorse gets you a lighter punishment. but what does that practice actually presuppose about what punishment is for? if you're a pure deterrence or incapacitation person, remorse only matters insofar as it predicts lower recidivism — but we don't give the same kind of discount for other things that predict lower recidivism just as well, like having a steady job lined up. so it looks like the practice is smuggling in something about desert: the remorseful person deserves less. but that's exactly what's at stake in the dennett/caruso debate, right? caruso says the whole idea of desert is incoherent once you take determinism seriously, and dennett wants to keep a compatibilist version of it. so my question is: does the remorse discount practice actually presuppose a desert-based view of punishment, or can it be justified on purely forward-looking grounds? and if it does presuppose desert, does caruso's position force him to say we should abolish remorse discounts, or is there a way for him to keep them without buying into the desert framework?
