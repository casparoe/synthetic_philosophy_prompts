I'm a novelist and something's been bugging me about how the literary world works, and I think it might have implications for what we even mean by "art." Here's the practice. When a book comes out labeled as a novel, we judge it by one set of criteria — prose, structure, whether the characters feel alive, that kind of thing. But if it later emerges that the book is heavily autobiographical — that the narrator basically is the author, what they're calling autofiction now — the whole evaluative framework shifts. Suddenly critics are asking whether the author is being honest about their own life, whether they've been fair to the real people depicted, whether the whole project is self-indulgent. Same words on the same pages. But the book's status as fiction vs. something-else determines what counts as a relevant criticism.

And it's not just reviewers. Prize committees do this explicitly: the Booker and the National Book Award have seperate fiction and nonfiction categories, and a book's eligibility can turn entirely on how it's classified, even when the text itself is ambiguous. A book can be disqualified from the fiction prize because it's "really" memoir — not because of anything in the prose, but because of facts about the author's life.

What theory of art does this practice presuppose? It seems like it presupposes that the author's relationship to the material is part of the work — that you can't evaluate the text purely as a text, that the act of imagination (or lack of it) matters to the artistic status of the thing. But then what do we do with the fact that basically all novels draw from life to some degree? Is there a principled line, or is the practice just tracking something sociological?

And here's the comparison that really throws me: in the visual arts, nobody cares if a painter works from photographs or from life. We don't say "this painting is just autobiography with the faces changed." In fact, in conceptual art, the artist's intention and relationship to the object is often treated as partly constitutive of the work — but in a way that expands what counts as art, not in a way that demotes it to "mere" memoir. So the literary world seems to have it backwards relative to the art world.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of art that makes this practice coherent. I want to know: what does the practice strictly presuppose (as opposed to what it merely fits well with), whether its commitments are consistent across the fiction/nonfiction boundary and across the literature/visual-art comparison, and what would have to change in how we talk about books if we replaced it with a view on which fiction and autobiography are continuous rather than separate kinds — where the only question is whether the writing is good, period.
