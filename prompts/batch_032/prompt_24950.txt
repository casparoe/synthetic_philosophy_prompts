I am teaching an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and the midterm take-home asked students to engage closely with Russell's "On Denoting." The question was designed to test whether they can distinguish what the 1905 paper actually accomplishes from what Russell later built on top of it — a distinction that separates students who have read carefully from those who have absorbed a smoothed-over textbook synthesis. Here is the question I set:

**Question:** In "On Denoting" (1905), Russell argues that definite descriptions are incomplete symbols that disappear under logical analysis, and he deploys this analysis to solve several puzzles, including the problem of non-referring descriptions like "the present King of France." Suppose we now ask whether the same analysis extends naturally to empty proper names — names like "Vulcan" or "Santa Claus" that fail to refer to anything. Does the machinery of "On Denoting" already contain what is needed to handle such names, or does the extension require additional claims that Russell only developed in his later work? Explain, paying close attention to the scope distinction (primary vs. secondary occurrence) and its role in the theory.

**Student answer:**

Russell's theory of descriptions analyzes a sentence of the form "The F is G" as asserting that there exists exactly one thing that is F, and that thing is G. Formally: ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x) ∧ Gx). This means that when a definite description fails to refer — as with "the present King of France" — any atomic sentence containing it comes out false, because the existence clause is not satisfied. This was Russell's solution to the problem of how we can meaningfully talk about non-existent things without invoking Meinongian subsistence.

The same machinery extends straightforwardly to empty proper names. Russell held that ordinary proper names are really disguised definite descriptions. So "Vulcan" might be analyzed as something like "the planet orbiting between Mercury and the Sun that causes the observed perturbations in Mercury's orbit." Since there is no such planet, sentences like "Vulcan is a planet" come out false on Russell's analysis, exactly as they do for non-referring definite descriptions. This shows that the theory of descriptions, as presented in "On Denoting," already contains everything needed to handle empty names — no additional machinery is required.

The scope distinction is crucial here. When a definite description appears in a negated sentence, we must decide whether the description takes wide scope (primary occurrence) or narrow scope (secondary occurrence). For "The present King of France is not bald," the wide-scope reading asserts of the present King of France that he is not bald — false, since there is no such king. The narrow-scope reading denies that there exists a unique present King of France who is bald — true. The same ambiguity arises with empty names: "Vulcan is not a planet" can be read with wide scope (asserting of Vulcan that it is not a planet, which is false) or with narrow scope (denying that there is a unique perturbation-causing planet that is a planet, which is true). Russell's view is that the narrow-scope reading is the correct logical form in all such cases involving non-referring terms.

One might object that this makes every positive sentence containing an empty name false, which seems too sweeping. But Russell can handle apparent counterexamples. "Vulcan does not exist" looks true, not false. On the analysis, "Vulcan exists" unpacks as "There exists a unique perturbation-causing planet," which is false, and its negation "Vulcan does not exist" is therefore true on the narrow-scope reading. So the theory handles empty names smoothly without any need for Russell's later distinction between logically proper names and ordinary proper names — that distinction is a separate development motivated by epistemological concerns about acquaintance, not by any gap in the theory of descriptions itself.
