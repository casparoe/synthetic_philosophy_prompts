I have been trying to work out what I think about group decision procedures — specifically, when a group should vote on the sub-questions that determine an outcome rather than voting on the outcome directly. My reactions to a series of cases do not seem to fit any principle I can state, and I would like your help finding one, or learning which of my verdicts I should abandon.

Here are the cases:

1. A three-judge appellate panel must rule on a contract dispute. Liability requires both that a valid contract existed and that it was breached. The panel votes directly on the ultimate question: liable or not liable. Judge A votes liable; Judges B and C vote not liable. The court finds no liability, 2–1. Verdict: unproblematic.

2. Same panel, same legal question, but this time each judge votes separately on the two premises, and the court's conclusion is determined by whether each premise commands a majority. The judges' views are: Judge A — contract yes, breach yes (so would vote liable); Judge B — contract yes, breach no (so would vote not liable); Judge C — contract no, breach yes (so would vote not liable). On the premise votes, "contract existed" passes 2–1 (A and B), and "breach occurred" passes 2–1 (A and C). The court therefore finds both premises true and enters a judgment of liability. But Judges B and C, a majority of the panel, would each vote not liable if asked directly. The procedure has produced an outcome that a majority of the panel rejects. Verdict: this procedure is defective. A group decision procedure should not generate a conclusion that a majority of the group would reject if voting on the conclusion directly.

3. A five-member university tenure committee must decide on a candidate. The rules require a majority finding of excellence in each of research, teaching, and service; the candidate receives tenure only if all three thresholds are met. The committee votes on each area separately. The votes come out: research 4–1, teaching 3–2, service 3–2. The candidate receives tenure. Verdict: unproblematic. This is premise-by-premise voting, yet I feel none of the unease I felt in case 2.

4. A jury in a criminal trial must decide guilt, which requires proof beyond reasonable doubt of both actus reus and mens rea. The judge instructs the jury to deliberate on each element separately and to convict only if they find both elements proven. The jury finds actus reus proven 10–2 and mens rea proven 10–2, and returns a guilty verdict. Verdict: unproblematic — indeed, this seems like the right way for a jury to proceed. Structurally it resembles case 2, but I cannot bring myself to condemn it.

5. A three-member ethics committee must decide whether to approve a study. The rules require majority approval on each of three criteria — minimal risk, valid consent, and scientific merit — and all three must pass. The members vote as follows:
- Member 1: risk yes, consent yes, merit no → would vote to reject
- Member 2: risk yes, consent no, merit yes → would vote to reject
- Member 3: risk no, consent yes, merit yes → would vote to reject
On the premise votes, risk passes 2–1, consent passes 2–1, and merit passes 2–1. The study is approved. But every single member would vote to reject the study if voting on the conclusion directly. The procedure has produced an outcome that is unanimously opposed. Verdict: this is even worse than case 2. The procedure is clearly defective — indeed, absurd.

6. A five-member appointments committee must decide whether to hire a candidate. The rules require a majority on each of two criteria: qualifications and interview performance. The committee uses premise-by-premise voting. The votes are:
- Members 1 and 2: qualifications yes, interview yes → would vote to hire
- Member 3: qualifications yes, interview no → would vote not to hire
- Members 4 and 5: qualifications no, interview yes → would vote not to hire
Qualifications pass 3–2; interview passes 4–1. The candidate is hired. But Members 3, 4, and 5 — a majority — would each vote not to hire if asked directly. Verdict: this bothers me, and it seems structurally identical to case 2. Yet my reaction is slightly milder here than in case 2, and I am not sure why. Perhaps because the two premises feel more like genuinely separate judgments — qualifications versus interview performance — than the two premises in the contract case, which both feed into a single legal conclusion in a tighter way. But I am not confident this distinction holds up.

What I am after is a principle that tells me when premise-by-premise voting is acceptable and when it is not — one that explains why I accept it for the tenure committee and the jury, reject it for the appellate panel and the ethics committee, and feel an intermediate unease about the appointments committee. Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts? If not, which should I revise, and what does the best available principle predict about a case I have not considered: a three-person committee choosing a conference venue, where the choice is determined by majority votes on affordability, accessibility, and capacity, and the members' preferences are such that the premise-by-premise procedure selects a venue that no single member actually wants?
