I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting an essay on how fiction handles a problem that moral philosophy has lately made explicit: the question of who has standing to blame. The philosophical idea, roughly, is that a moral judgment can be correct on its merits and yet the person voicing it can lack the standing to do so — because of hypocrisy, complicity in the very wrong they condemn, or a failure to attend to their own comparable faults. I want to read _The Scarlet Letter_ through this lens, because Hawthorne's novel seems to have been built around exactly that problem a century and a half before philosophers gave it a name.

I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously, and that is faithful in both directions — not reducing the novel to an illustration and not distorting the philosophy to fit the fiction. Specifically:

Where does Hawthorne dramatize something the standing-to-blame literature gets right? Dimmesdale condemns sin from the pulpit while concealing his own; his moral claims about adultery are not false, but his act of publicly judging Hester seems objectionable in a different register. Does the novel show that this is a distinct kind of moral failure, one that the philosophical distinction between the truth of a judgment and the standing to make it captures well?

Where does the novel complicate the philosophical picture? Dimmesdale is not simply a smug hypocrite; he loathes himself, scourges himself in private, and his sermons grow more anguished and powerful precisely because of his hidden guilt. Does the novel suggest that private self-punishment can restore standing, or does it treat that as another form of evasion? And what about Chillingworth: he is not a hypocrite in the straightforward sense — he does not share Hester's sin — but his blame is clearly morally deformed by coldness, obsession, and a usurped authority. Does Hawthorne imply that standing to blame requires something beyond non-hypocrisy, something like a proper motive or a relationship to the blamed person that Chillingworth has forfeited?

Where does the novel resist the philosophical framework? The Puritans' blame of Hester is collective and institutional, expressed through law, the scaffold, the crowd, and the letter itself. The standing-to-blame literature tends to model the problem in individualistic terms: one person blaming another. Does Hawthorne show us something about social and institutional blame that the philosophical models miss — for instance, that a community can collectively lack standing even when no individual member is a straightforward hypocrite, because the community's moral authority is itself the thing in question?

And finally, what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot? I suspect it has something to do with how blame feels from the inside — Hester's silent knowledge of who else should be on that scaffold, the way the letter reshapes her perception of every person who looks at her, the strange intimacy between the blamed and those who blame while knowing their own hidden fault. If the philosophy gives us the structure, the novel gives us the experience of living inside that structure, and I want to know whether that experience challenges the structure itself.

I know the novel and I know the philosophical terrain. What I need is a reading that moves between them honestly, saying where they illuminate each other, where they pull apart, and what the novelist knew that the philosophers are still catching up to.
