I've been teaching a course on philosophy of law for a few years now, and every time we hit the unit on punishment I end up with the same uneasy feeling. The debate between retributivists and consequentialists about the justification of punishment has been running since at least Bentham and Kant took their positions, and the basic moves haven't changed much in my lifetime. Retributivists say that punishment is justified because wrongdoers deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrongdoing; consequentialists say that punishment is justified only insofar as it produces better outcomes — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation. Each side has stock objections to the other: retributivists point out that pure consequentialism would permit punishing the innocent if it produced good enough results, while consequentialists point out that retributivism seems to endorse inflicting suffering for no further gain, which strikes them as pointless cruelty. Both sides have developed more sophisticated versions — retributivists now often speak in terms of censure or communication rather than bare suffering, and consequentialists have built in side-constraints to rule out punishing the innocent — but the refined versions still talk past each other, and nobody seems to move.

I have a working hypothesis, but I'd like it tested by someone who knows the literature better than I do. My suspicion is that this isn't really a disagreement about facts or about the logical structure of justification, but about what kind of thing would count as a justification of punishment in the first place. The retributivist wants an answer to the question "Why does this person deserve this?" and won't accept an answer that only talks about future benefits to society. The consequentialist wants an answer to "What good does this do?" and won't accept an answer that treats suffering as intrinsically appropriate. If that's right, then the two sides are answering different questions under the same heading, and the impasse is structural — not something more evidence or cleverer arguments could resolve. But I'm not sure. Maybe there's a hidden empirical question (about, say, whether human beings actually have the kind of free will that desert requires), or maybe the disagreement is really about which of two competing intuitions to privilege when they conflict in edge cases, and that's a different kind of disagreement entirely.

What I'd like from you is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey of the positions or a verdict on who is right. And given your diagnosis, I want to know what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a change of question — or whether we should expect the impasse to be permanent and why.
