Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about what Lewis's triviality results actually show about indicative conditionals. One of them thinks the results force us to give up the idea that indicative conditionals have truth conditions at all — "if A then B" doesn't describe a state of the world, it's just a device for expressing that the conditional probability of B given A is high. The other thinks this is an overreaction: conditionals do have truth conditions, and the triviality proofs just show we need to be more careful about which background assumptions we're willing to drop.

Make it a real back-and-forth where they actually respond to each other's points, not alternating monologues. Both should be sharp and well-read — neither is a strawman. They should push on specific technical issues: whether the Ramsey thesis is supposed to survive conditionalization, whether the no-truth-value view can handle embeddings (negations and disjunctions of conditionals), whether context-sensitivity can block the proofs without being ad hoc, and what the real costs of each position are. But keep it readable — someone who knows the basics of the debate should be able to follow without drowning in notation.

Start with the dialogue itself — no preface, no commentary, no character introductions before the first line of speech.
