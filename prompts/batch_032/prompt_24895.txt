I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and I have noticed that the slogan "the mind extends into the world" — or, in its folksier register, "cognition ain't all in the head" — gets deployed with the casual confidence of a settled result. But the argumentative work it is asked to do varies wildly from one deployment to the next. I have seen it used to underwrite at least four distinct claims:

1. That my notes app is literally part of my memory system in the same sense that my hippocampus is.
2. That an LLM retrieving from a vector database is engaged in something we should call "thinking."
3. That deleting someone's external hard drive constitutes a harm of the same kind as a concussion.
4. That the individual mind is a fiction and we should treat coupled systems — person-plus-tool, person-plus-other-person — as the real unit of cognitive analysis.

I want to know what the slogan actually amounts to, stripped of its rhetorical force. What are the distinct precise theses that "the mind extends into the world" could be expressing, ordered from the weakest (nearest to a platitude) to the strongest (most radical or least plausible)? For each thesis: is it trivially true, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or something that is almost certainly false? And then: which of the four arguments above actually gets the reading it needs, and does that reading survive scrutiny?
