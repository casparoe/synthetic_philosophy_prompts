So I'm taking this course on conditionals and we've been going through Lewis's triviality proofs, and I'm trying to write an essay about whether the triviality results actually force us to give up truth conditions for indicative conditionals, or whether there's a way to keep truth conditions if we're willing to give up something else. I've got some rough notes slash ideas and I'd love comments — like, does this argument actually work? Am I missing something obvious? Is this even an interesting angle or has it been done to death?

Here's what I've got so far.

The standard story is: Lewis showed that if you assume indicative conditionals have truth conditions (i.e., they express propositions that are true or false at worlds), and if you assume that the probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent — the Ramsey test slash Adams's thesis — then you get triviality. Basically all probabilities collapse to 0 or 1, or at least the conditional probability function gets forced to be trivial in some way.

But there are a few different triviality results and they make different assumptions. The first one (the 1976 paper) assumes that the conditional "If A then B" expresses the same proposition across different probability functions. If you deny that — say, the conditional is context-dependent or expresses different propositions relative to different credal states — then the first proof doesn't go through. That seems like a plausible move to me.

The second proof doesn't assume that though. It just uses the law of total probability and the assumption that P(If A then B | C) = P(B | A ∧ C) for all C. That one seems harder to dodge.

But here's my thought: what if we deny that the conditional operator is a binary propositional connective at all? Like, what if "If A then B" doesn't express a proposition that's a function of the propositions expressed by A and B individually? Maybe it expresses a proposition, but one that depends on more than just the truth values of A and B at each world — maybe it depends on something like similarity relations between worlds, or causal structure, or something else that isn't captured by a truth table. The thing is, Lewis's proofs don't seem to assume truth-functionality — they just assume that there IS some proposition expressed, and that the probability of that proposition equals the conditional probability. So even if the conditional isn't truth-functional, the triviality still goes through as long as it expresses a proposition.

Unless... I'm wondering if there's a way to deny that conditionals express propositions at all while still being "truth-conditional" in some broader sense. Like, maybe conditionals express something like a restriction operation on the context, rather than a proposition — that's the dynamic semantics move. But then you're not really saving truth conditions, you're going dynamic, which feels like giving up on the thing I wanted to save.

Another angle I'm thinking about: what if we deny that probability is the right way to think about the acceptability of conditionals? Like, maybe Adams's thesis is just wrong, and the fact that we often judge P(If A then B) ≈ P(B|A) is a pragmatic phenomenon, not a semantic one. But then we'd need some other story about why the triviality results feel so compelling — like, why does it seem so natural that the probability of a conditional should equal the conditional probability? If we just say "it's a cognitive illusion" that feels like a cop-out.

I guess my main question is: is there any way to hold ALL of the following simultaneously:
1. Indicative conditionals have truth conditions (express propositions)
2. The probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability
3. Probabilities aren't trivial

And if not, which one should we give up, and why? I'm leaning toward giving up (2) and saying Adams's thesis is a useful heuristic but not literally true, but I'm not sure I can motivate that without it feeling ad hoc.
