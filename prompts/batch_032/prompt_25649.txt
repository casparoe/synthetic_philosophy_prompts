I'm taking a graduate seminar on distributive ethics, and we've spent the last two weeks on Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" We've worked through the standard cases — the scarce drug that can go to one patient or five, the cave rescue with the rising tide — and the familiar fault lines are clear enough. But I'm interested in a particular structural feature of Taurek's view that I think hasn't been stressed enough in our discussions: his coin-flip proposal treats all claims as equal in strength regardless of how those claims arose. I want to probe whether that egalitarianism about claims survives contact with cases where the parties are not equally situated with respect to how they came to need rescuing — not because of any prior relationship to the rescuer, but because of their own autonomous choices.

Your task: construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications.

1. It must be a rescue scenario in the spirit of Taurek's own examples — realistic, no trolleys, no science fiction, no perfect predictors. One paragraph maximum for the scenario itself.

2. In the scenario, there are two groups that cannot both be saved. The larger group has come to need rescue through a course of action they knowingly and autonomously chose, having been adequately warned of the risk. The smaller group needs rescue through no fault or choice of their own — they are in their predicament through sheer bad luck.

3. The "save the greater number" principle must give an intuitively wrong answer in this case: it would tell us to save the larger group, but that feels like the wrong thing to do.

4. Taurek's own coin-flip proposal must also give an intuitively wrong answer: it would give each group a 50% chance, but the smaller group seems entitled to more than a coin-flip.

5. The intuitively correct answer should be to save the smaller group with certainty, or at least with a probability substantially greater than 50%.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following:

- Walk through the reasoning of each view — save the greater number, Taurek's coin-flip, and the view that says save the smaller group — and state clearly what verdict each delivers and why.
- Identify the specific feature of the case that is doing the work to drive these verdicts apart. Is it the differential assumption of risk? The fact that the larger group's predicament is self-imposed? Something else?
- Explain what happens if you remove that feature: does the case collapse back into a standard Taurek scenario where the coin-flip is more plausible, or does something else happen?
- Finally, give the strongest response a defender of Taurek could make against this case. Would Taurek bite the bullet and say we should still flip a coin? Would he deny that autonomous choice affects the strength of a claim to be rescued? How would that response fare?
