A friend of mine who's into philosophy sent me this summary of the Dennett–Caruso debate after listening to a podcast about it. I don't know the debate well enough to judge it, but I'm supposed to write a paper touching on punishment and free will, so I'd like to know what I can trust here. Here's what he wrote:

"So the debate is basically this: Caruso is a hard determinist — he thinks everything we do is caused by factors outside our control, so nobody has free will, and therefore retributive punishment is never justified. His positive proposal is that we should stop punishing people entirely and instead treat criminal behavior as a public health problem, like a disease. Dennett, on the other hand, is a compatibilist who thinks we do have free will in the sense that matters, so he defends retributive punishment: people who do bad things deserve to suffer for it, and that's why we lock them up. The core disagreement between them is really about whether determinism is true — if it is, Caruso wins; if it isn't, Dennett wins. They also disagree about what would actually happen if we adopted Caruso's view: Dennett thinks it would be a disaster because dangerous people would just walk free, while Caruso thinks a public health approach would be more humane and effective."

I can tell this is probably oversimplified, but I'm not sure where exactly it goes wrong. Can you go through it and tell me which parts a careful defender of each position would accept, which are misrepresentations, and whether the framing of the core disagreement (determinism true → Caruso wins) even captures what they're actually arguing about?
