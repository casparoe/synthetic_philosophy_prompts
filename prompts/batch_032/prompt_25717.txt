I'm giving a talk next month at a mid-sized philosophy department's colloquium — the faculty is mostly philosophers of mind and epistemologists, but there are also two social psychologists who work on motivated reasoning and one clinical psychologist who studies addiction and denial. My thesis, in a sentence, is that self-deception as it's standardly understood in the philosophical literature is strictly impossible: you cannot simultaneously believe P and believe not-P, and you cannot intentionally cause yourself to believe something you know to be false. What we call self-deception is always better explained by some other mechanism — biased evidence-gathering, selective attention, willful ignorance, akrasia-adjacent phenomena, or plain old hypocrisy — none of which require the paradoxical state that the term "self-deception" seems to name.

I know this is a minority view (though not a fringe one — I'm drawing on Mele's deflationary account and some of the skeptical arguments going back to Sartre and Canfield), and I expect the room to push back hard. The epistemologists will worry about what happens to doxastic voluntarism; the philosophers of mind will want to know what's left of folk psychology if I'm right; and the psychologists will have cases — clinical denial, the spouse who "doesn't see" the affair, the student who "knows" they'll fail but "believes" they'll pass — that they think my account can't handle.
