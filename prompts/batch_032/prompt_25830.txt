Here's the question I gave:

"Newcomb's problem presents a conflict between two seemingly compelling principles of rational choice: the dominance principle and the principle of maximizing expected utility. Explain the problem, lay out both arguments, and then assess which one you think carries the day. Be precise about what each principle says and why it applies (or fails to apply) in this case."

And here's the student's answer:

"Newcomb's problem involves a superintelligent Predictor who presents you with two boxes: one transparent, containing $1,000, and one opaque, which contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. You can choose either to take both boxes (two-boxing) or to take only the opaque box (one-boxing). The Predictor has already made its prediction and placed the money accordingly: if it predicted you'd one-box, the opaque box contains $1,000,000; if it predicted you'd two-box, it contains nothing. The Predictor is extremely accurate—say, 99%—and you know this.

The dominance argument says: whatever the Predictor has done, you're better off taking both boxes. If the opaque box is empty, two-boxing gets you $1,000 instead of $0. If it's full, two-boxing gets you $1,001,000 instead of $1,000,000. So two-boxing dominates one-boxing, and since dominance is a bedrock principle of rational choice, you should two-box.

The expected utility argument says: given the Predictor's accuracy, if you one-box you almost certainly get $1,000,000, whereas if you two-box you almost certainly get only $1,000. The expected utility of one-boxing is roughly $990,000 (0.99 × $1,000,000 + 0.01 × $0), while the expected utility of two-boxing is roughly $11,000 (0.01 × $1,001,000 + 0.99 × $1,000). Since maximizing expected utility is what it means to be rational under uncertainty, you should one-box.

The conflict arises because these two principles pull in opposite directions. The dominance principle says the choice is easy because one act is better no matter what. But the expected utility calculation says the states aren't independent of your choice—your decision provides evidence about which state obtains.

I think the expected utility argument carries the day, but not for the reason most people give. The real issue isn't about evidential vs. causal decision theory. It's that the dominance principle only applies when the states of the world are causally independent of your choice. In Newcomb's problem, they aren't: the Predictor's prediction is causally downstream of whatever factors determine your choice. So the dominance argument rests on a misapplication—you can't treat 'the opaque box is full' and 'the opaque box is empty' as states that are unaffected by your decision. This is essentially the same point Lewis makes when he says that Newcomb's problem is just a standard case of decision under risk once you properly specify the states. The correct states aren't 'box full' and 'box empty' but rather something like 'I am the kind of person who one-boxes' and 'I am the kind of person who two-boxes,' and relative to those states the dominance argument doesn't get off the ground.

One objection to this is that the money is already in the box, so your choice can't causally affect it. But this misses the point: the Predictor's accuracy means your choice is evidence about what's in the box, and rational choice should be guided by what your choice tells you about the world, not just by what it causally changes. This is why evidential decision theory gets the right answer here, and why Newcomb's problem is often taken as a counterexample to causal decision theory."
