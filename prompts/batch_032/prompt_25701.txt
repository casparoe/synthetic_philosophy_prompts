I'm a high school junior, and over the past few months I've been working through some introductory ethics material on my own — mostly SEP articles and a handful of lecture recordings. One thing I can't stop turning over is the paradox of deontology. The way I understand it: deontological ethics says there are agent-relative constraints — for instance, a prohibition against killing an innocent person — that hold even when violating the constraint would prevent many more violations of that very same constraint by others. You can't kill one to stop five murders. But if the underlying point of the prohibition is that killings are bad, then the deontologist seems to care more about who does the killing than about how many killings there are. That looks less like a principled moral stance and more like an obsession with keeping one's own hands clean — as though the real object of concern isn't the victim but the agent's purity. I've seen this called a paradox because the constraint appears to defeat its own rationale: the very thing that makes the rule worth having is the thing you sacrifice by following it in hard cases. Some philosophers apparently think this is a serious problem for deontology; others argue that once you properly understand agent-relative reasons — or the distinction between doing and allowing, or the idea that moral rules aren't justified by their consequences in the first place — the paradox dissolves.

I'm trying to get a handle on how damaging this objection actually is, and I'd like your honest credence rather than a survey of the literature. Please give me a number between 0 and 1 for the claim: the paradox of deontology is fatal to deontological ethics. By "fatal," I mean that a reasonable person who fully grasps the paradox should, on that basis alone, reduce their credence in deontology below 0.5 or abandon it outright.
