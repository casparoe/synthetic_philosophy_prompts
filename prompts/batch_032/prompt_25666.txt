I teach a philosophy of biology course, and each year we spend two weeks on definitions of life. Before I assign the standard papers again, I want to give my students a clear statement of what any adequate definition must accomplish — the desiderata. Please write the list.

What must a definition of life get right? Include the cases it must classify correctly (or explain why they resist classification), the distinctions it must not collapse (e.g., living versus capable-of-living, individual organism versus collective, alive versus once-alive), and results from biology it cannot contradict. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag which are non-negotiable and which reflect commitments a theorist could reasonably abandon.

Then, briefly, score the main approaches — metabolic, evolutionary, information-based, and any others you take to be serious contenders — against the list. I am most interested in which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular candidates, and whether any pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, which would make the list itself a result.
