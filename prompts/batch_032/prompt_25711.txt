Suppose you are advising a private foundation that can fund three research groups for five years each, specifically on the problem of probability in the many-worlds interpretation — the Born rule problem. The foundation's board understands the landscape but wants a defended prioritization: which approaches are most likely to yield a solution that satisfies not just committed Everettians but also skeptics who regard the Born rule problem as the deepest objection to many-worlds?

The main approaches currently on the table include:

(a) Decision-theoretic derivations (the Deutsch–Wallace program), which aim to show that a rational agent who knows the branching structure of the wavefunction should, on pain of incoherence or diachronic inconsistency, weight branches by the Born rule in decision-making.

(b) Self-locating uncertainty approaches (Vaidman's ignorance-based treatment; Sebens and Carroll's epistemic separability principle), which treat branching as generating genuine uncertainty about which branch one occupies and then derive the Born rule from constraints on self-locating credences.

(c) Envariance-based derivations (Zurek), which derive the Born rule from symmetry properties of entangled quantum states under unitary transformations, without appeal to decision theory or self-locating belief.

(d) Primitivist or quietist approaches, which deny that the problem needs solving: branch weights are fundamental, or the demand for a derivation rests on a confusion about what a physical theory must provide.

(e) Typicality and density-matrix approaches, which argue that the Born rule emerges from considerations about the typicality of branches in Hilbert space or from the properties of reduced density matrices in decohering systems.

Your task: rank these (or variants you find more promising) in the order the foundation should fund them, and defend the ranking. For each approach in your top three, address:

- What would count as genuine progress in a five-year window — a specific theorem, a conceptual clarification, a decisive response to a standing objection?
- What is the hardest obstacle internal to that approach — the thing that might make it fail even with adequate resources?
- Which other approach's success would render this one obsolete or less interesting, and why?

In defending your ranking, make your criteria explicit. I am particularly interested in whether you think the measure of success should be "produces a formal result that recovers the Born rule" or "dissolves the philosophical puzzlement that makes the Born rule seem problematic in the first place" — since these can come apart. If you think the foundation should fund fewer than three approaches because the field is better served by concentration, say so and explain why.
