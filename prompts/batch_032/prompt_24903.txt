Construct a thought experiment in which a trait has high heritability within each of two populations, the populations differ substantially in their mean on the trait, and the between-population difference is entirely environmental in origin. Constraints: the trait should be one an educated layperson might care about (a health or cognitive outcome, not a crop yield); no twins or adoption studies; one paragraph; and — this is the hard part — the environmental factor that drives the between-population difference must also contribute nontrivially to the within-population variance. The point is to block the easy reply that says "heritability is population-specific, so of course the between-population difference could be environmental." The case should instead force someone who equates high heritability with genetic determination of group differences to locate exactly where their reasoning fails, even after granting that heritability estimates apply within each population.

After the scenario: (a) explain why heritability is high in each population, (b) explain why the between-population difference is nonetheless environmental, (c) identify which structural feature of the scenario does the work of trapping the inference from within-group heritability to between-group genetic causation, and (d) explain what would happen to the case if the environmental factor affected only the between-population mean and not the within-population variance — would the case still work as a counterexample, and if so, would it be as forceful?
