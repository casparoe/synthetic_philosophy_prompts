I'm working on a piece about the psychology and philosophy of forgery — specifically, about why we care that an object is "the real thing" even when a copy is perceptually indistinguishable. The literature offers at least two broad families of explanation. One is what I'll call the causal-contact view: we value the original because it stands in the right kind of causal chain leading back to the maker's hands; the object carries a kind of historical residue, even if we cannot see it. The other is what I'll call the social-recognition view: we value the original because it is the object that the relevant community has singled out as the bearer of the achievement — the one that was actually produced in the act of creation we honor, regardless of whether it has any special intrinsic or causal properties that a copy lacks.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment that isolates these two explanations and makes the causal-contact view look hollow while leaving the social-recognition view intact. The specifications:

- The copy must be genuinely perceptually identical to the original — no hidden watermarks, no degradation, no appeal to the idea that "if you looked closely enough you could tell." Stipulate a perfect duplicate.
- The scenario should not involve fine art, high-end collectibles, or anything whose value is driven by resale markets. Use an ordinary object that a normal person might care about owning the original of.
- The original and the copy must be equally good at performing whatever function the object has. No appeal to the idea that the original works better than the copy.
- After presenting the scenario, walk through what each explanation says about why — or whether — someone should prefer the original, and show that the causal-contact story rings hollow while the social-recognition story does not.
- Identify which feature of the case does the work of prying the two explanations apart, and say what would happen if that feature were removed.
- Finally, include a note on how confident you are in the key claims your analysis rests on, and single out the point you are least sure of — for instance, whether the case might inadvertently rely on a contested reading of what the causal-contact view requires, or whether a defender of that view has a plausible reply you have not fully addressed.
