I am a retired physicist—I spent four decades designing experiments, fitting models, and learning the hard way which Bayesian procedures survive contact with real data—and in my retirement I have turned to formal epistemology. Next month I present a paper at a workshop on probability and conditionals. My thesis is that the correct solution to the Judy Benjamin problem is the minimum cross-entropy update, but not for the reasons usually given. The standard defense appeals to minimizing information gain in the abstract, which strikes many epistemologists as an ad hoc information-theoretic kludge with no proper epistemic motivation. My argument is different: I claim that in any real physical setting where a conditional message of the Judy Benjamin type could arrive, the MRE update is the unique procedure that commutes with the symmetries of the underlying stochastic process—symmetries that any rational updater must respect unless they can point to a specific physical feature that breaks them. In short, the MRE solution is not an information-theoretic curiosity; it is forced by the structure of the world.

The audience will be roughly fifteen people: eight formal epistemologists, most of whom favor either Adams conditioning or some variant of imaging and regard MRE with suspicion; three Bayesian statisticians who have extensive practical experience updating on partial information but have likely never encountered the Judy Benjamin problem by name; two decision theorists who will want to know whether my symmetry argument carries over to decision contexts; one philosopher of physics who will be my most sympathetic listener but also the one best positioned to find a hole in the symmetry claim; and my host, a senior epistemologist who thinks the whole Judy Benjamin literature is a detour from more important questions about conditionals.
