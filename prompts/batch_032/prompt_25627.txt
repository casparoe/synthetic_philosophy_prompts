As a journalist covering cognitive science and philosophy of mind, I keep running into a pattern that I don't have a clean label for, and I'd like your help carving it out precisely.

The pattern is this. When people — including neuroscientists, when they speak off-guard — describe conscious experience, they almost invariably reach for a spatial metaphor. There is a place where "I" am, and sensory inputs are brought to that place. Visual experience feels like watching a screen; auditory experience feels like listening to a stream played for me somewhere inside. When I decide to raise my hand, it feels as though the decision happens in that same place and then gets sent outward. The metaphor is so natural that it structures experimental design: researchers look for the brain region where information is "integrated" or "bound together" and "enters awareness," as if awareness were a room with a threshold. I have noticed, though, that a number of philosophers and some neuroscientists treat this picture as deeply misleading — not just a harmless façon de parler but a conceptual error that distorts research. They seem to argue that there is no such place, no inner observer, no finish line where unconscious processing becomes conscious. Instead, they offer a picture in which multiple streams of processing compete, and what we call consciousness is not a presentation to an audience but something more like a narrative under constant revision, with no single draft counting as the final one.

Here is what I want to know, and I want it in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. Does the spatial-metaphor picture I described have an established name in the literature, and what exactly does that term denote — what are its essential features as the literature defines them?
2. What is the name of the competing, no-single-audience picture, and what is its most precise formulation — the one its defenders would accept as fair?
3. Where exactly does the disagreement lie: is it about the phenomenology (what experience actually feels like from the inside), about the neural implementation (what the brain is doing), or about the conceptual framework we should use to talk about either?
4. What is the strongest argument that the spatial-metaphor picture is not merely a metaphor but captures something real that the competing picture cannot account for?
5. Are there versions of the spatial picture that survive the standard critiques — for example, ones that drop the idea of a literal place but keep the idea of a functional role — and do those versions have names of their own?
