I'm giving the best-man toast at the wedding of two close friends. They met in law school, both now teach constitutional law at different universities, and their first real conversation — the one they still tell people about — was an argument about Jon Elster's *Ulysses and the Sirens*. Specifically, whether constitutional precommitment is genuinely analogous to Ulysses binding himself to the mast, given that constitutions are drafted by some people to bind other people, not by a single agent binding his own future self. They disagreed fiercely that night and have been disagreeing productively ever since.

The wedding will have about a hundred guests. Roughly a third are legal academics who know the precommitment literature cold and will catch every allusion; another third are family members who have never heard of Ulysses outside of high-school mythology; the rest fall somewhere in between. I need a toast — maybe four or five minutes spoken — that uses the Ulysses-contract idea as its central metaphor for marriage without becoming a seminar paper. The toast should acknowledge the genuine philosophical problem (if you bind yourself against your future self, who is really sovereign — the earlier self who tied the knots or the later self who has to live inside them?) but land on something warm and true about what these two particular people are doing today. I do not want it to sound as though I am papering over the difficulty; I want the difficulty to be part of why the commitment matters. The families should feel included, not lectured at, and the colleagues should feel the idea was handled with real precision, not just name-dropped.
