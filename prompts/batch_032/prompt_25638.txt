Please compare the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism with Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity, and assess the relationship between them. The separateness of persons objection — advanced most prominently by Rawls and Nozick — holds that utilitarianism errs by treating the boundaries between distinct individuals as morally insignificant, aggregating benefits and harms across persons as though they were a single subject of experience. Parfit's reductionism, by contrast, argues that personal identity is not a deep further fact but consists merely in psychological continuity and connectedness, and that the boundaries between persons are in an important sense less sharp than we ordinarily suppose. These two views appear to pull in opposite directions: the objection charges utilitarianism with respecting the separateness of persons too little, while Parfit's account suggests that separateness itself is less fundamental than the objection presupposes. Your task is to work out whether Parfit's view genuinely undermines the separateness of persons objection, or whether the objection retains its force even if we accept reductionism about personal identity. In your analysis, consider whether the moral significance of person-boundaries depends on their being metaphysically deep, or whether it can rest on other considerations — for instance, on the distinctness of experiences, on the structure of agency, or on the claims that separate lives make on practical reason.
