I've been noticing a pattern in moral criticism that I can't quite analyze, and I'm hoping there's a philosophical literature that has already mapped it. The pattern is this: someone makes a valid moral criticism of another person's conduct. The target does not dispute the facts or the norm being invoked. Instead, the target points to something about the critic — a past similar transgression, a present complicity in the practice being criticized, a benefit the critic derives from the very thing they are condemning — and claims that this disqualifies the critic from making the criticism. The criticism isn't false, but it's presented as somehow out of bounds.

What puzzles me is that this move sometimes feels legitimate and sometimes doesn't, and I can't articulate the principle that sorts the cases. A reformed wrongdoer who criticizes others for the same wrong seems to have more standing, not less. But a hypocrite who condemns others while secretly doing the same thing seems to have lost something. And there are harder cases: someone who benefits from a practice they publicly condemn, someone whose past wrongdoing was in a different domain entirely, someone who criticizes a harm they are currently inflicting without acknowledging it.

I'm not looking for advice on how to handle these situations. I'm asking whether there is an established concept or framework that explains what is going on here — what exactly is lost when a critic's standing is undercut, what the necessary conditions are for losing it, and where the boundary lies between cases where the deflection is legitimate and cases where it is a fallacy. If there are multiple competing accounts, I'd want to know what they disagree about and which cases force the disagreement into the open.
