I keep noticing a pattern across several different kinds of arguments — in bioethics, in economic policy, in everyday moral reasoning — and I'm wondering whether it's a single phenomenon with a name, or just a family resemblance. Here are three instances:

1. A hospital ethics committee debates whether to approve a drug trial that will almost certainly harm a few participants but is expected to yield a treatment that saves many more future patients. Some members say the math clearly favors approval; others say you can't use people that way.

2. An economist proposes a trade policy that will eliminate thousands of jobs in one industry but create even more jobs in another. Critics say the displaced workers aren't compensated by the fact that different people somewhere else get jobs.

3. In a discussion about punishment, someone argues that harsh sentencing for a particular crime deters many more crimes than it causes suffering to the convicted, so it's justified. An opponent replies that the suffering of the convicted person and the suffering of the hypothetical future victims aren't the same currency — they happen to different people.

In each case, one side wants to aggregate costs and benefits across persons the way you would across time-slices of a single person, and the other side insists that the boundary between persons makes that move illegitimate. I can feel the force of the objection, but I can't tell whether it's one objection or several, whether it has a canonical statement, and what its limits are supposed to be. After all, we do make interpersonal trade-offs all the time — we tax people to build roads that others will use more, we draft soldiers to defend a population, we quarantine the infectious to protect the vulnerable. So the principle can't be that you can never trade off one person's interests against another's. But then what is the principle? Is there an established name for this cluster of concerns, and if so, what is its most precise formulation, and how do its defenders distinguish the trade-offs it rules out from the ones it permits?
