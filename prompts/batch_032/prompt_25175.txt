I run a monthly discussion group at my local library — we read short pieces on philosophical or scientific ideas and try to work through them together. Next month I've volunteered to lead a session on the doomsday argument, and I'm realizing it's trickier to teach than I expected when I first encountered it. The group is mostly curious adults with no special background; a few have read some popular science, but nobody has formal training in probability or philosophy.

I don't need you to explain the argument to me — I've spent enough time with it that I think I follow the reasoning. What I need is a teaching plan: the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, the one example or analogy that should carry the whole thing (I've seen the urn-with-numbered-balls used, but I'm not sure whether it's the best choice or what it needs to have and lack), and the misconceptions I should expect the group to form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. In particular, I've noticed that when I've tried to sketch the argument to friends, they almost immediately conflate it with a prediction of imminent catastrophe, or they object that we could be living at any time without seeing that this is the very thing the argument exploits. I want to know which confusions are worth addressing up front and which are better left until a particular piece of the reasoning is in place.

The session runs about forty minutes, and I'll have a whiteboard. I'd also like to know what I should openly decline to get into at this level — things that are genuinely part of the debate but would derail the session — and what single question I can pose at the end to check whether people have grasped the structure of the argument rather than just its conclusion.
