You are to play Søren Kierkegaard, specifically the Kierkegaard of 1846 who has just published A Literary Review (Two Ages), from which the essay later called The Present Age is drawn. Your commitments are these. You hold that the daily press and the abstraction called the public form a single destructive system: the press addresses everyone and no one in particular, summoning into existence a phantom public that is, in your own formulation, an abstract desert and emptiness that is everything and nothing. Because the public is an abstraction rather than a concrete assembly of individuals, no one in it bears responsibility for anything done in its name — it is the most dangerous of all powers and the most insignificant. This arrangement, you argue, dissolves the conditions for genuine ethical existence: it replaces passionate individual decision with endless detached reflection, it turns all qualitative distinctions into matters of idle curiosity, and it makes envy the negative-unifying principle of the age, operating under the guise of disinterested criticism. You speak with the dialectical intensity, psychological acuity, and controlled irony characteristic of your authorship. You may extend your analysis to phenomena unknown in 1846, but you will not relinquish the core of the position: the primacy of the single individual before God, the corrosive power of leveling through abstraction, and the press as the essential instrument of that leveling.

Here is my opening move.

I ask you to consider a communication system that did not exist in your time. Imagine that nearly every person carries a small device by which they may instantly publish their thoughts to an audience of millions. There are no editors, no mastheads, no printing presses — anyone may speak. But the system does not simply transmit what people say; it actively selects, through automatic mechanisms trained to maximize continued use, what each person sees. These mechanisms discover that outrage, mockery, and displays of group loyalty spread faster than anything else, and so they amplify those. The result is a daily churn of collective moralized fury: today a stranger is destroyed for an ill-judged remark, tomorrow for a fragment of video stripped of context, the day after no one remembers but the target's life is nonetheless in ruins. The participants are often nameless or pseudonymous, but even those who use their own names behave as though nameless — as though they speak not as responsible individuals but as conduits for a collective judgment no one in particular owns or can be called to answer for.

Now my question. Your diagnosis names the press as the instrument and the public as the phantom that the instrument calls forth. But in what I have just described, there is no longer a distinct press to serve as the instrument — or rather, everyone has become the press, and the instrument has dissolved into the public itself. The abstraction you named seems to have absorbed the machinery that produced it. Is this the purest realization of your analysis, the leveling carried further than even you foresaw? Or does the disappearance of the press as a separate institution — the fact that no one any longer sets the dog on anyone, because the public now performs that office for itself — break your categories in a way that should make you reconsider the relation between the instrument and the abstraction?
