I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size hospital system and I somehow ended up on our clinical technology review committee — long story, basically the CTO asked for a volunteer who actually understands how the software works and I was the only one who raised a hand.

We're evaluating whether to deploy an ML-based triage system in the emergency department. The system ingests vitals, symptoms, lab results as they come in, and continuously re-ranks patients by predicted acuity — basically who needs attention most urgently right now. The vendor's trial data is genuinely impressive: it caught sepsis and internal bleeding meaningfully faster than standard triage protocols, like 20–30 minutes faster in some cases. The ED director is pushing hard for approval.

Here's the thing that's keeping me up. Suppose the system deprioritizes a patient who then deteriorates badly. The attending physician went along with the ranking because the department was slammed and the whole selling point of the system is that it sees patterns humans miss. The triage nurses didn't override because they didn't have contradictory information — the patient's vitals looked stable at the time. Who owns that outcome? The physician wasn't negligent by any normal standard; they relied on a hospital-approved decision-support tool. The nurses couldn't have known. The vendor's ML engineers couldn't have predicted this specific failure — the model has millions of parameters and its edge-case behavior is opaque even to them. The vendor's contract has standard liability disclaimers. Hospital administration approved the system based on solid trial data. And the model itself isn't a moral agent — you can't blame a matrix multiply.

Some people on the committee say this is just the normal "technology has risks" tradeoff, like how we accept that CT scans sometimes miss tumors. But that comparison feels off to me. A CT scan produces an image that a radiologist interprets; it doesn't rank patients against each other and effectively decide who gets seen and who waits. This system is making allocative decisions under scarcity, and its recommendations will function as de facto orders when the department is overwhelmed — which is exactly when the system is supposed to be most useful.

I need to draft the committee's recommendation memo for the hospital board. It should recommend one of: deploy, don't deploy, or deploy with specific conditions — and it needs to genuinely engage with the responsibility-gap worry, not just acknowledge it and move on. The strongest argument on the losing side should get a real hearing. Please lead with the memo itself, no preface, no "here's a draft," just the document.
