I've spent the last several months trying to understand the species problem on my own, without any formal background in biology or philosophy. What drew me in was a simple observation: two field guides for the same region will sometimes list different numbers of warbler species, or disagree about whether two populations of oak trees are separate species or just varieties of one. I started reading about how biologists actually decide these things, and I quickly discovered that there isn't one agreed-upon answer to the question "what is a species?" — there are at least two dozen species concepts in the literature, and the disagreements among them are not just about edge cases. The biological species concept says that species are groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. The phylogenetic species concept says that species are the smallest diagnosable clusters of organisms that share a unique evolutionary history. These two concepts pick out different things in the world, and their defenders often seem to be having entirely different conversations.

What I find frustrating is that I can't easily tell how much of this disagreement is about the facts of evolution and how much is about what we want the word "species" to do for us. So I'd like to try a thought experiment: imagine a committed advocate of the biological species concept and a committed advocate of the phylogenetic species concept agree to an adversarial collaboration. They sit down together and try to produce a single document they can both sign — not a compromise, but a precise map of their agreement and disagreement.

Please draft that document for me. It should open with a numbered list of claims both sides can affirm — about populations, gene flow, common descent, the branching pattern of evolution, the practice of taxonomy, and anything else they genuinely share. Then it should present a disagreements table. For each row, state the point of disagreement in language each side would accept for its own position; identify the crux (the sub-question that, if settled, would resolve that row); and, where possible, describe a concrete empirical case or line of evidence both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded. If you think some portion of their dispute is verbal — a disagreement about how to apply the word "species" rather than about the underlying biology — please flag that and explain why.
