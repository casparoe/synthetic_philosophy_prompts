Here's an argument that came up in my philosophy of mind seminar and I can't shake it. It seems to show that if you accept the standard takeaway from the Chase-and-Sanborn coffee-taster case, you end up having to deny that we have priviledged access to any of our own experiential changes, which feels way too strong.

(1) Chase and Sanborn both used to love the office coffee and now dislike it. Chase insists his taste-experience of the coffee hasn't changed — what changed are his hedonic standards (he got more refined). Sanborn insists his taste-experience has changed — same standards, different quale. Both are sincere, both are competent tasters, and there's no behavioral test or third-person fact that could settle who's right. All the external evidence — what they say, what they can discriminate, their facial expressions — can be accommodated either way.

(2) If there's no third-person fact that settles whether Chase's experience changed or his standards did, then either (a) there's a first-person fact that settles it (a private, intrinsic quale that only Chase can access), or (b) there's no fact of the matter at all.

(3) The whole point of the thought experiment — at least as Dennett uses it — is to make option (a) look suspect. If qualia are private, intrinsic, and ineffable, how could they settle anything? Chase and Sanborn have equal "access" to their own qualia, yet they disagree about what those qualia are like. The notion of a private fact that only one person can check starts to look like the notion of a fact that nobody can check.

(4) So if the thought experiment succeeds, we're pushed to (b): there's no fact of the matter about whether Chase's experience changed or his standards did. The distinction between "my experience changed" and "my standards changed" doesn't carve reality at the joints.

(5) But here's the problem: the Chase/Sanborn setup isn't special. For any two people whose introspective reports diverge about whether their experience changed or their judgment changed — which covers basically every case of changing tastes, preferences, or perceptual judgments over time — there's no behavioral test that can settle it. If I say cilantro started tasting soapy to me and you say you just got pickier about herbs, we're in exactly the same boat as Chase and Sanborn.

(6) So if there's no fact of the matter in the Chase/Sanborn case, there's no fact of the matter in any of these ordinary cases either. That means we have no privileged access to whether our own experiences have changed — we can't even be wrong about it, because there's nothing to be right or wrong about.

(7) But that's absurd. I know perfectly well that when I stopped liking cilantro, my experience of it changed (it started tasting soapy), and I didn't just change my standards about what counts as a good herb. If a theory of mind tells me there's no fact of the matter about that, the theory is broken, not my introspection.

So something has to give. I'm most reluctant to give up (7), but I can see that (5) is doing a lot of work — maybe the cilantro case is different from the Chase/Sanborn case in a way that matters. Which premise is the real culprit, and what do I lose if I reject it?
