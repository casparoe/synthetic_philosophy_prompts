I'm a journalist covering the intersection of artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind, and I've spent the better part of a year interviewing researchers on both sides of a debate that keeps surfacing in my pieces. The question is deceptively simple: when we say a large language model "believes" that Paris is the capital of France, or that it "wants" to give a helpful answer, are we stating a fact about the system, or are we merely adopting a useful predictive shorthand? The two camps I've been talking to — call them the realist camp and the instrumentalist camp about AI belief — agree on far more than their public arguments would suggest. Both accept that the intentional stance has genuine predictive utility for LLMs. Both accept that LLMs lack much of what humans have: phenomenology, sustained agency, embodied grounding. Both reject the caricatures attributed to them by the other side. But they keep circling the same points without landing, and I suspect a disciplined adversarial collaboration would clarify what is actually at stake.

Before I write my long-form piece, I want to see what that collaboration would produce. Draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith effort to isolate their disagreement. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — what both sides affirm about LLM architecture and training, about the predictive power of the intentional stance, and about what each side is *not* asserting. Second, a disagreements table with the precise points of divergence, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording of its position. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions on which the larger dispute turns. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete test, case, or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction stated in advance. If any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, flag it and explain why. Keep the entire document to 700 words maximum — tight enough that every sentence has to earn its place.
