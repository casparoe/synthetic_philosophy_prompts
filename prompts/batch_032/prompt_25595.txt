So I've been going through a rough patch with my brother — long story but basically he did something that really hurt our family, and I've been going back and forth on how much to blame him, given that he's struggled with addiction and I'm not sure how much choice he really had. A friend of mine who did some philosophy in college told me about this thing called Frankfurt cases and said they basically show that you can be morally responsible even if you couldn't have done otherwise. Here's how he explained it:

"Imagine a guy, Jones, who's deciding whether to vote for a particular candidate. Unbeknownst to him, a neuroscientist has implanted a chip in his brain that will force him to vote for that candidate if he shows any sign of deciding not to. But Jones decides to vote for the candidate on his own, so the chip never activates. Jones couldn't have done otherwise — if he'd tried to vote differently, the chip would've kicked in — but he still seems responsible for his vote, since he made the choice himself. So the principle of alternative possibilities is false: you don't need the ability to do otherwise to be morally responsible. And that means determinism is no threat to free will, because even if all our actions are determined, we can still be responsible for them as long as we act on our own desires."

My friend says this is basically settled — that Frankfurt pretty much singlehandedly refuted the idea that free will requires alternative possibilities, and most philosophers now accept that determinism and moral responsibility are compatible. I want to believe this because it would help me think clearly about my brother's situation, but something about the jump from "Jones is responsible even though he couldn't do otherwise" to "determinism is no problem for free will" feels too quick to me. Is my friend's summary fair? What would someone who works on this stuff say is right about it and what's oversimplified or wrong?
