A colleague in my reading group has been arguing that deliberately inducing irrationality in yourself can be a straightforward case of rational precommitment. Their argument runs through an analogy with Ulysses and the Sirens. Ulysses wanted to hear the Sirens' song without steering his ship onto the rocks, so he had his crew bind him to the mast and plug their own ears with wax. The standard moral drawn from this: it can be rational to restrict your own future freedom of action when you know your future self will have preferences you now disavow.

My colleague wants to extend this to cases where the restriction works not by physical constraint but by making you genuinely irrational — unable to reason correctly about your situation. Their central example is a version of Parfit's hitchhiker: you are stranded in the desert, a driver will take you to town only if you promise to pay, but you know that once you arrive you will have no self-interested reason to pay and the driver knows this too, so your promise is not credible. The proposed solution is to take a drug, before setting out, that makes you irrationally vengeful toward those who break promises, so that your future self will pay out of irrational rage, thereby making your promise credible to the driver. A second example: a rational kidnapper who would benefit from being known to be the sort of person who irrationally punishes defiance even when doing so is costly, and who therefore takes steps to cultivate that irrational disposition in himself.

My colleague says these are simply Ulysses cases: you foresee that your future self will have bad preferences (the hitchhiker's future self will not want to pay; the kidnapper's future self will not want to carry out a costly punishment), so you rationally arrange in advance for that future self to be constrained — and if the only available constraint happens to be induced irrationality, that is no different in principle from binding yourself to a mast.

I am not convinced the analogy carries this weight, but I am having trouble making the disanalogies precise. Can you audit it? I would like three things:

First, a table of correspondences: what in the Ulysses story maps to what in the rational-irrationality cases, noting any places where the mapping is already strained before we even reach the disanalogies.

Second, an identification of where the analogy breaks, with a clear separation between breaks that matter for the intended conclusion and those that are merely cosmetic.

Third, a verdict on whether the conclusion — that deliberately inducing irrationality in yourself can be a straightforward instance of rational precommitment — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the Ulysses analogy was quietly importing features of physical restraint that do not carry over to the irrationality cases.
