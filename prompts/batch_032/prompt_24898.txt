I've been working through Quine's *Word and Object* in a philosophy of language seminar, and I keep returning to a question the syllabus doesn't address. Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis—the claim that multiple incompatible translation manuals can be consistent with all possible behavioral evidence, with no fact of the matter as to which is correct—was developed against the backdrop of mid-century behaviorism and a picture of translation as something a field linguist does by formulating analytical hypotheses. The famous "gavagai" example turns on the idea that pointing and assenting cannot settle whether the native term picks out rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit stages, because all these hypotheses are consistent with the same stimulus conditions.

Modern large language models do something that looks like translation without constructing anything like a translation manual. They are trained on enormous multilingual corpora and learn to map between languages through statistical regularities in co-occurrence patterns across parallel and monolingual texts. They produce fluent, context-sensitive translations that often capture register and idiom at a level that impresses fluent speakers. And they do this without explicit hypotheses about what foreign words mean—there is no step where the system formulates candidate meanings and tests them against behavioral evidence.

Quine died in 2000, before any of this existed. I am trying to work out, from his broader commitments, what he would have made of LLM-based translation. Would he have seen the LLM's output as simply one empirically adequate translation among many, with no special claim to correctness—just another set of analytical hypotheses that happens to work, while others could work equally well? Or would the fact that LLMs achieve something like convergent agreement among fluent speakers across a vast range of novel sentences put pressure on the claim that behavioral evidence underdetermines translation? And there is a deeper question about what counts as evidence: Quine's radical translator only has patterns of assent and dissent under stimulus conditions, but an LLM's training corpus contains the internal structural relationships among billions of sentences in both languages, including the accumulated decisions of generations of human translators. Would Quine have counted this as just more behavioral evidence, or would he have argued it smuggles in semantic content in a way that begs the question against indeterminacy?

I would like you to reason through what position Quine would most likely have taken, drawing on his behaviorism, his holism about meaning, and his naturalism. Please also flag the point in your reasoning where you are least confident—the interpretive move or assumption that, if wrong, would most change the answer. I am less interested in a definitive pronouncement than in seeing the argument mapped out clearly, including where it might fail.
