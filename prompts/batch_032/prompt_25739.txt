**Question:** Explain Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that and evaluate its significance for the view that intelligent action requires prior theoretical knowledge.

**Student's answer:**

Ryle draws a distinction between knowing-how (practical knowledge, like knowing how to ride a bike) and knowing-that (propositional knowledge, like knowing that Paris is the capital of France). He argues against what he calls the "intellectualist legend," which holds that intelligent action always involves first entertaining a proposition in the mind and then acting on it. Ryle's central argument is a regress argument: if every intelligent act required a prior act of considering a proposition, then that prior act of considering would itself need to be an intelligent act, which would require yet another prior act of considering, and so on ad infinitum. Therefore, intelligent action cannot be explained purely in terms of knowing-that; knowing-how is more basic. Ryle concludes that knowing-how is not a species of knowing-that but a distinct kind of knowledge altogether. One important upshot is that skills and practical abilities cannot be reduced to or fully captured by sets of propositions. This shows, for example, that an expert chess player's ability is not simply a matter of possessing a large stock of true propositions about chess, but consists in a capacity to apply those propositions intelligently in context—a capacity that is itself a form of knowing-how.
