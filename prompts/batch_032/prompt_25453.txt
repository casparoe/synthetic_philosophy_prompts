For a piece I'm writing on the ethics of moral deference, I've been speaking with philosophers who argue that there's something problematic about forming moral beliefs purely on the testimony of others. One of them put the point this way: moral understanding is like understanding a joke. If someone tells you a joke is funny but you can't see why, you might trust their judgment, but you don't possess the understanding that constitutes genuinely getting the joke. By the same token, if someone tells you that a certain action is wrong but you can't see why, you might trust their moral judgment, but you don't genuinely grasp the wrongness. The analogy is deployed to support a strong conclusion: that moral testimony, unlike testimony about ordinary facts, cannot transmit genuine moral knowledge—at best it yields a kind of second-class cognitive state.

I want to press on this analogy. Please give me a systematic audit: lay out the correspondences (what in the moral case plays the role of the joke, the punchline, the listener, getting it), identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely superficial. Then offer a committed judgment: once the disanalogies are accounted for, does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying weight it cannot bear? If there is a better analogy for what is going on with moral testimony, I would like to hear it.
