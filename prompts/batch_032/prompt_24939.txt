H.L.A. Hart died in 1992, well before the widespread adoption of algorithmic risk-assessment instruments in criminal justice — tools that use statistical models trained on historical data to estimate a defendant's likelihood of reoffending, and whose outputs are consulted in bail, sentencing, and parole decisions. These tools raise a cluster of issues that cut across Hart's core concerns in both legal philosophy and the philosophy of criminal law: the nature of legal rules versus discretionary standards, the relationship between law and morality, the justification of punishment, and the role of responsibility and choice in criminal liability.

I would like you to construct a reasoned prediction of Hart's likely position on the use of algorithmic risk assessment in criminal sentencing. Please address the following specifically:

(1) Would Hart have regarded algorithmic risk scores as an unacceptable form of punishment for predicted rather than actual conduct — a departure from the retributive side of his mixed theory of punishment — or would he have seen them as a legitimate extension of the forward-looking, consequentialist considerations he already endorsed in Prolegomenon to the Principles of Punishment?

(2) How would his distinction between primary and secondary rules, and his account of the internal aspect of rules, bear on the question of whether opaque algorithmic recommendations satisfy the rule-of-law virtues he defended — publicity, clarity, and the capacity to guide conduct?

(3) Hart insisted on the separability of law and morals in The Concept of Law, yet also defended a limited form of legal moralism in his debate with Devlin. Would he have seen the use of risk scores trained on arrest data that may encode past policing biases as raising a problem internal to legality — a failure of the rule of recognition, perhaps — or as a moral problem external to law's validity?

(4) What would Hart have made of the fact that these tools are typically introduced through administrative or judicial practice rather than legislation? Would this matter for him, given his account of the relationship between rules and discretion and his treatment of judicial law-making in the postscript to The Concept of Law?

Please ground your prediction in Hart's actual published commitments rather than in a generic legal-philosophical take on technology. Where his views would have been underdetermined by his published work, flag the ambiguity and explain which of his commitments pull in which direction. The aim is a plausible Hart, not a straw man or a ventriloquised version of your own views on algorithmic justice.
