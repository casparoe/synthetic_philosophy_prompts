I cover the philosophy and cognitive science beat for a magazine, and a few weeks ago I sat in on a talk at a conference that's been quietly driving me crazy ever since. The speaker was making the case for evolutionary debunking in metaethics — the idea that once you see where our moral intuitions came from, you should stop treating them as evidence of anything. I walked out convinced. Then on the flight home I started poking at it and realized the same structure seems to take down way more than morality, and now I can't tell if the argument is too strong or if I'm just missing an obvious move.

Here's the argument as I reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Our core moral intuitions — things like "hurting innocent people for fun is wrong" — were shaped by natural selection. Evolution favored whatever gut-level moral responses helped our ancestors cooperate, avoid getting punished, and pass on genes. It didn't favor responses that track some independent moral truth, because from a fitness perspective all that matters is the behavior, not whether the belief behind it is true.

(2) If a belief was produced by a process that doesn't track the truth about its subject matter, then that belief is unjustified unless you have some independent reason to think it's true. And "it feels obvious" or "everyone agrees" doesn't count as independent.

(3) So our core moral beliefs are unjustified. Moral realism is in trouble — we can't treat our moral intuitions as evidence of moral facts.

Fine. But here's what hit me at 30,000 feet:

(4) Our perceptual beliefs — that there's a laptop in front of me, that the person next to me is speaking French — were also shaped by natural selection. Evolution didn't select for perceptual systems that track reality as it really is; it selected for systems that help organisms survive and reproduce. A frog's visual system doesn't represent flies "veridically" in some deep metaphysical sense, it represents them as edible moving dots. Why think our own perceptual systems are different in kind rather than just in degree?

(5) Our mathematical and logical intuitions — that 2+2=4, that modus ponens is valid — were also shaped by cognitive structures that evolution built. We didn't evolve to do set theory; we evolved to track approximate quantities of food and predators and to reason about social exchanges. The fact that these capacities later got repurposed for abstract math doesn't change where they came from.

(6) If premise (2) is right, then our perceptual and logical beliefs are also unjustified. But that's absurd — we can't coherently function, let alone do science, if we're not justified in trusting our senses and our basic reasoning.

(7) So either premise (2) is too strong, or there's a relevant difference between moral beliefs and perceptual/logical beliefs that saves the latter but not the former. But the speaker was sharp and has definitely heard this objection before, so I assume there's a move here that I'm not seeing.

I'd rather keep my moral realism than give up on perception and logic, but I want to know what I'm paying. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's a way to keep the debunking argument for morality without it eating the rest of cognition, I want to know what that looks like — and whether the fix leaves the original argument with any teeth.
