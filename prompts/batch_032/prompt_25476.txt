So I'm in this philosophy seminar on intergenerational ethics and we've been stuck on the nonidentity problem for like three weeks. My professor has this annoying (but effective) habit of giving us her verdicts on cases without ever stating the principle she's using, and our final paper is basically "figure out what principle I'm running and defend or attack it."

Here are the cases she's ruled on so far — all of them involve choices that affect *which* people come into existence, not just what happens to the same people:

- **14-year-old girl**: Decides to have a child now. The child has a life worth living but worse than the different child she'd have if she waited a few years. Verdict: wrong.
- **Depletion**: Society depletes rather than conserves. People 200 years later have lives worth living but much worse than the different people who'd exist under conservation. Verdict: wrong.
- **Genetic condition, future cure**: Woman has a condition. If she conceives now, the child inherits it and has ten years of moderate suffering before a cure arrives. Life worth living overall. If she waits ten years, she has a different child without the condition. Verdict: wrong not to wait.
- **Preconception negligence**: Woman drinks heavily the week before conceiving, causing mild permanent cognitive effects in the child. Life worth living. If she'd waited a week, different sperm, different child. Verdict: wrong.
- **Same-number policy**: Policy A yields a million future people at wellbeing 20. Policy B yields a million different future people at wellbeing 30. Verdict: wrong to pick A.

So she's clearly not running the narrow person-affecting view. But at least three principles fit all her verdicts: impersonal total utilitarianism, some kind of responsibility or Kantian view, and Parfit's Same Number Quality Claim. They all say "wrong" on every case above.
