A colleague in my philosophy department keeps a running list of social scenarios on which she has recorded her intuitive verdicts about whether they involve genuine joint action — the kind of shared activity that implicates collective intentionality — as opposed to mere aggregates of individual behavior. She has declined to state her criterion, and I am trying to work it out from the cases. Here are her verdicts so far:

- A string quartet performing a piece: joint action.
- Two pedestrians who happen to walk in the same direction for several blocks: not joint action.
- A corporate board voting on a merger after discussion: joint action.
- Traders on an exchange floor during a busy hour: not joint action.
- A jury deliberating toward a verdict: joint action.
- A crowd fleeing a burning theater: not joint action.
- Two friends who are co-hosting a dinner party and, without discussing it, each start moving furniture to clear space, adjusting their movements to each other in real time: joint action.
- A book club that meets on the first Tuesday of every month, whose members read the assigned book and discuss it together: joint action.
- Passengers on a city bus, all riding to the same terminus: not joint action.

Next week she plans to rule on the following case. A car crashes on a rural highway. Four strangers stop their cars. Without exchanging a word, one begins directing traffic, another calls 911, and two others coordinate to lift debris off an injured person. They have never met, made no prior agreement, and will likely never encounter one another again. But in the moment, each monitors what the others are doing and adjusts their own actions accordingly.

I would like you to do four things. First, identify the most plausible rule or rules that fit all nine of her earlier verdicts — there should be at least two genuinely different principles that handle the cases equally well, and you should state each clearly. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about the car crash case. Third, give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and what verdict she will deliver, with your reasons for preferring that interpretation over the alternatives. Fourth, propose a further case — one not on her list — that would cleanly discriminate between the candidate rules, and explain why it would settle the matter.

Please also indicate how confident you are in your prediction and single out the point in your analysis you are least sure about.
