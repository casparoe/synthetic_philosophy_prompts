I'm working on a piece about the ethics of cognitive enhancement and I keep coming back to Flowers for Algernon — the Daniel Keyes novel, not just the short story. You know the arc: Charlie Gordon starts with an IQ around 68, gets the experimental surgery, rockets up to genius-level, and then has to watch Algernon the mouse deteriorate knowing the same thing's coming for him. What I want to do is read the novel through the lens of the debate about whether cognitive capacity is morally relevant. There's a real philosophical fight here: Singer and others argue that what matters is sentience, not smarts — that a human with severe cognitive disabilities and, say, a pig with comparable mental capacities deserve equal moral consideration. The counterposition says no, higher cognitive capacities do matter, that a being who can plan, reflect, and form deep relationships has a different kind of moral status that isn't just about feeling pain.

The novel seems to take a position on this but I'm not sure it's consistent. Early Charlie is mocked and patronized by his coworkers (and, more brutally, rejected by his mother), and the book clearly condemns that. But then genius Charlie starts to see his former self as almost a different person — he calls him "that Charlie" and feels alienated from him, even embarrassed. Is the book saying cognitive capacity changes who you are in a morally relevant way, or is it showing us that this attitude is itself a kind of self-deception the reader is meant to recoil from? And there's a formal question too: the whole novel works by giving us access to Charlie's interiority through the progress reports, an interiority that's literally disintigrating by the end. Does the form of the novel — the fact that we care about Charlie because we're inside his head — beg the question against the sentience-only view?

I'd love a reading that takes both the novel and the phil debate seriously, and that's honest about where the novel complicates or resists the positions rather than just illustrating them. Also, tell me which assumptions your reading depends on most and how it would shift if you dropped them. Like, if you assumed the novel is ultimately on Charlie's side throughout vs. ironizing him at various points — how much turns on that?
