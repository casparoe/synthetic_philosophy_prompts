I'm working on a paper about the moral significance of the lying/misleading distinction, and I need a thought experiment that puts real pressure on the standard view. Please construct one to the following specifications.

The standard intuition in the literature is that lying (asserting what you believe false) is morally worse than merely misleading (asserting only truths but arranging them so the hearer draws a false conclusion). I want a case that reverses this: Speaker A lies, Speaker B merely misleads, and yet B's conduct is intuitively worse than A's. The case must be realistic — set it in a professional or institutional context (medicine, law, finance, journalism) rather than a trolley-like contrivance — and no longer than a paragraph. The reversal must not come from B's misleading causing drastically worse consequences; the asymmetry should be intrinsic to the communicative acts themselves, not a downstream causal story.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:

1. State exactly what each speaker says and why A counts as lying while B counts as merely misleading. Be precise about the truth-values of each assertion and the false belief each speaker intends the hearer to form.

2. Argue for the verdict that B's conduct is morally worse than A's. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work to flip the usual asymmetry — that is, what variable you have isolated that makes misleading the graver wrong here.

3. Take a committed position on what this case shows. There are (at least) two interpretive options. The strong reading: the lying/misleading distinction carries no intrinsic moral weight at all; context does all the work, and the standard asymmetry is an artifact of the cases philosophers have chosen. The moderate reading: the distinction is real and normally tracks something morally relevant, but it can be swamped or reversed when other features of the communicative situation shift. Pick one and defend it against the other. Do not hedge or split the difference.

4. Anticipate the best response from a defender of the position you rejected. How would they try to redescribe the case to preserve their view — for instance, by arguing that B's conduct actually involves an implicit lie, or that A's conduct isn't a lie in the relevant sense, or that the asymmetry survives if we focus on the right normative factor? Explain why that redescription either fails on its own terms or concedes enough to your side that the dispute becomes merely verbal.
