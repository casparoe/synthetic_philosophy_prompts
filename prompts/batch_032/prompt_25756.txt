I keep noticing this thing when I engage with fiction and I'm wondering if there's a name for it in philosophy. It's not the standard "why do we feel emotions for characters we know aren't real" puzzle — I know that's been discussed to death. It's something more specific about the architecture of the response.

Here's what I mean. Last week I was watching a show and a character I really like was about to make this catastrophically bad romantic decision, and I felt this surge of genuine frustration — not at the writers for making him do it, not a meta-level "this is bad writing" reaction, but at *him*, the character, like I wanted to reach through the screen and stop him. And I noticed something odd: if you had paused the show and asked me "is this character real?" I would have said "of course not, it's fiction" without a moment's hesitation. My belief that he doesnt exist was fully intact and fully accessible. But the frustration didn't seem to consult that belief at all. It was like my emotional response and my explicit belief were running on parallel tracks that dont communicate.

This feels different from suspension of disbelief, because suspension of disbelief suggests I'm temporarily setting aside my knowledge that it's fiction. But I wasn't — the knowledge was right there, I just wasn't using it to gate the emotion.

So my question is: is there an established term for this specific configuration — where you have a standing belief that, if it were consulted, would block or modulate an emotional response, but the emotional response fires anyway because the two systems operate independently? And if there is, how does it relate to the broader paradox of fiction? Are these the same thing at different levels of description, or are they distinct problems?

Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert. Don't just give me a label — I want to know what the concept carves at, what its boundaries are, and whether it actually solves anything or just redescribes the puzzle.
