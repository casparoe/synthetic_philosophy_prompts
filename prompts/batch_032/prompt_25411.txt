**Exam Question:**

"In his 1963 paper 'Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?', Edmund Gettier presents two counterexamples to the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. Explain the structure of one of these counterexamples in your own words, making clear why it is thought to refute the JTB analysis. **Student Answer:**

Gettier's paper targets the view that knowledge is justified true belief, often called the JTB analysis. On this view, S knows that P if and only if (i) P is true, (ii) S believes that P, and (iii) S is justified in believing that P. Gettier's insight is that these three conditions can all be satisfied even when the belief is only true by luck, and in such cases we intuitively deny that S has knowledge.

Consider Gettier's first case. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a job — the company president told him so — and Smith also knows that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith then infers the proposition "The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." As it happens, Smith himself gets the job, and Smith also happens to have ten coins in his pocket, though he doesn't know this. So Smith's belief is true (the man who gets the job does have ten coins in his pocket — it's Smith), and Smith is justified in believing it (he has good evidence about Jones and validly deduces the existential claim). Yet we don't think Smith knows it, because his belief is true for reasons entirely unrelated to his justification. The belief is accidentally true. This shows that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge: the three conditions can be met without knowledge being present.

Gettier himself recognized that the problem arises because Smith's justification rests on a false premise — namely, that Jones will get the job. He therefore proposed adding a fourth condition to the JTB analysis: the belief must not be inferred from any false lemma. This is the "no false lemmas" condition. On this revised account, S knows that P if and only if P is true, S believes that P, S is justified in believing that P, and S's justification does not depend on any false intermediate step. This condition neatly handles Gettier's original cases because in both of them the subject's reasoning passes through a falsehood (Jones will get the job; Jones owns a Ford). By blocking inferences that rely on false premises, the no-false-lemmas condition ensures that the truth of the belief is properly connected to the justification, eliminating the element of luck that made the original cases problematic.

I think this solution is largely successful. It captures what went wrong in Gettier's own examples without requiring us to abandon the JTB framework entirely — we simply add a fourth necessary condition. The no-false-lemmas condition also has the virtue of being independently plausible: we generally think that reasoning from false premises is epistemically defective, even if it sometimes leads to true conclusions. One might worry that the condition is too strong, since we often rely on background assumptions that are strictly false but harmless (e.g., "the clock is working"), but these can be handled by refining the condition to apply only to essential false lemmas rather than all false beliefs anywhere in one's cognitive background. With this refinement, the no-false-lemmas approach offers a clean and economical solution to the Gettier problem.
