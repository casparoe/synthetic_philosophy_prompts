I'm writing an essay on the moral difference between lying and misleading, and I want to use John Patrick Shanley's play *Doubt: A Parable* as a central text. The play is built around speech acts that hover between outright false assertion and calculated implicature: Father Flynn's sermons, his explanations to Sister Aloysius, his private conversations with Sister James. Meanwhile, Sister Aloysius tells a flat-out lie at the climax — she claims to have contacted Flynn's former parish and uncovered a history of misconduct, when she has done no such thing — and the play ends with her breaking down and confessing that she is wracked by doubt.

I would like a reading of the play through the philosophical literature on lying versus misleading. Where does the play dramatize distinctions the philosophers draw — for instance, between what is asserted and what is merely implicated, or between deceiving someone and causing them to deceive themselves? Where does it complicate those distinctions, perhaps by showing how power relations and institutional authority affect what counts as misleading, or by making the audience share Sister Aloysius's uncertainty about whether she has been lied to at all? And what does the play's title and its closing confession suggest about the relationship between epistemic doubt and moral judgment in cases of deception — does the play imply that our moral evaluation of a lie or a misleading statement depends on our prior certainty about the speaker's guilt or innocence? I am not looking for a verdict on whether Sister Aloysius was right; I want to know what the play shows that the philosophical debate misses, and where the philosophy exposes something the play leaves ambiguous.
