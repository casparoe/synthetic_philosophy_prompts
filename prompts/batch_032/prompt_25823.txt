I recently listened to a philosophy podcast whose host summarized epiphenomenalism and what he called its fatal problem in roughly the following way. I want to know whether this is a fair account before I repeat it in something I'm writing.

As the host put it: Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are caused by brain activity but have no causal powers of their own — they're like the heat given off by a running engine, real but inert. The devastating objection, he said, is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If epiphenomenalism is true, then when I say or think "I am in pain," that judgment is caused entirely by neural events, not by the pain itself. The pain could be absent and, provided the same neural events occurred, I would still form the judgment that I am in pain. But then our judgments about our own conscious states are completely disconnected from those states — we have no more reason to trust them than we have to trust a thermometer whose readings are caused not by the temperature but by some unrelated mechanism inside it. And that means epiphenomenalism makes consciousness unknowable even to the person having it, which is absurd. Since we plainly do know when we're in pain, epiphenomenalism is self-defeating.

He concluded that the view collapses under its own logic and that no serious philosopher of mind still defends it.

I'm not a specialist, but I recall that epiphenomenalism has had actual defenders, and the host's move from "the pain doesn't cause the judgment" to "the judgment is disconnected from the pain" struck me as possibly too quick. I'd like a careful audit: which parts of this summary are accurate, which are subtly wrong or conflate distinct claims, and whether the conclusion — that the paradox straightforwardly refutes the view — follows from what an epiphenomenalist would actually grant.
