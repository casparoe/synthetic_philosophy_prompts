What is your actual credence that Aumann's agreement theorem has substantial normative bite for real-world disagreement — that is, that persistent disagreement among roughly epistemic peers about factual or descriptive matters is rarely if ever rationally permissible once the theorem's core insight is properly appreciated?

I want a single number between 0 and 1, not a survey of positions. Then I want the number decomposed into the sub-questions your credence turns on. For each sub-question, give a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification. Then show how they combine — multiplicatively, as a weighted average, or however your reasoning works — to yield your overall credence.

At minimum, address these sub-questions:

1. Can the common prior assumption be defended or adequately approximated for the kinds of real-world disagreements where the theorem is supposed to matter (e.g., disagreements about economic policy, historical claims, or religious propositions), or is it a fatal idealization?

2. Granting common priors, are failures of common knowledge — of each other's rationality, of each other's posteriors, or even of the fact that they disagree — the main obstacle to convergence, or is something else doing the blocking?

3. Even if Aumann's exact conditions rarely hold, does some nearby result (e.g., the no-trade theorems, Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis's result that communicating posteriors back and forth forces convergence, or results on convergence under approximate common knowledge) still force a strong presumption of convergence among rational agents?

Finally, identify which of these sub-questions your overall credence is most sensitive to — where a small shift in that probability would most change your final number — and describe what kind of evidence or argument, empirical or philosophical, would most shift your probability on that sub-question.
