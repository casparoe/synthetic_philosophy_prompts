I've been reading about the "wrong kind of reasons" problem in metaethics. As I understand it, the issue is that some considerations give you reasons to hold an attitude without bearing on whether the attitude is fitting or correct. The standard example: an evil demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him, so you now have a reason to admire him, but this doesn't make him admirable. What confuses me is why philosophers treat this as a deep unresolved puzzle. It seems like we just need a way to operationally distinguish wrong-kind reasons from right-kind ones — a detection heuristic or filtering mechanism. Humans clearly do this: we recognize the demon's threat as irrelevant to the demon's admirability and discount it. If the concern is edge cases where the heuristic fails, that's just a standard classification problem with the usual precision/recall tradeoffs. Why is this considered a conceptual problem rather than an engineering one? And if philosophers can't solve it, doesn't that undermine AI alignment work that assumes we can specify what it means for a model to respond to the "right" reasons?
