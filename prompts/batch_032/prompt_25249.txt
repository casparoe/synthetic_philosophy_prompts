I recently read a chapter in an introductory philosophy book that summarizes the debate about norms of assertion. The author writes something like this:

"There are three main views about what norm governs assertion. The knowledge norm, defended by Timothy Williamson, says you should assert p only if you know p — which basically means you should only say things you're certain of. The truth norm says you should assert p only if p is true, and the belief norm says you should assert p only if you sincerely believe p. The knowledge norm faces an obvious problem: the lottery case. If you hold a ticket in a fair lottery with a million tickets, you don't know you'll lose, even though it's overwhelmingly probable. But it seems perfectly fine to assert 'I won't win.' So the knowledge norm is too strict. The truth norm handles this easily — it's true that you won't win, so the assertion is fine. The belief norm handles it too — you believe you won't win. For this reason, most philosophers now favor either the truth norm or the belief norm, and the knowledge norm has largely been abandoned."

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and something about this summary feels off to me, but I can't articulate exactly what. Is this a fair characterization of the debate? What would a defender of the knowledge norm say in response, and which parts of the summary are accurate versus misleading?
