A reductionist and an anti-reductionist about testimonial justification have just completed a year-long adversarial collaboration. The reductionist holds that a hearer's justification for accepting testimony is always reducible to other epistemic sources — perception, memory, and inductive inference, and in particular to an inference about the speaker's reliability based on the hearer's past experience with testimony. The anti-reductionist holds that testimony is a basic source of justification: a hearer can be prima facie justified in believing a speaker's assertion without needing positive reasons concerning the speaker's reliability, just as one can be prima facie justified in trusting one's perceptual experience without first amassing independent evidence that perception is reliable.

Draft the joint statement they would publish. Structure it as follows:

(1) AGREED CLAIMS. A numbered list of claims both can sign. These should cover: what testimony is and what distinguishes it from other sources of belief; the fact that testimony can transmit knowledge and that we depend on it pervasively; the fact that defeaters — evidence of unreliability, inconsistency with background beliefs, cues of insincerity — can override testimonial justification on either view; and what both agree is genuinely at stake in the debate. Do not make the agreed claims so thin that they are vacuous.

(2) DISAGREEMENTS TABLE. A table where each row isolates a single point of disagreement. For each row, state the proposition in neutral terms, then give the reductionist's position and the anti-reductionist's position, each phrased so that its own proponent would endorse the wording. Rows should cover at least: whether testimonial justification requires the hearer to possess positive reasons about the speaker's reliability; whether the justificatory structure of testimony is fundamentally the same as that of perception or fundamentally different; and what the case of young children acquiring knowledge from parental testimony shows about the structure of testimonial justification.

(3) CRUXES. Identify the sub-questions on which the dispute turns — the ones such that if they were settled, at least one side would revise its view. For each crux, describe a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and state each side's prediction or interpretation of that case. If either side thinks a crux is empirically decidable, say how; if either side thinks it is conceptual rather than empirical, say why.

(4) VERBAL DISAGREEMENTS. If any part of the dispute turns on different uses of terms like "justification," "reduction," "basic," or "default," identify it and propose a reformulation that isolates whatever substantive disagreement remains after terminological differences are set aside.

The document should read as something both authors would actually put their names to — precise about differences, fair to both views, and useful to a third party trying to understand the state of the debate.
