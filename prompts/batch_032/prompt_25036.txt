I've spent the better part of this year trying to settle, for my own work on scientific explanation, what I think laws of nature are — and I've ended up more puzzled about the state of the debate itself than about the positions in it. The Humean and anti-Humean camps have been at this for decades: on one side, the view that laws are nothing over and above the best-system summary of the total distribution of local matters of particular fact (the Lewisian mosaic); on the other, the view that laws involve some kind of real necessitation or governing relation that is not reducible to the pattern of instances. Both sides agree on the empirical data — the same physical theories, the same observed regularities. Both sides agree that laws support counterfactuals and underwrite explanation in a way that merely accidental regularities do not. And yet the positions have refined themselves without converging: Humeans have gotten more sophisticated about what counts as the "best system," anti-Humeans have developed increasingly careful accounts of what the governing relation might be, and I cannot see that anyone's mind has changed.

I have started to suspect that the impasse is not simply that the question is hard. It feels like something structural: the two sides might be disagreeing about what an account of laws is supposed to do, or about what counts as an acceptable primitive, or about whether a theory of laws is answerable to modal intuitions or to a prior commitment to ontological parsimony. But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I would rather not gesture vaguely at "different standards of explanation" without being precise about what the difference actually is.

So: diagnose this debate. What kind of disagreement is it? Is it a substantive metaphysical dispute that could in principle be resolved, a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, a verbal dispute about the word "law," or two projects answering different questions under the same heading? Support the diagnosis with the structural features of the positions that reveal the nature of the disagreement — not a survey of the literature, but an analysis of what each side demands of a theory and why those demands pull apart. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an honest acknowledgment that no progress should be expected and why.
