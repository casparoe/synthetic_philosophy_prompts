I've been teaching an undergraduate ethics seminar this semester, and we spent the last three weeks working through the paradox of deontology. I thought I understood my own position going in, but the more cases we worked through in class, the less sure I became. The paradox, stated quickly, is that deontological constraints seem to forbid an act type — killing, lying, torturing — even when performing one instance of that act would prevent many more instances of the very same act type. But if the act type is genuinely worth constraining, shouldn't you want fewer instances of it overall? And if not, what exactly does the constraint amount to — a kind of fetishism about keeping one's own hands clean?

I sat down and wrote out my honest, pre-theoretical verdicts on a set of cases that came up in discussion, plus a couple I added myself. Here they are:

1. **Push.** A runaway trolley will kill five people unless I push a large stranger off a footbridge, killing him but stopping the trolley. Verdict: impermissible. I may not push.

2. **Shooter.** A gunman in a café is about to kill five people. The only way to stop him in time is to shoot him dead. Verdict: permissible. This feels fundamentally different from Push, and I'm confident about it.

3. **Intermediary.** A killer has captured five people and will shoot them unless I personally kill a sixth innocent person. (The killer wants to make me complicit; if I refuse, he kills the five and releases the sixth unharmed.) Verdict: impermissible. I cannot kill the one, even to save five from being murdered.

4. **Lying to a Murderer.** The classic case: a murderer asks where my friend is, and I lie, saying she's out of town. Verdict: permissible. Lying is a lesser wrong than killing, and the stakes clearly justify it here.

5. **Five Lies.** I could tell one lie that would prevent five other people from telling lies of comparable seriousness — say, lies on job applications about fabricated credentials. Verdict: impermissible. This feels wrong even though the arithmetic is the same as in the murderer case: one lie prevents five. Something about the fact that what I'm preventing is merely lying, rather than murder, seems to matter.

6. **Self-Defense Against One's Own Future Violations.** I've been dosed with a drug that, in one hour, will cause a psychotic episode during which I will kill five innocent people. The only antidote requires me to kill one innocent person right now. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. If agent-relativity is what separates Shooter from Push — I may kill the threat himself, but not an innocent bystander — then shouldn't this case be more like Shooter? The five future killings would be my own, and agent-relative constraints are supposed to give me special reason to avoid my own violations. Yet the case feels much closer to Push.

7. **Torture.** A terrorist has planted a bomb in a crowded city. Torturing him will reveal its location and save hundreds. Verdict: impermissible. Torture is categorically wrong.

8. **Torture to Prevent Tortures.** Same setup, except the bomb is not an explosive but a device that will slowly torture a hundred people to death. Verdict: still impermissible. But I notice this makes me more uncomfortable than Push does, and I'm not sure why — if anything, the symmetry between act and prevented harm should make the tradeoff more tempting, not less.

I'm trying to figure out whether a single principle can capture all or most of these verdicts, or whether I need to abandon some. In particular, I want to know: what is the best precise statement of a deontological constraint that fits my pattern of verdicts? Which clauses are motivated by which cases? Which of my verdicts does the principle force me to revise, and what does it predict about the drug case that has me stuck? And does the resulting principle actually resolve the paradox, or does it just relocate the tension? Please take a definite position — propose a specific principle and defend it, rather than surveying the menu of options.
