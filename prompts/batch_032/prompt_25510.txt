My friend and I have been going in circles on this argument he built. He thinks it shows deontological constraints are incoherent. I don't buy the conclusion but I can't find the bad step.

(1) If it's wrong to kill an innocent person, that's because killing an innocent person has some bad-making feature — call it W.

(2) If W makes an act wrong when you do it, then W also gives you reason to prevent that act from being done by others, when you can.

(3) So the very thing that grounds the prohibition on killing also grounds a reason to prevent killings.

(4) In a case where you can prevent five killings by committing one killing yourself, the reason described in (3) is a reason to commit the one killing — since that's how you prevent the five.

(5) But the prohibition described in (1) says you must not commit the one killing.

(6) You can't have a reason that both forbids an act and requires it, under the same description. That's a contradiction.

(7) So either deontological constraints aren't grounded in bad-making features of acts (but then what grounds them?), or the constraint gives way when enough of the same kind of violation is at stake (but then it's not a constraint, it's a threshold), or one of the premises is false.

I'm tempted to reject (2) but I worry that makes deontology rest on something weirdly self-regarding — like my reason not to kill isn't about the victim at all, just about keeping my own hands clean. I'd rather keep (2) and reject something else, but I don't know what. Which step fails, and what's the bill?
