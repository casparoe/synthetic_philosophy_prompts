I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working at the intersection of moral psychology and normative ethics. Recently a colleague in the psychology department lent me a popular book on social psychology and morality, and one chapter in particular has been nagging at me. The chapter is about what the author calls "the death of character," and it runs through the classic situationist experiments: Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study (where seminary students stepped over a groaning man when they were told to hurry), Isen and Levin's dime-in-the-phone-booth finding (where discovering a coin dramatically increased helping behavior), and of course the Milgram obedience studies. The author's gloss is that these experiments collectively demonstrate that what we call "character traits" — honesty, compassion, courage — simply don't exist as stable features of persons. Behavior is overwhelmingly driven by situational factors we're not even aware of. The author then claims that virtue ethics, which makes the cultivation of stable character traits central to the moral life, is therefore empirically refuted. The practical upshot, according to the book, is that we should stop thinking in terms of character and virtue, and instead focus entirely on designing environments and incentive structures that make good behavior the path of least resistance.

I know enough to recognize that this is a version of the situationist critique associated with philosophers like Gilbert Harman and John Doris, and I'm aware that virtue ethicists have responded — I've seen references to work by Julia Annas, Rachana Kamtekar, and others — but I haven't yet worked through that literature carefully. The book's summary sounds forceful and the experiments it cites are real, but I have a nagging sense that the author is running together claims of different strengths and perhaps attributing to virtue ethics a more naive empirical psychology than most virtue ethicists actually hold. Could you help me sort out what in this summary a virtue ethicist would grant, what they would dispute, and whether the book's practical conclusion about abandoning character-talk actually follows from the experimental record?
