I've been puzzling over infinite tasks — the kind where you do infinitely many things in a finite amount of time by doing each one twice as fast as the last. I've worked out what I think about a bunch of specific cases, but I can't figure out what principle, if anything, ties my verdicts together. Here they are:

1. **Achilles and the tortoise (the original Zeno version).** To run 100 meters you first cover 50m, then 25m, then 12.5m, and so on — infinitely many sub-journeys. Verdict: this is no obstacle at all. You just run the 100 meters. The infinite breakdown is a redescription, not a real problem.

2. **Thomson's lamp.** A lamp starts off. A switch is flipped after 1 minute, then after 30 more seconds, then after 15 more seconds, and so on, so that by the 2-minute mark infinitely many flips have occurred. Is the lamp on or off at exactly 2 minutes? Verdict: the question has no answer. The description simply fails to determine a final state. It's not that the lamp is on, or off, or both, or neither — the setup is incomplete.

3. **The π printer.** A machine prints the decimal digits of π one at a time: the first digit takes 1 second, the second takes 1/2 second, the third 1/4 second, and so on. After 2 seconds, all infinitely many digits have been printed. Verdict: this is perfectly coherent. The output is the full decimal expansion of π, and the machine's state at any finite time is well-defined — it's printed some finite prefix.

4. **The endless counter.** Same setup as the π printer, but instead of printing digits, the machine adds 1 to a counter at each step, starting from 0. After 2 seconds, what number is on the counter? Verdict: no answer. There is no such thing as the result of adding 1 infinitely many times — the counter doesn't converge to anything.

5. **Bernadete's walls.** A ball rolls toward a wall. At distance 1/2 meter from the wall, there's a barrier programmed to stop the ball if no barrier before it has stopped it. At 1/4 meter, another barrier with the same rule. At 1/8 meter, another. And so on. What happens to the ball? Verdict: the description is contradictory. The ball can't get past all the barriers — there's always one more — but no individual barrier stops it, since each one only acts if none before it did, and there is no first barrier. The scenario describes an impossible situation, not just an underspecified one.

6. **Infinite decisions.** A person decides, at each step in a convergent series of moments before noon, whether to raise their left hand or their right hand. They make infinitely many such decisions by noon. Verdict: impossible. A decision is a mental act that takes some minimum nonzero time. You can't squeeze infinitely many of them into a finite interval.

Now, cases 2 and 3 bother me the most. They have the same structure — an infinite sequence of actions in finite time — yet I'm sure the π printer has a definite outcome and I'm equally sure Thomson's lamp doesn't. What's the difference? Is it that the π printer's partial outputs converge to a limit while the lamp's on/off sequence doesn't? That would also explain case 4 (the counter diverges, so no outcome) and case 1 (Achilles' position converges to the finish line, so it's fine). But then what do I do with case 5 — is convergence even the right kind of explanation for Bernadete's walls, where the problem seems to be a circular dependency rather than a failure of convergence? And case 6 seems to involve a completely different issue, which makes me worry I'm just ad-hoc-ing a separate principle for each case.

So my question is: is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that cleanly separates the coherent supertasks from the incoherent ones across all six cases? If you can find one, what does it say about a case I haven't considered — say, a machine that checks each integer for primality in the same accelerating fashion? And if no single principle works, which of my verdicts should I reconsider?
