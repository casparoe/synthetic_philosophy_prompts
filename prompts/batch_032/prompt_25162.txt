I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and I've been going back and forth on the James-Clifford debate for a seminar I'm taking. You know the shape of it: Clifford says it's wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe on insufficient evidence — epistemic recklessness is a moral failing. James comes back and says look, when you're facing a genuine option that's live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence genuinly doesn't settle it one way or the other, you're not just allowed but required to let your passional nature decide. Waiting for more evidence is itself a choice with risks.

I've been trying to think through a real case where this tension actually bites, and I keep coming back to the 1975 Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA. The short version: in 1974 a group of molecular biologists (Berg, Baltimore, etc.) published a letter calling for a voluntary moratorium on certain recombinant DNA experiments until the risks could be assessed. Then they convened at Asilomar in February 1975 to figure out what guidelines should govern the research going forward. The thing is, at that point nobody actually knew whether recombinant DNA was dangerous. There were plausible nightmare scenarios — engineered pathogens escaping the lab, unintended ecological consequences — and there were also plausible arguments that the fears were overblown and the technology was going to transform medicine. The evidence was radically incomplete in both directions.

So here's what I'm trying to sort out. If you analyze the Asilomar moment strictly through the James-Clifford lens, what happens? On a Cliffordian read, the scientists didn't have sufficient evidence that the experiments were safe, so they shouldn't have proceeded — or at least shouldn't have *believed* they were safe. But here's the symmetry problem: they also didn't have sufficient evidence that the experiments were dangerous, so by the exact same Cliffordian logic they shouldn't have believed they were dangerous either, and a moratorium premised on an unjustified belief in danger looks epistemically irresponsible too. Clifford seems to demand we suspend judgment, but you can't suspend judgment about whether to *act* — the research either goes forward or it doesn't, and doing nothing is still a decision.

On the Jamesian side, this looks like a textbook genuine option: live (the stakes were real), forced (proceed or don't, there's no third way), and momentous (the downstream consequences were enormous either way). So the scientists were entitled — maybe obligated — to let their passional nature decide. But whose passional nature? Paul Berg's caution? James Watson's impatience? The public's fear? And does James give us any resources for adjudicating between competing passions, or does "let your passional nature decide" just relabel whatever you were going to do anyway?

I guess my deeper question is whether the Asilomar episode actually vindicates one of these positions, or whether it reveals that both are missing something important. The conference is often held up as a model of scientific self-governance, but I can't tell if that's a win for James (scientists made a pragmatic leap under uncertainty), a win for Clifford (they hit pause and gathered more evidence before deciding), or a win for neither (maybe the whole thing was really about liability and public relations, not epistemology at all). Would love to hear how you'd map this.
