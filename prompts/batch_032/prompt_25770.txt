I am a novelist, and I have come to suspect that my characters' failures of will are doing too much plot work and too little psychological work. When a character acts against her own settled judgment, I tend to reach for a stock phrase — desire overwhelmed reason, the flesh is weak — and move on. I want something better: a procedure I can run on a scene, ideally during revision, to determine whether a given lapse is a credible instance of akrasia rather than a plot-convenient stumble, a genuine change of mind, or some other phenomenon I have mislabeled as weakness of will.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions I ask about the character and the situation, in order, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. It must distinguish genuine akrasia — acting against one's own better judgment while the judgment remains in place — from at least the following neighbors:

(a) compulsion or addiction, where the character no longer experiences the action as chosen;
(b) a sincere change of values between the resolution and the act, such that by the time she acts she no longer judges the act to be worse;
(c) self-deception about what one's judgment actually was;
(d) ignorance of the particulars at the moment of action (she knows the general principle but fails to see that it applies here);
(e) cases where the character never really formed the intention — she only said she did, or performed the intention for an audience including herself, without the intention's having the right kind of grip on future action.

The procedure should also flag where the boundary between akrasia and these neighboring phenomena becomes murky in ways the philosophical literature itself acknowledges, so that I know when I am in genuinely contested territory rather than simply confused.

I want the procedure to incorporate, in a form I can actually use, what the literature on hyperbolic discounting says about preference reversal over time: the way a smaller-sooner reward can swamp a larger-later one as the moment of choice approaches, even though the agent sincerely prefers the larger-later reward when both are distant. If that framework helps distinguish akratic episodes that will feel inevitable from those that will feel arbitrary or undermotivated, I want that distinction built into the procedure.

Please run the procedure on two characters from my current draft:

1. A woman who has been sober for fourteen months, whose commitment to sobriety is genuine and deeply considered, and who — at her sister's wedding, with the champagne right there — accepts a glass and drinks. She knows what she is doing as she does it. She could refuse. She has not revised her judgment that sobriety is better for her life. She simply takes the glass.

2. A man who has resolved to leave his marriage, spends the morning alone rehearsing the words, and then at dinner looks across the table at his wife and says nothing, and continues saying nothing for another year. He tells himself afterward that the moment was not right, but he also knows, somewhere, that the moment was as right as it was ever going to be.

For each, tell me what the procedure classifies the episode as, and which specific features of the case drive the classification. If the procedure yields an answer I will not like — for instance, classifying as non-akratic something I would have called a paradigm case of weakness of will — flag that and explain why the procedure says what it says.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of cases it will systematically misclassify, what psychological terrain it has no purchase on, and where I will still need to rely on my own sense of the character rather than on the framework. I am not a philosopher; the procedure needs to be usable by someone who can reason carefully about a character's mental states but who does not have the technical vocabulary and should not need it.
