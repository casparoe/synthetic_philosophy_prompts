I've been going down a rabbit hole with Dennett's heterophenomenology and I think I'm sold. The basic move is: if you want a science of consciousness, you treat subjects' first-person reports as data — but as data about what they *believe* they're experiencing, not as an incorruptible window onto consciousness itself. You take those reports seriously, you record them faithfully, but you don't grant them any special epistemic authority. As a novelist, this is basically how I relate to my characters. Their self-narrations matter, but I'm not obligated to treat them as the final truth about what's going on in their heads — the story can reveal they're wrong about themselves.

What I want to know is: what would actually count against this view? Not the standard objections I've already read (the ones about "losing the phenomenon" or whatever). I want a map of what would have to be true for heterophenomenology to fail as a method. What kind of discovery, argument, or conceptual shift would genuinely undermine it? Rank them from decisive to merely awkward. And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested — don't give me the impression there's a consensus among philosophers of mind where there isn't one.
