I'm a novelist — literary fiction, the kind where sentences get workshopped to death — and over the last few years I've found myself drawn to panpsychism. Not in a woo-woo way. It's more that when I'm deep in a character, trying to render what it's like to be someone who isn't me, I keep bumping into this intuition that consciousness isn't the kind of thing that just switches on at a certain level of neural complexity. It feels more like something that's there in the grain of things, and what brains do is filter and organize it into a unified perspective. I know the standard objection is the combination problem — how do all these little micro-experiences add up to one big macro-experience like mine? And I've read some of the responses (the phenomenal bonding relation, the idea that combination is only a problem if you assume subjects can't overlap or fuse). But I want to know what would actually count against this view. Not just what critics say, but what a defender of panpsychism should honestly admit would be a problem. What kind of evidence from neuroscience or physics would be awkward? What would a completed theory of consciousness have to look like for panpsychism to come out looking silly? Are there versions of the combination problem that even the best responses don't touch? And here's the thing I really want to know: has the view been qualified so much that it doesn't actually predict anything different from its rivals? If it has, is that a problem for a metaphysical view, or is that just how metaphysics goes? I'd like the defeaters ranked from "this would be fatal" to "this is a paper cut," and I want an honest answer about whether I'm just aesthetically attached to a picture that's unfalsifiable.
