AI labs routinely subject large language models to "dangerous capability evaluations" before deployment. In these evaluations, researchers test whether a model "knows it is an AI," whether it "pursues power" or "resists shutdown," whether it can "deceive" human evaluators, and whether it "reasons strategically" about its situation. The evaluation reports are written in unapologetically intentional language: models are described as having beliefs, goals, plans, and character traits. Yet many of the same researchers, when speaking to the broader scientific community or the public, insist that these systems are autoregressive next-token predictors — pattern-matchers trained to imitate human text, with no genuine beliefs, intentions, or agency.

Your task is to reconstruct the philosophy of mind that this evaluation practice presupposes, and to assess whether the practice is coherent.

Please structure your response in the following four sections, with approximate lengths as indicated.

1. The practice (roughly 200 words). Describe the evaluation practice in more concrete detail. Give one or two specific examples of the kind of test administered and the kind of conclusion drawn from it. Be precise about what the researchers say the model is doing, and about the intentional vocabulary they use to say it.

2. Candidate presuppositions (roughly 400 words). Identify the main philosophical views of mind and mental-state attribution that could make sense of the practice. Consider at least: (a) Dennett's intentional stance, according to which we attribute beliefs and desires to a system when doing so predicts its behavior, without committing to the existence of internal representational states; (b) a realist view on which sufficiently capable language models literally possess beliefs, goals, and reasoning processes; (c) an instrumentalist or fictionalist view on which intentional language is a useful shorthand that carries no metaphysical weight. For each candidate, say what the practice looks like if that view is true, and what the practice commits its practitioners to.

3. Strict presuppositions versus mere compatibility (roughly 350 words). Distinguish what the evaluation practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Which of the candidate views are ruled out by the practice as currently conducted? Which views are compatible with the practice but not required by it? Pay special attention to the question of whether the intentional stance alone is sufficient: does the practice require that models actually have beliefs and goals, or only that they behave as if they do? If the intentional stance is sufficient, does the "stochastic parrot" characterization become misleading, or can the two be reconciled?

4. Consistency and consequences (roughly 350 words). Assess the internal consistency of the practice. Is there a genuine tension between the intentional language of capability evaluations and the deflationary language of "next-token prediction," or can a single coherent view accommodate both? If there is a tension, which side should give way? Finally, describe what would change in evaluation methodology if we replaced the presupposed view with its strongest rival. Would we design different tests? Would we draw different conclusions from the same test results? Would we stop doing capability evaluations altogether?
