I'm a PhD student in economics and last week two people from my cohort got into a long argument about the Allais paradox during a happy hour. I was nursing a beer and mostly listening, but now I keep turning it over and I can't figure out who had the better of it. Here's my best reconstruction from memory:

The Allais choices, for anyone who needs the reminder: first you pick between A (1M for sure) and B (89% chance of 1M, 10% chance of 5M, 1% chance of nothing). Then you pick between C (11% chance of 1M, 89% nothing) and D (10% chance of 5M, 90% nothing). Most people pick A over B and D over C, which violates the independence axiom — if you factor out the 89% chance of 1M that A and B share, the remaining sub-choice is structurally identical to the C-vs-D choice, so picking A then D is inconsistent.

(1) Dan kicked it off by saying the paradox shows expected utility theory is just descriptively false, and more than that, the independence axiom isn't even normatively compelling. He said certainty has a special value that the axiom simply assumes away, and people who prefer the sure million aren't making a mistake — they're expressing a legitimate preference for eliminating downside risk entirely.

(2) Mira pushed back: the paradox doesn't show independence is false, it shows people are bad at seeing structural equivalence. She said when you rewrite the lotteries to make the common consequence obvious — both A and B give you an 89% chance of 1M, so that component cancels — people flip their preferences. That proves the original pattern was a cognitive error, not a deep preference.

(3) Dan said that's too quick. Even after you explain the restructuring, plenty of people still feel the pull of A over B, and maybe that's not stubborness. Certainty isn't just one more point on the probability scale — a guaranteed outcome eliminates a whole dimension of experience: you don't spend the interval between choice and resolution anxious about what might happen. He said regret and anticipation are real, and a theory of rational choice that ignores them is just stipulating that they don't count.

(4) Mira said that's exactly the problem: you're smuggling psychological states into a normative theory. The independence axiom is about what you have reason to prefer, not about what choice feels better beforehand. Regret is a consequence of the choice-plus-outcome pair, not a feature of the lottery itself, and you can factor it in if you want — just put regret terms directly into the outcome descriptions. But then the axiom still holds for those expanded outcomes. The paradox isn't a counterexample to the axiom; it's a reminder to specify outcomes properly.

(5) Dan came back: "But then you've made the axiom unfalsifiable. If any apparent violation can be handled by saying 'ah, but you didn't include the psychological outcome,' then independence isn't a substantive constraint at all — it's just a bookkeeping rule. And anyway, if I include the peace of mind from certainty as part of the outcome, then my preference for A over B is perfectly consistent with the axiom, and the paradox dissolves. So which is it — is the paradox a cognitive error, or is it evidence that we need richer outcome descriptions? You can't have it both ways."

(6) Mira said those aren't in tension: the paradox is a cognitive error for people who haven't thought about outcome descriptions, and it dissolves for people who have — either way, independence survives. Dan said that's a neat story but it's not what the experimental literature shows: people who've had the restructuring explained still exhibit the effect, and they can give articulate reasons for it. At some point calling it an "error" is just refusing to take their reasons seriously.
