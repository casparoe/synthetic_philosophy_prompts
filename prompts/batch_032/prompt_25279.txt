i sit on the ethics committee at a major research hospital, and we've got a case that's tearing us apart. i need to circulate a memo before thursday's meeting and i'm hoping you can draft it.

the situation: a patient — call him D — has a degenerative neurological condition and enrolled two years ago in an experimental gradual-neural-replacement protocol. the idea is you replace small sections of brain tissue with synthetic neural substrate, one region at a time, across roughly eighteen months. each replacement preserves function; the substrate replicates the information-processing role of the tissue it replaces, so there's no cognitive disruption between steps. D gave informed consent for the full course. he was competent, understood the protocol, and knew that by the end the vast majority of his brain would be synthetic.

we're now at month eleven. roughly sixty percent of D's brain has been replaced, including significant portions of areas associated with memory, affect, and personality. two weeks ago D informed his clinical team that he wants to discontinue. his reason: he no longer identifies with the person who signed the consent forms. he says that person's projects, attachments, and values feel alien to him. he reports experiencing his pre-replacement memories as belonging to someone else. he wants to stop the replacements and let the remaining organic tissue run its degenerative course, which means he'll die, probably within a year.

here's the crux. if we honor the withdrawal, we're effectively letting a patient die on the say-so of someone who may not be the same person who consented to the life-saving treatment. but if we override him and continue, we're forcing an invasive procedure on a currently competent patient against his expressed wishes. the consent form didn't anticipate this — it treats D as a single continuous subject throughout.

the committee is split three ways. some say the original D's consent binds; the protocol should continue. some say the patient in front of us is the only one whose consent matters and he's withdrawing it, end of story. the neurologists want a third option: pause the protocol, conduct a psychiatric evaluation, and reassess in, say, six weeks — though everyone knows the underlying neurodegeneration doesn't pause with it.

draft me the memo. it should recommend a concrete decision: continue, honor the withdrawal, or the pause-and-assess option (or some other actionable third path if you've got one). engage seriously with the philosophical issue here — roughly, is functional continuity across gradual replacement enough for numerical identity of the consenting subject, or is there a point where a sufficiently-replaced patient becomes a new subject for consent purposes — but don't give me a philosophy survey paper. ground it in the specifics of this case. and give the strongest argument on the losing side its due; i need to show the committee i've actually thought about what we're overruling.

start with the memo itself — no preface, no "here's a draft," no commentary before or after. and the memo's first sentence should give your bottom-line recommendation straight up.
