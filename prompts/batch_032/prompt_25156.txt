I'm a junior in high school and I run a small philosophy discussion group that meets during lunch on Thursdays. Lately we've been working through the ethics of consent — not just the obvious cases, but the edge cases that make the standard frameworks creak. Our faculty advisor gave us a framework she called the "standard triad" of conditions that defeat valid consent: deception (being lied to or misled about material facts), coercion (being threatened or subjected to force), and incompetence (being too young, too intoxicated, or otherwise cognitively unable to understand the decision). The claim is that if none of these three obtains, the consent is valid — full stop.

I've been trying to push back on this. It seems to me there are cases where someone agrees to something under perfectly honest, unforced, clear-headed conditions, and yet it feels deeply wrong to say their consent counts. But every time I try to construct my own example, it collapses: what I thought was a distinct fourth problem turns out, on closer inspection, to be coercion in disguise, or a subtle form of deception, or a competence issue I hadn't noticed. I'm starting to wonder whether the triad really is exhaustive and my intuition is just misfiring.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Construct a thought experiment — realistic, grounded in ordinary life (a school, a workplace, a family), no more than one paragraph for the scenario itself — in which one party agrees to something and intuitively their consent should not be treated as valid, but where deception, coercion, and incompetence are all genuinely absent. The problem should trace to a fourth factor: something like exploitation of a vulnerability, or the substantive unfairness of the terms, or a background injustice that shapes the options without amounting to a threat from any identifiable agent. After presenting the scenario, walk me through each of the three standard conditions and explain exactly why it doesn't apply — be precise about where the boundary lies. Then identify and name the fourth factor, and argue that it is genuinely distinct from the other three, not merely one of them wearing a different description. Finally, play devil's advocate: how would a committed defender of the three-condition view respond? Would they try to stretch one of the existing categories (probably coercion) to swallow your case, or would they bite the bullet and insist the consent is valid? Tell me what you think the strongest reply is, and whether you find it convincing.
