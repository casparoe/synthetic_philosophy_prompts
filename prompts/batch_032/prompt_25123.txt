I am the director of organizational development at a large teaching hospital. Over the past year I have been reading Foucault — principally *Discipline and Punish* and parts of *The Birth of the Clinic* — and I have become increasingly unsettled by how much of our institutional life appears to run on the mechanisms he describes: continuous monitoring, normalization through metrics, the examination as a site where observation and judgment merge, the production of compliant subjects through timetables and spatial arrangements. I am not a scholar and do not claim to have mastered the material, but I now find myself unable to look at our resident evaluation system, our patient-satisfaction dashboards, our mandatory wellness modules, or our architecture of observation rooms without seeing them as disciplinary technologies. This is no longer an abstract interest; it has become personally urgent because I help design and maintain these systems.

I want to turn this unease into something my institution can actually use. Please write me a decision procedure — an audit instrument — that a small working group of clinicians and administrators (none of whom have read Foucault) can apply to any existing or proposed institutional practice to determine whether it functions as a disciplinary technology in Foucault's sense, and if so, what kind and with what likely effects. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we can ask about a practice, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a negative finding, and where it hands the question to a person because the judgment is one of values rather than facts.

Constraints: the procedure must be usable in a two-hour meeting by people with no background in the theory. It should flag false positives — practices that look disciplinary on a superficial reading but are not, or that Foucault's framework would not classify that way. It should also flag the limits of the framework: places where Foucault's analysis was specific to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century institutions and may not transfer cleanly to ours, or where his own ambivalence (about whether discipline is strictly oppressive or also productive, for instance) means the procedure cannot settle the matter.

Please then run the procedure on three practices from my own institution:

1. Our system of 360-degree evaluations for residents, in which attending physicians, nurses, and patients all submit quantitative ratings that are aggregated into a single score used for promotion decisions.
2. A proposal to install continuous video monitoring in all ICU rooms, with the stated aim of improving patient safety and allowing remote supervision by attending physicians.
3. Our mandatory annual implicit-bias training module, which all staff must complete, which records completion and time-on-task, and which includes a self-assessment questionnaire whose aggregate results are reported to department heads (without individual names).

For each, tell me what the procedure yields and whether the result is one I should expect my colleagues to resist.

Finally, please identify the assumptions about Foucault's framework that your procedure depends on — for instance, about whether disciplinary power is always objectionable, about whether it can be separated from productive or caring functions, about whether the Panopticon is the right metaphor for digital surveillance — and tell me how the procedure's recommendations would differ if those assumptions were relaxed or reversed. I want to know where the reasoning is most likely to fail, and which argument or consideration would lead to a different audit result if it turned out to be weightier than I currently think.
