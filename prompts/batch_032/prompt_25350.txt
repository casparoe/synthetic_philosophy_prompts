I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, and I've been tracking a pattern in our departmental seminars that I can't find a precise label for. It's somewhere in the neighborhood of verbal disputes and metaontological deflationism, but none of the standard categories seems to fit it exactly, and I'd like help carving it at the joints.

Here are three instances. First: two of our faculty spent most of a colloquium arguing about whether holes exist. After forty minutes, it emerged that they agree on every underlying fact — they agree the cheese has a perforation, that you can put your finger through it, that the perforation isn't made of anything, that "there's a hole in the cheese" is a perfectly acceptable thing to say in ordinary English. What they disagree about is whether, given all those facts, the sentence "holes exist" — read as an ontological claim, not a loose manner of speaking — is true. Neither thinks the other has made a factual error about the cheese; each thinks the other is applying an incorrect standard for what it takes to exist. Second: a structurally identical dispute about whether numbers exist. Both parties agree on all the mathematical facts — they agree that "2 + 2 = 4" is true, that there is no largest prime, that mathematical practice doesn't require us to locate numbers in spacetime. They disagree only about whether, given all that, "numbers exist" is true. Again, neither thinks the other is mistaken about mathematics; they think the other is using the wrong criterion of existence. Third: a dispute about whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that constitutes it. Both agree on all the modal facts (the lump could survive being squashed; the statue couldn't), all the temporal facts (the lump existed before the statue was formed), and all the material facts (they're made of the same stuff). They disagree only about whether the statue is a distinct object from the lump. And again, neither thinks the other has misdescribed the modal, temporal, or material situation.

In all three cases, the disagreement survives full agreement on every first-order fact that can be stated without the contested vocabulary. And yet the participants don't treat the dispute as merely verbal — they think something substantive is at stake, something about which standard is correct, not just which words are convenient. I know Chalmers's framework of verbal disputes and the method of elimination, I know Hirsch's quantifier variance where the two sides mean different things by the quantifier and so both speak truly by their own lights, and I know the deflationist line that these disputes are shallow. But what I'm seeing doesn't match any of these perfectly. It's not a Chalmers-style verbal dispute, because the disagreement persists after all terms are disambiguated — they're not using "exist" in different senses, they're using it in the same sense but with different stringency thresholds. It's not quantifier variance, because they don't seem to mean different things by "there is" — they mean the same thing but disagree about where the line gets drawn. And it's not straightforward deflationism, because the disputants themselves don't experience the dispute as shallow, and they seem to be disagreeing about something normative — which standard one ought to apply — rather than something factual.

Is there an established term for this specific configuration: agreement on all non-ontological facts, shared meaning of the ontological vocabulary, but disagreement about the standard of application? If there is, what are its boundaries, and how exactly does it differ from verbal disputes, quantifier variance, and deflationism? If there isn't, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it doesn't collapse into one of those neighboring categories?
