Last week two of my graduate students got into a sustained argument during our epistemology seminar, and they asked me to referee. I'd like to hear your adjudication before I give them mine.

The topic was whether Goldman's fake-barn case is properly classified as a Gettier case. Here is the exchange as I recall it:

(1) CHEN: The fake-barn case is obviously a Gettier case. Henry sees a real barn, forms the belief "that's a barn," and his belief is true and justified — yet we all agree he doesn't know it's a barn because he's in fake-barn country. That's exactly the Gettier recipe: justified true belief without knowledge.

(2) OKONKWO: I disagree. In Gettier's original cases, the belief is inferred from a falsehood — the disjunction-introduction case, the man with ten coins who will get the job. The justification always runs through a false lemma. In fake barn, Henry's justification is entirely sound: he sees a real barn in good light. There's no false intermediate step. So the structure is different.

(3) CHEN: That's just parochialism about the original examples. Gettier's real contribution was showing that justification and truth can be connected in the wrong way — that the truth of the belief can be lucky relative to the justification. The fake-barn case does exactly that: Henry's belief is true, but given his evidence, it easily could have been false. That's the same underlying phenomenon.

(4) OKONKWO: But then you've collapsed the distinction between two kinds of epistemic luck. In Gettier's cases, the luck intervenes between the justification and the truth — the justification points one way, and something else makes the belief true. In fake barn, the luck is in the environment: Henry's perceptual faculties are working perfectly, and his belief is formed reliably on this occasion. The problem is that he's in a globally hostile environment. That's environmental luck, not intervening luck. If you call both "Gettier cases," you lose that distinction.

(5) CHEN: I'm not sure the distinction does the work you think it does. Whether the luck is "intervening" or "environmental," the upshot is the same: the belief is true in a way that doesn't credit the believer's epistemic performance. That's what matters for knowledge. The false-lemma feature was an artifact of Gettier's particular examples, not the deep point.

(6) OKONKWO: But the distinction matters because the diagnoses are different. For intervening-luck cases, we can try to patch the analysis by ruling out inference from falsehoods. For environmental-luck cases, that patch does nothing — you need a modal condition, like safety or sensitivity. If you insist on calling both "Gettier cases," you obscure the fact that they demand different theoretical responses.

(7) CHEN: That's a pragmatic point about theory-building, not a point about whether fake barn is a Gettier case. The question is whether it's a counterexample to the JTB analysis. It is. Whether it's a counterexample of the same structural type as the original ones is a further question, and I'm happy to grant they differ structurally. But that doesn't make it not a Gettier case — it makes it a different species of Gettier case.

(8) OKONKWO: I think you're using "Gettier case" to mean "any JTB counterexample," which makes the term trivial. If every counterexample to JTB is a Gettier case by definition, then of course fake barn is one — but then we've learned nothing by calling it that. The interesting question is whether fake barn reveals the same underlying defect in the JTB analysis that Gettier's original cases revealed. I say it reveals a different defect.
