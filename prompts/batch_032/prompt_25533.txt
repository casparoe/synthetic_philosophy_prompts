**Question:** Explain Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and outline the multiple drafts model he proposes as an alternative. What is the role of "fame in the brain" in this model?

**Student's answer:**

Dennett argues that most theories of consciousness, even those that reject dualism, implicitly rely on what he calls the "Cartesian theater" — the idea that there is a place in the brain where sensory inputs are brought together and presented to an inner observer, a kind of homunculus, who then experiences them. Dennett's central objection is that this picture leads to an infinite regress: if the homunculus is what does the experiencing, then we need to explain how the homunculus itself is conscious, which just pushes the problem back a step.

As an alternative, Dennett proposes the multiple drafts model. On this view, there is no single place or moment where consciousness happens. Instead, the brain processes information in many parallel streams simultaneously, constantly editing and revising its representations. Different neural coalitions compete with one another, and the one that wins — the one that achieves the most influence over behavior and verbal report — becomes the conscious experience. This competitive process is what Dennett means by "fame in the brain": a representation becomes conscious when it gains enough influence to shape what the person says and does, analogous to how a person becomes famous once they are widely talked about.

The multiple drafts model thus replaces the idea of a single conscious "moment" with a distributed, competitive process. There is no final draft, only a series of provisional ones, and what we call consciousness is simply the draft that happens to be most influential at any given time. This also explains why we can be mistaken about the timing of our conscious experiences, as shown in phenomena like the color phi illusion.
