I've been covering behavioral economics and game theory for nearly a decade, and there's an argument I first heard at a workshop on rational choice that I've never managed to settle to my own satisfaction. The speaker was using the dollar auction—a game Martin Shubik introduced, where participants bid for a dollar bill with the rule that both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their bids, but only the highest bidder gets the dollar—to make a point about escalation. But the more I turned the argument over, the more it seemed to prove something that can't be right, or at least something that doesn't square with a world full of protracted litigation, military quagmires, and infrastructure projects that double their budgets while everyone watches.

Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it from my notes, laid out step by step. (1) Once the bidding in a dollar auction passes the one-dollar mark, both remaining bidders are already facing a net loss: the current high bidder will lose something if they win, and the current second-highest bidder will lose their entire bid if they drop out. (2) At that point, the second-highest bidder faces a choice between dropping out and absorbing a certain loss, or raising the bid by the minimum increment and hoping the other player drops out—thereby turning a larger certain loss into a smaller possible loss. (3) Since a smaller loss is better than a larger one, the rational move is always to raise. (4) This reasoning applies symmetrically to both players at every subsequent step, so once the bidding crosses the dollar threshold, it will escalate without limit—or at least until one player exhausts their resources. (5) Rational players, foreseeing this trap from the very beginning, should therefore never make an opening bid. The entire game unravels by backward induction: no rational person would bid even one cent, knowing that doing so commits them to an inescapable escalatory spiral. (6) And yet, dollar auctions run in experimental laboratories reliably produce escalating bids well past the dollar mark, often reaching three or four dollars before someone capitulates. (7) More troublingly, real-world situations that share the same incentive structure—arms races, lawsuits whose costs dwarf the damages at stake, failing military interventions, corporate projects that outrun their original business case—seem to trap sophisticated, well-advised actors with alarming regularity.

So the argument appears to prove that rational agents would never enter a dollar auction or any structurally analogous situation, while the evidence suggests that something in the neighborhood of rational agency does enter them, and often. I can see several places where the reasoning might slip, but each one seems to demand a price I'm not sure I want to pay. If I reject the backward induction step, I seem to be giving up on a standard solution concept that works perfectly well in finite extensive-form games like chess. If I reject the premise that reducing a certain loss is always the rational move, I need an alternative account of practical reasoning under sunk costs—and that alternative might end up licensing behavior that looks straightforwardly irrational on any standard theory. If I argue that real-world escalation isn't really a dollar auction because the game has an uncertain endpoint or because the players are not strictly rational, I've merely pushed the problem elsewhere without explaining why the structural analogy feels so tight, or why experimental subjects who understand the game perfectly still walk into the trap. Which step in this argument should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
