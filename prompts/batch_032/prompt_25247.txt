I'm on the clinical AI oversight committee at our teaching hospital, and we're deadlocked on this one. The proposal is to deploy an ML triage system in the ED that would autonomously assign acuity scores and — here's the sticking point — discharge low-risk patients without any physician sign-off. The model was trained on five years of our own data and actually outperforms our junior residents on retrospective metrics, so the clinical case looks solid. But the legal side is a mess. The vendor's contract has the standard indemnification language, and our counsel says that if the system clears a patient who then crumps, liability almost certainly lands on the attending who's nominally "supervising" the deployment, even though nobody actually reviews those low-risk discharges in practice.

Half the committee says this is no different from adopting any other tool — we don't sue the pulse-ox manufacturer when someone desats — and the other half says there's a qualitative difference when the system is making the discharge call de facto autonomously, creating a gap where no human is genuinely accountable for the decision. The latter group keeps saying things like "a decision was made but nobody made it," which sounds like a slogan but I think there's a real argument under there. I need to draft a short decision memo for the chair: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It has to engage the responsibility-gap objection seriously rather than just gesturing at it, and give a rationale that both the clinical faction and the legal-and-ethics faction can live with. The chair also wants me to address the obvious fallback — "just require a physician to review every discharge" — and say whether that actually solves the problem or just relocates it.
