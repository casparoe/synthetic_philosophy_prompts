I've been reading about hedonism as a theory of well-being — the view that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad — and I keep running into the heterogeneity objection. The worry, roughly, is that the things we call pleasures are so phenomenologically diverse that it is unclear they share any common property at all. The pleasure of solving a proof, the pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of hearing a friend's voice after a long absence, the pleasure of winning an argument, the pleasure of morphine — these do not feel like instances of the same kind of thing. If "pleasure" is just a catch-all label for whatever we happen to like or pursue, then hedonism risks becoming either vacuous (it tells us to maximize pleasure, but "pleasure" just means whatever we maximize) or false (if it insists on a specific phenomenal quality, many of the things we call pleasures lack it). And the problem cuts deeper than phenomenology: even if we set aside what pleasures feel like and focus on their functional role, it is not obvious that the pleasure of anticipation, the pleasure of relief, the pleasure of flow, and the pleasure of sensory gratification all play the same kind of role in our psychology.

Before I evaluate how well particular versions of hedonism handle this, I want to know what an adequate theory of pleasure would have to deliver. Write the desiderata: what must a theory of pleasure get right to count as solving the heterogeneity problem? Which cases must it accommodate (e.g., the pleasure of anticipation, the pleasure of relief, intellectual pleasures, aesthetic pleasures, pleasures that seem to have no bodily location, pleasures whose intensity and duration trade off in counterintuitive ways)? Which distinctions must it respect (e.g., between pleasure and desire-satisfaction, between pleasure and mere absence of pain, between occurrent pleasure and dispositional enjoyment, between first-order pleasures and higher-order pleasures about one's own mental states)? Which empirical results from psychology or neuroscience must it be consistent with? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Flag which requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are parochial commitments of particular camps. And if you suspect that some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — that any theory of pleasure that meets some requirements will necessarily fail others — say so and explain the tension. A brief note on how the main approaches (e.g., attitudinal theories, felt-quality theories, functionalist accounts) fare against the list is welcome, but the criteria themselves are what I am after.
