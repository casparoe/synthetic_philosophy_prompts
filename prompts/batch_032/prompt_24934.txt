So I've been thinking about resentment lately — like, when it feels justified to resent someone and when it doesn't. Not in a abstract philosophical way (I'm not a philosopher, I just fell down a rabbit hole after a bad falling-out with a friend). I started keeping track of my gut reactions to different situations, real and imagined, and now I'm trying to figure out if there's a coherent principle behind them or if I'm just inconsistent. Here's what I've got:

- A colleague takes credit for my work in a meeting. I resent them. Seems clear.
- A friend forgets we had plans because their parent had a medical emergency. I don't resent them at all.
- A friend forgets we had plans because they were binge-watching a show and lost track of time. I resent them, though less than the colleague case.
- Someone with severe schizophrenia says something cruel to me during an episode. I don't resent them — I just feel sad and want them to get help.
- A person diagnosed with antisocial personality disorder lies to me and manipulates me for their own gain. I resent them, strongly, even though I believe their condition means they literally cannot empathize or grasp why what they did was wrong.
- A coworker with OCD interrupts meetings constantly with compulsive rituals. I don't resent them; I see it as something to be accommodated.
- Someone who grew up in an extremely abusive household and never had a chance to learn emotional regulation screams at me during an argument. I'm genuinely torn — part of me resents them, part of me thinks they never had a fair shot at turning out differently.
- A stranger cuts me off in traffic because they're texting. I resent them.
- A stranger cuts me off in traffic because they're rushing someone to the hospital. I don't resent them.

The ones that bug me are the sociopath case and the abusive-upbringing case sitting next to the schizophrenia and OCD cases. In all four, there's a sense in which the person "can't help" being the way they are — but I'm pulling in totally different directions. With the schizophrenic person and the OCD coworker, I drop resentment entirely. With the sociopath, I don't. With the abuse survivor, I'm stuck in the middle.

Is there a principle that makes sense of all these verdicts? And if not, which ones should I reconsider? Also, what would your principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: someone commits a serious crime while in the grip of a severe addiction — not a one-time lapse, but a years-long substance use disorder that has demonstrably rewired their decision-making. Should I resent them, or not?

Please keep your answer to under 400 words — I want the principle stated clearly and then applied, not a essay.
