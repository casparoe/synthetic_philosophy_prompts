As I understand it, the puzzle runs like this. All emeralds observed so far have been green. But define "grue" as "green if first observed before some arbitrary future time t, and blue if first observed at or after t." All emeralds observed so far were also first observed before t, so the evidence equally supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue." Yet we unproblematically project "green" into the future and find "grue" absurd. The question is what licenses this asymmetry — why are some predicates projectible and others not? To me this looks like a special case of something I deal with daily: given a finite training set, infinitely many functions fit the data perfectly but diverge on held-out points. We handle this with regularization, inductive biases, choice of hypothesis class, and so on. Goodman's own move, as I gather, was to appeal to "entrenchment" — the idea that "green" has a history of successful past projections and "grue" does not, and that this history is what makes a predicate projectible.
