A friend in my political philosophy seminar walked me through this and I can't find the flaw. (1) The Condorcet jury theorem says: if each voter on a binary question has a better-than-50% chance of being correct, and votes are independent, then as the group grows the probability that a majority is correct approaches 1. (2) On many political questions — minimum wage, trade policy, criminal justice reform — ordinary voters are at least slightly better than a coin flip. They have some relevant experience, they consume some news, they're not just guessing randomly. (3) Their votes are independant enough — they're not all reading the same newspaper or talking to the same people. (4) So for a large electorate, the majority is almost certainly right on these questions. (5) Therefore, on these questions, I should defer to majority opinion over my own judgment and over expert opinion. (6) But this is absurd. If 65% of the public believes something false about vaccine safety, the majority is just wrong, and I shouldn't update toward them. (7) So something's broken in (1)-(4). But when I try to reject (2) or (3), I worry I'm also undermining the epistemic case for democracy altogether — and I don't want to end up there. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
