I'm in a philosophy of language reading group and we've been doing Moore's paradox for a couple weeks now. I'm supposed to write up my "philosophical profile" on it — basically where I've landed on the main issues — so the others can poke holes in it before I write my term paper. I've got five commitments and I'm worried some of them might not play nice together, but I can't quite put my finger on where the problems are. Here they are:

(1) Moore-paradoxical assertions (like saying "It's raining but I don't believe it") are irrational because assertion is governed by a knowledge norm: you should assert p only if you know p. When you assert p, you represent yourself as knowing p, and knowing p entails believing p — so the second conjunct flatly contradicts what the first conjunct represents about your epistemic state. That's the whole story for the assertion case.

(2) The knowledge norm isn't just a rule of thumb — it's constitutive of assertion. An utterance that isn't backed by knowledge doesn't just violate a norm; it fails to be a genuine assertion in the first place, the way a move that violates the rules of chess isn't a bad chess move but not a chess move at all.

(3) Moore-paradoxical thoughts (silently thinking "p but I don't believe p") are a different beast. Their irrationality comes from a failure of self-knowledge — you're in a defective epistemic position with respect to your own mind. But it's not the same kind of logical or pragmatic contradiction you get in the assertion case.

(4) There are genuine cases of self-deception where someone can have the thought "p but I don't believe p" and it's not irrational — it's an accurate description of a divided mind. Think of someone who has all the evidence their partner is cheating but can't bring themselves to consciously acknowledge it. They might sincerely think "my partner is cheating on me, but I don't believe it" — and that thought is true, not irrational.

(5) The transparency of belief (Evans's point that when asked whether you believe p, you answer by looking to whether p is true, not by introspecting) explains why Moore-paradoxical thoughts are irrational: you can't coherently determine that p is true and then, on that basis, conclude you don't believe p. The procedure for answering the belief question just doesn't output that result.

So those are my five. I can feel that (4) is pulling against something but I can't quite articulate what, and I'm also not sure whether (1) and (5) are giving compatible explanations or whether one makes the other redundant. Could you go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity? And if I had to give one up, which revision would cost me the least?
