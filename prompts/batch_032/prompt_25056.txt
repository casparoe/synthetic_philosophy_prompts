As part of research for a novel I am writing about religious doubt, I have been sitting in on an undergraduate philosophy of religion course. The professor has asked me to help with grading, and I have been given the following exam question and a student's answer to evaluate. I am not a trained philosopher, so I would appreciate a rigorous, point-by-point assessment.

Exam question: "Explain J.L. Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness, making clear how it differs from the evidential problem of evil. Then assess whether the skeptical theist's response to the problem of evil also works as a response to the hiddenness argument."

Student answer:

"Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness begins with the premise that a perfectly loving God would desire a personal relationship with every human being. For such a relationship to be possible, a person must believe that God exists. Therefore, if a perfectly loving God existed, he would ensure that everyone believes in him. Yet there are people who genuinely seek God but do not find him—these are what Schellenberg calls 'nonresistant nonbelievers.' Their existence is incompatible with a perfectly loving God, so such a God does not exist.

The argument differs from the evidential problem of evil in an important way. The problem of evil turns on the existence of gratuitous suffering; even if one could explain why God permits suffering, the hiddenness argument would remain, because it concerns the absence of belief rather than the presence of pain. The two arguments are therefore independent challenges to theism.

Skeptical theism holds that because our cognitive faculties are limited, we are not in a position to judge whether God has morally sufficient reasons for permitting the evils we observe. This response can be extended to the hiddenness argument: if we cannot discern God's reasons for permitting evil, we likewise cannot discern his reasons for remaining hidden. Moreover, we can imagine plausible reasons God might have—for instance, God might withhold clear evidence of his existence to allow for a more authentic, freely chosen faith, much as a parent might step back to let a child develop independence. Since skeptical theism shows we cannot rule out such reasons, Schellenberg's argument fails."
