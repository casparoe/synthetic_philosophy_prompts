My mom died two months ago. She had ALS, and near the end she was on a ventilator, fully conscious but unable to move anything except her eyes. She'd made it clear, years before, that she didn't want to live that way indefinitely, and she'd signed an advance directive. But when the moment came — when the doctors said she could probably live another six months on the vent, mostly lucid, or we could extubate and let her go — my sister and I still had to say the words. We said to extubate. She died about four hours later, with us holding her hands.

I'm not a philosopher. I run a small bakery. But the hospital where she died has an ethics committee, and they've asked me to give a ten-minute talk at their annual ethics symposium next month — the "family perspective" slot. The audience will be mostly doctors, nurses, and a handful of clinical ethicists. I've been reading around (probably too much) and I keep comming back to the doing/allowing distinction — the idea that there's a moral difference between killing and letting die. Some of the reading says the distinction is mostly bogus, that withdrawing a ventilator is morally the same as giving a lethal injection and we should just own that. Other stuff says no, there's a real difference, and the law gets it basically right by treating them differently. I honestly don't know where I land, and I think that's part of what I want the talk to be about: not resolving the philosophy, but showing what it looks like when you have to live inside the distinction, when the words you say to a doctor are the difference between your mom dying now or dying in six months.

Can you write me the talk? It should be about ten minutes spoken. I want it to be honest about the philosophical difficulty — not a sentimental "I made peace with it" story, because I haven't. The doing/allowing stuff should do real work in the structure of what I say, not just get name-dropped. But it also has to land for clinicians who've never read a philosophy paper, and for the one or two ethicists in the room who've read all of them. I don't want to lecture anyone; I want to give them something true from the inside.
