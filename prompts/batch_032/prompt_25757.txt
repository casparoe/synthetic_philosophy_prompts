I'm a novelist working on a book whose protagonist struggles with whether her choices matter in a world that feels to her already decided. In the course of my research I've been reading around in decision theory and philosophical logic, and I came across a passage in a popular book on rational choice that summarizes something called the Death in Damascus paradox. I don't have the training to tell whether the summary is fair or whether it's quietly nudging me toward conclusions the paradox doesn't actually support. Here is the passage, reconstructed from my notes:

"A man is told by a perfectly reliable oracle that he will die in Damascus tomorrow. He decides to flee to Aleppo. But if the oracle is perfectly reliable, then he will die in Damascus no matter what he does — perhaps his horse throws a shoe on the road, or he changes his mind en route and turns back. His attempt to flee is therefore futile. The paradox is meant to show that if the future is genuinely knowable in advance, we lack free will in any meaningful sense: our choices cannot alter what has already been foreseen. The author goes on to claim that this refutes causal decision theory, which holds that you should always choose the action that causally produces the best outcome, because in this case fleeing to Aleppo causally leads to death in Damascus just as surely as staying does. The lesson, according to the book, is that rational choice requires taking into account what your choice reveals about the world — what it is evidence for — and not merely what it causes."

I suspect this summary runs together several things. The free-will conclusion feels as though it belongs to a different debate than the decision-theoretic one, and the claim that fleeing "causally leads" to death in Damascus strikes me as exactly the sort of move the paradox is designed to put pressure on rather than settle. But I don't know the literature well enough to be confident in these hunches, and I need to know whether I can trust this book as a guide for my own thinking about agency and fate.

Could you go through this summary and tell me which parts a philosopher working on decision theory would accept, which parts they would find misleading or simply wrong, and whether the lesson the book draws actually follows from anything accurate in the summary?
