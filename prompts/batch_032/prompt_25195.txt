I've been deep in Borges for a novel I'm working on — the one with the library, you know the kind of thing — and it's made me realize I have no idea what I actually think about time. Not the physics kind, but the what-is-it-really kind. I've got strong gut reactions to a bunch of temporal ideas I've either written or read or just lived with, and I want to know if there's a coherent view underneath them or if I'm just a mess.

Here are my verdicts, as honest as I can make them:

1. When I wrote a story where every choice branches off a new world — pure Borges, I know — it felt true to something about regret, the sense that the unchosen life is still somehow real, still happening somewhere. But I don't believe it. I don't think the universe actually splits every time I decide between tea and coffee. So my writer-self believes it and my person-self doesn't. Is that a real tension or just me being precious?

2. The eternal return — Nietzsche's version where everything repeats exactly, infinitely — I find that viscerally horrifying in a way that feels like evidence against it. If a view of time makes me want to crawl out of my skin, my gut says it's false. But I don't trust that as an argument, and I don't know what to do with the fact that I'm having it anyway.

3. Presentism — only the present moment exists, the past is gone and the future isn't real yet — this feels like common sense. It's what I believe when I'm not thinking about it. But here's the problem: I also believe that a story's ending can change the meaning of its beginning. The final page reaches back and transforms chapter one. If the past is gone, how can that be? And that's not just a literary point; I think it's true of life too. The meaning of an event depends on what comes after, and that seems to require that the event is still somehow available to be reinterpreted. So I'm a presentist who also believes the past isn't finished. That can't be right.

4. The block universe — all moments equally real, the whole thing just sitting there in four dimensions — I hate this. It makes narrative impossible. If everything already is, then nothing is at stake. A story is about things that might not have happened; if they were always going to happen, why tell it? But I worry that hating a view isn't the same as refuting it, and that my reasons for hating it are just professional self-interest dressed up as philosophy.

5. Borges's argument in "A New Refutation of Time" — roughly, that since we can't prove the self persists from moment to moment, time itself might be a fiction — I find this electrifying. When I read it I thought: yes, this is what I've been trying to say in fiction for years. But I don't believe it. I can't. I live in time, I age, I have deadlines. The argument is beautiful and I'm almost convinced while I'm reading it and then I put the book down and I'm not.

6. Here's the case I keep going back and forth on. When I'm writing well, really deep in it, time vanishes. Hours pass and I don't notice. This isn't just distraction — it feels like I'm touching something real about time, like the ordinary experience of time as a flowing river is the illusion and the writing-trance is the truth. But I also think that's exactly the kind of thing people say to make themselves feel profound, and it's probably just a psychological state with no metaphysical implications. I can't settle this one.

7. A character who remembers the future — not predicts it, remembers it. I wrote one once and it felt completely different from just telling a story in reverse. It felt like I was commiting to a view where the future is as fixed as the past. And I recoiled from that. I don't want the future to be fixed. But I can't give a reason why the past being fixed is fine and the future being fixed is terrible, except that one has already happened and the other hasn't — which is just restating the distinction, not justifying it.

So: is there a single view of time that makes sense of these verdicts? Or at least most of them? I want you to tell me what I believe — what the best candidate theory is, which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it says about the case I haven't been able to settle (the writing-trance one). Don't give me a menu of options and leave me to choose; I want a committed answer. Tell me what position I should hold and why, and which of my intuitions I need to get over.
