Teaching decision theory next term and was skimming one of those pop-philosophy books students always cite, and it's got this paragraph on the Pasadena paradox that I'm pretty sure is wrong in at least a couple places but I want to be sure I'm not overreacting. Here's the passage:

'The Pasadena game goes like this: flip a fair coin until you get heads. If it takes n flips, you win (−1)^(n−1) × 2^n/n dollars — so you gain on odd n and lose on even n. The expected value is the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln(2), about 69 cents. So you should be willing to pay anything less than that to play. The twist is that because the series converges only conditionally, you can rearrange its terms to produce any expected value you want — make it a million dollars, make it negative a million. The upshot is that expected value theory can't handle games with conditionally convergent payoffs, since the result is just an artefact of how you describe the game.'

I've got three worries but I'd love a systematic take. First: is the expected value actually ln(2)? My recollection is that's only true under one particular ordering of the state space, and that the whole point is there's no privileged ordering — so saying 'the expected value is 69 cents' is already begging the question. Second: what does 'rearranging the terms' cash out to, physically? Like, are we actually changing the game or just redescribing it? The summary makes it sound like a cheap trick about description, but I dimly recall Nover and Hájek making a deeper point about the lack of a canonical ordering of states. Third: is the moral right? Does conditionally convergent expectation really mean expected value theory 'can't handle' the game, or just that we need to be more careful? Go ahead and be pedantic, I want to know which sentences I'd have to correct if a student wrote this.
