My mom was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's last year, and last week her neurologist offered her a spot in a clinical trial for a new kind of treatment. The way it works is they inject these nanobots that gradually replace damaged neurons with synthetic ones—functionally identical, same connections, same firing patterns, everything. Over about eighteen months, essentially all the neurons in her brain would be swapped out, one by one, while the synthetic ones pick up right where the biological ones left off. The idea is that by the end, the Alzheimer's pathology is gone because there's nothing left for it to degrade, but her memories, personality, and cognitive function are preserved—maybe even improved.

My mom is terrified. Not of the procedure itself, but of what it means. She keeps saying things like, "I won't be in there anymore—it'll just be a thing walking around with my memories, and everyone will treat it like it's me, but I'll be gone." She's asked me whether she'd still be herself at the end, and honestly, I don't know what to tell her. On one hand, if you replace one neuron, she's obviously still her. And if you replace a second, same thing. It seems like at no individual step does she stop being herself. But at the end, every original neuron is gone. So when did she disappear—if she did? I feel like there's a real puzzle here and I can't just dismiss her fear as confused, but I also can't bring myself to say "yeah mom, you'd basically die." 

I guess what I'm asking is: is her fear philosophically well-founded? Would she survive the procedure? And if you think she would, what's the best way to explain it to someone who feels, in her gut, that a total replacement means she's no longer the one having the experiences? I'm supposed to help her decide whether to enroll by next month and I feel stuck between two intuitions that both seem right.
