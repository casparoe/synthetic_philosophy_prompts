I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether we are permitted to discount the well-being of future people purely because they live in the future. The argument kept looping because we had no shared standard for what a theory of intergenerational discounting is even supposed to do. By the end I realized that before I take a side, I need the specification laid out in full.

So: write the desiderata. What must an adequate theory of how we should value the well-being of future generations relative to present well-being actually deliver? I want the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results from economics and ethics it must be consistent with, and the puzzles it must dissolve or at least squarely address. For each requirement, give the consideration that makes it a requirement. Flag which are non-negotiable and which are contested between camps—for instance, between those who defend a positive pure rate of time preference and those who regard it as ethically indefensible; between those who treat the Ramsey formula as the right framework and those who reject it; between those who think uncertainty about extinction settles the matter and those who think it merely complicates it. I especially want to know whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—whether, for example, the demand for impartiality across generations and the demand for sensitivity to the opportunity cost of capital pull in opposite directions and cannot both be fully honored. That is the part that would have saved me three hours.
