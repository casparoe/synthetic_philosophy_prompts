I'm a science journalist and an editor at a magazine -- we cover everything from cosmology to behavioral economics, readers are smart but not specialists. We have a hard rule against lying: no fabricated quotes, no invented data, no saying something happened when it didn't. But that rule only covers the easy cases. The hard ones are the editorial calls where every sentence is technically true but the piece as a whole might be misleading someone.

I'm talking about things like: we leave out a study's third experimental condition because it was underpowered and the graph is already crowded. We swap a technical term for a more familiar one that's not quite right but reads better. We quote someone accurately but we picked the one sentence that makes them sound foolish when the full interview was nuanced. We write "the study found X" when the study found X in a subgroup analysis that wasn't preregistered and the primary outcome was null. We use a metaphor that's vivid and gets the gist across but implies a causal mechanism that isn't there.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run in maybe 15 minutes while editing a piece, without having read the philosophy literature myself. Take whatever the best philosophical work says about the lying/misleading distinction -- implicature, what's said versus what's communicated, the role of speaker intentions, all of that -- and turn it into a checklist or a sequence of questions I ask about a specific editorial choice. The procedure should classify the choice as: clean, misleading, or "this is a judgment call the procedure can't settle, you have to decide."

The procedure has to handle the fact that in journalism you're always selecting and simplifying -- the question is where selection becomes distortion. It should flag the points where the procedure itself relies on a judgment that a reasonable person could make differently.

Then run the procedure on two or three realistic cases from science journalism. Make at least one of them a case where the procedure gives an answer I'm not going to like -- where it says "clean" but my gut says otherwise, or where it says "misleading" about something I'd normally let slide. And tell me honestly what the procedure can't catch: the cases where I'll follow every step and still end up misleading a reader.
