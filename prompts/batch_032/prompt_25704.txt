I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about Parfit's hitchhiker case and whether the notion of "rational irrationality" is even coherent. Neither of us gave ground, and I've been turning the argument over in my head since. I'd like you to go through our exchange and tell me who is right about what — not just who wins overall, but a point-by-point adjudication of the moves we each made.

Here is the setup we both accepted. Parfit asks us to imagine a hitchhiker stranded in a desert. A driver will stop only if she believes the hitchhiker will not rob her. The hitchhiker knows that, once inside the car, the rational thing to do — on a narrow, self-interested conception of rationality — is to rob the driver: there is no chance of being caught, the payoff is large, and the driver will be miles away before anyone notices. The driver, being rational, anticipates this and drives on. But suppose the hitchhiker can take a drug before setting out that makes him, for a limited time, genuinely unable to harm anyone — a change in his dispositions and decision procedure, not merely a constraint on his physical options. The driver would then stop, the hitchhiker would survive, and he would be better off than if he had stayed rational. So it seems rational to make yourself irrational.

My friend's position, stated bluntly, is that the whole idea is incoherent. His first move was: you cannot rationally decide to become irrational, because the very act of weighing reasons and deciding presupposes the rationality you are trying to eliminate — it is like trying to jump over your own shadow. I replied that we do analogous things all the time: burning bridges, signing contracts with penalty clauses, Odysseus tying himself to the mast. Those are all cases of rationally constraining your future self. He answered that those are not cases of becoming irrational. Odysseus did not make himself irrational; he merely limited his options. He could still hear the sirens and want to go to them; he just could not act on the desire. That is rational self-binding, not rational irrationality. The drug, by contrast, changes your very preferences or decision procedure — it makes you a different kind of agent.

I pushed back: the pre-drug self can still run an expected-utility calculation. Taking the drug leads to being picked up and surviving; not taking it leads to dying in the desert. The calculation is straightforward. What could be irrational about acting on it? My friend replied that the calculation treats the future irrational self as a reliable instrument for achieving the present self's goals, but an irrational agent is, by definition, not a reliable instrument. The drug might make you trustworthy, but it might also make you do any number of other irrational things — you cannot selectively engineer your irrationality to serve your rational purposes. I said this was an empirical objection to a particular drug, not a conceptual objection to rational irrationality as such. If the drug specifically and reliably prevents you from harming innocents and has no other effects, the calculation goes through.

At this point my friend shifted ground. He said: if the drug merely makes you unable to harm innocents, it is not making you irrational at all — it is making you more moral, or at least more consistent with moral norms you might already endorse. The whole puzzle, he argued, only gets its grip if we assume that robbing the driver is the rational thing to do. But if that assumption leads to the conclusion that rationality demands its own abandonment, then we have a reductio of the narrow, self-interested conception of rationality — not a vindication of the drug. I answered that this was a different argument from the one he started with. He began by claiming the notion of rational irrationality was internally incoherent; now he was saying it reveals a flaw in a particular theory of rationality. Those are distinct claims, and conceding the second does not establish the first.

We went in circles from there. I want you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each step in the argument, tell me who had the better of it and why. And I want a committed verdict: do not tell me both sides have a point and leave it there. Tell me which arguments succeed, which fail, and who is more right overall — and whether the concept of rational irrationality survives the exchange intact, needs reformulation, or should be abandoned.
