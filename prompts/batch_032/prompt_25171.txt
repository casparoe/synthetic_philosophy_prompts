Last weekend at a dinner party, I watched two friends — Elena, a clinical psychology PhD student, and Marcus, who's in a philosophy of science program — get into a long argument about psychiatric diagnosis. They were specifically discussing borderline personality disorder. Elena had just described a patient she'd seen in her practicum who'd made real progress with dialectical behavior therapy, and Marcus kind of grimaced. Someone asked him why, and he said he's been reading Ian Hacking on looping effects and he's increasingly convinced that personality disorder diagnoses aren't real in the way we tend to assume.

Elena pushed back hard. She said BPD has a well-replicated factor structure, shows substantial heritability in twin studies, and responds differentially to specific treatments like DBT — if that's not a real condition, what is? Marcus said that's not the kind of reality he's questioning. His point, he said, is that when you classify people as having BPD, they learn what the classification means, they reshape their self-understanding around it, they join online communities, they start describing their experiences in the vocabulary of the diagnosis — and this changes what BPD looks like in the population. The classification loops back and alters the thing classified. That doesn't happen with, say, the influenza virus. So psychiatric kinds are ontologically different from medical kinds.

Elena replied that this is a difference of degree, not kind. Diabetics also change their behavior after diagnosis — they monitor their blood sugar, they change their diet — and those behavioral changes become part of the clinical picture of managed diabetes. Every diagnosis has feedback effects. Marcus said the difference is that for BPD, the feedback doesn't just affect the course of the condition; it partly constitutes what the condition is. The diagnostic criteria for BPD include things like identity disturbance and chronic feelings of emptiness, which are themselves shaped by the cultural and clinical categories available for making sense of one's inner life. You can't separate the condition from the classification in the way you can separate a virus from the test that detects it.

At this point a third friend, Sam, jumped in and said Marcus seemed to be arguing that BPD isn't real, but all he'd actually shown is that it's a different kind of thing than a viral infection — which everyone already knew. Marcus said no, his claim was stronger: looping effects make psychiatric categories unstable in a way that undermines the realist project of carving nature at its joints, because the kind itself shifts in response to being named. Elena said that's just a fancy way of saying we should do more longitudinal research, and that instability in a category's boundaries doesn't mean the category fails to pick out something real — after all, species boundaries shift too, and no one says species aren't real.

I stayed out of it, but I've been turning the exchange over in my head. Could you go through their back-and-forth move by move and say who has the better of each point? I'm less interested in who wins overall than in understanding exactly where the disagreement bottoms out and whether either of them is missing something important.
