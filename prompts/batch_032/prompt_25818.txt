I've been chewing on this thing I keep seeing in arguments and I wonder if there's a name for it in philosophy. Here's the pattern. Person A does something wrong. Person B calls them out, and B's criticism is totally accurate — the facts are right, the moral reasoning is sound. But B is themselves guilty of the very same thing (or something close to it). Now, nobody's saying B's criticism is false because B is a hypocrite. The point is different: it feels like B just doesn't get to make that criticism. Like they've lost the ticket to say it, even though what they're saying is true.

Two examples to make it concrete. First: a politician who voted to authorize an invasion gives a speech condemning another country for invading its neighbor. Everything he says about the wrongness of the invasion is correct. But there's this widespread reaction — including from people who agree the invasion is wrong — that he of all people shouldn't be the one saying it. Second, smaller scale: my cousin, who dropped out of college after one semester and has never held a job for more than six months, loves to tell his younger brother that he's "not applying himself" and "needs to take responsibility." The things he says about responsibility might be true! But coming from him, it feels like a violation of some norm, not just annoying hypocrisy.

I can think of nearby ideas — ad hominem (but that's attacking the arguer instead of the argument, and here the argument is fine), tu quoque (but that's "you do it too so you're wrong," and I'm not saying B is wrong), and hypocrisy (but that's a character trait, and what I'm tracking is a normative fact about whether B is in a position to speak). None of them quite nail it.

So my question: is there an established term for this — the loss of standing or entitlement to make a sound criticism because of one's own conduct? And if there is, I want more than just the label. I want to understand what exactly the phenomenon is and where its edges are. Give me exactly five things: (1) the name of the concept, if there is one, (2) a precise definition that distinguishes it from nearby ideas, (3) the best account of why the standing deficit is genuinely normative and not just a social queasiness we should get over, (4) a boundary case where it's genuinely unclear whether standing is lost, and (5) whether the concept applies only when the speaker is guilty of the same wrong, or also when they're complicit in a broader or different way. One or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion.
