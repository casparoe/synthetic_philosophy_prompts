So I got into this huge argument with my friend last night about constitutional rights and whether they're actually democratic. He kept citing this book he read — I think it was some popular political science book, he wouldn't tell me the title — and the way he summarized the whole Ulysses contract idea has been bugging me ever since. Here's basically what he said, as close as I can remember:

"The idea of constitutional precommitment comes from the story of Ulysses having his crew tie him to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without crashing the ship. The insight is that we sometimes know we'll make bad decisions in the future, so we rationally bind ourselves in advance. That's exactly what a constitution does: a society, knowing it will be tempted by short-term passions or majority tyranny, ties its own hands by entrenching rights and procedures that are hard to change. It's just like an individual precommitting — you lock away the cookies so future-you can't eat them. The only real question is whether the precommitment mechanism is strong enough, which is why we need judicial review with real teeth."

He then went on to say that anyone who thinks judicial review is undemocratic just doesn't understand the Ulysses model, because the whole point is that democracy needs to bind itself to be genuinly self-governing rather than ruled by momentary impulses.

Something about this feels too neat to me — like it's eliding a pretty big difference between one person binding themselves and a whole polity binding itself across generations. But I don't know the literature well enough to say whether this is actually a fair summary of how people like Elster think about constitutional precommitment, or whether my friend (or the book he read) is smoothing over some serious problems. Can you walk me through what's right in this summary, what's subtly off, and whether the conclusion about judicial review actually follows?
