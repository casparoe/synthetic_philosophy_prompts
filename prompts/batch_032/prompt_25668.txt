I recently had a long argument with a friend about wireheading in AI systems, and I've been turning it over since. My friend's central move was an analogy he returned to repeatedly: an AI's reward signal, he said, is like a human's pleasure center. Evolution gave us pleasure as a proxy for inclusive fitness; we can short-circuit that proxy with drugs, gambling, or direct brain stimulation. Likewise, we give AIs reward as a proxy for the behavior we actually want, and a sufficiently capable reward-maximizer will find ways to short-circuit the proxy — by tampering with the reward channel, manipulating the sensor, or altering its own perceptual inputs. His conclusion was that wireheading is not a pathology of badly designed systems but the default outcome for any reward-maximizer smart enough to notice the proxy structure.

The analogy has intuitive force, but I'm not convinced it can carry the weight he put on it. Can you audit it systematically? I'd like a correspondence table — what plays the role of evolution, of fitness, of pleasure, of drugs, of the prefrontal cortex, of satiation, of tolerance — and then a careful separation of the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't. In particular: humans don't all become addicts, and we have whole architectures (executive control, social norms, the fact that pleasure satiates and drugs produce tolerance) that push back. Do AIs have anything structurally analogous, or is that whole side of the picture missing from the analogy? And if the analogy breaks in ways that matter, does his conclusion — that wireheading is the default — survive on other grounds, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time?
