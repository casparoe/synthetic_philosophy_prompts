I've been working through the literature on the badness of death and I've arrived at a set of views that feel right to me individually, but I'm worried they might not all fit together. I'd like an audit. Here's where I stand:

1. Epicurus is wrong: death can be bad for the one who dies.
2. The deprivation account is correct: death is bad for the person who dies because, and only because, it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed.
3. The timing problem — if death is bad, when is it bad? — has a satisfactory answer: death's badness is an atemporal relational property. It doesn't need a temporal location, any more than the wrongness of a broken promise needs one.
4. Lucretius's symmetry argument fails. There is a genuine asymmetry between the time before we exist and the time after we exist: we have no forward-directed interests with respect to the past, but we do with respect to the future. So it's rational to fear death but not to regret having been born later than we might have been.
5. Accepting the deprivation account does not commit me to the view that we should regret not bringing into existence every merely possible person who would have a good life. There is a morally significant difference between harming an existing person and failing to benefit a merely possible one.
6. For a given death, how bad it is tracks the net goods the person is deprived of — a death that costs you 40 good years is worse than one that costs you 5.

I can feel that some of these pull against each other, but I'm not sure which tensions are real and which are merely apparent. Go through these commitments and identify the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, and say what would have to give in each case. I'm especially interested in whether (3) and (6) can both be true, and whether (4) and (5) rely on asymmetries that are compatible or in covert conflict.
