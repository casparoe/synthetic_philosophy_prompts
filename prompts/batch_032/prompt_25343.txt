At a philosophy reading group I attend, two members got into a prolonged argument about two-dimensional semantics, and I'm hoping you can help me sort out who was right about what.

The background: we had been reading Chalmers on the necessary a posteriori. The question was whether the two-dimensional framework genuinely explains how there can be necessary a posteriori truths, or whether it merely restates the phenomenon in more technical language.

Here is roughly how the exchange went.

Maya opened by saying the framework does real explanatory work. Take "water is H2O." The primary intension captures what we grasp a priori: roughly, "the watery stuff is H2O." This is false in some worlds considered as actual — worlds where the watery stuff is XYZ — so the primary intension is contingent, which explains why the statement is a posteriori. The secondary intension, evaluated with the actual world fixed, is true in all worlds considered as counterfactual, which explains why the statement is necessary. So the framework does not merely note that the statement has both properties; it shows how they arise from different dimensions of meaning.

Daniel pushed back. He said Maya's account of the primary intension already smuggles in the a posteriori element. Why does the primary intension of "water is H2O" vary across worlds considered as actual? Because we do not know a priori which substance plays the water-role. But that is precisely the fact we were trying to explain. The framework says: the statement is a posteriori because its primary intension is contingent. And why is the primary intension contingent? Because we cannot know a priori that water is H2O. That looks circular.

Maya replied that this is not circular — it is just that the framework does not purport to explain why some primary intensions are contingent and others are not. That is a separate question about the structure of epistemic space. What the framework explains is how a single statement can be both necessary and a posteriori, by showing that these properties attach to different semantic values. Before two-dimensionalism, it was genuinely puzzling how a statement could be necessary if its truth depends on empirical discovery. The framework dissolves that puzzle by distinguishing two ways of evaluating a statement across worlds.

Daniel countered that the puzzle is not dissolved, just relocated. The original puzzle was: how can empirical discovery be required to know a necessary truth? The two-dimensionalist answer is: because the primary intension is contingent. But now we need to know why the primary intension and the secondary intension come apart in just this way for statements like "water is H2O." And the answer seems to be: because of empirical facts about the actual world — specifically, that H2O rather than XYZ is the watery stuff. So we are back where we started: empirical facts determine which necessary truths hold. The framework has not explained this dependence; it has built a notation for describing it.

Maya said Daniel was demanding that the framework explain something it was never meant to explain — namely, why the actual world is the way it is. The framework explains the semantics of necessity and apriority, not the metaphysics of why H2O rather than XYZ fills the oceans. That the secondary intension depends on what the actual world is like is a feature, not a bug: it is exactly how the framework captures the empirical contribution to meaning.

Daniel replied that if the framework's explanatory ambitions are that modest, then it is not clear it does more than the pre-theoretic distinction between "what we can know by reflection" and "what we can know only by investigation" — a distinction everyone already accepted. The promise of two-dimensional semantics was to give a rigorous account of the necessary a posteriori, but on Maya's telling it simply assigns the necessity to one formal object and the aposteriority to another, without illuminating the connection between them.

At this point the discussion moved on, but I have been turning it over since. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of Maya's and Daniel's points, say whether it is correct, partially correct but missing something, or mistaken, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: who has the better case, and what — if anything — is the most important consideration that neither of them raised?
