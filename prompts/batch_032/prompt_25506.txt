I've been chewing on this for weeks and I'd really value a fresh set of eyes. I teach political philosophy — mostly grad seminars on democratic theory — and a student handed me an argument that I can't shake. It's about the Condorcet jury theorem and whether the epistemic defense of democracy proves way too much. I'll lay it out as cleanly as I can.

(1) The Condorcet jury theorem (CJT) says: for a binary choice with a correct answer, if each voter has probability p > 0.5 of voting correctly and votes are independant, then as the number of voters goes up, the probability that the majority is correct approaches 1.

(2) Many epistemic democrats appeal to CJT (or something in its neighborhood) to argue that democratic procedures are truth-tracking: if citizens are on average more competent than a coin flip, and their votes are independent enough, then majority rule will tend to produce correct decisions about justice, policy, and so on.

(3) Now here's the move: if this reasoning works for political questions, it should work for any domain where the same structural conditions hold — a binary question with a right answer, average person better than chance, independent votes.

(4) Take weather forecasting. Suppose we ask a million randomly selected adults: "Will it rain in Chicago tomorrow, yes or no?" The average person, with a smartphone and a glance at the sky, is probably slightly better than chance at this — say p = 0.53. And we can poll them independently (no deliberation, just private guesses).

(5) By CJT, the majority of a million such people will be correct with probability vanishingly close to 1.

(6) So it looks like we should be able to replace meteorologists with massive independent polls of laypeople for at least some forecasting tasks.

(7) But that's absurd. A poll of a million amateurs won't outperform a trained meteorologist with good models, and no one thinks we should dismantle the National Weather Service in favor of daily Gallup rain surveys.

(8) So either: (a) the CJT doesn't actually support the epistemic case for democracy, or (b) there's a relevant difference between political questions and weather questions that blocks the analogy, or (c) premise (4) is false in a way that also threatens premise (2).

I want to keep the epistemic case for democracy — I think there's something deeply right about the idea that democratic procedures, under decent conditions, tend to track the truth better than the alternatives. But I'm struggling to find a principled way to block the weather case without also gutting the political case. Where does this argument go wrong?
