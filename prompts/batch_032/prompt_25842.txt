In most common-law systems, the standard of proof in criminal cases is "beyond a reasonable doubt," while in civil cases it is "preponderance of the evidence" — a much lower bar. The same defendant, on the same body of evidence, can be acquitted of murder and yet found liable for wrongful death. Courts and commentators justify this asymmetry by pointing out that a false criminal conviction is worse than a false civil judgment: liberty and stigma are at stake in the one case, usually only money in the other.

I want to understand what view of knowledge, belief, or justified judgment this two-tiered practice presupposes. In particular: does the practice presuppose that whether a given body of evidence is sufficient for accepting a proposition depends on what is practically at stake in being wrong — so that you can "know" something for civil purposes but not for criminal ones? Or can a view on which epistemic standards are stakes-invariant explain the practice equally well, by treating the different standards as different institutional decision rules rather than different epistemic thresholds? And if the practice does presuppose that epistemic standards shift with stakes, is it consistent with other features of the same legal systems — for example, the rule that judges should not consider the consequences of their rulings when determining what the law is, or the fact that the same rules of evidence govern both civil and criminal trials? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
