A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about what it means for something to be true, and I am still turning it over. I am going to reconstruct the argument as faithfully as I can from my notes, and I would like you to adjudicate it — point by point, not just a global verdict. I will not tell you which side I was on.

The argument began when my friend said that the pragmatist view of truth — roughly, that truth is what works — captures something important that correspondence theories miss. I asked him to elaborate.

He said: Take a scientific claim like "electrons exist." What makes it true is not some mysterious correspondence between a sentence and a mind-independent fact. What makes it true is that the concept of electrons lets us predict and control phenomena — it works. If it stopped working, we would revise it. Truth is success in practice.

I pushed back: That seems to conflate truth with utility. A belief can be useful without being true. Someone might believe they are more competent than they actually are, and that belief might boost their performance. It works. But it is false.

He answered: William James actually considered that kind of case. If the belief really never leads to any practical failure — if it never crashes into experience — then calling it "false" is an empty gesture. What would the falsity consist in? Truth is a species of the good; a belief that consistently produces good outcomes just is true.

I said: That makes truth relative to individuals. What is useful for you might not be useful for me. If your belief in your own competence works for you but my belief in my incompetence works for me, are both true? Truth has to be public and objective.

He replied: That is exactly why Peirce's version is better than James's. Peirce defines truth as the opinion that would be reached by an ideal community of inquirers in the long run, after all the evidence is in. That is objective — it does not depend on what any individual finds useful. But here is the problem: the "long run" never arrives. We are always in the middle of inquiry. So Peirce gives us a definition of truth but no practical criterion for what to believe right now. James fills that gap.

I said: I am not sure James fills the gap so much as changes the subject. Peirce's method is the scientific method — fallibilism, self-correction, community. For empirical questions, we do not need James's "will to believe." We follow the evidence where it leads and hold our conclusions tentatively.

He said: But not all questions are empirical. James's point is that there are live, forced, momentous options where the evidence does not decide — religious belief, moral commitments, even the belief that life is worth living. In those cases, you have to decide, and James says it is legitimate to believe on passional grounds. And if acting on that belief enriches your life, then by James's lights the belief is true.

I said: That is where I think the view comes apart. James seems to be running together two different things: an ethics of belief — when it is permissible to hold a belief — and a theory of truth — what makes a belief true. Even if it is permissible to believe in God on passional grounds, that does not make God exist. The permissibility of believing P and the truth of P are different questions.

He said: But that separation is exactly what James rejects. For him, truth happens to an idea — it is something that becomes true through the process of being verified in experience. A belief that you hold on passional grounds and that proves itself in the living — that transforms your experience in ways that continue to bear fruit — that belief has become true. The distinction between epistemology and metaphysics you are insisting on is precisely the one pragmatism dissolves.

That is roughly where we left it. Please go through this exchange and adjudicate each move. For each point made by either of us, say whether it is basically right, basically wrong, or partly right and partly wrong, and explain why. Then give an overall assessment: is one of us more right than the other, or is the debate itself misframed in some way that neither of us noticed?
