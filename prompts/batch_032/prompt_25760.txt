I'm a PhD student in epistemology, preparing a unit on the epistemology of disagreement for an upper-level undergraduate seminar. The class has about 15 students and meets for 75 minutes. I want an in-class activity that makes students feel the tension between conciliationist and steadfast responses to peer disagreement before we read the papers—something where they form a judgment, discover that an epistemic peer disagrees, and then have to decide what to do, all before anyone names the positions.

The activity should be structured so that the rules, incentives, and information asymmetries generate both pulls: some students should find themselves wanting to revise substantially toward their peer, others should feel entitled to stick with their original judgment, and ideally a given student feels both pulls at different moments. The debrief then names conciliationism and steadfastness and maps specific moments in the activity onto the arguments we will read.

Constraints: no real money, though small prizes are fine. The activity itself should run about 35–40 minutes, leaving the rest for debrief and discussion. Students should not hear the terms "conciliationism" or "steadfastness" until the debrief.

Please give me:

(1) The full activity design—materials, setup, rules, a round-by-round timeline, and the scoring or payoff scheme.

(2) A debrief script that walks through what happened and maps it onto the philosophical positions, with specific questions to ask students at each stage.

(3) An honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.

(4) A candid assessment of which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot capture and that I will need to supply myself in discussion.

Lead with the activity design itself in the first sentence—no preface, no restating of the question.
