I'm taking a course on the metaphysics of social kinds, and one analogy that keeps surfacing in the readings and in seminar is that race is like money. The thought is that both are socially constructed — they depend on collective attitudes, conventions, and practices rather than on mind-independent natural facts — yet both are undeniably real in their causal effects. The analogy is typically deployed against the view that if race isn't biological, it isn't real: after all, nobody would say money isn't real just because its value depends on collective acceptance rather than on intrinsic physical properties of the paper or metal.

I want to audit this analogy properly. Can you walk me through what maps onto what — the correspondence between the two domains — and then identify where the mapping breaks? I am especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do (supporting the claim that race is real in roughly the way money is) and which are merely superficial. For instance, money has a fairly explicit institutional structure with codified rules, designated authorities, and clear criteria for what counts as currency; race, by contrast, is messier, more contested, and lacks a central institution that declares racial categories. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or does it just mean the analogy needs to be stated more carefully? More broadly, does the conclusion — that race is real in the same general sense money is — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing more argumentative work than it can bear? If it does not hold up well, is there a better analogy, or does the conclusion itself need to be reformulated?
