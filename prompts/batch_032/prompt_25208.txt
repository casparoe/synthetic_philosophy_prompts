I am a fifth-year PhD student, and next month I present my dissertation's central argument at the department colloquium — the last major hurdle before I go on the job market. The argument has become unexpectedly personal for me: I began the project sympathetic to Humean approaches to laws of nature, but the more I worked through the literature on dispositions, the more I became convinced that the problem of finks and masks is fatal to the conditional analysis in a way that the standard replies cannot fix. My paper argues that attempts to rescue the conditional analysis by relativizing the stimulus-manifestation conditional to "normal conditions" or "the absence of interfering factors" either presuppose the dispositional structure they aim to reduce, or else become so gerrymandered that the analysis forfeits its reductive ambitions. I then contend that this failure puts genuine pressure on Humean accounts of laws, since those accounts typically require a reductive analysis of dispositions to avoid primitive modality.

The audience will be my department's faculty and graduate students. Roughly half work in metaphysics — several publish on dispositions, powers, and grounding, and one is a well-known dispositional essentialist. The other half work in philosophy of science, and most are Humeans about laws who are skeptical of appeals to primitive modality. There are also two epistemologists who rarely speak in metaphysics talks unless the discussion turns to the logic of counterfactuals, at which point they reliably have sharp objections.

I need a Q&A prep sheet. Please give me the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrased as my colleagues would actually phrase them. For each, provide a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
