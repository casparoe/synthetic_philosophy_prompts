I'm giving a paper at a workshop on early modern mathematics and philosophy next month, and I'm nervous about the Q&A. My thesis is that Hobbes's geometrical project was philosophically serious and not just the crankery it's usually dismissed as. Specifically, I'm arguing that his insistence on kinematic construction — lines as paths of moving points, surfaces as paths of moving lines — wasn't just an eccentricity but a genuine alternative foundation for geometry, one that makes mathematical knowledge causal knowledge (you know a theorem because you know how to construct the figure). And I'm arguing that his rejection of algebraic methods as "not proper demonstration" follows coherently from this commitment, even though it led him to some empirically false claims, like his conviction that he'd squared the circle. The audience is about two-thirds historians of mathematics, one-third historians of philosophy.
