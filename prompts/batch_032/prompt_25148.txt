I've been trying to sort out what I think about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real in any morally or metaphysically serious sense, and my gut keeps giving me different answers depending on the case. I'd like you to help me find the principle — or small set of principles — that best captures the pattern, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I'd have to give up.

Here are the cases I keep coming back to. (1) In an MMORPG, a player spends hundreds of hours earning a rare sword; another player exploits a bug to steal it from their inventory. My gut says this is a genuine wrong — the victim has really lost something, not just within the game's fiction. (2) A digital artist creates a unique 3D sculpture file stored only on their laptop; a hacker deletes it with no backups. My gut says: real destruction, morally on a par with smashing a physical sculpture. (3) Same artist, same file, but this time the hacker copies it and leaks it online. The original still exists. My gut says this is a wrong, but it doesn't feel like the same kind of wrong as the deletion — the artist hasn't lost the object, they've lost control over it. (4) In a VR social space, a user makes gestures that simulate groping another user's avatar; the recipient feels violated and describes it as a real assault. My gut says this is a real harm — not identical to physical assault, but real in a way that matters for how we should regulate the space and treat the perpetrator. (5) In a single-player RPG, I kill a non-player character who begs for mercy. My gut says this isn't a harm at all — no victim, nothing wrong. (6) A community in a shared virtual world builds an elaborate memorial to a deceased member; a griefer destroys it. My gut says this is a real harm, and worse than the sword theft in case 1 — the memorial has a kind of sacredness the sword lacks. (7) A player scams another out of virtual currency purchased with real money, worth about $50. My gut says: real theft, roughly as serious as stealing $50 cash. (8) Same scam, but the currency was earned entirely through gameplay with no cash-out mechanism — it can only buy in-game items. My gut says: still a wrong, but noticeably less serious than case 7. And I'm not sure I can defend that distinction.

What I'm after is a principle — or a small set of them — that draws the line between the virtual things that matter and the ones that don't. Is the key factor replaceability? Connection to real-world value? The presence of another person's intentions or felt experience? The type of entity involved (a person's avatar vs. an NPC vs. a pure digital object)? Something about the social practices the object is embedded in? I'd like you to state the best principle you can that fits as many of my verdicts as possible, flag which verdicts it forces me to abandon or revise, and apply it to a few cases I haven't listed: What about destroying an AI-generated artwork no human has ever seen? What about a virtual assault carried out by an AI agent rather than a human? And if the principle that captures my verdicts ends up messy and disjunctive, I'd like to know whether there's a simpler one that does almost as well, and what the trade-off is.
