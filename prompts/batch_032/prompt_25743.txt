So my reading group has been stuck on the same argument for like two months. Half of us are drawn to Scanlon-style contractualism — wrongness is about principles no one could reasonably reject, and the test is supposed to be pairwise: you compare the strength of one person's complaint against another's, not the number of complaints. The other half think this can't possibly work, because it seems to rule out aggregation. Like, if you have to choose between saving one person from a horrible death and saving a thousand people from losing a finger, the Scanlonian seems stuck saying "each of the thousand has a weaker claim than the one, so it's not obvious we should save the thousand." That just sounds deranged to the aggregation side.

We want to stop going in circles and actually figure out where the real disagreements are. So here's what I want you to write: the document that would come out of a serious adversarial collaboration between a committed Scanlonian contractualist and a committed aggregation-friendly consequentialist (or whatever label fits — someone who thinks the numbers count directly in moral reasoning).

The document should include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Stuff about what the problem is, what's at stake, what constraints any adequate view has to satisfy. Not just "we both agree suffering is bad" — make it substantive.

2. A disagreements table where each row states a precise point of disagreement, worded so each side would accept the formulation of it's own position. The Scanlonian's view should be stated in terms a Scanlonian would nod along with, and same for the aggregation person.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where, if you settled them, at least one side would update. I'm guessing one crux is whether reasonable rejection is fundamentally pairwise or whether it can consider the aggregate weight of many individual claims. Another might be something about what makes a complaint "stronger" in the first place — is it just the size of the harm to that person, or does it matter how the harm compares to what others are facing?

4. For each crux, give at least one concrete case or line of reasoning that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or judgement recorded. Don't just describe the case — have each side go on record about what they think the right answer is and why they're opponent gets it wrong.

If you find that part of their disagreement is actually verbal — like they're using "reasonable rejection" to mean different things — say so and explain. But don't paper over genuine disagreement just to make things tidy. The point is to figure out what's left once you strip away the talking-past-each-other stuff.
