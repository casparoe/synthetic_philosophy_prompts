[Exam question]
Explain how Kant's Formula of Universal Law generates moral duties, distinguishing where appropriate between different kinds of duty. Illustrate your answer with at least one example of your own devising (i.e., not one discussed in lecture).

[Student answer]
Kant's Formula of Universal Law states: "Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you at the same time can will that it become a universal law." A maxim is the subjective principle on which an agent acts — it specifies both what the agent is doing and the end or reason for which they are doing it. To test whether an action is permissible, you ask whether you could consistently will that the maxim on which you propose to act become a universal law that everyone follows. If you cannot, the action is morally impermissible.

Kant identifies two distinct ways a maxim can fail this test, and these correspond to two different kinds of duty. First, some maxims cannot even be conceived as universal laws without generating a contradiction. These give rise to perfect duties — duties that admit of no exception and that one must always fulfill, such as the duty not to make false promises. For example, consider the maxim: "When I need money, I will make a lying promise to repay it, even though I know I cannot do so." If this maxim were universalized, everyone would make lying promises whenever they needed money. But the practice of promising depends on a background of trust that promises will be kept. In a world where everyone followed this maxim, the very institution of promising would collapse — no one would believe anyone's promises, so the concept of a promise would become incoherent. Since the maxim's universalization destroys the practice the maxim itself relies on, it yields a contradiction in conception, and acting on it violates a perfect duty.

Second, some maxims can be conceived as universal laws without any logical contradiction, but a rational agent still cannot will them as universal laws. These give rise to imperfect duties — duties that are binding but admit of latitude in how and to what extent they are fulfilled, such as the duty to develop one's talents. For example, consider the maxim: "I will spend my life in idle amusement rather than cultivating any of my abilities." We can conceive of a world in which everyone follows this maxim — there is no conceptual contradiction in imagining a universalized practice of idleness. However, a rational agent cannot will such a world, because a world where no one develops any talents would be a worse world, with less art, less knowledge, and less human flourishing. Since any rational agent must prefer a better world to a worse one, willing this maxim as universal law would contradict rational volition. The maxim therefore fails the test, and we have an imperfect duty to develop our talents.

In summary, the Formula of Universal Law sorts maxims into three categories: those that pass the test (permissible actions), those that fail by contradiction in conception (violations of perfect duty), and those that fail by contradiction in willing (violations of imperfect duty).
