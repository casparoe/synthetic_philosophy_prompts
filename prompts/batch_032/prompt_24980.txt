I've been teaching practical reason on and off for about fifteen years, and there's a conversational move I keep seeing that I've never found a satisfactory label for — maybe you can help me pin it down, becuase it's driving me a little nuts.

Here's the thing. Two people are arguing about whether someone — call him Paul — ought to do something: leave his job, give money to a cause, visit his difficult mother, whatever. The first person says Paul ought to do it. Paul (or someone defending him) says "but that's not something Paul cares about — it doesn't connect to anything he values." Now, the first person has two ways to go. Path A: "Actually, given what Paul does care about — his health, say, or his desire to think well of himself — he does have a reason to do this, he just hasn't seen the connection." Path B: "It doesn't matter what Paul cares about; he ought to do it regardless — the reason stands on its own."

What interests me is this: in real arguments, people switch from Path A to Path B midstream without seeming to notice they've done it. They'll try to ground the reason in the agent's existing psychology, fail to make it stick, and then just assert the reason as if the failure never happened — but they'll phrase it in a way that sounds like a continuation of the same point. Or they'll start with the freestanding normative claim, get pushback, and retreat to "well, surely you care about not being a hypocrite, and here's why this follows from that" — again, no acknowledgment that the kind of reason being offered has changed. I've started calling it "reason-switching" in my own head, but that's too clumsy and doesn't capture the stealthiness of it.

I know this is somewhere in the neighborhood of the Williams/Korsgaard/internalism/externalism terrain, but I'm not asking for a summary of that debate. What I want is narrower: is there a name for this specific dialectical move — the unmarked slide between grounding a reason in an agent's existing motivations and asserting it independently of them? And if there isn't, what should we call it, and how do we distinguish it precisely from ordinary equivocation, from shifting the burden of proof, and from simply changing the subject? I'm after the carving, not the label. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert.
