I've been working through an argument in infinite ethics that a visiting speaker sketched at our colloquium, and I cannot locate the mistake, though the conclusion strikes me as unacceptable. The argument runs as follows:

(1) If the universe is spatially infinite and roughly uniform, it contains infinitely many morally considerable beings.

(2) For any two states of the world that differ only in the welfare of finitely many beings, standard cardinal arithmetic tells us the total quantity of welfare is the same in both states: adding or removing a finite quantity does not change an infinite cardinality.

(3) An act is morally right or wrong only if it makes a difference to the overall value of the world — that is, only if the world in which the act is performed differs in total value from the world in which it is not.

(4) Therefore, if the universe is infinite in the relevant sense, no finite act — no act we can actually perform — is morally right or wrong.

(5) But we are certain that some finite acts are morally right or wrong (e.g., torturing a child for amusement is wrong, and the wrongness does not depend on unsettled cosmological facts).

(6) So at least one of (1), (2), or (3) must be false.

I am inclined to hold onto (5) at all costs. But I cannot see a clean way out. Rejecting (1) means betting the entire force of morality on a speculative cosmological claim — if it turns out the universe is infinite, morality evaporates. Rejecting (2) seems to require revising Cantorian arithmetic, which looks like a non-starter. Rejecting (3) means abandoning the idea that morality is about making the world better or worse, which seems to gut consequentialism and perhaps to threaten any view that takes welfare seriously. Yet some philosophers claim to have a way out. Which premise should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
