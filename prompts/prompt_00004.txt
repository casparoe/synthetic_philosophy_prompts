I've been reading David Hume's writings on probability, induction, and his skeptical arguments about inferring future events from past experience, and I keep wondering how he would have reacted to the doomsday argument. The argument, in its Carter-Leslie form, uses Bayesian reasoning and something like the self-sampling assumption to suggest that we should update toward believing humanity's extinction is nearer than we might naively think, based purely on our own temporal position among all humans who will ever have lived. Hume was deeply concerned with the justification of inductive and probabilistic reasoning about unobserved cases, and he was famously suspicious of arguments that smuggle in unwarranted assumptions about the uniformity of nature or about our epistemic position being "typical" in some cosmically loaded sense.

Given his views on custom and habit as the real basis of our inductive practices, his skepticism about a priori reasoning yielding substantive conclusions about matters of fact, and his general resistance to arguments that purport to derive strong conclusions from thin premises, do you think Hume would have accepted the doomsday argument's core inference (from "I am a random sample among all observers" to a probabilistic update about humanity's lifespan)? Would he have diagnosed the self-sampling assumption as another instance of reasoning beyond what experience licenses, similar to his critique of causal necessity? I'd be curious to hear a reconstruction of how his epistemological commitments would bear on this specifically modern puzzle, including whether his distinction between demonstrative and probable reasoning gives us any traction on where exactly the argument would go wrong (if it does) by his lights.
