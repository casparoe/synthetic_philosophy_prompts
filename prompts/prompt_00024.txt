I've been working through the surrogate goals literature in a reading group on acausal interactions, and something is bugging me that I can't quite resolve. The basic idea of surrogate goals is that an agent facing potential threats from a would-be extortionist can protect its "true" values by publicly (or credibly) committing to treat some other, less important goal as if it were a top priority—so that any threat-maker is incentivized to target the surrogate instead of the real target, since the agent has structured its incentives to make surrogate-threats just as costly to make (for the threatener) as they're not worth carrying out.

Give me an example that illustrates the following conceptual wrinkle: how does an agent adopt a surrogate goal in a way that's genuinely credible to a sophisticated threatener, without that very credibility-generating mechanism making the surrogate goal "load-bearing" enough that it effectively becomes a second true vulnerability rather than a decoy? I want a concrete scenario (it doesn't have to be about AI—could be a diplomatic, evolutionary, or interpersonal case) where the mechanism the agent uses to make its surrogate commitment believable (reputation, precommitment devices, emotional investment, etc.) itself creates a new exploitable target, so that the surrogate goal strategy seems to just relocate the vulnerability rather than neutralize it. Then say a bit about whether you think there's a principled way out of this regress for a sufficiently reflective agent, or whether surrogate goals are best understood as merely shifting the problem rather than solving it.
