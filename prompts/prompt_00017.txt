I was working through the units of selection literature for a seminar paper, and I keep getting stuck on a move that gene selectionists make that seems to me either trivially true or substantively false, depending on how it's cashed out. Dawkins and others argue that genes are the fundamental unit of selection because they are the only entities with sufficient longevity, fecundity, and copying-fidelity to serve as long-term "replicators," while organisms and groups are mere transient "vehicles" or "interactors" that get assembled and disassembled every generation. My worry is that this replicator/interactor distinction quietly presupposes what it's trying to prove: of course genes look like the privileged unit if you've already decided that persistence-through-copying is the criterion that matters, but a group selectionist could just as easily insist that what matters is differential reproductive success of the actual causally efficacious units in the selection process, in which case interactors (organisms, or even groups) have a much better claim to being "what selection acts on," with genes merely serving as the bookkeeping device that tracks the results.

I'd like you to argue for the gene-centric view against this objection. Specifically, defend the claim that genes, and not organisms or groups, are the true units of selection, in a way that doesn't just stipulate the replicator criterion by fiat but gives independent, principled reasons for thinking replicators rather than interactors deserve the title. Feel free to engage with Hull's replicator/interactor terminology, Sober and Lewontin's causal-process objections to genic selectionism, or Okasha's multi-level selection framework if it helps sharpen the argument.
