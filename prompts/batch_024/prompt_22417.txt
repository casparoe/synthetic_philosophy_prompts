I spent thirty-five years as a population geneticist, and I worked alongside a man named Gerald Voss for the better part of the first fifteen of them — first as his postdoc, and then as a junior colleague in the same department. He died on Tuesday at eighty-three. Only in the last two years, since my retirement, have I begun to read philosophy seriously — first the history and philosophy of science, and then, almost by accident, the philosophy of biology — and I find that I keep returning to the units of selection debate, which was, I now realize, the whole of Gerald's career. He was one of the last working scientists who argued, in the strong sense, for group selection: that natural selection could operate at the level of groups of individuals, and that this was not merely a restatement of kin selection or inclusive fitness. He worked on the social amoeba Dictyostelium, on the cheater mutants that decline to build the stalk and instead ride up in the spore head, and he spent the 1980s and 1990s trying to show that such systems were counterexamples to the "selfish gene" orthodoxy. For most of those decades the orthodoxy prevailed, and he was treated as a marginal figure; I was close enough to watch, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since.

I am giving the eulogy at his memorial service next Saturday. The room will be mixed: a handful of evolutionary biologists who know the debate intimately and who will catch every reference, and the rest of his family and his friends from the department, who will not. I need the eulogy to comfort them — his daughter in particular has told me she wants the room to understand that her father's life's work mattered — but I also need it to say nothing that Gerald himself would have considered false. He was an empiricist to the bone and he despised flattery, and he would have hated a eulogy that told the room he had been vindicated by the modern revival of multilevel selection, because he knew, as I did, that the revival is not the same as his theory: the equivalence results that now reconcile inclusive fitness with multilevel selection dissolve the very conflict on which he defined his career, and the Nowak et al. paper of 2010, which he read and underlined, attacked a reading of Hamilton's rule that he had never actually endorsed. What I want the eulogy to do is what I have only recently, in my philosophy reading, learned to name — to honor the question rather than the answer, and, if I am honest, to say something true about the difference between a unit of selection and a level of selection, which is the distinction he spent his life trying to get people to see. I am not sure how to say any of that to a room of grieving people who are not philosophers, and I am not sure how to keep it from sounding like a lecture to the few in the room who are.

Please write the eulogy. It should run about five minutes when spoken. The units of selection debate should do real structural work in the speech — it should shape what can and cannot be said about Gerald's career, not merely decorate it — and it should land as affection, not as a correction.
