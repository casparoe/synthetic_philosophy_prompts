I recently retired after four decades working as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and in my newfound spare time I've been working through the metaphysics of science literature—Lewis, Armstrong, Maudlin, Carroll, and more recently some papers by Barry Loewer defending the Humean Best-System Analysis against the so-called "circularity" or "explanatory impotence" objection. The objection, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: if laws are nothing over and above the most economical and informative summary of the total Humean mosaic of particular facts, then it seems laws can't genuinely explain the very regularities they merely redescribe—explanation seems to require some asymmetric, non-supervenient relation of "governing" that the Humean has explicitly forsworn. Loewer and others reply that this worry conflates metaphysical grounding with the ordinary explanatory practice of subsuming events under general patterns, and that non-backtracking counterfactuals can still do real explanatory work even on a Humean supervenience base.

I'd like to see this dispute worked out in more dialogue form than the papers give it, since I find that watching two people actually needle each other clarifies the stakes better than reading position statements in sequence. Could you write a dialogue between a convinced Humean (Best-Systems style) and an anti-Humean who holds that laws involve genuine relations of natural necessitation (or some other robust governing relation, primitivist or dispositionalist, whichever you find easiest to make precise) as they argue specifically about whether Humean laws can explain anything at all? I'd like each side to press the other on concrete cases—perhaps something like why a particular conserved quantity is conserved, or why a specific counterfactual about a dropped object is true—rather than staying at the level of slogans, and I want neither side to simply concede or fail to answer the other's sharpest objection.
